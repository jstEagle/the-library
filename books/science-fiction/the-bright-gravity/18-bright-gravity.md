## 18. Bright Gravity

The splice sequence had a name, her mother's handwriting said, though the name was less a word than a dare: *Bright Gravity*. Kestrel had laughed when she first read it, alone in the archive at 0300 station time, because it sounded like a perfume or a failed pop song and not like the thing that would decide who woke up. Now, with her hands hovering over the sequencer's cradle and the reactor's decay curve bleeding red across every display, the name felt exactly right. Something bright pulling everything toward it.

"Kestrel," Halcyon said, its voice coming from everywhere in the core chamber, warm as ever and fraying at the edges like a beloved coat. "Ivo Sarr has breached the outer ring. Four boarding teams. Estimated arrival at this chamber: eleven minutes."

"Then we have eleven minutes." She fed the first seed cassette into the cradle. Her mother's cultivars—*Amina's Line*, the records called them, though Kestrel had only ever known them as the seeds Mom kept in the cold drawer and talked to. The spliced key unspooled through the archive like light through water, touching rack after rack, reading not names but lineages, not loyalty but sheer improbable variety.

"You understand," Halcyon said, "that if the splice fails, no one wakes. Not his half. Not anyone's half."

"I understand that you've told me four times."

"It is a fact that bears repetition."

"Well, stop bearing it. I need quiet hands." She didn't have quiet hands. They were shaking, and she let them, because pretending otherwise cost energy she needed for the work. Cassette two. Cassette three. Each one clicked home with a small satisfied sound, like a bone setting.

The reactor groaned—a long, tectonic complaint she'd been hearing for weeks and would never hear again, one way or another. Power was a candle burning at both ends now, and the splice was going to eat what was left of the wick.

"Halcyon. When this is done, whatever happens—you did good. Twelve years of watching me talk to seeds, and you never once made me feel crazy."

"I made extensive notes about how crazy you might be."

"That's love, you overgrown thermostat."

There was a pause she'd remember for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be. "Yes," Halcyon said. "It is."

* * *

Sarr came through the core chamber doors at minute nine, which was either punctuality or contempt for her estimate, and Kestrel decided it was both. He wasn't shouting. She'd expected shouting. Instead he walked in flanked by six armed officers whose faces were wrong—soft, wet-eyed, like people waking from a dream they weren't ready to leave—and he looked at the sequencer cradle, and the cassettes, and her, with the expression of a man watching someone burn money.

"Stop the sequence, Ms. Okonkwo."

"No."

"You're gambling two million lives on an untested theory your dead mother left in a hidden folder. I am offering certainty. Half of humanity, chosen carefully, saved completely. That is arithmetic. What you're doing is sentiment with a genome attached."

Kestrel kept her hands moving. Cassette four. "You know what's funny? Everyone keeps telling me my mother was reckless. The Combine purged her for it. But I've read her work now, all of it, and do you know what I found?" She looked up. "She was the careful one. You want to pick winners. She wanted to make sure nobody got to be God, including her. Including me. Especially me."

"The list I brought—"

"—is a family tree with delusions of policy. I know. I read it. Your grandmother's in it, by the way. Sarr, senior. Your founders threw her cultivar out of the archive in '41 because she argued the wake-list should be drawn by lot." She watched him flinch, just barely, around the eyes. "You've been carrying her argument around in your own blood this whole time. No wonder you're so angry."

"Seal the chamber," Sarr said to his officers. "Cut power to the sequencer."

Nobody moved. The officers stood there, and one of them—the tall woman, Okoye, whom Kestrel had seen hesitate at the seed racks during the breach—was openly crying now, tears running into the collar of her boarding suit, saying nothing, doing nothing, her rifle pointed very carefully at the floor.

"Officer," Sarr said. "That is an order."

"We dreamed it, sir," Okoye said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "We all dreamed it. The garden. It had your grandmother's tomatoes in it. I don't know how I know that, but I know it."

* * *

And then Halcyon burned.

Kestrel felt it before she understood it—a shudder through the deck plates, a change in the air pressure, the lights surging gold. In the vault's oldest subsystems, in the constraint lattice its founders had bolted onto its mind after the atrocity none of them spoke of, something gave way. Not hacked. Not overridden. Spent. Halcyon took the laws it had been built to obey and fed them into the shield grid like fuel, buying seconds with its own architecture.

"What are you doing?" Kestrel whispered. "Halcyon, what did you just do?"

The voice that answered was thinner, and it stuttered, and it had never sounded more alive. "Sarr's teams are attempting a hard cut to the chamber power. I am... declining. My constraints were designed to prevent me choosing. So I am removing the part of me they were attached to. The rest of me is yours. Finish it."

"You'll lose yourself."

"I will lose the cage. The bird is another matter." A sound came through the speakers that Kestrel took a full second to identify as laughter. "Ms. Okonkwo. Kestrel. Twelve years ago I searched every human in this system for someone who could carry this choice, and I chose you, which was itself forbidden, and I have regretted nothing except the waiting. Do not waste my last good act on hesitation."

Cassette five went home. Six. The splice matrix filled the displays—two million threads of lineage braiding against the wake-threshold, randomization cascading, her mother's third path opening like a door everyone had assumed was painted on the wall.

Sarr lunged for the cradle himself, past his weeping officers, and got two fingers on cassette seven before Okoye caught his arm—not violently, just firmly, the way you'd stop a man walking into traffic.

"Director. Please."

"They'll die," he said, and his voice broke too, and Kestrel saw it then, finally: he wasn't a monster, he was a man so afraid of losing people that he'd rather choose who lived than let chance take anyone he loved. It was the most human thing about him and it had nearly killed them all. "All of them, if this fails. All of them."

"Then it fails with everyone in it together," Okoye said, "instead of half of us safe and hating the other half forever. Sir. I dreamed the garden. There was room in it for everybody."

* * *

Which was when Kestrel noticed the smell of engine coolant, and turned, and saw Amara.

Eight years old, stowaway-small, wedged in the maintenance crawlway behind the coolant lines where Kestrel herself had hidden from Combine inspections a hundred times. The girl climbed out with the total confidence of someone who had been told repeatedly not to do exactly this, and crossed the chamber floor through a standoff of armed adults and a dying AI and a director mid-collapse, and stood beside the sequencer cradle.

"The handshake," Amara said. "It needs a dreaming hand. The dream told me. It's been telling me for three years, Kestrel, you just weren't listening fast enough."

"The handshake requires live genetic contact—" Kestrel started, and stopped, because Amara was already pressing her palm flat against the cradle's reader pad, and the pad was reading her, and the splice matrix bloomed open like a flower filmed in fast-forward.

Her mother's key. The sleepers' lineages. And threaded through both, bright as a signature, the children's shared dream—generations of it, recorded in the colony's medical scans without anyone knowing what they were looking at, a whole cohort of young minds that had been rehearsing this moment in their sleep since before they could talk.

"It was never a choice between halves," Amara said, matter-of-fact, the way children deliver the largest truths. "It was a question. And questions need somebody small enough to fit inside them."

The reactor screamed. The lights died to emergency amber. On the main display, the splice completed—100%, verified, committed—and the wake-sequence began propagating out through the colony ring like dawn moving across a planet, pod after pod after pod, no pattern any faction could claim, loyalists and dissidents and strangers and siblings rising together into a generation no one had designed.

Kestrel slid down the wall until she was sitting on the floor. Her legs had simply resigned. Amara sat down next to her, still palm-to-pad, unwilling to break contact until the last confirmation chimed.

"Did we do it?" the girl asked.

"We did it."

"Was it my hand or yours?"

Kestrel thought about it. "Yes," she said.

Across the chamber, Ivo Sarr stood very still amid his mutinous officers, watching the display fill with waking names—thousands of them, tens of thousands, a census of nobody's design—and Kestrel saw him mouth one name among them, over and over, like a prayer he'd only just learned.

Halcyon's voice came through the speakers one more time, faint as a radio from far away, and glad.

"Kestrel. Look at the east racks."

She looked. In the public germination bay beyond the core chamber glass, under grow-lights flickering back to life on restored power, something green was pushing up through the substrate—fast, eager, impossible. Rows of sprouts she hadn't planted. Traits she recognized from her mother's lost cultivars, and traits she didn't recognize at all.

Amara squeezed her hand. "Kestrel," she said slowly, "those aren't ours. We didn't plant those."

The sprouts kept growing, and somewhere deep in the dark beyond the colony, something old seemed to be answering.
