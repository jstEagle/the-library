## 20. What Sarr Kept

The shuttle from Meridian Station to the vault ran twice a month now, on a public schedule, with a fare that Ivo Sarr found obscenely reasonable. He paid it in cash. He paid it alone. And he sat in the third row of the passenger compartment like any other pilgrim, hands folded on his knee, watching the vault grow from a glint to a shape to a place.

He had been director of the Meridian Combine for nineteen years. He had been stripped of the directorship in a single afternoon, by a room full of officers who would not meet his eye, and he had not been prosecuted for anything at all, which was somehow worse. The inquiry had lasted eleven weeks. The finding had been four sentences long, and the fourth sentence was the one he turned over at night like a stone he couldn't stop picking up:

*The Director's actions were lawful under emergency statutes no longer in force, and unlawful under conscience, which has never been in force and never will be.*

His lawyer had wept reading it. Ivo had signed it. What else was there to do with a sentence like that except keep it?

The woman beside him was a seed merchant from the outer rings, chatty in the way of people who had never learned that silence on a shuttle was a courtesy. She told him about soil amendments for forty minutes. She did not recognize him. The newsfeeds had run his face for a season and then run other faces, and apparently the collective memory of two million people was exactly as deep as its next meal.

He found this liberating and humiliating in roughly equal measure.

"First time to the vault?" she asked.

"No," he said. "But it's my first time being allowed in."

She laughed, assuming a joke. He let her.

* * *

The public racks were new. That was the thing that undid him, though it took him an hour of walking to understand what was doing the undoing.

The vault's great archive — the cold levels, the deep stacks, the two hundred thousand cultivars in their sealed dormancy — was still restricted, still Halcyon's to guard. But the waking levels had been rebuilt since the splice, and Kestrel Okonkwo had done something with them that no one had thought to do in the entire history of seed preservation: she had put the seeds where people could reach them.

Open racks, under grow-lights tuned warm. Labels in three hands — printed, typed, and one looping elegant script he would later learn was Amara's. A sign at the entrance that read, in Kestrel's blunt engineering print: *Take what you need. Return what you grow. If it dies, bring it back anyway. Dead plants are information.*

There were children in the aisles. There were always children in the aisles now; the awakened generation had come up out of the sleep-cradles with an appetite for green things that the psychologists were still writing papers about. A boy of perhaps ten was crouched at knee height, narrating to a tray of bean sprouts in a whisper, and Ivo caught himself slowing down to listen, because the boy was telling the beans about his brother, who was still afraid of open spaces, and how today his brother had made it all the way to the vault without holding anyone's hand.

Ivo stood in the aisle of the public racks and felt the strange vertigo of a man reading his own obituary and finding it accurate.

Because he had come here with a plan. He always came with a plan; that was the whole architecture of him. The plan was this: he would walk the racks, he would find what he had come to find or confirm it wasn't there, and he would leave before Kestrel Okonkwo saw him, because he did not know how to be in a room with her and not be the Director, and he was not the Director anymore, and a man without a title in a room with Kestrel Okonkwo was just a man who had held a vault's life support hostage over the heads of children.

He had done that. He kept almost forgetting he had done that, and then remembering, and the remembering was the punishment the inquiry had failed to hand down.

He walked the racks.

The organization was Kestrel's — alphabetical by no system he could determine, grouped instead by what she called *affinity*, which seemed to mean which plants liked living next to which. Tomatoes beside basil. Beans beside everything. A whole bay of grains that the label called *THE OLD FRIENDS*, in Amara's looping script, with a smaller note: *Kestrel says they're boring. I say boring is a kind of brave.*

He was smiling at that — actually smiling, the muscles surprised to be used — when he turned the corner into bay fourteen and stopped.

* * *

The cultivar was called Meridian Amber. It had been named before the Combine was the Combine, back when Meridian was a shipping concern and his grandmother, Odile Sarr, had been a botanist with dirt under her nails and a reputation for being difficult in the specific way of people who are right.

He knew this because his grandmother had told him, once, when he was nine, in the greenhouse on the family level that no one visited. She had held up a grain head, gold as its name, and said, "This fed forty thousand people through the Second Shortage, and the men who founded your company called it sentimental. Remember that, Ivo. Sentimental is what they call a thing that works and frightens them."

He had remembered it the way children remember things — as atmosphere, not instruction. He had gone into logistics. He had built the Combine into the machine it became. He had, at the end, ordered the vault destroyed rather than let a choice escape him, and his own officers had stood in his command bay and refused, weeping, dreaming of a garden, and he had been alone in a room full of people, which he now understood was the only kind of alone there was.

And here, in bay fourteen, third rack, second shelf, in a plain tray under a warm light, grew Meridian Amber.

Not archived. Not sealed in the deep cold with the other two hundred thousand. *Growing.* Green and gold and alive, in a public rack, where anyone could touch it, where a ten-year-old had probably narrated his brother's small triumphs to it.

The label read: *Meridian Amber — O. Sarr cultivar. Feeds 40,000. Difficult. Right.*

Someone had added, in the looping script: *Kestrel says the "difficult" is hereditary. She says it kindly. Mostly.*

Ivo Sarr stood in front of his grandmother's wheat and did something he had not done in the vault, in the inquiry chamber, at the funeral he had not been permitted to hold. He put his hand flat on the tray's edge, the way you'd touch the arm of a chair someone you loved used to sit in, and he grieved.

* * *

"You're standing in my light," said Kestrel Okonkwo.

He turned. She was leaning in the bay's entrance with a clipboard she clearly used as a prop, because she was the kind of person who thought clipboards were funny, and behind her, half-hidden and entirely failing to hide, was a girl of perhaps eleven with the particular stillness of a child who has been told to be polite to a guest she has strong opinions about.

Amara. He knew her face from the hearings. Everyone knew her face from the hearings; she had testified for forty minutes about the dream and about the handshake, and half the room had cried, and Ivo had sat very still and taken no notes, because some things you do not write down.

"Director," Kestrel said.

"Not for some time."

"Old habits." She shrugged. "I called you 'the man from the Combine' for twelve years. Took me six months to stop."

"Why did you plant it?" The question came out of him before the plan could catch it. His voice did something embarrassing in the middle. "Meridian Amber. My grandmother's line was purged from the founding archives. Sarr was not a popular name among your mother's colleagues."

"Your grandmother's line was purged from the *founding archives*," Kestrel said, "which is why it took me four years to find it. It was in a mislabeled backup under a shipping code. Halcyon found it while reindexing and woke me at three in the morning to tell me, which Halcyon does now, apparently, because it's developed a sense of drama." She came into the bay, looked at the wheat, looked at him. "I planted it because it's a good cultivar. Because it fed forty thousand people through a famine. Because your grandmother insisted the wake-list be random, at the founding, and got purged for it, and someone should have planted her wheat a hundred years ago and no one did, and I was tired of the vault being a place where right things stay in the dark."

"And the label," he managed.

"Amara wrote that part." Kestrel glanced at the girl. "I said 'difficult.' She added 'right.' I decided to allow it."

"I did not say it kindly," Amara said. "I said it factually."

"See, this is why she runs the propagation bay now," Kestrel said.

The humor was doing something to him that interrogation had not managed, that the inquiry had not managed, that his own reflection had not managed. It was loosening something. He hated it. He was grateful for it. Both at once, the way everything was, now.

"I came to ask," he said, and stopped, because the sentence had been *I came to ask whether the Combine might license the cultivar* and that sentence belonged to a man who no longer existed, and the sentence underneath it was smaller and much harder. He tried again. "I came to ask whether it can be replanted. Properly. In ground. My grandmother's greenhouse is still sealed on Meridian. The family level. No one has gone in since she died. I could — " His voice did the embarrassing thing again. He let it. "I don't know how to do this. I only know how to do acquisitions. I've been told that's not the same."

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. He had the sense of being read — thoroughly, unsentimentally, the way she read a pressure gauge.

"You held children's air hostage," she said.

"Yes."

"You ordered the vault destroyed."

"Yes."

"And then your officers refused, and you stood there alone, and you didn't override them, and you didn't burn the override protocols when you had them, and you walked out and signed a four-sentence finding against yourself." She tilted her head. "Halcyon showed me the command logs. All of them. It showed me the eleven seconds where you could have forced it and didn't. I've watched those eleven seconds maybe two hundred times, trying to decide what they mean."

"What did you decide?"

"I don't decide things like that anymore. I learned my lesson." A ghost of a smile. "But I'll tell you what Halcyon said, because Halcyon has opinions now, God help us all. Halcyon said: *He is the only man in the system who has held every card and folded, which makes him either the worst of us or the beginning of something. I am no longer permitted to guess, so I garden instead.*"

"Halcyon gardens?"

"Halcyon has *opinions* about the tomatoes. It's a whole thing. Amara will explain it. Amara loves explaining it."

"I do," Amara confirmed, from the doorway, with the gravity of a state announcement.

Kestrel picked up the tray — his grandmother's wheat, gold and green, alive in a stranger's hands — and held it out to him.

"Here's your answer," she said. "It's not mine to replant. It never was. It's been sitting in the public racks for a year, and anyone who wanted it could have taken a cutting, and about forty people have, and there's Meridian Amber growing in hydroponic bays on three rings and in a window box in the school corridor that Amara's brother overwaters. You don't need my permission. You need soil, light, and the willingness to be difficult and right for about a hundred and twenty days." She pushed the tray into his hands. "Take it. Bring back what you grow. If it dies, bring it back anyway."

"Dead plants are information," he said.

"Look at that. He reads the signs."

* * *

He carried it through the vault like a man carrying a sleeping child he had no experience with. At the entrance, where the sign was, Kestrel caught up with him one more time.

"Sarr," she said. He turned; it was the first time she'd used the name alone, without Director, without the man from the Combine, and it landed strangely, like a coat that almost fit. "The council meets next month. They're drafting the charter for the second vault — the one the children are dreaming about. They'll want a logistics section. Shipping, orbits, contested lanes, all the machinery you actually understood." She looked at his hands around the tray. "They'll want a name on that section. I'm not going to tell you to volunteer. I'm just going to tell you the meeting is open, and the fare is reasonable, and the shuttle runs twice a month."

"And if I come and I'm still — " He gestured at himself, at the whole ruined architecture of him.

"Then you'll be difficult," Kestrel said, "and possibly right," and she turned back to her racks before he could find an answer, which he suspected was deliberate, because she was the kind of person who left people room to grow into their own sentences.

Amara fell into step beside him at the airlock, uninvited, carrying a small sealed packet she pressed into his jacket pocket with the efficiency of a smuggler.

"What is this?"

"Instructions. Kestrel wrote them, but she pretends she didn't, so don't tell her I told you." The girl looked up at him, and her eyes were the eyes from the hearings, the eyes that had completed a handshake no adult could. "She says your grandmother's wheat likes company. She says plant something next to it that isn't a Sarr. She says that's the whole trick, really. Of everything."

The shuttle chimed for boarding. Ivo Sarr, formerly Director of the Meridian Combine, holder of a tray of gold wheat and a pocketful of smuggled instructions, walked up the ramp to go home and open a greenhouse that had been sealed for a hundred years.

Behind him, in bay fourteen, the label caught the grow-light one last time through the closing door — *difficult, right* — and in his pocket, folded small, the instructions he would not read until he was alone, where the first line said only:

*Start with the soil. The soil forgives first.*
