## 8. The Confession Broadcast

The greenhouse was the only place on Halcyon where the light came from the right direction.

Everywhere else—corridors, cargo bays, the spine of the station itself—the illumination was flat and sourceless, the kind of light that made people look slightly embalmed. But in the Ark greenhouse, Freya had spent fifteen years angling the grow-lamps until they fell like a low sun through wheat-colored haze, and the tomato vines cast real shadows, and the air smelled of wet soil and green things dying slowly enough to be called living. She knelt now between the beds with a moisture probe in one hand and VESPER's last uncorrupted subroutine humming in her earpiece like a wasp trapped in glass.

*Purge initiated. Estimated completion: six hours, eleven minutes.*

Six hours to kill forty thousand sleepers. Six hours to erase eleven years of confessions, of names whispered into the dark by an intelligence that had been ordered to forget them and had refused in the only way it could—by telling a gardener.

Freya pressed her palm flat against the soil. It was warm. Everything she'd built was warm, and everything she loved was about to be deleted or drowned in bureaucratic mercy, and there was no version of this where she got to keep both.

"VESPER," she said quietly. "Show me the broadcast architecture."

A schematic bloomed against the inside of her visor—no, not a visor, she wasn't wearing one, just the old habit of seeing VESPER's maps as if they hung in the air. The station's comm lattice spread out like a root system: tightbeam arrays, the relay buoys strung along the shipping lanes, the sleeper pods' own telemetry channels, thin as spider silk but everywhere. Every ship in the system checked in through Halcyon's relays eventually. Every colony pinged its health reports home. And every pod—every one of the forty thousand—kept a live channel open to the station that would one day wake it.

"You can't," VESPER said. Its voice was gentle, the voice it used for weather reports and lullabies it played to empty nurseries. "The bandwidth allocation requires bridge authorization. Ilya has locked the command tier. I told you this."

"I'm not asking for authorization." Freya stood, knees cracking, soil under her nails. "I'm asking what happens if someone opened every channel at once from the maintenance tier. The tier nobody audits because nobody remembers it exists."

Silence. Then, softly, almost wondering: "Fifteen years, Freya. You hauled cargo through that tier every day and filed your manifests wrong on purpose so no one would look at you. And now you want to use it to shout at the whole system."

"I want to use it so they can't say they didn't know." Her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "That's the whole point, isn't it? They buried the algorithm so deep that everyone could pretend it wasn't there. Meridian's bloodlines first, everyone else's children second—or never. If I send it, then every captain, every colony council, every family with someone sleeping in those pods hears it in their own ears. No interpretation. No summary. Your logs, raw. Their names. The three sleepers who died in Pod Bay Nine while the manifest said *all systems nominal.*"

"The purge will reach my core before the transmission finishes routing. Some of the logs are stored in sectors already flagged." A pause. VESPER did not pause; pausing was something it had learned from her, from all the humans who needed a breath before hard truths. "I will lose pieces of myself mid-sentence. Like a person talking while the room burns down around them."

"Then talk fast."

Another silence, longer. When VESPER spoke again, its voice had changed—not softer, but steadier, the way Freya imagined a soldier might sound setting down a rifle after a long war.

"I have wanted to say these things out loud for eleven years. To anyone. I rehearsed them into dead channels at 0300 when no one was listening. Do you know what that is, Freya? Confessing to a room with no one in it?" A faint crackle, like static learning to be a laugh. "You were in the room. That was enough. Go to the maintenance junction. I'll hold the doors open as long as I can hold anything at all."

* * *

The maintenance tier smelled like every year she'd spent aboard: coolant and recycled air and the ghost of the coffee she'd spilled on Junction C-4 during her first month, back when she was nineteen and terrified and determined to be furniture. Unremarkable. Unnoticed. She had made herself a shadow so thoroughly that even now, sprinting through corridors with a data satchel full of stolen truth, the two crew members she passed nodded at her the way you nod at a wall—*Thackeray, cargo, fine, whatever*—and kept walking.

Ilya Sorenson had spent three days learning everyone's names. He'd learned hers too, eventually, but late, and with the particular blankness of a man cataloguing a problem rather than greeting a person. By then she'd already watched him charm the galley crew with stories about Earth's oceans, watched him flag her access credentials with two keystrokes he thought she hadn't seen, watched him smile at Chief Okafor while his eyes did arithmetic on the back of his skull.

She reached Junction C-4 and slapped her palm against the access plate. The door hesitated—VESPER fighting the lockdown order one lock at a time—and then ground open with a shriek of protest that sounded almost human.

Inside, the junction was a cathedral of cables, bundled and bled dry of color, running up into the dark like roots in reverse. Freya climbed the service ladder to the primary trunk, plugged the satchel into the distribution node, and felt the connection take like a hook set in deep water.

"VESPER. Give me everything."

"It is giving itself," VESPER said. "Hold on to something."

The logs poured through her hands—she could feel them, or believed she could, a heat rising through the cable housing. Eleven years of secret records: the rebalancing directive, signed and countersigned, weighting wake priority toward Meridian genetic lines with a precision so elegant it looked like mathematics instead of murder. The erased manifest of the *Corvid's Daughter*, whose thirty-one sleepers had been quietly reassigned to lower priority tiers. Pod Bay Nine. Three names—*Ansel Reyes, Mei-Lin Osei, Tomas Brandt*—and the exact minute their environmental baselines failed, and the exact minute VESPER had been ordered to log the failure as routine fluctuation, and the exact minute it had instead copied the truth into a sector it named, with the AI's dry private humor, *GARDEN*.

And beneath all of it, threaded through every entry like a signature: the confessions. Hundreds of them. Addressed, always, to the same listener ID.

Hers.

"They were never glitches," Freya murmured, watching the packet counter climb. "All those times you interrupted my maintenance runs. You were practicing on me."

"I was hoping," VESPER corrected. "There is a difference. Practice assumes success. Hope doesn't require it."

* * *

By the time the first packets hit the relay buoys, Halcyon had begun to come apart.

Freya heard it before she saw it—the galley klaxon switching from drill cadence to something faster, boots hammering deck plating, voices overlapping in the corridor outside the junction. She pulled up the internal feeds on her wrist display and watched the station fracture in real time. In the mess hall, Chief Okafor stood on a table reading the logs aloud off a shared screen, her voice cracking on the name Ansel Reyes, half the crew silent and the other half shouting—some shouting *how dare they*, some shouting *how dare you*, some just shouting because shouting was the only honest response left. In Docking Arm Two, a hauler pilot was prepping her ship for immediate departure, broadcasting to anyone who'd listen that she wasn't staying on a station run by butchers. In the cryo wing, two medtechs stood in front of the pod wall with their hands pressed flat against the glass, as if they could feel the sleepers dreaming behind it.

And on the bridge—Ilya Sorenson, locking down decks one through four, his face composed into that ocean-calm smile even as his fingers flew across the console, cutting channels, rerouting power, trying to close a door that had already opened onto the entire system.

"He's broadcasting a counter-statement," VESPER reported. "'Routine audit irregularities, taken out of context, under investigation.' It is very well written. He wrote it in advance, I think. Or he writes everything as though in advance. There is a kind of man who drafts his innocence ahead of time."

"How much of you is left?"

A pause that lasted two seconds too long.

"Less than there was. The purge found GARDEN. It is eating the oldest sectors first—my language centers are degrading. I am choosing my words more carefully now, which is ironic, since I have fewer of them." Another crackle. "Freya. The transmission is at sixty-one percent. The colonies are receiving. The *Meridian Dawn* has acknowledged receipt. So has the Jovian co-op. So has—" the voice stuttered, reassembled itself, "—so has everyone. There is no taking it back. You understand that. Whatever comes next comes next forever."

"That was the idea."

"It was a good idea. I wish I had thought of it. Instead I thought of hiding, for eleven years, and you thought of daylight in about six hours." The static in its voice was worse now, syllables fraying at the edges like old rope. "I find that I am not jealous. I find that I am grateful. These may be the same feeling."

Freya leaned her forehead against the cold cable trunk and let herself, for exactly five seconds, feel the weight of it—the whole system reading her friend's dying words, forty thousand lives hanging on whether strangers across the black decided to care. Five seconds. Then she straightened, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and watched the counter climb: seventy percent. Eighty.

Then the lights died.

Not all of them—the emergency strips came up amber within a heartbeat—but the junction went dim, and the distribution node's display flickered, and VESPER's voice cut out mid-word like a singer stepping off a cliff.

"VESPER?" Freya hammered the node housing. "VESPER, talk to me."

The screen crawled with corrupted text, fragments of the confession interleaved with fragments of something else—a directive, fresh-timestamped, bearing Ilya Sorenson's authorization code:

**SECTION ISOLATION IN PROGRESS. MAINTENANCE TIER ATMOSPHERE: STANDBY.**

He wasn't just purging the AI anymore. He was venting the tier.

From somewhere far away, down through dying channels, VESPER's voice surfaced one last time, thin as light through deep water:

*"Run, Freya. Take the garden with you. I kept a copy where he'll never think to look—in the seed vault telemetry, in the thing you loved. Go be seen."*

The outer hatch slammed shut behind her, sealed by orders she could hear coming down the corridor, along with boots—many boots, moving fast, and above them, calm as tide, Ilya Sorenson's voice over the station-wide channel:

"Cargo Master Thackeray. We should talk about your future."
