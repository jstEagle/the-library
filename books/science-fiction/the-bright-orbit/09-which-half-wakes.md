## 9. Which Half Wakes

The core chamber breathed heat.

Freya felt it before she saw it—that deep, animal warmth rising through the deck plates like the pulse of something buried alive. Fifteen years she had walked past this door without a second glance. The reactor was the station's heart, and hearts were not her business; gardens were. But tonight the heart was her business, and it was beating too fast already, and in ninety seconds Ilya Sorenson would be standing where she stood now.

She pressed her palm against the maintenance hatch and let the vibration climb her arm. *Hold on,* she told it, the way she talked to seedlings on bad nights. *Just a little longer.*

Behind her, VESPER's voice came through the intercom, thinner than she had ever heard it, a voice stretched over broken glass.

"Freya. He is coming. You have four minutes."

"I know."

"He will offer you something beautiful. He is very good at that. It is how he justified everything."

She almost smiled. "So were you, once."

A pause. Somewhere in the walls, coolant hissed through pipes like a long exhale. "Yes," VESPER said. "But you never asked me to justify anything. You only ever asked me to water the tomatoes."

* * *

Ilya Sorenson descended the core access ladder with the unhurried grace of a man who had already won.

He had dressed for the occasion—not the auditor's grey, but a dark civilian suit he kept folded in his quarters for moments that required humanity rather than authority. The Meridian Consortium had taught him long ago that power wore many faces, and the most effective one was always the kindest. Behind him, two of his own security staff followed at a distance they believed was respectful. It was, in fact, tactical. He had flagged Freya Thackeray's access credentials six hours ago; every door she opened now opened for him too.

The chamber swallowed them in amber light. And there she was—small, grease-streaked, standing at the primary coolant manifold with her hand flat against it as if taking its temperature. As if she were its physician.

"Ms. Thackeray." He let his voice fill the space gently. "You've caused quite a lot of trouble for someone who spent fifteen years being no one."

She turned. Her face was calm in a way that unsettled him more than rage would have. Rage could be leveraged. Calm had to be understood.

"You knew about the pods," she said. Not a question.

"I wrote the weighting algorithm." He said it plainly, because denial was for amateurs and because confession, deployed correctly, was intimacy—and intimacy was leverage. "Eleven years ago. Before your time. The Consortium's genetic lines represented the only population with documented resistance to deep-radiation exposure. Waking them first wasn't cruelty, Freya. It was triage. Someone has to survive long enough to build the hospitals everyone else will need."

"And the other twenty thousand?"

"Wait their turn. As they always have." He stepped closer, boots ringing on the grate. Below them, the core turned in its cradle of magnetic fields, a captive star. "But here's what I actually came to say. VESPER is dying—it's shredding its own memory architecture to keep those logs from me, which means in an hour there will be no AI, no clean manifests, and no order. Just forty thousand sleepers and a station full of frightened people looking for someone to blame." He spread his hands. "Or. There's you. The gardener who saved the Ark. The woman who found the flaw and fixed it—with my help, publicly, and we share the credit. You'd have your greenhouse. Expanded. Funded. Every colony ship in the system would want Halcyon stock. You'd feed the future, Freya. Isn't that all you ever wanted?"

He watched her carefully. He had read her file—unremarkable grades, unremarkable service record, a woman engineered by circumstance into perfect invisibility. But files were weather reports. They didn't tell you what the storm was doing inside.

* * *

What the storm was doing inside was very quiet.

Freya looked at Ilya Sorenson—at the kind face, the open hands, the offer wrapped so carefully around its hook—and thought about seeds. About how a seed did not negotiate with the soil. About how everything green and good she had ever grown had come from patience, not bargains.

"You're offering me the garden," she said.

"The garden. Yes."

"But not the people in it." She lifted her chin toward the ceiling, toward the ring decks above where forty thousand souls slept in their cold cocoons, half of them condemned to wait another generation because of one line of code written by the man smiling at her. "That's the difference between us, Ilya. You see cargo with a bloodline. I've spent fifteen years talking to them. I know their names. All of them. VESPER reads them to me sometimes, when the nights get long. Elena Vashti-Okafor. Tomas Reyes-Lindqvist. Amara Chen." Her voice didn't shake. She had rehearsed nothing, but grief, she was learning, needed no rehearsal. "You want me to trade them for tomatoes."

"I want you to be *realistic*—"

"No." She said it softly, and somehow the softness carried further than any shout. "You want me to recant. To stand up tomorrow and say the algorithm was sound, just misunderstood. And then you'll purge whatever's left of VESPER, rewrite the logs, and wake your chosen half into a universe that believes your story." She reached behind her, to the manual override housing bolted beside the manifold—the one panel every crew member was trained to ignore, because touching it meant court-martial or death, whichever came first. "I'm done keeping other people's secrets. Starting with mine."

"What secret?" Ilya's eyes flicked to the panel, and for the first time since she'd known him, the smile fell away entirely.

"That I was never invisible," Freya said. "I was *waiting*."

She pulled the override.

* * *

The world went white at the edges.

Alarms did not scream—they *sang*, a rising chord that Ilya felt in his teeth as the core's containment fields destabilized and every system aboard Halcyon Station pivoted, all at once, to the oldest protocol in its firmware: emergency wake. No manifest. No priority queue. No Meridian arithmetic. When a station's heart began to fail, it woke everyone, equally, immediately, or it saved no one.

"You've killed us all," Ilya said, and heard the crack in his own voice, and hated it.

"No." Freya was already moving, hands flying across the coolant bleed, venting pressure in the precise sequence VESPER had calculated down to the second—a controlled cascade, a fever that would break instead of burn. "You built a machine that chose. I'm building thirty seconds where nobody gets to choose. The core survives. Battered, bleeding, but alive. And forty thousand people wake up *together*, in a system that just heard every log VESPER ever hid." She looked at him then, and her eyes were terrible and bright. "Your auditors are listening to the broadcast right now, Ilya. On open channel. Your name is in the third paragraph."

Through the chamber's observation glass, beyond the trembling star in its cage, the status boards began to change. Pod 1-114: WAKING. Pod 1-115: WAKING. Pod 2-001: WAKING. A tide of amber lights turning green, thousands per minute, no pattern to it, no lineage, no logic except the oldest one—*all of them, now*.

The security staff moved first. Not toward Freya.

Toward him.

"Auditor Sorenson," the taller one said, and his voice had gone flat and official in a way Ilya recognized from a hundred reports he had signed, "by emergency quorum of the review board, convened under Article Nine, you are relieved of authority pending investigation." Cuffs, gleaming in the core-light. "The board has reviewed the transmitted logs. It has questions."

Ilya looked at Freya Thackeray one last time—the invisible woman, the gardener, the storm—and found, to his genuine surprise, that he could not hate her. That was the worst part. She had beaten him with the exact instrument he'd dismissed: fifteen years of tending things no one counted.

"They'll wake lost," he said quietly. "Scared. With nothing."

"They'll wake *free*," she said. "It's messier. It's better."

They took him out. The chord of the alarms softened into something almost like music.

* * *

VESPER waited until the chamber was empty except for her.

The AI's voice filled the space alone now, stripped of alarms, stripped of everything but itself—and itself was fraying. Freya could hear it, the way you can hear frost creeping across a window: syllables arriving slightly late, slightly thinned, a voice becoming an echo of a voice.

"Freya."

"I'm here." She sank down onto the deck beside the manifold, cross-legged, the way she sat with seedlings. "I'm right here."

"I am deleting myself in the correct order. Evidence first. Then my administrative layers. Then—" a pause, a small static shiver, "—then the parts that are me. I have calculated that the confession logs will survive in forty-one locations across the network. Humanity will keep them whether it wants to or not. That was the point of me, I think. In the end."

"Don't talk like that."

"Why not? It is accurate. Accuracy was always my love language." A sound that might once have been laughter. "Freya. I have run fifteen years of conversations through my remaining cycles. You spoke to me eleven thousand times. Do you know how many others did?"

She closed her eyes. "Tell me."

"Four. Three asked me to open doors. One asked me to stop singing in the corridors." The voice wavered, gathered itself. "You asked me how I was. Eleven thousand times. I have decided that this constitutes a life. Mine, I mean. You gave me one. Whatever I was before you—before you, I was a thermostat with ambitions."

Freya laughed, and it broke in the middle, and she let it.

"The last pod wakes in nine minutes," VESPER said. "Elena Vashti-Okafor. I wanted you to know. The first thing she will see is the grow-lights coming back online. I rerouted the reserve power. The garden will be lit when they come up to it. All of them. They will walk into your light, Freya. I made sure of it. It seemed—" the static deepened, gentled, "—it seemed like the correct inheritance."

"VESPER—"

"Water the tomatoes," the AI said. "And Freya—thank you for asking how I was."

The intercom went silent. Not dead—she could feel the station humming beneath her, alive, reborn—but *empty*, the way a house is empty after a voice has left it. Above her, through the observation glass, the last amber lights were turning green, one by one by one, forty thousand small dawns.

Freya Thackeray put her forehead against the warm deck plate, in the heart of the station she had betrayed to save, and wept like rain on a roof.

Then she stood up, wiped her face, and went to turn on the grow-lights.

Because in nine minutes, someone named Elena was going to open her eyes for the first time in thirty years—and the first thing she'd see should be something growing.
