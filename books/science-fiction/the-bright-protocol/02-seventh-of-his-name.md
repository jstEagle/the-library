## 2. Seventh of His Name

The clone registry on Kestrel's Landing was kept in a filing cabinet. An actual cabinet, with actual drawers, in the basement of the administration block, because the colony had learned the hard way that anything worth keeping should be keepable without electricity. The drawer labeled "Personnel — U" stuck, as it always did, and I yanked it with the particular violence I reserve for objects that remind me of myself.

Underhill, D. Six entries. Seven, counting mine, though mine hadn't been inked yet — I still had to sign my own intake form every year on the anniversary of my decanting, a ritual invented by some administrator who clearly believed bureaucracy could substitute for meaning.

I read the names anyway. Underhill, Dominic. Underhill, Dominic. Underhill, Dominic. Four more times after that. Same name, same handwriting by the third entry onward, because by then the clerks had stopped pretending these were different men and just let each Dominic sign for his predecessor. It saved paper. The colony was very good at saving things it shouldn't have to save.

Six dead men with my face. Well — five dead, one missing, depending on how you counted the original, whose body had never been recovered and whose death certificate read, in the coroner's careful hand, *presumed deceased, cause undetermined*. Presumed. As if he'd wandered off into the permafrost for a stroll and gotten lost. As if a man who had spent eleven years climbing the same tower every morning would suddenly forget the way home.

I closed the drawer. It stuck again on the way shut. Everything about my existence stuck.

* * *

The murder file lived in my quarters, not the archive, because I had inherited it the way you inherit everything when you're a clone: involuntarily, completely, and without anyone asking whether you wanted it. A grey folder, corners soft from handling, containing everything the colony knew — which is to say, almost nothing — about the death of Dominic Underhill, First of His Name, engineer, beacon-keeper, murdered man.

I made tea I didn't drink and opened it for the four-hundredth time.

Page one: incident report, dated nine years ago. *Beacon tower found unsecured at 0600. No sign of Underhill. Personal effects intact. Blood evidence present in lower stairwell, quantity consistent with fatal injury. Search suspended after six days due to storm season.*

Page two: inventory of the blood. They'd been thorough about the blood. Three point one liters' worth of spatter and pooling, mapped and photographed, as if the colony's one forensic technician had thought that if she described the death precisely enough, the life might come back with it.

Page three onward: nothing. No suspect. No motive. No weapon. Eleven years of service, and the file's final page was a form requesting permission to declare him dead so his ration allocation could be reassigned. Permission granted. Signature illegible.

I used to think the emptiness of the file was incompetence. Then I spent enough evenings holding it to notice what competence looks like when it's trying to look like something else. Pages numbered correctly. Gaps where pages should be — you can feel them, the way you can feel a missing tooth. Someone had removed material. Someone had wanted this file thin.

And here is the thing about being the seventh copy of a murdered man: you don't get to be a neutral reader of your own file. You read it with his eyes. You read it with hands that remember — not metaphorically, not poetically, but in the literal, cellular way that borrowed memory works when the transfer takes — the weight of a torque wrench, the smell of ozone off the tower's capacitor bank, the specific cold of the lower stairwell where three liters of him had pooled.

The memory transfer was supposed to be partial. That's what they told me, anyway, before they told me anything: skills, language, basic orientation, enough to make the replacement economically viable. What they didn't mention was that memory doesn't respect categories. You can't hand someone the knowledge of how to calibrate a phase array without handing them the ten thousand mornings that knowledge was built out of. So I woke up in a vat of amniotic warmth knowing how to fix a beacon and also knowing a woman named Edda who laughed through her nose, and a dog called Biscuit who was afraid of stairs, and the exact sound the tower door makes when the seal is failing — a low wet click, like a knuckle cracking underwater.

None of those things were mine. All of them were mine. I have stopped expecting the distinction to hold.

* * *

That night the borrowed memories came the way they always came when I opened the file — not as recollection but as weather, moving through me.

* * *

He is climbing the tower at dusk, and his knees hurt, and he is thinking about dinner. This is the last full memory of him I have, and it is about dinner. Lentils again. He is composing a complaint about lentils with the seriousness other men reserve for philosophy, and the tower hums under his palms the way it always does, and the sky over Kestrel's Landing is doing its evening trick — going green at the horizon, bruising upward into violet — and he thinks, not for the first time, that no one back on Earth will ever believe the color of this sky, and that this is somehow the colony's best-kept secret.

There is a light on in the lower level that shouldn't be on.

He notices it the way you notice anything wrong at the end of a long day: late, and with irritation first, fear second. He tells himself it's Rask's people doing an inspection. He tells himself a lot of things in the next ninety seconds, and I know all of them, and none of them help.

The stairwell. The wet click of the door seal. A voice he knows — I know it too, and this is the cruelty of the inheritance, that I carry the voice and can never place it, the way you can carry a song and lose the words. The voice says something quiet. Not threatening. Almost apologetic. And then —

Then the memory ends. Not fades. Ends, like a cut wire. Whatever the transfer techs harvested, they harvested it from a brain that stopped mid-sentence, and so the last thing the first Dominic Underhill ever knew, the last thing I know of being him, is the sound of his own breath changing — the small animal surprise of a body understanding danger before the mind consents to it.

I wake from that memory the way you wake from falling. Every time. Nine years of waking like that, and my heart has never once learned that the fall already happened, to someone else, somewhere else, and I am only the echo reading about itself in a grey folder.

* * *

Morning came grey and windless, which on Kestrel's Landing means the storms are thinking about it. I put on my coat, took the murder file out of respect rather than need — I know it by heart; taking it was a gesture, and gestures are the only prayers I trust — and walked out to the cemetery on the ridge above the settlement.

We bury our dead standing up here. Permafrost logic: you don't dig down, you stack up, cairns and marker stones facing east toward the tower, because the founders decided the dead should have something to look at. The original's cairn is the largest, mostly because five successive Dominics kept adding stones to it, each of us unable to explain to the others why. There is no body under it. There is a shirt of his, folded, and a calibration tool he was fond of, and a data slate someone sealed in resin that I have never switched on, because some part of me believes — unscientifically, stubbornly — that it's addressed to whoever comes next, and I'm not finished earning the right to read it.

"Morning," I said, to the cairn, because talking to it had become unavoidable around year two of my assignment. "Still no leads. Still no body. Still me."

The wind said nothing, being wind.

I sat down on the frost-heaved ground, which is what I actually came to do, and did the thing I do maybe twice a year: I tried to talk to him properly. Not the wry monologue I perform for my own benefit, but the real transaction, the one where a man asks his dead original for something.

"I need to know who killed you," I said. "Not for justice. Justice left with the resupply ship that never came. I need it because your file has holes in it, and holes mean someone's still alive who made them, and I don't like living in a story where I can't find the editor."

Nothing. Of course nothing. But sitting there, saying it aloud, I noticed something I had somehow never noticed in nine years of visits, which is embarrassing and true and probably says everything about grief that needs saying: the cairn's easternmost stone — the newest one, the one I added last spring — had been moved. Not fallen. Moved. Set slightly aside, deliberate as a signature, and beneath it, where no stone should rest, was a small rectangle of corroded metal.

A key. Old-style, magnetic, tower-grade. I knew it the way I knew the click of the tower door: with his hands, before mine understood.

I turned it over. Stamped into the shank, nearly worn away, was a string of characters in the original's own notation system — the idiosyncratic cipher shorthand he used in his maintenance logs, the one the archive had officially classified as "engineer's personal abbreviations, non-standardized."

I sat there a long time with the key in my fist, feeling the cold come up through my gloves, feeling something older than cold come up through the borrowed wiring of me.

Someone had visited the grave. Recently — the frost scar on the displaced stone hadn't healed. Someone who knew the cipher. Someone who wanted the seventh Dominic to find what the first Dominic had hidden, in the one place only a Dominic would ever kneel.

Or someone wanted me to stop digging, and this was a leash dressed as a gift.

I looked east, past the cemetery's standing dead, to the tower on its ridge, lit pale against the green morning sky, humming its signal loop into the dark for a purpose no one remembered commissioning — the loop I maintained, daily, faithfully, without once asking it what it was saying.

For the first time in nine years, I asked.

And the tower, being a machine, said nothing. But far below, in the settlement, the school bell rang early — early and wrong, a stuttering pattern I'd never heard — and I would learn soon enough why eleven children had run out into the cold that morning, all of them pale, all of them clutching the same drawing of a tower made of light.

But that was later. For now there was just the key in my hand, warm where my skin had held it, and the long walk down toward a question that had finally learned my name.
