## 10. The Man Who Came Back

The shuttle came in on a Tuesday, which felt like a personal insult. Tuesdays were my maintenance days. I had a schedule. I had a thermos of synth-coffee and a list of capacitor banks to bleed down at the tower, and I did not have, anywhere on that list, an entry for *the first contact with Earth in nine years arrives unannounced while you're elbow-deep in a coolant manifold.*

The tower told me first. It always tells me first — a soft harmonic shift in the carrier wave, the way a dog lifts its head before the thunder comes. Nine years I'd listened to this signal, and it had never once flinched at anything. Not the storms that sandblasted the western face. Not the night Rask's security teams ran live-fire exercises within a kilometer of the array. But at 0641 local, the beacon's tone bent, just slightly, the way a held breath bends.

I pulled up the approach telemetry and watched a shape descend through the cloud deck over Meridian Flats. It was too clean to be ours. Everything we owned out here had been repaired so many times it was mostly anecdote — a shuttle that was forty percent original parts and sixty percent stubbornness. This thing coming down was whole. Unscarred. Its hull caught the dawn light and threw it back like it had somewhere better to be.

I stood there with coolant on my hands and my heart doing something embarrassing against my ribs, and I thought: *Earth.*

Then, immediately after, in the smaller voice I keep for honesty: *Oh no.*

* * *

You have to understand what nine years means on Halcyon-4. It means children born here who've never seen a ship that wasn't a museum piece. It means a generation raised on ration schedules and recycled air and the polite fiction that Earth's silence was a scheduling problem. It means Mara Voss teaching history classes where "the war" is something that happened to other people, far away, in a story whose ending got lost in transit.

It also means that when the shuttle touched down on the flats outside the settlement dome, half the colony turned out to watch, standing in the cold in their patched coats, and nobody said a word. Three hundred people, silent, watching a hatch they'd dreamed about open for the first time since most of them could remember.

The figure who descended the ramp wore an Earth envoy's grey coat — actual Earth-issue, not a reproduction — and walked toward us across the frost-crusted regolith like she was crossing a lobby. She stopped ten meters from the crowd line. Councilor Ade Rask stepped forward to meet her, because that's what councilors are for, and I hung back near the edge where I always stand, holding my thermos like a prop in a play about a normal man.

"Representative Ilse Marchetti," she said, and her voice carried flat and clear across the flats. "Office of Colonial Continuity. I'm here regarding your beacon."

Not: *I'm here to save you.* Not: *We've come back for you.* Not even: *Hello.*

*I'm here regarding your beacon.*

The crowd's hope made a sound. It wasn't a word. It was more like the noise a building makes when something inside it shifts — a settling, a small structural disappointment. I watched old Tomas Wren, who'd waited nine years by the comm terminal every single evening, turn and walk away before she'd finished her second sentence. He knew. People out here know how to read the weather in a stranger's face.

Rask, to his credit or his discredit, kept his expression perfectly level. "Councilor Ade Rask. Welcome to Halcyon-4, Representative. You said *regarding*—"

"The beacon is broadcasting on protected frequencies," Marchetti said. "It has been for eleven years. My orders are to see it shut down."

And there it was. Hope arriving wearing the face of a threat, which, in my experience, is the only way hope ever arrives anywhere worth going.

* * *

They convened an emergency session in the council hall within the hour, which is fast for us; our emergency sessions usually take two days of arguing about whether something qualifies as an emergency. The war had apparently taught everyone efficiency by proxy.

I wasn't invited. I went anyway, on the grounds that the beacon was mine — not legally, maybe, but in every way that mattered, the way a scar is yours — and I took a seat at the back beside the water reclamation display, next to Mara Voss, who gave me a look that said she'd been expecting me and disapproved of me in roughly equal measure.

"You're not supposed to be here," she murmured.

"Neither are you."

"I teach half the people in this room. I'm always supposed to be here." She folded her arms. "Listen to her, though. Really listen."

So I listened.

Marchetti stood at the front of the hall with a data slate and the particular posture of someone who has rehearsed being unwelcome. She was perhaps fifty, close-cropped hair gone silver at the temples, and she had the kind of face that had been arranged by decades of briefings into something neutral enough to survive any room. But underneath it — and I want to be fair to her here, because fairness became important later — there was a tiredness that no briefing manufactures. The tiredness of someone who has crossed four light-years to deliver news she already knows will land like a verdict.

"Halcyon-4 was designated a provisional settlement under the Expansion Act," she began. "Its beacon was licensed as a navigation aid. Sometime in the years following your establishment, that license lapsed, and the signal continued anyway." She let that hang. "Continued, and changed. The transmission profile we're seeing now doesn't match any registered protocol. It's modulating on cycles our analysts can't model. And it sits directly across the guard band of several military channels."

"Military channels," Rask repeated carefully. "The war's still on, then."

Something moved behind Marchetti's eyes — quick, controlled, gone. "The war's status isn't the subject of this meeting."

"No," Rask said softly. "But it's the subject of every other one we've had for nine years. Forgive me if I notice its absence now."

I'll say this for Ade Rask: he could gut you with courtesy. I watched him do it, and I watched Marchetti absorb it without blinking, and I understood that these two recognized each other instantly — two administrators, two keepers of arithmetic, two people who had both learned that the truth is a resource to be rationed. They were going to be either allies or enemies, and either way the rest of us were going to be the material they worked with.

"The beacon constitutes interference with critical Earth defense infrastructure," Marchetti continued. "Under the Emergency Powers Act, I'm authorized to order its immediate decommissioning. I'd prefer cooperation. Compliance looks better in the record than seizure."

"And if we refuse?" That was Deniz Okafor, from the agricultural bench, and a low murmur ran through the hall at the audacity of it.

Marchetti looked at him for a long moment. "Then marines seize it," she said. "I have six aboard the shuttle. They are very good at their jobs, and I would genuinely rather they spent this deployment bored."

Mara's hand found my forearm under the bench. Her grip said: *say nothing.* Her eyes said: *you were about to say something.* She was right on both counts.

Because here's what I couldn't stop thinking, sitting there in the smell of recycled air and floor polish: *the signal changed.* Eleven years of static, and then — right around the time the children started dreaming — the beacon began modulating on rhythms no analyst could model. Marchetti hadn't come because of a licensing lapse. Licenses don't cross four light-years with marines attached. She'd come because the beacon had started *saying something*, and whatever it was saying, Earth wanted it stopped badly enough to spend a shuttle and an envoy on a colony officially forgotten.

She knew. Or she suspected. Either way, she was lying about why she was here, and I recognized the lie, because I'd been living inside its twin for months.

* * *

After the session broke up — no decision announced, which meant a decision had been made and was being aged like meat — I found myself walking the perimeter road with Mara, because neither of us suggested it and both of us did it.

"She's frightened," Mara said.

"She's an envoy with marines."

"That's not contradictory." Mara kicked a stone off the path with more force than the stone deserved. "Dominic, I watched her hands during the session. When Rask asked about the war. She flinched. Not visibly — but I watch children lie about homework all day, I'm calibrated for it. Something about the war frightens her. And something about your beacon frightens her more."

"Maybe the beacon's jamming their channels. Maybe it really is that simple."

"Do you believe that?"

I thought about the tower. About the way its tone had bent when the shuttle came down — not jammed, not interfered with, but *aware*, if I let myself use words like that, which lately I couldn't seem to stop doing. I thought about thirty children drawing a structure none of them had ever seen, and one of them describing the interior of a maintenance shaft sealed before she was born.

"No," I said. "But I don't know what I believe instead, and 'the beacon is haunted by something that talks to children' is not a theory I can say out loud without losing my access codes."

Mara stopped walking. The wind off the flats pushed her coat against her, and for a moment she just looked at me with the patience she used on the ones who wouldn't read.

"You've known something for weeks," she said. "Since you came to the school. Since Priya described the shaft. You've been carrying it around like a hot coal, and today a woman from Earth landed and demanded the thing be silenced, and you're still carrying it alone." She shook her head slowly. "Whatever it is, Dominic — whatever you think the beacon is — the clock just started. She didn't come here to negotiate."

No. She hadn't. And I knew it, because I'd watched her file out of the hall and immediately confer with her security officer, and I'd seen the officer glance — twice, three times — at the tower on the ridge, the way you check a door you intend to come back to.

* * *

That evening I climbed the tower, because it was Tuesday and the capacitors still needed bleeding and because routine is a rope and I was holding on with both hands.

From the catwalk, the settlement spread out below like something fragile set down on something vast: the dome, the fields under their grow-lights, the little rivers of lamplight along the walkways. And beyond it, the flats, where the Earth shuttle sat floodlit with a marine sentry pacing around it, a small bright island of elsewhere.

The beacon hummed beneath my boots. I put my palm flat against the housing, an old habit, a superstition I'd inherited along with everything else — the original used to do it too, according to his logs, though he'd never written down why.

"What are you doing?" I asked it. Out loud. To a machine. On a Tuesday.

The carrier wave shifted under my hand. Just slightly. The way a held breath bends.

Down on the flats, the shuttle's floodlights cut out, all at once, and in the sudden dark I saw the marine sentry look up at the tower — at me — and then turn and speak into her collar.

Nine years of silence, and now the universe had sent us a woman with a shutdown order and a secret of her own. I thought about Tomas Wren walking away from the ramp before she finished speaking. He'd read it right. Whatever hope had come down that ladder, it had arrived armed, and afraid, and lying.

Tomorrow there would be a formal compliance deadline. Tonight there was just me, the tower, and the growing certainty that Ilse Marchetti hadn't come to end our isolation.

She'd come because the beacon had finally started telling the truth — and someone, somewhere, four light-years away, had decided the colony that built it should never hear what it was saying.

I bled the capacitors. I logged the anomaly. And in the log, under *signal behavior, external stimulus,* I wrote the only honest entry I'd managed in weeks:

*It knows she's here.*
