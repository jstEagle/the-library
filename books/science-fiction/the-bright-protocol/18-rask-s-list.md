## 18. Rask's List

The thing about secrets in a colony of eleven thousand people is that they don't stay secrets. They just change hands. I'd been holding mine for three weeks — a classroom full of children dreaming the same war — and I'd begun to think of it as a stone in my pocket, something I carried everywhere and set down nowhere.

I found Rask's secret on a Tuesday, in the supply annex, because the universe has a sense of humor that runs to the bureaucratic.

Let me back up. The annex sits behind the council building, a long prefab shed where the colony stores everything it can't afford to lose and most of what it can't afford to keep. I'd gone there for legitimate reasons: the school's protein allotment had been cut again, and I wanted to see the requisition forms with my own eyes before I composed the polite, useless letter I compose every quarter. Ade Rask had always answered those letters personally, with a paragraph about supply-chain entropy that managed to sound like an apology and a refusal in the same breath.

The annex clerk, a boy named Ferro with the permanent squint of someone who reads ledgers for a living, was at lunch. The door was unlocked. In eleven years on this colony, I have learned that every locked door on Halcyon Reach is eventually unlocked by necessity, and every necessity eventually becomes habit.

I found the protein manifests in the third drawer. I found the list in the second.

It wasn't labeled. That was the first thing I noticed, and it's the thing that has kept me up every night since. No header, no title, no *Emergency Provisioning Schedule* or *Resource Allocation Draft* — nothing that would survive an audit or a glance. Just a column of names. Four hundred and eleven of them. I counted twice, because I didn't want to believe the first number.

The names were organized by district, and within each district by what looked like household, and next to each name was a number. Not an ID number. A caloric figure, I realized, after I cross-referenced the first dozen against the standard ration tables every adult on this colony can recite in their sleep. The numbers were low. Lower than maintenance. Lower than the level at which the medical literature — what's left of it in our archive — starts using words like *atrophy* and *cardiac event*.

I knew some of the names.

Old Sefa Kell, who taught me knotwork when I was nine and still waved at me across the market square like I was someone worth waving at. The Okonkwo twins, who were in my class three years ago and drew me pictures of the sun, which none of them had ever seen properly. Tomas Reyes, the water reclamation tech, whose daughter is in my current class, whose daughter draws the tower of light every single morning now, patient as a monk, in colors that don't exist.

Four hundred and eleven names. And at the bottom of the page, in Rask's small, precise handwriting — the same handwriting that answered my requisition letters with such courteous regret — a single line:

*Projected shortfall at month nine: 411 persons, caloric baseline unsustainable. Distribution per attached. Review quarterly.*

Review quarterly. As if they were grain yields. As if they were weather patterns.

I stood in that cold annex with the list in my hands and I did the thing I imagine everyone does when they find the bottom of the world: I looked for a way it wasn't what it was. Maybe it was a drill. Maybe it was a projection, a worst case, a document that existed precisely so it would never be used. Maybe the numbers meant something else entirely.

But I'm a teacher. I've spent eleven years learning to read children's faces, and before that I spent six years learning to read a government's paperwork back on Earth, in the ministry job I fled. The list wasn't a drill. A drill has a cover page. A drill has a signature. This was a working document, updated — I could see the correction fluid, the marginal figures in two different inks — updated recently, maintained carefully, by a man who ordered information suppressed to prevent panic and had apparently decided that the information worth suppressing included four hundred and eleven human beings.

*

I don't remember walking out of the annex. I remember standing in the market square afterward, holding a bag of protein ration bars I had no memory of purchasing, watching people move around me — Sefa Kell's wave, someone's laugh, a child chasing a dog that didn't technically exist under the livestock charter — and thinking: *you're on the list, and you're on the list, and that child isn't old enough to be on any list.*

Here is the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing, standing there like a woman doing long division with people: the synthesizers are failing. I'd known that the way everyone knows it — the way you know a roof leaks, in the abstract, in the sound of distant dripping. Rask had cut the school's allotment. The council had cut everyone's allotment, quietly, a few grams at a time, the way you lower a body into cold water. And the colony's population, divided by the colony's remaining caloric output, divided by the months until Earth's resupply — a resupply now nine years late, a number that had stopped meaning anything — produced, somewhere in its long ugly tail, a remainder.

And the remainder had names.

The horror of it, the part that made my hands shake on the walk back to the school, wasn't that Rask had made the list. I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only currency I have left. If you'd put a terminal in front of me and told me the colony starves in whole or in part, and only part was on offer, I don't know — I genuinely do not know — that I wouldn't have started calculating too. That's the trap of triage. It looks like math. It feels like mercy. Every name on that list is preceded by a hundred names that *aren't* on it, children who get fed because someone else's grandmother doesn't, and Rask has presumably lain awake with every one of those hundred, and the list is what his sleeplessness looks like on paper.

No. The horror was smaller and worse than that. The horror was that the list existed, and the colony didn't know, and the four hundred and eleven were walking around buying protein bars and teaching knotwork and drawing towers of light, living inside a decision that had already been made about them, the way we all live inside decisions made about us — except this one had their names on it, in someone's handwriting, in a drawer.

And beneath that horror, in the cellar beneath the cellar, was the other thing. The thing I'd been carrying for three weeks.

The children's dream was true.

I'd spent those weeks half-believing it myself — telling the council, being dismissed, telling Dominic Underhill and watching his face do the thing a man's face does when his worst secret is confirmed by a seven-year-old. The dream was a record. A war room. A decision. Earth had engineered the abandonment of this colony, had made us the remainder in *their* arithmetic, and the beacon had been carrying the proof all along in a language only children could hear.

Two lists, then. Rask's, in the drawer, deciding who on this colony would starve. And Earth's, in the signal, deciding — nine years ago, or nineteen — that all of us already had.

*

I taught my afternoon class. Of course I taught my afternoon class. That's the covenant of the job: the world ends, and then there's a lesson plan.

Twenty-two children sat in their seats and did their sums, and eleven of them had dreamed the same dream the night before, and I watched them the way I imagine Rask watches the synthesizer readouts — looking for the number that tells you how much time is left. The dream had been advancing. That was the word Dominic used, *advancing*, as if it were an army. First the tower. Then the interior. Then the war room, the table, the faces, the moment of decision. The children narrated it to me in fragments at recess, the way they narrate everything, overlapping, correcting each other, utterly without the gravity the material deserved.

"It's the same man every time," Pia Okonkwo told me — one of the twins, the one who draws the sun. "The tired one. He's putting something into the light. He's hiding it where the big machines can't look but the small heads can."

"Where the small heads can," I repeated.

"Because grown-ups only listen to numbers," she said, with the magnificent contempt of the seven-year-old, and went back to her drawing.

I stood in the schoolyard in the gray afternoon with Pia's words in my ears and Rask's list in my memory, and I understood — finally, completely, with the vertigo of a floor giving way — what I was actually holding.

I was holding two truths, and each one was a weapon, and they pointed at each other.

If I exposed the list — took it to the colony, read the names aloud in the market square — the colony would tear itself apart before the synthesizers failed. Eleven thousand people, nine years deep into isolation, holding together on nothing but routine and the promise that Earth would remember them. Show them the promise is a lie *and* the council has already chosen its dead, and you don't get justice. You get a mob with a nine-year head start on despair. Rask's list would kill no one that exposure didn't kill faster.

And if I exposed the dream — the beacon's message, Earth's crime — the same thing, only worse, because the dream's truth was the reason the list existed. The abandonment wasn't an accident of war. It was a decision, made in a room by faces the children could describe. The colony's whole fragile structure — work, ration, sleep, hope — was load-bearing masonry built over a hollow. I'd seen what happens when you knock out a load-bearing wall. Everyone in this colony has seen it; we live in prefab buildings and we know exactly which walls hold the roof up.

So: silence. Keep both truths. Keep the stone in the pocket, and add the second stone, and walk around being a teacher, and let four hundred and eleven people walk around being alive on borrowed arithmetic.

That was the sensible choice. That was the choice Rask had made, in his way, and the council had made, and Earth had made, a whole chain of sensible people each deciding that the truth was too heavy to lift and too sharp to set down.

I hated it. I want that on the record, wherever records go after colonies are written off. I stood in the schoolyard and I hated the sensible choice with my whole body, because I'd spent eleven years teaching twenty-two children at a time that the truth was the one thing you hand someone even when your hands are shaking. That was the whole curriculum, underneath the sums. *The truth, even when your hands are shaking.*

And now my hands were shaking, and the truth was four hundred and eleven names, and I was going to put it in a drawer.

*

I went to see Rask that evening. I told myself it was to confront him. It was mostly to look at him — to see whether the man who kept that list looked like a monster, because if he did, I could dismiss the part of me that understood him, and if he didn't, then God help all of us.

He received me in his office above the council chamber, a small room with a window that looked out on the annex, which struck me, in retrospect, as either a coincidence or a confession. He looked tired. He always looked tired — he'd looked tired at the meeting where he dismissed the children's drawings, tired at the meeting where he'd backed Marchetti's shutdown order — but that night the tiredness had a texture I recognized, because I'd been wearing it for three weeks. It was the tiredness of a person carrying weight in a place no one can see.

"Mara." He gestured to the chair. "The requisition letter. I was going to answer it tomorrow."

"I wasn't here about the protein allotment." I sat down. My hands, I noted with a kind of distant academic interest, had stopped shaking. Fear does that sometimes; it saves its trembling for later, when there's time. "I was in the annex today. Second drawer."

I watched his face. I'd come to see if he looked like a monster, and what I saw instead was the smallest possible movement, a stillness like the stillness of a man hearing a floorboard creak above a hidden room. He didn't deny it. He didn't reach for a denial. He just looked at me for a long moment, and then at the window, and then back, and something in his shoulders let go — not relief, exactly. Resignation's quieter cousin.

"How much did you read?"

"Four hundred and eleven names."

"Four hundred and nine," he said. "I removed two last week. The Reyes girl's cardiac screening came back. She's moved to the protected column." He said it flatly, without pride, the way you'd report a weather figure, and I understood that this — this bookkeeping of mercy, this moving of a child between columns — was the whole of his inner life now. "You're wondering if I sleep."

"I wasn't."

"I don't. But not for the reason you think." He folded his hands on the desk. "I learned the format from Earth, Mara. Did you know that? The abandonment protocols — the ones the beacon's been screaming about, the ones in your children's dream — they include a triage schedule. Standard annex. I've read the fragments the children describe. We built our colony's emergency codes on Earth's templates, and Earth's templates assume that when the numbers stop working, you write names in a drawer and you don't tell anyone, because telling anyone is the one variable that makes the numbers worse." He smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all year. "I'm not the crime here, teacher. I'm the echo. They taught us the shape of this decision before we ever needed to make it."

"You could publish it. The whole colony could—"

"Could what?" And here his voice didn't rise; it dropped, which was worse. "Vote on who starves? Watch the four hundred and eleven spend their last months knowing? Watch the other ten thousand spend theirs knowing they were fed by subtraction?" He shook his head. "You of all people should understand what a truth costs. You're sitting on one yourself. The beacon. The dream. You've known for weeks what Earth did to us, and you've told no one but Underhill, because you know — you *know* — what it does to a colony to learn it was thrown away. You made my choice before I ever made mine. You just did it with dreams instead of names."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. I remember the chair, and the window, and the annex below with its unlocked drawer, and then I was standing, and Rask was looking up at me with something that was almost gentleness.

"You'll keep it," he said. Not a question. "You'll keep it, because the alternative is the riot, and you're a teacher, and teachers understand what children can survive knowing." A pause. "And one day, maybe, you'll bring me the other list. The one in the signal. And we'll decide together which truth this colony dies of."

I walked out. The corridor was cold. Somewhere below, in the annex, in a drawer, four hundred and nine names lay in the dark, and two had just been saved by a cardiac screening, and I thought: *he moved a child between columns the way I move a student between reading groups,* and I had to put my hand against the wall.

*

That night I didn't sleep, and around the third hour of not sleeping I did the thing I'd been not-doing for three weeks.

I went to find Dominic Underhill.

His quarters at the tower's base were lit — I could see the light from the maintenance road, one warm window in a black landscape, and I thought: *there's a man keeping a vigil over a message he can't read, in a colony keeping a vigil over a lie it can't survive, on a world keeping a vigil over a crime it was built to forget.* Everyone on Halcyon Reach is a drawer with something hidden in it. The difference is that some drawers are about to be opened whether we like it or not.

He answered my knock looking like a man who'd been expecting the knock for days. Behind him, on his desk, the original's cipher notes lay open, and the diagnostic screens glowed with the signal's slow, sleep-keeping rhythm, and I saw that he'd been cross-referencing — the dream imagery, the children's drawings, the dead man's numbers — with the focused desperation of someone who has finally found the lock and is hunting for the key.

"Mara," he said. "You look like you found something."

"I found a list," I said. "Four hundred and nine names. And I found out that our council learned its mercy from the same people who wrote the message your beacon is carrying." I stepped inside, out of the cold, and closed the door on the dark. "And I've decided I'm done keeping truths in drawers. So. The children dream a war room. You dream a dead man's memories. Let's open them together — because Rask thinks the colony can only survive one truth at a time, and I think he's wrong, and I think the proof is in that signal, and I think we're running out of nights."

Dominic looked at me for a long moment. Then he turned the cipher notes around on the desk so I could read them, and pointed at a fragment — a date, half-decoded, a name, and beneath them a single recovered word that stopped my breath, because I'd heard it already, in a child's voice, in the schoolyard, describing the tired man at the table:

*Ordered.*

"He hid the rest where the big machines can't look," Dominic said quietly. "But the small heads can. So tonight, Mara Voss, we learn to dream like children."
