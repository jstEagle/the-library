## 21. Two Dominics

The vault opened the way all of the original's secrets opened: reluctantly, and only after I'd made a fool of myself.

The dream fragment had come to me three nights running — not through the children's channel, whatever that was, but through my own borrowed memory, surfacing like a body in still water. A sequence of taps on the tower's foundation plating. Four long, two short, one long. In my waking life it meant nothing. In the memory that wasn't mine, it was a knock. The original used to knock on things before he trusted them. Doors, machines, people. *If it doesn't answer back,* he'd written once in his journal, *it can't lie to you.*

So at four in the morning, alone in the tower's lowest level with a maintenance mallet and the distinct feeling of a man about to be committed, I knelt beside the foundation plating beneath the primary coolant junction and knocked.

Four long. Two short. One long.

For ten seconds, nothing. Long enough for me to compose the log entry explaining to no one why I'd been hammering the floor. Then, deep under the plating, something answered back — a hum, low and resonant, the same frequency the children dreamed, the frequency I'd spent weeks pretending was coincidence. A section of plating the size of a coffin lid slid aside on rails that hadn't ground in decades, revealing a shaft lit by emergency strips that were somehow, impossibly, still charged.

"Of course," I said to the empty room. "Of course you tied it into the beacon's resonance field. You absolute showman."

I want to be clear about what I felt in that moment, because later accounts would make it sound like triumph. It wasn't. It was the feeling of standing at your own front door and realizing someone else has a key — except the someone else is you, and the door has been waiting thirty years for you to come home.

I climbed down.

* * *

The vault was small. That surprised me. I'd expected a cathedral; I got a pantry. Three meters by four, shelved with data cores wrapped in anti-static film, a workbench with tools laid out in the order of some procedure I didn't recognize, and a chair. One chair, positioned facing the far wall, where a screen hung in a frame of scavenged alloy.

He'd built a viewing room. He'd known someone would come down here eventually, and he'd wanted them seated for it. There was something both considerate and unbearably presumptuous in that, and I recognized the combination immediately because I saw it in the mirror every morning.

The data cores were labeled in his handwriting — my handwriting, though mine had degraded over six copies into something lazier, rounder. His letters were sharp. Angry, almost. Seven cores. Six were marked with dates spanning the last two years of his life. The seventh bore no date, only two words:

*FOR HIM.*

Not "for whoever finds this." Not "for the colony." For him. For me, though he couldn't have known there would be a me — couldn't have known the council would authorize a clone line from his preserved tissue, couldn't have known they'd run it seven times, couldn't have known that the seventh attempt would wake up with his memories intact and his murder unsolved and his beacon still singing its nonsense song into a sky full of static.

Except maybe he could know. Maybe that was the point of the word *him.* He wasn't addressing a specific person. He was addressing a continuity. Whoever came next. Whoever I became.

I sat in the chair. My hands weren't steady, which annoyed me, because they were his hands and I resented the idea that he'd have been nervous. Then I remembered that he knocked on doors because he was afraid of what answered, and I felt a little better about the shaking.

I slotted the core.

* * *

The screen flickered, resolved, and there he was.

I have seen photographs of the original Dominic Underhill. Official portraits, registry images, the one candid shot the council kept from a harvest festival where he's laughing at something outside the frame. None of them prepared me for motion. Photographs are evidence. This was presence.

He looked older than his pictures. Thinner. He'd set the camera up carelessly and it framed him slightly off-center, half of him against the vault wall, and he clearly couldn't be bothered to fix it. He was wearing the same model of maintenance jacket I was wearing. He noticed me noticing — or would have, if he could see across thirty years — and gave the camera a look of dry apology.

"If you're watching this," he said, "then either the council finally grew a conscience, or you figured out the knock." A pause. "It'll be the knock. The council doesn't have a conscience. I've checked."

His voice did something to me that I'm going to struggle to describe honestly, so let me try dishonestly first: it sounded like a recording. Honest version: it sounded like the voice I hear inside my own head when I think in complete sentences. Every man sounds like himself to himself. Hearing the source of that sound, externalized, wearing a face I shaved every morning — it was less like meeting a stranger than like being read aloud.

"They'll have cloned me by now," he went on. "They won't be able to help themselves. I'm the only certified beacon engineer on this rock, and the protocol requires one, and dead men are easier to replace than to mourn. So. Statistically, you exist. Hello."

He rubbed his face with both hands, and I watched my own gesture performed by its inventor, and something in my chest folded up small.

"I don't know what they told you about how much comes through the tissue transfer. When they briefed me on the program — before I objected, before I understood what objecting would cost me — they said memory retention was 'variable.' Which is bureaucrat for *we don't know and we're not going to find out on our own budget.* So I don't know if you remember being me. I don't know if you remember the night I died." He looked directly into the lens, and I looked directly back, thirty years through a wall of light. "If you do — I'm sorry. That wasn't supposed to be part of the inheritance. Some things should stay with their owner."

I remembered. Of course I remembered. I remembered the corridor outside the comms room, the smell of scorched insulation, the angle of the ceiling tiles, the last thought — not fear, not pain, but irritation that he'd left the beacon's diagnostic cycle unfinished, that someone would have to redo his work. Dying mid-task. Even my death had been a maintenance log entry.

"You're wondering why I built this," he said. "Why I hid a vault under the tower instead of taking the evidence to the council, or broadcasting it myself, or doing any of the ten sensible things available to a man who's found proof that Earth murdered eleven thousand colonists on purpose."

He stood, walked out of frame. The camera held on the empty wall for a moment, and I heard drawers opening, the clink of tools. When he came back he was holding a data slate, old style, cracked at the corner.

"Because sensible gets you killed," he said. "I know. I checked that too."

* * *

Here is what he told me, sitting in that coffin-sized room, talking to a man who wouldn't exist for years: the whole shape of it, laid out without decoration, the way you brief someone on a defect you've found in a load-bearing structure.

The war that stranded us wasn't an accident of distance. It wasn't bad luck, wasn't a supply chain casualty, wasn't the fog of some distant conflict reaching out blindly to touch a colony nobody important remembered. Earth's high command had engineered it. The convoy routes were rerouted deliberately. The resupply windows were closed deliberately. Halcyon Reach — our colony, our eleven thousand souls, our failing synthesizers and our brittle little normalcy — had been selected for abandonment as a cover. Something had happened during the war's opening months. Something the government needed buried, and a lost colony full of witnesses was exactly the kind of grave you dig when you want a crime to disappear.

He had the records. Fleet routing orders. Command transcripts. A signed directive with a name attached to it — a name I would spend chapters learning to say aloud without my jaw tightening. He'd pulled them during a maintenance rotation on the relay buoy network, back when the buoys still carried Earth traffic, back when nobody thought a colonial technician could read military encryption because nobody thought colonial technicians existed at all.

"And here's the part I need you to understand," he said, leaning toward the camera, "the part I got wrong for two years, the part that killed me." He held up the cracked slate. "My first instinct was to tell everyone. Shout it from the tower. Let the colony rise up, let Earth choke on its own secret. And then I sat down and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said: eleven thousand people, one shuttle, no fleet, no allies, and a government that has already demonstrated it will kill every one of us to protect a lie. Rebellion isn't justice. Rebellion is just suicide with better attendance."

"So I didn't shout. I encoded. I took the truth apart and hid it in the only channel Earth can never fully silence — the beacon loop itself, the one signal they have to leave open because shutting it down confirms everything. And then—" he laughed, short and bitter, a sound I knew intimately because I made it at least twice a week— "and then I discovered that hiding a message inside a signal isn't enough. Signals decay. Keys get lost. Thirty years from now, some poor clone is going to be staring at my beautiful cipher with no idea what's inside it. The message needs a living key. Something that carries the decoding pattern somewhere no purge order can reach."

He paused. And I knew — I knew with the sick certainty of a man reading his own autopsy — what was coming.

"The children," I said, out loud, to a dead man in a chair. "You put it in the children."

"Not in them," he said, as if he'd heard. "In the pattern around them. Young neural architecture resonates with certain carrier frequencies — it's in the literature, buried, unfunded, inconvenient. The beacon's modulation window sits right in that band. Adults filter it out. We've got too much noise upstairs. But children in deep sleep—" he spread his hands— "children in deep sleep are open channels. They won't decode it consciously. They'll dream it. Fragments. Over years. And those fragments will interlock, because the message wants to be understood, and young minds are the most patient decoders humanity ever built."

"You used our kids as a cipher wheel," I said. My voice sounded strange in the vault. Flat. "Eleven thousand adults and you picked the ones who still believe in monsters under beds."

"I know," he said quietly. "Believe me, I know. I've had two years to hate myself for it, and I've gotten very good at it. But understand the alternative. If I encode it for adult minds, Earth finds out — Earth always finds out, they have people here, they've always had people here — and they shut the beacon, and they sterilize the colony, and the message dies with everyone who could have carried it. Children survive purges. Children get adopted by the people who kill their parents. It's monstrous, and it's also the only vector that works, and I will answer for it. Not you. Me."

He set the slate down. Walked to the camera. Squatted in front of it, close enough that I could see the fatigue in his eyes, the gray coming in at his temples, the small scar above his eyebrow that I'd caught on a coolant valve at nineteen and never questioned because I assumed I'd always had it. I had. He'd given it to me before I was born.

"So now the hard part," he said. "The part I've rewritten eleven times, and every version sounds like either a command or a plea, and you deserve neither."

* * *

He sat back down in the chair. Took a breath. And spoke to me — not to the camera, not to posterity, but to me, specifically, precisely, the way you speak to someone whose face you can imagine even if you can't see it.

"Whoever I become. That's what I'm calling you, because I refuse to call you a copy. Copies are objects. You're a continuation. Everything I am passed through flesh and memory into you, and that makes you not my duplicate but my heir, and heirs get told the truth.

"Finish the work. Decode the message. Get it out through Earth's last open loop, whenever that comes — and it will come, because empires always announce their retreats, they just dress them up as scheduling changes. Broadcast it. All of it. The routing orders, the directive, the name. Let the colony hear what was done to them. Yes, it will cost you. It may cost everyone. I've run the scenarios until my eyes bled, and every path that ends with the truth spoken runs through wreckage first. There is no clean version of this. I looked. I'm sorry.

"But listen to me. Listen, because this is the only thing I have left to give you, and it's more valuable than the vault, more valuable than the cipher, more valuable than the proof.

"Whatever it costs — however it goes wrong, however many people look at you afterward like you broke something that was holding up their sky — you are forgiven. Now. In advance. Before you've done anything. Because I know exactly what you'll be carrying when you make the choice, because I'll be carrying it too, because we are the same man separated by a crime scene and a decade. And I cannot leave you alone with that weight. I won't. The whole point of building a person after me was so that somebody would finish this who didn't have to do it unaccompanied.

"You're not betraying the colony by telling them the truth. The colony was betrayed the day the convoys stopped coming. You're just the first one brave enough to say it out loud.

"One more thing, and then I'll let you go, because I can see you fidgeting — yes, I fidgeted too, we're allowed." He smiled, and it cracked something open in me that I'm not going to describe, because some things belong to the two of us and no one else. "When this is over — whatever 'over' means — go stand at my grave. Not as my ghost. As yourself. Say something in your own words. I spent my whole life being diligent and careful and dutiful, and it got me murdered in a corridor with an unfinished diagnostic. You get to be something else. You get to be the one who finished it.

"That's not a burden. That's a promotion.

"Good luck, Dominic. Whichever number you are."

He reached forward and the recording ended, and the screen went dark, and I sat in his chair in his vault wearing his jacket behind his face, and I did not move for a long time.

* * *

Grief is supposed to be for people you've lost. Nobody warns you about grief for a man you never met, whose death you remember in first person, whose forgiveness you just received for sins you haven't committed yet. I sat in the dark and shook, quietly, thoroughly, the way machinery shakes when it's been holding a tolerance too long, and when it was over I wiped my face with a sleeve that smelled like coolant and thirty years of the same job, and I stood up.

Six data cores remained on the shelf. The evidence. The routing orders. The name. Enough to burn down a government, if anyone was left listening on the other end of the loop.

I gathered them into my jacket pockets, all seven — no, six, the seventh was still in the player, and I hesitated over it. Take it or leave it? Leave it, some practical instinct said. The vault is the safest place in the colony. But the vault had already been found once, by me, via a knock, via a dream. Dreams were getting around lately. And there were people in this colony — I could name at least two, and one of them wore a council badge — who would very much prefer that this room stayed sealed forever.

I took the core. I'd carry his voice with me. It seemed only fair; I'd been carrying everything else.

I was halfway up the ladder when I heard it — footsteps overhead, several sets, moving with the deliberate coordination of people who'd rehearsed this. Then the tower's main power cycled, and the lights above me died, and through the plating under my hands I felt the beacon's hum stutter, falter—

—and hold. My maintenance loop. The one I'd hidden the core behind. Still running.

A voice echoed down the shaft, amplified, official, and familiar. Councilor Ade Rask, addressing the tower, the colony, and me.

"By emergency authority of the Colonial Council, this facility is hereby secured. Dominic Underhill: surrender yourself and all materials in your possession."

I looked down into the dark of the vault, at the empty chair, and I swear the room felt warm around me, like a hand briefly squeezed my shoulder.

"Well," I said. "You heard the man."

And I started climbing, toward the light, toward the guns, carrying the truth in six cores against my chest.
