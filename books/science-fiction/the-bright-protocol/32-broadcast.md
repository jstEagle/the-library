## 32. Broadcast

The storm had a voice. I'd lived on Meridian long enough to know that the wind didn't howl so much as argue — a low, persistent grumbling at the edges of the tower, like the planet itself filing a complaint about the weather. Tonight it argued against me, and I argued back with my hands, feeding power cables through the comm array's access panel while the marines' crashed shuttle burned orange somewhere down the ridge behind me.

I want to say I felt calm. I want to tell you that when a man holds the end of the world in his two hands, his hands go steady and his mind goes clear and some music swells. That's what the original's journal entries sounded like, anyway, all that measured resolve. But the truth is my hands shook so badly I stripped a connector twice, and the only music was the wind arguing, and the only thing clear in my head was Ilse Marchetti's face when she'd said, *You don't understand what you're carrying.*

She was right. I didn't understand it. I carried it anyway.

* * *

Let me back up an hour, because an hour ago I was still a man who could have turned around.

The old comm array sat on a shelf of basalt four kilometers from the colony dome, a skeleton of lattice and dish left over from the first landing, back when Earth still pretended we were pioneers instead of evidence. The walk had taken me most of the night. My suit heater was dying by degrees — the readout said forty percent, then thirty-one, then numbers it declined to explain further — and every step through the frozen scree felt like walking on broken crockery.

Behind me: three marines whose shuttle I'd pulled out of a fireball, wrapped in thermal blankets in the array's equipment shed, breathing. Alive because I'd stopped to dig them out when every rational calculation said run. I keep trying to decide whether that makes me sentimental or just slow. The original would have had an opinion. He had opinions about everything; I've been reading them for months now, this dead man's handwriting looping through my head like a second pulse.

Ahead of me: the array's control bunker, a concrete box half-buried in the ridge, its door swollen shut with frost until I put my shoulder into it hard enough to see stars that weren't in the sky.

Inside, the equipment was older than the beacon. Older than the colony's second generation. Vacuum tubes, if you can believe it — actual glass-and-filament relics the founders had kept because solid-state electronics died too fast in Meridian's radiation belts. The original Dominic had maintained this place too. His fingerprints were on everything, literally: greasy smudges on the panel edges, a coffee ring fossilized on the main console, a maintenance log in his cramped slanted hand going back eleven years before his murder.

I sat down in his chair. It fit me. Of course it did.

* * *

Earth's transmission window closed in under two hours. I knew this because a guard named Okafor — a good man doing a bad job, which is most of us — had leaned close to my cell and whispered the number like he was passing contraband. Thirty-six hours, he'd said, and that had been yesterday, and between then and now there had been a vault, a coup, a prison, a lock that sang itself open at the frequency children dream in, and a very long cold walk. Time moves strangely when you're the message. Sometimes you're the messenger. Mostly, lately, I've just been luggage.

I powered up the array one subsystem at a time, the way the original's log instructed, and the bunker filled with a hum I felt in my teeth. The main transmitter woke with a sound like a struck bell heard underwater. On the ancient CRT display, a single line of text crawled up:

**UPLINK STATUS: EARTH LOOP 7 — OPEN. TIME REMAINING: 01:47:22.**

One hour, forty-seven minutes. Somewhere past the dark, past the war, past all the distance light gets tired crossing, a receiver on Earth was listening for the last time. After this window, the loop would fold shut like a hand closing, and Meridian would become what Earth's high command had always intended it to be: nothing. A rounding error. A crime with no witnesses.

Except the witnesses were eleven children who'd been dreaming someone else's memory for a season. Except a teacher who'd traded away her own safety to buy mine. Except a councilor who'd learned cruelty from the people he thought he was protecting us from, and an envoy who'd finally decided that following orders was just murder with paperwork.

Except me. Seven of me, counting the grave.

* * *

I loaded the message the way the original had designed it to be loaded — not as data, not as a file you could corrupt or quarantine, but as a broadcast protocol stitched directly into the transmission sequence. He'd built it paranoid. He'd built it knowing that whoever finally sent it might be sending it with one hand while holding off the world with the other. Every fragment was redundant. Every fragment was checksummed. You couldn't send half of it. You couldn't edit it. You pressed one key, and the whole terrible architecture of the truth went out at lightspeed, complete, indivisible, addressed to everyone.

That was the part that made my stomach turn over, sitting there in the humming dark. Not the content. I'd watched the vault recordings twice now — the war room footage, the abandonment order with its tidy bureaucratic signature block, the testimony of a man who'd known he was going to die for copying it. I'd had time to make my peace with the content.

It was the addressing. The original hadn't built this message for Earth's government. He'd built it for *everyone*. When I pressed that key, it wouldn't just go up the loop to Earth. The beacon network would rebroadcast it across every channel the colony owned — every screen in every hab unit, every speaker in the mess hall, every earpiece, every child's tablet. Mara had told me once that the colonists' screens were wired into the same trunk lines as the beacon's emergency override, some ancient safety provision nobody had ever revoked.

Everyone would hear it at once. The mothers. The farmers. Rask. The children who'd already dreamed it and never known why they woke up crying.

There is no version of this where I get to be gentle, I told myself. There is only the version where I get to be true.

My finger hovered over the key. Underneath it, worn nearly blank by a decade of a dead man's thumb, was a small brass plate. I bent close. Etched into it, almost gone:

**FOR WHOEVER I BECOME.**

He'd known. Sitting in this chair, years before the murder, before the clones, before any of it — he'd sat here and machined a little brass plate for a man who didn't exist yet, and he'd trusted him. Trusted *me*. With the colony. With the war. With the whole rotten beautiful species.

"Okay," I said out loud, to him, to the hum, to nobody. "Okay."

I pressed the key.

* * *

Nothing happened.

For three full seconds, nothing happened, and I aged a decade per second, and then the vacuum tubes lit up one bank at a time like a city coming back after a blackout, and the needle on the output meter swung hard right and *stayed* there, and the message began to climb the sky.

I watched it go. That sounds like nothing, but I watched it go — the waveform crawling across the CRT, dense and layered, the original's cipher unfolding itself into clean signal, the testimony converting itself into something any receiver in the system could hear. Data became voice became history. Eleven years of silence, ending.

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor and it's not a kind one, the storm found the antenna.

The first gust hit the array like a fist. The whole lattice groaned, and the dish swung two degrees off bearing, and my output meter sagged, and I was out of the chair and into the wind before I'd decided to move, hauling on the manual azimuth crank with both hands while sleet needled the back of my neck. The crank fought me. The storm fought me. Somewhere below in the equipment shed, three marines I'd saved were sleeping through the apocalypse, and above me the message kept transmitting, degraded, stuttering, checksums failing and re-failing—

—and then the beacon answered.

I didn't do anything. I swear on all seven of my names, I didn't do anything. But the tower four kilometers away, the tower Rask had seized and Marchetti had ordered dark, the tower with its core hidden behind my maintenance loop — it flickered back to life. I saw it from the ridge, a blue-white stab through the storm clouds, and my meter jumped, and the signal locked clean. The beacon was rebroadcasting. Amplifying. Carrying the message the way it had always been meant to carry it, the way the original had designed it to carry it, using the resonance frequency the children had been dreaming for months.

The children. Oh. *Oh.* They weren't just receivers. They'd never been just receivers. They were part of the circuit. The original had built a message that needed a whole colony to deliver it — needed young minds to hold the fragments, needed the beacon to weave them together, needed someone like me to press the key at the only moment in history when the loop was open. It wasn't a recording. It was an instrument, and we had all been playing it without knowing.

The dish held. The signal held. And down in the valley, the colony dome began to light up.

* * *

I stood at the bunker's observation slit and watched every screen on Meridian come alive at once.

From four kilometers out, the dome looked like a lantern with a candle inside it — windows flickering as displays woke unbidden, hab units glowing, the mess hall, the school, the admin block where Rask had built his quiet arithmetic of who would eat. I couldn't see faces. I couldn't hear voices over the wind. I could only see the light, spreading window to window, street to street, until the whole dome was lit brighter than I'd ever seen it, brighter than festival nights, brighter than the landing anniversary.

Every screen. Every speaker. The abandonment order, read aloud in the flat voice of declassified record. The war room footage. The original's testimony, his voice — *my* voice, the voice I hear in my own head every day — saying *my name is Dominic Underhill and if you are hearing this, then I am dead, and what follows is the reason.*

Somewhere down there, a woman named Sef Underhill was hearing her husband speak from beyond his own grave. Somewhere down there, Ade Rask was listening to proof that his triage list had been written in Earth's own handwriting, learned and inherited, the crime reproducing itself in miniature. Somewhere down there, eleven children were hearing out loud the thing they'd only ever dreamed, and maybe — maybe — that was a mercy. Maybe it's easier when the nightmare finally says its name.

And somewhere down there, Ilse Marchetti was standing in whatever room she stood in, watching her career and her government and her entire chain of command come apart at lightspeed, and she had a sidearm, and she had marines, and she had thirty seconds to decide what kind of person she was going to be for the rest of her life.

I gripped the edge of the observation slit and waited to see whether the colony would burn.

That's the honest ending of this scene, the one I'll never put in a log: I expected fire. I expected riot, sirens, the dome's emergency lights strobing red. Everything I knew about fear said that a people shown the truth of their abandonment at the worst hour of their worst winter would tear themselves apart. Rask had bet everything on that arithmetic. Earth had bet everything on it. Fear compounds faster than truth; that's practically a law of physics.

The light stayed white.

Minute after minute, the dome stayed lit, calm, steady — no alarms, no flare of emergency beacons, no scatter of figures running in the streets. Just light, everywhere, all at once, like the colony had drawn a breath and was holding it. And then, at the edge of my vision, at the far perimeter near the agri-domes, a single lamp went up on a pole. An old work light, the kind farmers use during harvest extensions. Then another, further along the fence line. Then a third, and a fourth, and I understood what I was looking at and my knees nearly went.

They were answering. Lamp by lamp, beacon by beacon, the colonists were lighting everything they owned — not in rage, not in panic, but in acknowledgment. *We hear you. We witness. We are the witnesses.* The whole colony turning itself into a signal, echoing the message back at the sky, at Earth, at the dead man who'd sent it. Nobody had organized it. Nobody could have. It was just what a community does when the lie dies and something truer stands up underneath it.

I laughed. I'll admit it — I stood alone in a freezing bunker at the end of the world and laughed until it turned into something else, the way laughing does when you've been carrying a thing too long. Catharsis and dread in the same breath, exactly as advertised. Because the message was out, irrecoverably out, and the colony hadn't burned —

— but Earth was still listening. The loop was still open. And somewhere past all that dark, men and women with fleets at their disposal had just heard their crime read aloud in a dead engineer's voice, and the window wouldn't close for another fifty minutes, and fifty minutes is a very long time when you're waiting to find out whether the answer comes back as coordinates or as warships.

The CRT chimed. New text, crawling up over the fading waveform:

**INBOUND TRANSMISSION DETECTED — EARTH LOOP 7.**

Someone on the other end was answering.

I stared at the screen, my heart going like the storm against the lattice, and reached for the decode key.
