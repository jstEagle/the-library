## 33. The Colony Answers

The silence lasted eleven minutes.

I know because I was watching the clock on the comm array's wall, the old analog one with a hairline crack across its face that someone had never bothered to replace because, as the saying went around here, everything in this colony is cracked and still working. The broadcast had ended. My voice — and the original's voice, layered beneath it like a watermark — had finished telling nine thousand people that the government they'd spent their lives trusting had murdered them on paper thirty years ago and simply forgotten to tell their bodies.

Eleven minutes of nothing. No sirens. No shouting. Not even the wind, which had the decency to hold its breath along with everyone else.

I sat in the array's operator chair with my hands flat on the console, waiting for the sound of a mob, or marines, or history. What I got instead was the hum of the equipment and my own pulse, which had opinions.

Here's what nobody tells you about telling the truth: it doesn't feel like relief. It feels like stepping off a ledge and discovering, mid-fall, that you have no idea how far down the ground is.

* * *

The first light came from the direction of the hydroponics terraces.

I saw it through the array's north window — a small yellow bloom in the blue-black of the storm-washed evening, like someone had struck a match against the dark. Then another, two ridges over. Then a string of them along the river road, and then the whole eastern slope of the settlement flickering awake, lamp by lamp, window by window, until it looked like the colony had decided to become a constellation.

I didn't understand it at first. I'll admit that freely. I stood at the glass with my breath fogging it and thought, absurdly, *they're burning things.* That's what crowds do when the truth is bad enough, isn't it? They burn. I'd read about it in the historical archives. Earth's own archives, which felt appropriate — learning about riots from the people who caused this one.

But nothing burned. The lights just kept coming.

Then I heard it on the emergency band — not orders, not panic, but Mara Voss's voice, hoarse and steady, explaining to whoever was listening what the children had already figured out. The beacon had been speaking to them for months in the only language young minds could carry, and now every screen and speaker in the settlement had carried the message back out loud, and the children — her children, our children — were standing at their windows holding lamps.

"It's an acknowledgment," she said on the band, and I could hear her smiling through exhaustion. "They're answering him."

Him. The original. The dead man whose voice I'd just borrowed to indict a government. The colonists weren't rioting. They were *signaling back.*

I did the only reasonable thing a man can do when his entire species surprises him. I sat down on the floor of the comm array and laughed until it turned into something else, and then I let it be whatever it was for a while.

* * *

By midnight, the whole valley was lit.

Not the practical lights — those stayed off, conserving power the way they always had, because scarcity is a language we speak fluently here. These were the other lights. The decorative ones. The festival lanterns that came out twice a year for Founding Day and Harvest Close, hauled out of storage by people who hadn't been asked to do it and hadn't waited to be. Fishing boats on the reservoir ran their running lamps in slow circuits. The greenhouse domes glowed full-spectrum, every grow-light blazing, wasting calories of electricity on beauty for the first time in anyone's memory. Old Tomas Ferro climbed the water tower — eighty-one years old, bad knees, the kind of man who complains about stairs the way other men complain about weather — and relit the navigation beacon on top of it that hadn't functioned since before I was decanted.

From the ridge above the settlement, if anyone had been standing there, the colony would have looked like a signal fire the size of a civilization. Which, I suppose, is exactly what it was.

The message being sent wasn't *we revolt.* Revolt aims outward. This aimed up. Every lamp said the same thing in the same silent grammar: *We heard. We are here. We saw what you did, and we are still here, and you will look at us.*

It was, I thought, the most frightening thing the colony could possibly have done. Riots burn out. Mobs get tired. But nine thousand people quietly turning on every light they owned, in the dark, on a dying world, after learning they'd been abandoned on purpose — that's not rage. That's *witness.* And witness doesn't exhaust itself. Witness waits.

* * *

Ade Rask resigned at 6:14 the next morning.

I know the time because he insisted it be logged, which tells you most of what you need to know about Ade Rask. He did it from the council chamber, on the public channel, in a statement he had clearly written himself because no speechwriter alive would have left in the parts that hurt.

"I ordered information suppressed," he said. "I built a list of names to decide who would eat and who would not, and I told myself it was mercy. I learned the shape of that arithmetic from Earth — from the same protocols that wrote this colony off while signing the order. I have no defense except that I loved you, and love, unexamined, becomes a kind of violence." A pause. On the recording you can hear him set something down on the desk — later I learned it was his council seal, placed there deliberately, like a man laying down a weapon. "I resign. I will submit myself to whatever judgment the colony decides is just. And I would ask you to remember, when you render that judgment, that the list exists, and where I kept it, so that no one else ever has to make one in the dark again."

He published the triage list with the statement. All four hundred and twelve names. His own name was on it — third category, the one he'd assigned himself without ever saying so. The man had been planning to starve in the last wave and had never mentioned it to anyone.

Mara found me afterward at the tower, where I was doing the morning diagnostics out of pure habit, because when the world rearranges itself overnight, the one thing that helps is a task with steps.

"Did you hear?" she asked.

"I heard."

"He put himself on it."

"I heard."

She leaned against the doorframe, looking out at the valley, where the lanterns were still going — second night now, and word spreading to the outlying homesteads, farm lights joining in one by one like a sentence being written slowly across the dark. "I spent weeks deciding whether to expose that list," she said. "Weeks. I carried it around like a stone. And he just... handed it to everyone. Before anyone could take it from him."

"That's either courage or control," I said. "With Ade, it's usually both."

She snorted — the inelegant, honest laugh I'd come to trust more than any formal approval. Then her face settled into something quieter. "The children slept last night," she said. "Really slept. First time since the dream started. No shared dream, no war room, nothing. Just... ordinary kid nightmares. One of them dreamed about a dog. She was thrilled."

"The message finished delivering," I said. "Whatever the beacon needed them for — it's done. They're released."

Mara was quiet a moment. "Do you think it knew? The original. Do you think he knew they'd answer with lights?"

I thought about the vault. About the man in the recording who had addressed his final words to *whoever I become,* who had planned a message to outlive his murder, hidden inside a signal loop designed to be decoded by the youngest and least guarded minds on the planet. A man who understood, apparently better than any of us, that people who learn the worst truth about themselves don't always shatter. Sometimes they illuminate.

"Yeah," I said. "I think that was the whole design."

* * *

Not everyone lit a lamp. I want to be honest about that, because the version of this story that gets told later should not be a tidy one.

There were families who shuttered their windows and wanted the whole thing buried again — not because they doubted the evidence, but because doubt was easier than grief. There was a fistfight outside the supply depot between a man who called the broadcast treason and a woman who called him a collaborator, broken up by neighbors before it became anything worse. There was an elderly couple, the Brandts, who had lost a son in the war's final evacuation and who took the news of Earth's betrayal the way bedrock takes a flood: silently, immovably, changed forever underneath. Mrs. Brandt told the council — the rump of it, the three members who hadn't resigned — that she didn't want justice, she wanted her boy, and no broadcast in the universe was going to raise him, and could everyone please stop acting like the truth was a gift when all it did was take things.

She was right, in the way that grieving people are right — completely, uselessly, essentially. The truth is not a gift. It's a debt collector. It arrives with a ledger and it reads the ledger aloud and then it stands there waiting, and what you pay is yours to choose.

The colony chose lights.

And here is the detail I keep returning to, the one I've turned over so many times it's worn smooth: nobody organized it. There was no call to assembly, no chain of messages, no plan. Tomas Ferro climbed his water tower because he felt like it. The fishing boats fell into their circuits because boat crews talk to each other. The pattern emerged the way frost emerges on glass — each person doing the small obvious thing, and the small obvious things adding up to a design no one drew.

That's what the original bet on, I think. Not that the colony would forgive Earth. Not that it would rise up. That it would *recognize itself.* That a community built by people who'd been lied to from the beginning would look at the proof and say, in effect: yes. That sounds like them. And now watch us be us anyway.

* * *

On the third night, the lights changed.

I was back at the comm array — I'd hardly left it; sleep and I were negotiating terms — when the pattern shifted on the monitoring display. The valley's lights, tracked by the old survey satellites' leftover sensors, had been scattered, organic, random. Now they were moving. Deliberately. Blinking.

I pulled the feed up and watched, and my skin did something cold and strange, because the blink pattern wasn't random either. It was the beacon's acknowledgment handshake. The opening cadence of the Bright Protocol itself — the sequence the tower transmitted every day for thirty years, the one I'd maintained without understanding, the one the children had dreamed. The colonists were reproducing it in lamplight, across nine square kilometers of valley floor, blinking it up at the sky.

They weren't just acknowledging the message. They were acknowledging *the messenger.* The tower. Me, in a way I hadn't earned and couldn't refuse.

I keyed the local band. "This is Underhill at the comm array," I said, and my voice came out rougher than intended. "If you're trying to say what I think you're saying — message received. Tower acknowledges. All stations acknowledge." I hesitated, then added the line that had been sitting in my chest since the first lantern bloomed on the hydroponics ridge: "He would have seen this. I want you to know that. Wherever he is, he sees it."

Static. Then, one by one, the outlying stations checked in. Farmsteads. The reservoir crew. The school, where Mara's voice came through with twenty-six children audible in the background, awake, cheering at the radio like it was a match day. Even the medical station, even the jail — where, I learned later, the guards had let the detained marines watch the valley lights through the window, and one of the soldiers, a young corporal whose name I still don't know, had asked quietly whether the lights meant the war was over.

No one had an answer for him yet. But it was the first time anyone had asked a question about the future instead of the past, and I counted that as a border crossing.

* * *

Rask came to the tower on the fourth day.

He walked the whole way from the settlement on foot, alone, no security detail — he'd dismissed them along with everything else — and I watched him approach through the storm-scoured plain, a gray figure in a gray coat under a sky the color of unwashed wool, and I confess I did not open the door right away. The last time I'd seen him, he'd been declaring emergency authority over this building. The time before that, he'd been explaining to me, with terrible gentleness, why ruthlessness was a form of care.

But I opened it. Continuation has its obligations.

"Ade."

"Dominic." He looked older. Not weaker — older, the way stone looks older than soil, as though something soft had been removed. He held a flat case under his arm. "I'm not here to ask forgiveness. I'm here to deliver the council's remaining files. Full records. Everything we suppressed, including the founding-era material your original died over. The colony voted yesterday — what's left of the council, before we dissolved it entirely. Unanimous. Everything goes into the Protocol. Whatever you decide the record should be, it gets everything."

He set the case on the console. For a moment neither of us moved. Outside, the wind worried at the tower's guy-wires, singing its one long note.

"The list," he said finally. "People keep thanking me for publishing it. Putting my own name on it. They act like it was penance." He shook his head slowly. "It wasn't. I need one person to know that. It was arithmetic. I calculated that transparency would stabilize the settlement faster than concealment, and I acted on the calculation, and it worked, and I don't fully know how to be sorry about that, because being sorry would mean pretending I did it for a different reason than I did."

I studied him. The corrosion of good intentions, Mara had once called his story — but standing there, he seemed less corroded than *quarried,* cut down to load-bearing rock.

"Maybe that's the most honest confession anyone's made in this colony's history," I said.

"Don't romanticize me, Dominic. I'll be working the repair crews next month. Let me earn whatever I earn with my hands." He turned to go, then stopped at the door. "Your original built a message that made nine thousand frightened people turn on their lights instead of each other. I spent six years building systems that made them afraid of each other instead of the dark." He almost smiled. "You seven have better instincts than I ever did. Tell the others I said so. Tell yourself. You lot never listen."

The door closed behind him. I stood a moment with the case in my hands, heavy with thirty years of buried paper, and then I set it beside the console — beside the log I was going to have to write, the new protocol, the record that would outlive all of us — and I looked out the north window at the valley.

The lights were still on. Fourth night. Fifth. They would go on, I suspected, until the ships came, however long that was, however many winters. A colony that had learned it was founded on a crime had decided, collectively, without a single order given, to stand in the open and be seen — and having decided it, discovered that being seen was not the same as being destroyed.

The truth hadn't shattered us. It had switched us on.

I sat down at the console and opened a fresh log file, and the cursor blinked at me, patient as a lighthouse, waiting to see what I would sign.

What I didn't know — what none of us knew, that fifth night, with the valley glowing below and the case of sealed files at my elbow — was that the sky was about to answer back. Because at 0347 hours, the deep-space receiver woke every alarm in the tower with an inbound transmission from beyond the system's edge, and the header on that transmission was a single line of coordinates, and those coordinates pointed *here.*
