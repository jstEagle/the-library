## 4. Records Like Fresh Snow

The archive terminal in the Verdance administrative annex smelled like every archive terminal I'd ever known: ozone, old plastic, and the faint institutional conviction that nothing interesting had ever happened here. I'd been hauling cargo past this building for fifteen years without once going inside. Tonight I went inside.

I told myself it was routine. A hauler checking manifests, cross-referencing tonnage against his own logs, the kind of diligence that got a man a small commendation nobody read. That was the beauty of being invisible: you could walk into almost any room carrying a plausible reason, because nobody bothered to imagine you carrying anything else.

The night clerk was a young Combine functionary named Pell, all elbows and lanyard, who waved me toward the public terminals with the enthusiasm of a man waving me off a cliff.

"Three-hour session limit," he said. "No external drives. No printing."

"No printing," I agreed solemnly, as though I had ever printed anything in my life.

The terminal woke with the Combine's soft blue chime, that gentle corporate sound designed to make surveillance feel like hospitality. I pulled up the colony's arrival records first. Fifteen years ago, give or take a season. The month I landed.

I remembered that landing. That was the problem. I remembered the shuttle down through Verdance's amber haze, my hands still blistered from a fire suppression system that had failed to suppress much of anything, my ears still ringing with an alarm that had cut off mid-scream when its power did. I remembered stepping onto the tarmac with one duffel bag and a forged work credential, and a woman at intake who looked at my papers, then at my face, and decided—whether from kindness or indifference—not to look too hard.

So I typed the date into the search field, and the archive gave me back a page so clean it was almost beautiful.

*No arrivals logged on this date.*

Not sparse. Not incomplete. *None.* The manifest for that entire week existed—cargo inbound, equipment transfers, a shipment of irrigation couplings I could have recited from memory—but the passenger list was blank. Not redacted. Redaction leaves scars; you can see where the words were torn out, and torn-out words tell their own story. This was different. This was snowfall. A fresh, unbroken page laid gently over everything, as if no ship had ever carried people down to Verdance in the whole history of the world.

I tried the flight plans next. Orbital traffic records were supposed to be sacrosanct—even the Combine kept those meticulously, because collisions cost money and money was the only scripture they truly read. I searched the orbital corridor around the date I knew a ship had burned above us. I knew the corridor. I had watched it glow.

*Corridor 9-Kilo: nominal traffic only. One unmanned freight drone (Meridian Combine registry). No anomalies reported.*

One drone. I stared at that line for a long time. Somewhere behind my ribs, something cold and patient began to uncoil.

Because here is what paperwork can do that violence cannot: violence leaves ash, and ash can be found. But paperwork rewrites the sky itself. It reaches backward through time and edits what happened until the men who died become a clerical impossibility. You cannot mourn a ship that never flew. You cannot even be a witness, properly speaking, because witnesses require events, and events require records, and the record said the sky over Verdance that night had held nothing but a drone and stars.

I sat very still in the blue light and felt the vertigo come on—that specific nausea of a man whose memory has just been declared fictional by an institution with better handwriting than he has. Fifteen years I'd lived with the burning ship behind a welded door. Now I understood the door had never really been mine. They'd built a second door over it, out of clean pages, and called the room empty.

* * *

I should have stopped there. Any sensible invisible man would have stopped there. But grief makes a poor archivist, and I kept pulling threads.

The debris field. Every terraformer on Verdance knew about the meteor strike in the southern ice shelf—the one the survey teams mapped in my second year, the crater everyone politely ignored because it sat in a sector too cold to farm and too far to matter. I typed the coordinates.

The geological survey came up instantly. *Impact event, natural origin. Nickel-iron composition consistent with chondritic meteorite. Estimated mass 40 tonnes. No recovery recommended.*

Chondritic. I let out a breath I hadn't known I was holding, and it shook a little on the way out. Because I had hauled salvage out of that ice myself once—a job nobody questioned, since hauling was what I did—and I had stood on fused alloy that no asteroid ever smelted. I had run my gloved thumb along a weld seam in vacuum-forged hull plating, a seam with a manufacturing stamp worn nearly smooth, and I had told myself the same lie the survey report told: rock falls from the sky, rocks are natural, go home, Emrys, plant your seedlings, keep your head down.

Forty tonnes of chondrite. Someone had written that sentence. Someone had sat at a desk, looked at the spectral analysis of melted refugee-ship alloy, and typed *natural origin* with a straight face and probably a cup of tea. That was the part that made my hands cold—not the erasure itself, but how tidy it was. How *procedural*. Atrocity, it turns out, doesn't need monsters. It needs a form in triplicate and a clerk willing to believe the form.

I ran one more search, telling myself it was the last. Passenger lists, all vessels, all years, any name from the old crew roster I still carried in my head like shrapnel. Captain Ilse Marchetti. Navigator Dawit Tesfaye. Engineer Second Class—

I stopped typing before my own name. Some doors you don't open just to check if they're locked.

The results returned in under a second: *No matching personnel records found.*

Every name. Gone. Fifteen hundred souls, more or less—I'd never known the exact count, and now I never would, because the count itself had been deleted—and the archive didn't even offer me the courtesy of a redaction bar. Just the serene white nothing of a query answered honestly by a database that had been made dishonest at the root.

The terminal chimed softly. *Session ending in 15 minutes.*

And that's when I noticed the access log at the bottom of my screen. My queries, timestamped, itemized, flagged—no, not flagged, exactly. *Annotated.* Beside each search, in the quiet administrative language of the Combine, a single notation had appeared:

*Query pattern noted. Forwarded per protocol.*

Forwarded per protocol. To whom? To what? I thought of Voss—the administrator, Administrator Voss, whose voice came over the colony channel once a month with weather advisories and ration schedules and that particular smooth warmth of a woman reading words she had not written. I had met her twice in fifteen years. Both times she had remembered my name. I had assumed it was good management.

Now it felt like inventory.

* * *

I walked home through the dark between the hydroponics rows, and the colony looked wrong to me for the first time since I'd arrived. All those lights I'd tended, all that green I'd coaxed out of borrowed soil—it suddenly seemed less like a garden and more like a cover illustration. Something pleasant printed over the truth, the way *chondritic meteorite* was printed over a grave.

In my quarters I took down the tin box I kept at the back of the closet, under the spare thermal liner I never wore. Inside: a scrap of hull plating no bigger than my palm, its edge melted to a glassy curl. A manufacturing stamp, half-legible. And a photograph I had no right to still own—a grainy shot of a crew that officially never assembled, taken in a corridor that officially never existed, full of faces the database insisted had never drawn breath.

I looked at the photo for a long time. Then I looked at my hands, which had spent fifteen years learning to grow things quietly, and I understood that the silence I'd treated as survival had actually been rent—paid monthly, in installments, to an office somewhere that filed it under *protocol*.

The children dreamed of a burning ship. The archive said no ship ever burned. One of those things was a lie, and for fifteen years I had helped decide which one the colony got to believe.

My comm unit chimed. Colony internal, priority flag, sender: **Administrator Voss**.

The message was four lines long and warm as a heated blanket:

*Emrys. I see you've been doing some late-night research. Come by my office tomorrow, 0900. Nothing urgent—just want to talk about your future here. There may be an opportunity worth discussing. —V*

Your *future here*. As if I were a seedling she was deciding where to plant. Or whether to plant.

I set the comm face-down on the table, next to the tin box, and sat in the dark listening to the hydroponics pumps cycle, thinking about fresh snow: how beautiful it looks falling, how completely it buries, how a field under snow is not empty but merely *decided*—someone upstream chose what that ground would hold.

Tomorrow at 0900, I would find out what she'd chosen for mine.
