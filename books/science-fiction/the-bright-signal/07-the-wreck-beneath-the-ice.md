## 7. The Wreck Beneath the Ice

The trick to smuggling anything past the Meridian Combine is to make it boring. Boredom is their blind spot. A hauler filing a routine ice-survey manifest for Sector Nine gets a bored wave-through from the patrol buoy. A hauler filing the same manifest with a nervous sweat on his brow gets boarded, scanned, and audited down to his childhood vaccination records.

So I filed my paperwork like a man who had nothing in his heart but ballast tonnage, and I made sure my hands believed it.

Sefu Adeyemi sat across the cockpit from me, arms crossed, watching the buoy field slide past on the display. She hadn't spoken since we lifted from the pad at 0400, and I'd decided her silence was a kind of professional courtesy. You don't chat during surgery.

The two haulers riding in back—Okonkwo and Reyes, both of whom owed me more favors than they could count and had stopped asking why—had loaded survey drills, core samplers, and enough thermal charges to melt through forty meters of glacier. All of it legitimate. All of it logged. The Combine loved logs the way a spider loves silk: everything that touched the web got stuck in it, and everything stuck in it belonged to the web.

"Buoy Seven is pinging," Okonkwo said over the intercom. "Standard handshake."

"Answer it sleepy," I said. "We've done this run eleven times."

Reyes answered it sleepy. The buoy waved us through with all the enthusiasm of a customs clerk at the end of a double shift, and Verdance's southern ice sheet unrolled beneath us, white and featureless as a page no one had written on yet.

That was the official story, anyway. Fresh snow, blank slate, meteor strike of no particular interest, impact energy consistent with a small stony body, nothing to see here, move along. I'd read the geological survey myself, three nights ago, in an archive I was increasingly certain Voss knew I was reading. The report was beautifully boring. It was boring the way a locked door is quiet.

"You knew them," Sefu said finally. Not a question.

I kept my eyes on the altimeter. "Knew who?"

"Don't." Her voice was gentle, which made it worse. "You've been flying toward this crater like a man flying toward his own funeral. Nia drew you a picture, Emrys. She drew a man holding a child in front of a burning ship, and when I showed it to you, you went the color of the ice we're standing on. So don't insult both of us."

I flew for a while. The autopilot could have flown better, but my hands needed something to lie to.

"There were four hundred and six people on that ship," I said. "Give or take. The manifests were never finished. Refugees don't always get counted properly. They get counted the way you count sheep—not individually, just enough to know the flock's still there."

Sefu said nothing. She let me have the silence, which was more than I deserved.

"I wasn't cargo," I went on, because once the door cracks open it doesn't close again quietly. "Officially I arrived on Verdance fifteen years ago on a supply run, one more terraform contractor in a gray jumpsuit. Unofficially—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Unofficially I arrived in an escape pod that fell out of a burning ship, and the first person to reach me was a Combine security officer who looked at me for a long time and then said, 'You were never here.'"

* * *

The crater didn't look like much from the air. That was the first thing that made my chest hurt. Fifteen years ago it had been a wound in the sky—a ship coming apart in a long orange smear, debris raining down onto the ice sheet like the world's worst meteor shower. Now it was a shallow depression, drifted over, smoothed by wind, wearing its blanket of snow the way a grave wears grass. Nature had done the Combine's paperwork for it.

We set down at the rim. The outside temperature read minus sixty-one Celsius, and the wind had teeth. We went out suited, four figures in a white landscape, dragging a ground-penetrating radar sled that would tell us where to drill.

The radar told us within twenty minutes, and what it told us turned my stomach into a cold stone.

"Meteor strikes scatter," Reyes said, frowning at the readout. He'd surveyed impact sites on three worlds; he knew the grammar of falling rocks. "Debris cone, radial pattern, lots of small returns. This is—" He tapped the display. "This is a coherent mass. Forty meters down, there's a structure. Large. Intact-ish. And these—" he pointed at a cluster of dense returns off to the east, "—these are regular. Geometric. That's not rock. Rock doesn't come in rectangles."

"Fused alloy," Okonkwo said quietly. He'd run a spectral sample off the surface scarring while the rest of us argued about drill placement. "No asteroid I've ever heard of has an iron-nickel matrix with polymer binders and ablative scoring. This stuff was manufactured. This stuff was *built*."

Nobody said the obvious thing. Nobody had to. We were standing on a meteor strike that had never been a meteor strike, on a world where the paperwork said otherwise, and every one of us understood at the same moment that the snow under our boots was lying to us—or being made to lie.

We drilled. Thermal charges, controlled melt, forty meters down through blue ice that got older and darker the deeper we went, until the bore light found something that wasn't ice at all.

Hull plating. Curved, scorched, fused into the glacier at an angle, running farther than our lights could follow. Registry stencil still legible along one edge, half-eaten by heat but stubborn:

**AUR — 7741**

Sefu put her gloved hand flat against the metal and left it there a long time. Through the suit comms I could hear her breathing, slow and deliberate, the way you breathe when you're holding something enormous inside a small room.

"My grandmother came on a ship like this," she said. "Not this ship. But this kind. The founders promised passage to anyone who wanted to come build the gardens. Promised it in writing, in the founding charter. Then the charters got amended, and the promises got 'deferred,' and then the ships stopped coming, and everyone agreed not to ask why." She took her hand away. "Everyone agreed not to ask why, Emrys. For fifteen years. My daughter asks why in her sleep."

* * *

It was Okonkwo who found the pod.

He was tracing the edge of the hull section when his light caught a shape half-buried in the fused melt—a rounded shell, three meters long, ablation scars striped across it like tiger marks, hatch still sealed. An escape pod. One escape pod. There should have been dozens.

I knew that pod.

I want to be honest about what that moment was, because honesty is the whole point of this expedition and I've had fifteen years of practice being dishonest with myself. That moment was not triumph. It was not vindication. It was my own hands, in my own gloves, brushing ice off a hull number I had last seen from the inside, and my whole body remembering—unhelpfully, comprehensively—the sound the Aurelia made when it broke. Not an explosion. Explosions are quick. This was structural failure, a long groaning shriek of metal giving up, and then the air itself screaming as it burned, and then me, sealed in a pod, falling away from four hundred and six people whose names I mostly never learned, watching through a port the size of a dinner plate as the ship I was junior engineer on turned into the thing children on another world would dream about before I ever met them.

I knelt in the meltwater and I was not okay, and Sefu crouched beside me and did not pretend I was, and that was the kindest thing anyone had done for me in fifteen years.

"The hatch," Reyes said gently, after a while. "Emrys. The hatch."

The release mechanism was fused, so we cut it, slow and careful, sparks drifting in the bore light like orange snow falling upward. The inner seal cracked with a sigh of trapped air—fifteen years of cold, dead air that had been somebody's last breath of atmosphere—and the pod's interior lit dimly as our lamps swept it.

Empty, of course. One pod launched, one pod found, one survivor accounted for. Me. The arithmetic of my survival sat in that empty couch cradle and stared at me.

But bolted to the aft bulkhead, behind a panel Reyes pried open with the reverence of a man opening a reliquary, was a black box.

Flight recorder. Memory core. The Aurelia's actual brain, the ship's own account of its own death—the telemetry, the comm traffic, the final ninety seconds, everything the Combine's fresh snow had buried. Scorch-marked, frost-rimed, and according to Okonkwo's handheld diagnostic, which he connected with hands that were almost steady—

"—intact," he breathed. "Core's intact. Sealed cell, radiation-hardened. Fifteen years in a glacier and this thing remembers *everything*."

We stood there in the cut-open belly of a dead ship, four people around a box the size of a loaf of bread, and nobody moved to pick it up, because picking it up meant it was real, and real meant the next part, and the next part was going to cost somebody something.

Sefu picked it up. Of course she did. Mothers are constitutionally incapable of leaving the truth lying in the dark.

"We go home the boring way," she said. "Same manifest, same sleepy handshakes. And Emrys—" she held the core against her chest plate, and her visor reflected my face back at me, pale and older than I felt, "—when we're back inside atmo, you're going to tell me the rest. All of it. What you saw. What you agreed to. Why they let you live."

I looked back down the bore tunnel, at the curve of hull plating stamped AUR-7741 disappearing into the ice that had kept its secret longer than I'd kept mine.

"They gave me a new name and a garden," I said. "And I told myself tending it was enough. Planting things for people not yet born, so I wouldn't have to answer for the ones who never got born at all."

The lift back up the shaft was silent except for the winch. Above us, the jump window to Earth opened in nine days, and somewhere in the colony archive, Administrator Voss was already wondering why her ice survey data had a hole in it shaped exactly like a ship.

By the time we cleared the buoy field, I had decided I was done being boring.
