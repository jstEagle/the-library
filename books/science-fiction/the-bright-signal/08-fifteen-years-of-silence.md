## 8. Fifteen Years of Silence

The black-box core sat on my workbench like a confession I hadn't written yet.

It was the size of a loaf of bread, scorched along one edge, its casing pitted where fifteen years of ice had pressed against it like a patient interrogator. Sefu had wrapped it in a thermal blanket for the flight back, and the two haulers—Okonkwo and Reyes, good people who asked exactly zero questions, which is its own kind of loyalty—had carried it into my quarters without being told what it was. They knew anyway. You can't hold something that heavy with your hands and not feel it in your chest.

I hadn't slept. I'd made tea three times and drunk none of it. The garden outside my window was doing what it always did at this hour—breathing out its faint green fog of oxygen, minding its own business, which was more than I could say for me.

Because here's the thing about keeping a secret for fifteen years: you don't keep it in a box. There's no box big enough. You keep it in your body. In the way you never quite finish a sentence about your past. In the way you flinch when someone says *Aurelia*—except nobody ever said *Aurelia*, that was the whole point, the word had been amputated from the language so cleanly there wasn't even a scar.

Until a nine-year-old girl stood up in the communal hall and said hull number 7741, and every door I'd welded shut rang like a struck bell.

I put my hand on the core. It was cold. Of course it was cold; it had been dead in the ice since before Nia Adeyemi was born. But my hand shook anyway, and I thought: *you could still stop here.* Voss's offer was sitting in my inbox like a coiled thing. Quiet life. Clean records. A berth on the jump freighter, Earthside, away from all of it. All I had to do was remain the man I had been for fifteen years, which was no one at all.

Instead I did what cowards do best. I waited until dark, and then I told the truth—to one person, quietly, because even now I couldn't manage it loudly.

Sefu came at the second bell. She didn't sit. She stood by my seedling racks with her arms folded, looking at the black box the way you look at a snake you've been assured is harmless.

"Start anywhere," she said. "But start."

So I started.

* * *

Fifteen years ago I was twenty-four years old and the junior engineer aboard the *Aurelia*, a converted ore hauler carrying four hundred and six refugees out of the Meridian Combine's debt-labor colonies in the Belt. Four hundred and six. I've counted them so many times the number has worn smooth, like a stone in a pocket. I knew maybe thirty of their names. I fixed their air scrubbers and their water recyclers and I complained about the hours, because I was twenty-four and complaining was a hobby.

We were bound for Verdance. That was the promise—the founding covenant, they called it, signed by the first colonial assembly with great ceremony and greater relief. *Any who reach our orbit seeking refuge shall be received.* It's carved over the airlock of the original landing module. I used to walk past it some mornings and feel vaguely proud of a place I'd never seen, the way you can be proud of a country you're only arriving in.

The Combine had other paperwork. The refugees were listed as contract absconders, which under Combine law made the ship itself contraband, which made four hundred and six human beings cargo that had stolen itself.

I remember the last ordinary hour. I was in engineering, re-shimming a coolant pump, and the ship's hum was wrong—not broken-wrong, just *off*, the way a room is off when someone has just left it. Then the captain's voice on the intercom, calm in the specific flat way that means the opposite of calm: "All hands, brace. We have company."

Two Combine patrol cutters, running dark, sliding up out of Verdance's own shadow. They'd let us get all the way to the promised orbit before showing themselves. I understood later that this was procedure. You don't intercept a vessel in open space; witnesses are inconvenient there. You wait until it's inside your jurisdiction, where any wreckage falls on your own terms.

They fired a warning shot first. I want to be fair to history, such as it survives. There was a warning shot.

Then there wasn't fairness anymore.

* * *

"The pod bay," I told Sefu. My voice sounded like it belonged to someone describing weather. "I was closest to it. That's the whole story of my survival, right there—proximity. Junior engineers get the worst duty stations and the best escape routes, and nobody tells you that's what they are until you're using one."

"How many pods launched?"

"Eleven." I looked at my hands. "Mine was the only one that reached the surface intact. The cutters—" I stopped. Started again. "The cutters tracked the launches. Called them debris mitigation. There's a term for everything, if you're the one writing the terms."

Sefu hadn't moved. "And the ship?"

"The *Aurelia* burned for six hours." Fifteen years, and I could still give you the number. Six hours, because the hull was thick and the refugees sealed compartments and fought fires with hand extinguishers while the cutters stood off and watched, waiting, patient as ice. "People got to the observation deck near the end. I know because the pod telemetry picked up their band. They weren't calling for help anymore by then. They were just—talking to each other. Saying names. So somebody would know the names."

My tea had gone completely cold and I drank it anyway, because my hands needed a job.

"I watched it from the pod window. I told myself I was watching for debris fields, navigation hazard, that it was *responsible*. And then it broke apart over the southern ice shelf and the Combine sent a survey team, and the survey team found one pod, and the pod found one survivor, and the survivor was a junior engineer with no family and no record worth checking, and an administrator—a different administrator then, Voss's predecessor—sat across from me in a room that smelled like floor polish and offered me a deal."

"Which was?"

"A name. Emrys Okafor, terraformer, arrived on the supply run of that year, references verified, history clean. A work assignment far from the settlement. And in exchange—" Here it was. The sentence I had never said aloud, not once, not to myself in the dark, not into the recording of my own head where lies get cheaper. "In exchange, I confirmed for the official report that the *Aurelia* was an unregistered salvage scow that suffered a reactor failure. No passengers. No attack. No covenant broken. I signed a statement saying the fire was an accident, and the crater became a meteor strike, and four hundred and six people became a footnote in geology."

The seedling racks hummed their small humid hum. Somewhere outside, Verdance's wind moved through the young pines we'd planted eight years ago, and it sounded almost like the sea, if you wanted it to.

"You were twenty-four," Sefu said finally. "They had you alone in a room with your whole life in their hands."

"That's not an excuse. That's a description." I set the cup down harder than I meant to. "You want to know the worst part? It worked. Silence is *comfortable*, Sefu. It fits like old boots. I built a whole life inside it—I built a *garden* inside it, for God's sake, I grew things for children not yet born because I couldn't face the ones who'd already died. Every year the silence cost less to maintain. That's how you know it's rotting you. Not that it hurts. That it stops hurting."

"And now?"

"Now a nine-year-old dreams of hull 7741 and draws faces at the portholes, and the Combine wants to ship those children Earthside for 'evaluation,' and Administrator Voss has offered me a ticket out of here with my cowardice pre-approved." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "And I keep thinking about the names. They said names, at the end, so somebody would know. And the somebody was supposed to be me. I was the witness. I was the entire memorial. And I spent fifteen years being a locked door instead."

Sefu crossed the room and put her hand flat on the black-box core, the same gesture I'd made, but steadier. "This thing holds their voices too? The cockpit, the cabin, everything?"

"If the memory lattice survived—and it looks like it did—it holds the last six hours. Telemetry, comms, internal audio. It holds the warning shot and the six hours after it. It holds the names." I swallowed. "It also holds my pod launch. And the cutters' targeting chatter. Everything the official record says never happened, timestamped and certified by the ship that died."

"Voss will seal the archive before you can crack it. She'll have already started."

"She'll try. But archives aren't the only broadcast path on this rock." I thought of my hauler sitting in Bay Four, its long-range array rated for relay work across half a system, licensed to me, physically accessible to me, and for the first time in fifteen years the shape of my invisibility turned over and showed me its other side: a man nobody watches can go anywhere. "There's a jump window opening in thirty-one hours. After it closes, whatever evidence exists goes nowhere, gets buried deeper, and the children get evaluated Earthside until they stop dreaming. Before it closes—"

"You could make everyone see it."

"I could make everyone *remember* it. Seeing's easy. Remembering costs." I looked at the core, at my cold tea, at the garden fogging its slow breath against the glass. "I've paid fifteen years of interest on a debt I took on in a polished room when I was twenty-four. I'm done servicing it. I want it paid. All of it, principal and shame together, in public, where it counts."

Sefu nodded slowly, and then she did the thing that undid me more than anything else that night: she pulled up a stool, sat down across from me like a woman planning to stay a while, and said, "Then we'd better start with the names. Yours first. The real one."

And I opened my mouth to give it back to her—my actual name, the one I'd surrendered along with everything else—and that was when the comm panel chimed with a priority flag from the port authority:

**ALL CARGO MOVEMENT SUSPENDED BY ORDER OF ADMINISTRATOR VOSS. BAY FOUR HAULERS TO BE INSPECTED AT FIRST BELL.**

Thirty-one hours had just become considerably fewer.
