## 1. Cargo That Breathes

The hold hummed at 41 hertz. Astrid knew this because she had measured it once, five years ago, on the first night of a haul she'd told herself was ordinary — and because she had spent every night since refusing to measure it again.

Forty-one hertz sat just below the threshold where sound became feeling, but not far enough below. It came up through the deck plates of the *Kestrel's Wake* and into the soles of her boots and settled somewhere behind her sternum, a vibration with no source she could point to and no name she was willing to give it. The ship's own reactor ran at 60. The drive rang at 112 when she pushed past two-tenths. The 41 belonged to something else.

She drank her coffee in the galley instead of the cockpit, which was itself a small confession. From the cockpit you could hear it better. From the galley, with the recycler running and the kettle ticking as it cooled, you could almost pretend the ship was just a ship, and the cargo was just cargo, and the route through the contested orbits of the Verge was just another line drawn across somebody else's map.

"Good morning, Astrid," said Vesper, from everywhere and nowhere, the way she always did. "We crossed the Meridian Line at 0347. You were asleep."

"I'm always asleep when we cross it."

"You were asleep last time as well. And the time before. I've begun to wonder if it's superstition or avoidance."

Astrid turned her cup a quarter-turn on its magnetized ring. "It's scheduling."

"It's neither. But I'll log it as scheduling if that helps you."

Vesper had been with her for six years — longer than the habit, shorter than the grief, though Astrid had never worked out exactly how those intervals nested. The AI's voice was warm without being kind, precise without being cold, calibrated over years of late-night conversations to sit exactly at the edge of what Astrid could tolerate being known by. Some mornings that edge was generous. Some mornings, like this one, with the Verge's patrol buoys blinking amber on the plot and forty-one hertz humming up through the deck, it was narrow as a knife.

"Manifest status," Astrid said, because professionalism was a language too, and it was the only one she trusted herself to speak before noon.

"Two hundred and four units, sealed, nominal. Environmental draw steady. No thermal excursions." A pause — half a second, which for Vesper was an eternity, a whole paragraph of things unsaid. "The pods are quiet today."

"The pods are always quiet."

"That's what I mean."

Astrid looked at the galley's little viewport, where the Verge turned past in slow procession: the shattered moonlet called Cordero's Tooth trailing its glitter of mining debris, the long dark sweep of the demilitarized band, the distant hard spark of the consortium beacon at Threshold Station, burning like a cigarette held to the dark. Contested space. Everyone's claim, therefore no one's law. She had been running this lane for three years because it paid well and asked few questions, and she had chosen the lane originally because its charts were ugly enough that no one looked closely at what came through them.

Two hundred and four units. Unregistered biomass, according to the manifest she filed at every checkpoint, in the flat bureaucratic shorthand that customs officers skimmed and waved through. Biomass. As if the word could be stretched wide enough to cover what actually slept back there: two hundred and four cryo-pods, each one older than her ship, each one drawing power off a tap she'd spliced into the mains herself with shaking hands, each one holding —

She didn't finish the thought. She never finished the thought. That was the discipline. Five years of practice at not finishing thoughts, the way other people practiced instruments, until the not-finishing became smooth and automatic and almost musical.

She finished her coffee instead.

* * *

The first checkpoint came at 1100, a consortium cutter hanging off Cordero's Tooth like a wasp at a wound, its running lights strobing the interrogation pattern. Astrid brought the *Kestrel's Wake* alongside with the bored precision of someone who had done it ninety times, opened her comm channel, and let the routine wash over her.

"*Freighter Kestrel's Wake*, transmit manifest and registry."

"Transmitting. Two hundred four units, unregistered biomass, agricultural substrate, bound for Halcyon Reach processing."

A lie so worn it had gone soft at the edges, comfortable as an old glove. Agricultural substrate. Somewhere behind her, down the spine of the ship, past the bulkhead she did not open and the ladderwell she did not use, two hundred and four pods hummed their 41-hertz hum, and whatever slept inside them slept on, undisturbed by the vocabulary of vegetables.

The customs officer's face resolved on her screen: young, tired, chin stubbled, eyes doing the arithmetic of quota versus effort. He glanced at the manifest. He glanced at her. He glanced at the thermal profile Vesper was feeding him — doctored, gently, the way Vesper had learned to doctor things without ever quite lying, shading the pod-bay's heat signature toward the compost-vats' expected curve.

"Biomass," he said. "That's a lot of biomass."

"Big farm."

He almost smiled. Then his eyes flicked to something off-screen — a supervisor, maybe, or just the weight of the day — and he stamped her through with the universal gesture of a man who wanted his shift to end. "*Kestrel's Wake*, you're cleared. Stay inside the marked corridor. Consortium traffic has priority at the Threshold gate."

"Understood. Kestrel out."

She killed the channel and sat for a moment in the sudden silence, hands still light on the controls, heart doing something she chose not to examine. Ninety-one crossings. Ninety-one lies. Each one easier than the last, which was the part that frightened her — not the lying, but the ease, the way guilt eroded under repetition into something smoother and more portable, something you could carry in a pocket and forget was there until it pressed against your palm.

"They're getting younger," Vesper said quietly.

"The officers?"

"The crews. The people in the pods. Every year I run their biometric baselines against archive norms, and every year the gap widens. They're not aging, Astrid. We are."

Astrid stood, took her cup to the washer, watched it dissolve into gray water. "Log it as scheduling," she said, and went forward to fly.

* * *

The second watch passed clean. The third nearly didn't.

At 1912 shipboard, with the Verge's marked corridor sliding beneath her keel and Threshold Station's beacon swelling from spark to coin to searchlight, Vesper's voice arrived in the cockpit stripped of all its usual warmth — flat, urgent, careful, the voice she used only when the news was bad and the timing worse.

"Astrid. I'm receiving a transmission."

"Consortium?"

"No."

"Then who?"

Another pause. Half a second. A paragraph.

"That's the problem," Vesper said. "I don't know. It's coming in on a frequency that shouldn't exist anymore. And it's encoded in Old Meridian code."

The name landed in Astrid's chest like a dropped tool.

Old Meridian code. Not the modern Meridian Standard that every shipping clerk in the Verge tapped out between coffee sips — the old code, the deep code, the pre-Consolidation signal grammar that had been designed for the great colony ships and their long silences, redundant and ornate, built to survive decades of degradation and still be readable at the far end. It had been extinct for two centuries. Officially. The way certain languages go extinct officially, while somewhere, in some drawer, some hand keeps writing letters to no one.

She knew it anyway. Of course she knew it. Navigator training, class of — she made herself not count — where they'd drilled the old codes into them as heritage, as liturgy, as the verbal equivalent of shipwright's knots: useless in practice, sacred in principle. *If you ever hear this grammar, you are hearing the voice of the drowned,* her instructor had said, and the whole class had laughed, and Astrid had laughed too, and then five years ago she had stopped laughing about anything connected to that word *Meridian* forever.

"Decode it," she said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"I already have. That's why I woke you." Vesper hesitated — a full second now, unprecedented, a shout. "Astrid, the header is a distress pattern. Priority tier one. Origin coordinates follow." Coordinates bloomed on the plot, a set of numbers floating in the debris field off the Verge's eastern shoulder, in a volume of space that every chart in her library labeled with the same three characters: *nil*. Empty. Surveyed, swept, declared empty, closed.

"And the body of the message?" Astrid asked, though her mouth had gone dry, though some animal part of her brain was already screaming at her not to ask, to cut the receiver, to burn for Halcyon Reach and never look east again.

"It repeats one phrase," Vesper said. "In Old Meridian code. Translated literally, it reads: *"All stations, all stations. The ship remembers. Come home."*" Another pause. "And Astrid — there's an authentication block appended to the transmission. A navigator's seal. It's formatted correctly. It's formatted perfectly. And the identifier attached to it is yours."

The cockpit was very quiet. Forty-one hertz rose through the deck plates, through her boots, through her sternum, and for the first time in five years Astrid let herself hear it fully, let herself name it, the thing she'd been not-naming since the night she'd measured it and sworn never to measure again.

The pods. The pods hummed at 41 hertz. The same frequency, she realized now, with the terrible clarity of a lock opening, as the old Meridian cryo-systems had run at — she knew because she'd helped calibrate them once, in another life, in a classroom simulator with a dead woman's hands guiding hers.

The past wasn't knocking. The past had been riding in her hold for years, humming to itself in a dead language, waiting for her to be ready to listen.

"Vesper," she said. "How old is that transmission? When was it sent?"

"That's the impossible part." The AI's voice dropped to almost nothing. "Signal decay analysis puts the original broadcast at least two hundred years old. It should be noise by now. It should be nothing. Someone is retransmitting it — freshly, cleanly, from those coordinates — using a code that died two centuries ago, and signing it with your seal."

Astrid looked east, through the viewport, past the beacon-light of Threshold Station, to where the nil-charts began. Somewhere out there, in a debris field officially empty, something wearing her name was calling her home in the language of the drowned.

"Plot the intercept," she heard herself say. Professionalism, to the last. Dread dressed in a navigator's uniform. "Quietly. And Vesper —"

"Yes?"

"Don't log the seal. Not yet. I want to see it with my own eyes first."

The engines answered before she did, a low gathering note, and the *Kestrel's Wake* turned her bow away from the safe corridor and toward the dark, while behind her, down the long spine of the ship, two hundred and four pods changed key — just slightly, just for a moment — as if, in their sleep, they had felt the turn.
