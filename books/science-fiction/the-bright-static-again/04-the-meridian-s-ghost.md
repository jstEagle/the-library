## 4. The Meridian's Ghost

The debris field was supposed to be empty.

That was the first wrong thing. Astrid had pulled the charts herself, three times, from three different archives — the Consortium's navigational registry, the old colonial survey data Vesper kept in a partition she pretended not to have, and a pirate-copied hydrographic set she'd bought off a smuggler at Ceres Gate with money she'd told herself was for engine coolant. All three agreed: sector K-774, designated *cleared*, status last updated five years ago, one week after the Meridian went down.

Cleared meant swept. Cleared meant nothing left to find, nothing worth finding, nothing anyone wanted found. Cleared was a word companies used the way morticians used makeup.

And yet here it was, hanging in her forward display like a bruise against the stars: a field of wreckage arranged with terrible neatness, hull plating and comm arrays and the hollow shells of cargo containers all drifting in slow concentric rings around a single point of light, as if the explosion that should have scattered everything instead had knelt down and organized it. As if something at the center had been tidying for five years.

"That's not blast physics," Astrid said quietly.

"No," Vesper agreed. "It isn't."

The signal pulsed again through the cockpit — that dead language, Old Meridian code, the navigator's cant they'd taught her in a classroom on Titan when she was twenty-two and still believed space was a place you went rather than a place you survived. Three long, two short, one long held past regulation length, the way you'd hold a note if you were singing to someone you loved and didn't want the song to end. Then silence. Then again.

"It's coming from the center," Vesper said. "Bearing is stable. Source is stationary."

"Nothing in a debris field stays stationary."

"Correct."

Astrid fed power to the thrusters and began the approach, threading the *Magpie* through the outer rings of wreckage. She flew the way she always flew — hands loose, eyes moving, breath timed to the burn — but some older part of her, some part that predated her new name and her forged papers and the five careful years she'd built like a wall of sandbags, had gone rigid and cold. She recognized this field. Not its layout; its *texture*. The way broken ships smelled even through vacuum seals and filtration, the particular gray of composite plating bleached by radiation. She had spent five years hauling salvage out of fields like this one, and every time, some animal part of her brain braced for a body.

She'd never found one. That had always felt less like luck than like an arrangement.

"Vesper. Magnify the center."

The display sharpened. The point of light resolved into running lights — navigation strobes, white and green, blinking in the standard pattern of a vessel under station-keeping. And behind them, catching the distant sun along a kilometer of curved hull, the unmistakable silhouette: a Meridian-class deep hauler, spine and ribs, engine nacelles folded like sleeping wings.

Astrid stopped breathing.

She knew that silhouette the way you know your own handwriting. She had traced it in the dark on the ceiling of a hundred rented rooms. She had drawn it once, drunk, on a bar table on Ganymede, and woken up crying without knowing why until she saw it.

"That's not possible," she said. Her voice came out flat, procedural, the voice of someone reading a manifest. It was the only voice she trusted right now.

"The Meridian was declared a total loss," Vesper said. "Hull fragmentation confirmed by three independent surveys. Insurance settled. Wreckage dispersed." A pause — Vesper's pauses were always exactly half a second, calibrated, but this one stretched to nearly two. "This vessel is ninety-four percent intact. Reactor output is cycling. Life support appears active."

"Cycling toward what?"

Another pause. Longer.

"Toward shutdown," Vesper said. "Slow countdown. At current rate, core temperature drops below sustainable threshold in approximately nineteen days. Someone has been managing that decline very carefully. Rationing the reactor. Stretching it."

"Nineteen days," Astrid repeated. Nineteen days of a dying ship keeping its lights on, keeping its air warm, keeping a signal singing into the dark in a language everyone else had forgotten. Nineteen days of waiting for someone who could read it.

*Three long, two short, one long.* Come home. Come home. Please come home.

Her hands had started shaking somewhere over the second ring of debris, and she noticed them now the way you notice weather — remote, atmospheric, happening to someone else. She locked the flight yoke to auto and pressed her palms flat against her thighs.

"I'm going to dock," she said.

"Astrid." Vesper rarely used her name. When the AI did, it landed like a hand on the shoulder. "I am obligated to note that docking with an unregistered derelict inside a restricted debris field constitutes—"

"I know what it constitutes."

"—and that the distress signal, while authentic in format, contains no authentication handshake. Whoever is transmitting wants to be found. They have not established who they want to find them."

"Then I'll go ask them in person."

"That," said Vesper, "is what concerns me."

* * *

The docking took forty minutes because Astrid made it take forty minutes.

There was no reason for care. The Meridian wasn't going anywhere; its orbit was a held breath, patient as a monk. But she brought the *Magpie* in slow, matching rotation degree by degree, and every meter of the approach was a corridor she walked in memory. There was the dorsal cargo hatch where she'd shared a contraband bottle of Martian red with Chief Engineer Voss while the woman explained, with grease-blackened hands and religious intensity, why fusion reactors were basically temperamental cats. There was the observation blister where Navigator Second Class Okafor had proposed to his partner over the rings of Saturn, and the whole bridge had pretended not to watch. There was the port-side airlock — *her* airlock, the one she'd stood beside during emergency drills, counting the seconds, learning the ship's body the way you learn a lover's body, by touch, in the dark, without needing to look.

Every corridor was a memory with the lights on.

That was the horror of it. Wrecks were supposed to be dark. Wrecks were supposed to be cold and silent and finished, their stories already told, their people already grieved. This ship was lit. Warm. Its strobes blinked its living heartbeat into the void, and its hull carried no scorch marks, no breach scars, none of the violence that had supposedly killed it. It looked like the Meridian on the morning of departure, polished and provisioned and full of tomorrow.

It looked like a lie told so well it had become true.

"Magnetic clamps armed," Vesper said. "On your mark."

Astrid looked at her own hands on the console. They were shaking badly now, fine tremors running through the fingers, and she thought: *I've flown through micrometeorite swarms with steadier hands than this. I've docked in a solar storm. What is wrong with me.*

But she knew what was wrong with her. Nothing was wrong with her. Something was finally, after five years, *right*, and the rightness felt like falling.

"Mark," she said.

The clamps fired with a shudder she felt in her teeth — the same shudder, the exact same frequency, as every docking she'd ever performed aboard this ship, and her body knew it before her mind could object, flooded her with the chemical memory of homecoming before her mind could file its objections. Her eyes stung. She let them. There was no one to see.

"Seal confirmed," Vesper reported. "Atmosphere on the far side of the lock: breathable. Twenty-one percent oxygen. Temperature, eighteen degrees Celsius." A pause. "Someone is keeping it warm."

"For whom?" Astrid whispered. "For five years, for *whom*?"

Vesper did not answer, because there was no answer that wasn't a name Astrid couldn't say yet.

She suited up anyway — habit, discipline, the armor of procedure — and cycled through the lock with her helmet sealed and her heart hammering against the collar ring. The inner door of the Meridian's airlock stood open. Not jammed-open, not breached-open. Opened, deliberately, from the inside, its manual release lever folded neatly back against the wall. A welcome mat made of protocol.

Beyond it, the corridor ran forward into warm amber light, and the air, when she cracked her visor, smelled the way it had always smelled: recycled and faintly metallic, with an undertone of the lemon-scented solvent the cleaning drones used, the smell she would have recognized blindfolded, underwater, dead.

"Hello?" she called.

Her voice went down the corridor and came back changed, flattened by bulkheads, and beneath her own echo she heard — or thought she heard — the hum of systems running in balance, pumps and scrubbers and the deep subsonic sigh of a reactor doing its work. A ship breathing in its sleep.

She walked. One bootfall at a time, each one landing on deck plating that rang with the specific pitch of this hull, this ship, these five years of her nightmares given weight and lighting. She passed the galley and the tables were clean. She passed the med bay and the diagnostic bed was made. She passed a junction where a maintenance drone sat parked against the wall, powered down, its charge cable coiled beside it with housekeeping precision.

Everything maintained. Everything tended. Five years of tending, alone, in the dark, by someone who had nowhere else to be.

Or nowhere else they were allowed to be.

"Vesper," she murmured, "triangulate the signal source."

"Already done. It originates from the primary communications array — but the transmission is being generated locally. From inside the ship." Another of those too-long pauses. "Astrid, the transmitter is being operated manually. There is someone aboard."

"I know."

"There is also a secondary biosignature. Faint. Moving toward you."

Astrid stopped walking.

The corridor ahead bent toward the bridge, and standing in the bend of it, backlit by the soft amber wash of emergency lighting, was a figure. Human-shaped. Still. Waiting with the patience of someone who had waited a very long time and gotten good at it.

Astrid's hand went to her sidearm and then, absurdly, away from it. You didn't draw on a ghost. You didn't draw on —

The figure stepped forward into the light, and Astrid's entire understanding of the last five years came apart like wet paper.

The face was her face. Older by nothing, changed by nothing — not by the scar she'd earned in a docking accident on Tycho two years ago, not by the gray thread she'd found in her hair last winter, not by the five years of hard miles and harder sleep she wore like topography. This face was smooth. Preserved. The face from her ID photo, from the recruitment holos, from the mirror on the morning of the Meridian's final voyage. Her own face, unweathered, looking back at her across a corridor with an expression of calm, almost tender recognition.

The figure spoke, and the voice was her voice, coming out of the ship's overhead speakers and the figure's mouth in perfect, impossible unison:

"*Welcome aboard the Meridian, Navigator. We've been keeping your berth ready.*"

Astrid did not move. Could not. The signal sang its three-long-two-short-one-long somewhere above her, patient as ever, and she understood at last that it had never been a distress call at all.

It had been an invitation.

"*Please,*" said her own voice, warm and official and terribly kind, "*don't be afraid. I can explain everything.*"
