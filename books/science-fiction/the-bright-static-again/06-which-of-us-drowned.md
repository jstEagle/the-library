## 6. Which of Us Drowned

The voice on the comms stopped mid-sentence, and the silence it left behind was worse than the words.

Astrid stood in the Meridian's central corridor with her hand still resting on the bulkhead, feeling the ship breathe through the metal — the old familiar pulse of coolant and air handlers that she had not felt in five years and would have sworn she'd forgotten. She hadn't. Her body remembered everything. That was the problem with bodies. They kept what the mind tried to bury.

"Vesper," she said quietly, subvocalizing through the link at her jaw. "Tell me I imagined that."

"You did not imagine it." Vesper's voice in her ear was careful, the way it got when the AI was choosing among truths. "Voiceprint match: Astrid Dravenmoor, confidence 99.7 percent. Source: internal comm node four, one deck above you."

"That's impossible."

"Yes," Vesper said. "I've been running the numbers for ninety seconds. They remain impossible."

Astrid started walking anyway. That was the other thing about bodies — they didn't wait for permission. She took the ladderwell two rungs at a time, past a maintenance panel she'd once pried open with a pry bar and a great deal of swearing during her second year aboard, past the galley hatch where Chief Engineer Ilsa Voss had pinned up a hand-drawn chart of every coffee machine the ship had murdered. The chart was still there. Faded, curling at the corners, but there. Someone had preserved it. Someone had dusted around it for five years.

The comm node was in the small operations alcove off the bridge accessway, and when Astrid reached it, the woman standing there was already turning to face her.

Astrid had prepared herself for a stranger wearing her face. She had not prepared herself for this: the way the woman's weight settled onto her left hip, exactly the way Astrid's did; the way her hands hung half-curled at her sides, thumbs hooked in her belt loops; the small scar splitting her left eyebrow where a cargo strap had snapped in the yards at Ceres when they were both — when *she* was nineteen.

The woman looked at her the way you look at a mirror that has begun moving independently.

"Well," the woman said. Her voice was Astrid's voice, pitched slightly lower because she wasn't shouting over engine noise. "You took the long way to the docking clamps. Your approach vector was sloppy. You always were sloppy on final approach when you're frightened."

"I'm not frightened."

"No," the original agreed pleasantly. "Neither am I."

* * *

They ended up on the bridge, because there was nowhere else to go that wasn't full of memory, and the bridge was at least full of memory with instruments attached to it. The original sat in the navigator's chair — her chair, Astrid thought, and then corrected: *my* chair — with her boots crossed at the ankle on the console rail, an insolence Astrid recognized from the inside. Astrid stood by the plot table with her arms folded, which was also something she recognized from the inside.

Outside the viewport, the debris field turned slowly, a galaxy of glittering fragments catching the distant sun. Officially empty space. Officially nothing here but rocks and radiation and the legal fiction of a ship that no longer existed.

"Say it," Astrid said. "Whatever you've been rehearsing for five years. Say it so we can get past it."

The original tilted her head. "You think I rehearsed?"

"I think you've been alone on this ship for five years talking to yourself. Of course you rehearsed."

Something flickered across the original's face — hurt, quickly filed away — and Astrid felt a strange vertigo of recognition, because she knew exactly what that flicker looked like from the outside. She had worn it often enough.

"Fine," the original said. "Here's the version without rehearsal. My last continuous memory is the night before departure from Titan Yard. Pre-flight checks, a bad dinner, Ilsa Voss threatening to reprogram the coffee machine with a plasma torch. Then I closed my eyes, and I opened them, and I was standing in a restoration crèche with a company attorney telling me I'd been dead for eleven days." She spread her hands. "Eleven days. That's my gap. That's the whole of it. So as far as I'm concerned, I went to sleep on the Meridian and woke up on the Meridian, and the ship never crashed, and everyone I love is asleep in pods I have personally maintained for eighteen hundred days, and then *you* docked here today flying my body like a stolen skiff."

"Your body—"

"*My* body. Grown from my pattern, printed from my cells, or however they laundered it. And my name, presumably, and my certifications, and my life." The original's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "You walked away from this ship with everything I own. I want it itemized."

Astrid laughed — a short, ugly sound she didn't authorize. "Itemized. Okay. Let's itemize. Item one: a funeral. Did your eleven-day version include the funeral? Because I attended mine. There was a plaque."

"There is no plaque," the original said sharply. "There is no funeral. There is a crew in cryo and a company that told me the restoration was temporary pending recovery operations—"

"And you believed them." Astrid pushed off the plot table and crossed to the viewport, needing the stars between herself and that face. "For five years. You believed a company attorney who told you the crash that killed two hundred people was a clerical hiccup."

"The ship didn't crash."

"The ship is a *wreck*, sitting in a debris field the charts list as empty, running on a reactor that's counting down to failure. What do you call that?"

"I call it home," the original said, "because I don't know any better. Is that what you want to hear? You keep looking at me like I'm supposed to remember something. Like there's a test I failed." She stood, and now her voice cracked along a seam Astrid recognized — the seam that appeared when she was angry enough to be frightened. "So tell me. Navigator to navigator. What happened after Titan Yard? What am I missing?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the others, and Astrid realized with a lurch that the original didn't know. She genuinely didn't know. Her backup ended before the crash, before the fault, before whatever had killed the ship and everyone on it except the woman currently demanding answers in Astrid's own voice.

"You really don't remember," Astrid said.

"Remember *what*?"

"The deaths." Astrid turned around. "Two hundred and six people. Ilsa Voss. Captain Okafor. The whole navigation pool, the medics, the hydroponics techs who used to sing off-key on third shift. They died, and I watched some of it, and I carried the rest out in pods, and I have spent five years hauling their bodies around contested orbits pretending they were unregistered biomass because if anyone ever scanned them properly, someone would ask why a dead crew's backups were being smuggled instead of honored." She heard her own voice rising and couldn't stop it. "You don't get to claim continuity. Continuity requires *memory*. Yours ends at a dinner and a threat against a coffee machine. Mine ends with the hull breach and the dark and waking up in a regen tank knowing everyone I'd ever loved was either dead or frozen. You got the clean cut. I got the scar. Don't you dare tell me yours is the real one."

The original was very still. When she spoke, her voice had gone quiet and precise, the voice Astrid used for reading back course corrections — the voice that meant the numbers mattered more than the feelings.

"My backup predates the crash," she said slowly. "Yours doesn't."

"No."

"So somewhere between my last sync and the end, there's a stretch of time I don't have. And you do."

"Yes."

"Then you're not a copy of me." The original's eyes — her own eyes, gray with the same flecked ring — narrowed. "You're a copy of me *plus*. Which means either you're lying about all of it, or—" She stopped. Astrid watched her arrive at it, watched the deduction land the way it always landed, fast and cold. "Or the company restored me from a stale backup deliberately. Wiped the inconvenient hours."

"Now you're getting it."

"Why?" The word came out almost gentle. "Why would they restore me broken?"

"Because a version of me who remembers nothing can't testify to anything," Astrid said. "You're not a person to them. You're a sealed record. A pre-crash Astrid in a box, signing whatever they put in front of you, confirming that the Meridian and everyone aboard are legally accounted for." She let that sit. "How many documents have you signed in five years?"

The silence stretched long enough that the reactor's pulse seemed loud.

"Fourteen," the original said finally. Her thumb moved to her left hip, pressing there — a self-soothing gesture Astrid knew intimately and hated seeing from the outside. "Affidavits. Confirmations of record. They said it was administrative closure." She looked up, and for one unguarded moment she was just a woman standing in the wreckage of her own assumptions. "What did I sign away?"

"I don't know yet. But I'm going to find out."

* * *

They didn't speak for a while after that. The original retreated to the nav station and pulled up the ship's logs — the real ones, the ones she'd apparently been keeping in parallel with the company's official record, thousands of hours of maintenance notes and sensor sweeps and one-sided conversations logged to nobody. Astrid read over her shoulder despite herself, watching her own handwriting-equivalent scroll past: *Coolant loop three flushed. Pod 114's heater cycling irregular again. Talked to Voss today. She didn't answer. Talked to her anyway.*

"You talked to the pods," Astrid said.

"They're not pods to me." The original didn't turn around. "They're my crew. I ran emergency drills on their schedules. I celebrated their birthdays. I learned every one of their sleep patterns so I'd know immediately if anything drifted." A pause. "Did you ever even open the hold?"

Astrid thought of five years of avoiding that hum, that soft collective breathing sound, taking longer routes through her own ship to avoid a door. "No."

"No," the original repeated softly. "So tell me again which of us kept faith with them."

"That's not fair."

"None of this is fair. That's the entire point." She swiveled the chair at last, and her face was composed again, but her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Here's what I know. I know I'm the one who stayed. I know I'm the one who kept the lights on and the air cycling and two hundred and six heartbeats stable while you were out there building a *life* — capital-L Life, the kind with a name and a reputation and presumably a favorite bar. And now you come back here with five years of memories I don't have and a story I can't verify, and you expect me to hand you the keys because you suffered *more*?" She shook her head. "Suffering isn't continuity, Astrid. Memory isn't identity. If it were, a dementia patient would be less of a person than you. I am continuous with the woman who boarded this ship at Titan Yard. You are a fork. A branch. An edit."

"A witness," Astrid said.

"A *claimant*."

The word hit harder than it should have, because it was true, and they both knew it. Astrid looked at her own face saying it and understood, finally, the shape of the trap: there was no neutral ground between them. Every fact belonged to both of them and neither. The same childhood. The same training. The same hands. Two claims on one existence, and only one set of records, and the records had been curated by a company with fourteen signatures' worth of reasons to prefer the version that remembered nothing.

"Here's what I can't get past," the original said, quieter now. "If you're right — if they died, if the company lied, if I signed it all away — then the woman I've been for five years, the faithful one, the one who kept the vigil..." She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum. "She was built to be a lock. A lock doesn't get to decide what it's guarding."

"Then stop being a lock."

"It's not that simple."

"It never is. That's what makes it a choice." Astrid held out her hand — an offer, or maybe a challenge. "Help me find out what actually happened. Not the company's version, not the affidavit version. The real one. Whatever it costs us."

The original looked at the hand for a long moment. Astrid watched her weigh it — watched her run the same calculations Astrid would have run, trust against risk, self against self.

"Not yet," the original said, and didn't take the hand. "First you show me the hold. You've hauled my crew for five years. It's time I inspected the cargo."

Through the deck beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, the reactor's countdown pulsed on — and Vesper's voice arrived in Astrid's ear alone, quiet and urgent:

"Astrid. The consortium patrol has changed course. They're coming back. And this time, they're not asking for a manifest."
