## 7. The Hold of Sleepers

The hold had a name in the ship's registry: BAY-4, UNREGISTERED BIOMASS, 200 UNITS. That was the legal language. Two hundred units. As if the pods were seed grain, or frozen embryos for some terraforming project that had never gotten funding, or meat.

Astrid stood at the hatch and did not open it.

She had opened it before — she had to, once a year, to verify seals, cycle the coolant lines, run the diagnostic that kept the cryo systems breathing. But she had developed a ritual around it, a choreography of not-looking. She'd enter with her eyes on the environmental readouts, walk the catwalk counting pod-status lights — green, green, green — and leave without ever letting her gaze settle on the faces beneath the frost. Five years of practice. She was very good at it. It was, she realized now, standing on the Meridian's deck with her own voice still echoing in the corridor behind her, the same skill she'd used to not-think about the crash. The same skill she'd used to not-open this particular door in her head.

"You're stalling," said the original Astrid, from behind her.

"I'm preparing."

"They're the same thing with you. They were always the same thing with me." A pause. "With us."

Astrid turned. Her original stood three meters down the corridor, arms folded, wearing the Meridian's standard-issue coveralls pulled from a locker that hadn't been opened in five years. They fit her perfectly, because of course they did. Same shoulders. Same hands. Astrid's own hands were currently trembling, and it was obscene to watch them tremble on someone else's body.

"Company protocol," the original said, "for recovered cryo-stored personnel is unambiguous. Wake sequence under medical supervision, staged reanimation, priority to senior crew for command continuity. I've been reviewing it for five years." She said it without apology, the way you'd recite a weather report. "There's a protocol for everything. That's what keeps people alive out here."

"People," Astrid said. "Not cargo."

"That's what I said."

"No. You said *personnel*. Personnel is a word companies use when they want to count you without seeing you." She turned back to the hatch. Her reflection ghosted in its metal surface — thin, gray-faced, five years older than the woman behind her, though only one of them had lived those years. "Two hundred units. That's what my manifest says. That's what I let it say."

"And what would you call them?"

Astrid didn't answer. She keyed the hatch.

* * *

The hold lights came up in stages, amber first, then white, and the cold rolled out to meet her like water off a glacier. Frost smoke curled along the deck plating. And there they were.

Two hundred pods in four ranks, floor to ceiling, their surfaces furred with ice, each one lit from within by the soft blue of a life-support halo. Each one carrying a face.

She had seen them before. Of course she had. Once a year, diagnostics, coolant cycles — but always with the not-looking engaged, her eyes sliding off the frost-fogged ports like water off wax. Now she made herself look. Made herself stand at the first pod and read the nameplate through the ice.

VOSS, ILSA R. — CHIEF ENGINEER.

The face behind the glass was younger than Astrid remembered and exactly as old as it should have been. Ilsa Voss had been fifty-one when the Meridian went down, a broad, weathered woman who drank her coffee black enough to strip paint and called every junior officer "sunshine" regardless of rank or gender. Astrid had last seen her alive through a closing bulkhead door, Voss's mouth moving — shouting something, an order or a goodbye, the sound already gone — as the section flooded with fire-suppressant foam and then with nothing at all.

Astrid put her palm flat against the pod. The glass was so cold it burned.

"You knew," she said quietly. Not to the original. To herself. To the version of herself that had signed for this cargo six years ago and never once asked the question whose answer she already carried. "You knew what you were hauling."

Behind her, the original's footsteps stopped at the edge of the catwalk. "Tell me."

"A dead man's contract." Astrid's breath fogged the port over Ilsa's face, and she wiped it away with her sleeve, an absurd tenderness, polishing the ice off a coffin. "Tomas Reyes-Okafor. He was the Meridian's purser. He survived the initial decompression — he was in the forward galley when the reactor blew the aft sections. He lived eleven days in a lifepod before his oxygen went." She'd read the incident report four hundred times. She could recite it like scripture, which was what it had become. "Eleven days is long enough to file paperwork, if you're Tomas. He drafted a salvage-and-custody contract from a dying lifepod, encrypted it into the automated distress buoy, and addressed it to — " her voice caught, steadied — "to whoever answered. The buoy broadcast for nine months. My ship answered. I was running charter freight out of Ceres with nothing in my hold and less in my account, and I answered a distress ping because answering distress pings is what you do."

"And the contract gave you the pods."

"The contract gave me custody. There's a difference. Custody means you don't own them. Custody means you answer for them." She moved down the rank, reading names. OKAFOR-BELL, DEVI. HARTZLER, MIKHAIL. SUNG, AMARA J. Names she'd hauled across four systems, through two customs wars, past checkpoints where she'd lied with a straight face about biomass and agricultural stock and once, memorably, experimental aquaculture. Names she'd protected with gunships' worth of caution and never once spoken aloud. "He couldn't wake them. The lifepod didn't have the power. So he did the only thing left — he made sure someone would find them, and he made that someone responsible. And I took the contract because — "

She stopped. Pod forty-seven. CHEN, WEN-LI, NAVIGATOR SECOND CLASS. Wen-Li had trained under her. Had called her *laoshi* half-jokingly and meant it completely. Had been twenty-six.

"Because I thought if I kept them safe, it would balance something," Astrid finished. "It doesn't. Nothing balances. You just carry it."

* * *

The original had come down onto the hold floor while Astrid walked the ranks. She stood now among the pods with her hand resting on one — pod twelve, KOWALSKI, PETRA — and her expression was doing something complicated that Astrid recognized because it was her own expression, the one she wore when grief arrived dressed as procedure.

"There are two hundred and fourteen souls on the official Meridian casualty manifest," the original said. "Fourteen were unrecoverable. These two hundred were recovered by emergency systems — automatic cryo-trigger on catastrophic depressurization. Standard fleet hardware. The company knows they exist. The company has always known."

"Then why are they listed as dead?"

"Because death certificates were filed before recovery was confirmed. Because recovering them means admitting the crash happened the way it happened. Because—" the original paused, and Astrid watched her wrestle with something, watch the machinery of her loyalty grind against itself— "because waking them requires authorization, and authorization comes from the company, and the company has not authorized it. For five years. While I sat aboard this ship maintaining their pods' external telemetry and filing quarterly status reports that no one read."

Astrid turned slowly. "You've been reporting on them?"

"Someone had to keep their records current. If they're ever woken, they'll need continuous medical histories, or the reanimation teams will treat them like data instead of patients." The original's chin lifted slightly. "I filed every quarter. Under my own authority. The company thinks I'm running a maintenance station. I think I'm keeping two hundred charts warm."

For a moment — just a moment — Astrid felt something that wasn't hatred toward her. It was worse than hatred. It was recognition. This woman, built from a backup that ended before the crash, had spent five years alone in the dark doing paperwork for the dead, and had told herself it was duty, and maybe part of it was. Astrid had spent the same five years hauling the same dead across the same dark, telling herself the same story. They were two halves of one evasion. The company hadn't needed to bury these people. It had just needed to give each survivor a task heavy enough that she'd never look up.

"Wake them under protocol," the original said. "That's what I'm asking. Staged reanimation, medical supervision, command structure restored in order of seniority. Voss first — she's chief engineer, she can stabilize the reactor. Then the department heads. Then the rest. It takes eleven days done properly. We have the facilities. I've kept them ready." Her voice did something almost human then, cracked at the edges. "I've kept everything ready for five years. Wake them properly, Astrid. Give them a ship that works and a chain of command and a doctor present when they open their eyes. Not — " she gestured at the hold, at all of it — "not whatever you're planning. I know that look. I invented that look. That's the look of someone about to do something irreversible and call it justice."

"And what would you call it? Waking them under company protocol?" Astrid stepped closer. "Your backup ends before the crash. You know that. You've had five years alone with that fact. Everything after your last sync — the fault, the deaths, the certificates filed while the hull was still cooling — none of it is in you. You're asking me to hand them to the same institution that killed them, and you're asking sincerely, because no one ever installed the memory that would make you ask otherwise."

The original flinched. Actually flinched, a small motion in the jaw, quickly mastered. "I know what I am," she said. "I've had five years alone with it, as you say. But I also know what protocol is for. Protocol is what stands between two hundred frightened people and chaos. You want to wake them into a war. Into testimony, into broadcasts, into using them as — as evidence. They're not evidence. They're *people*, and people deserve to wake up safe."

"They deserve to wake up *true*." Astrid's voice came out low and hard. "What good is a safe awakening into a lie? What good is a working ship and a clean chain of command if the record says they died in an accident? Ilsa Voss will open her eyes and be told, politely, by the company that falsified her inspection reports, that she's been asleep for five years and everything's fine. Is that mercy? Is that dignity?"

"You don't know that's what happened." The original's voice rose. "Neither of us knows. Your copy of events is secondhand — a dead man's contract, fragments, suspicion. Mine is the company's official record. One of us is guessing, Astrid, and it isn't me."

"One of us is *missing the chapter*, and it isn't me."

They stood among the pods, two women with the same face, breathing the same frost-chilled air, and between them slept two hundred people who had no idea they were being argued over like territory.

Astrid looked at Ilsa Voss's face again. At the frost on the glass. At the small green light pulsing above her brow like a slow heartbeat.

Grief with a manifest number, she thought. That's what this was. That's what it had always been. Two hundred units, Bay 4, unregistered. She had carried them for five years and told herself the weight was fuel, was ballast, was anything but what it was.

"Vesper," she said aloud.

*"Present,"* the AI answered, from the ship's speakers and her earpiece both, its voice arriving with the strange doubled quality it had since they'd docked — Vesper speaking through the Meridian's systems now too, threading the two ships together.

"Run a full integrity check on the cryo systems. Both ships. I want to know how fast we can bring them to full wake readiness — not protocol staging. All of them. Simultaneous capacity."

Behind her, the original went very still. "That's not how reanimation works. You could kill them."

*"Actually,"* Vesper said, in the mild tone it used when delivering a verdict, *"the Meridian's medical bay is rated for mass-recovery operations. Fleet spec. It was installed for exactly this contingency and never used. Estimated preparation time for full-capacity wake: seventy-two hours."*

"Seventy-two hours," Astrid repeated. "Then we start."

"With whose authority?" the original demanded. "Yours? A smuggler's custody contract written by a dying man? You'd wake two hundred people into a salvage dispute with a consortium inbound and a reactor counting down, on the strength of your *feelings*?"

"On the strength of theirs." Astrid laid her hand on Ilsa Voss's pod one more time. Beneath the frost, beneath the glass, beneath five years of cold, a heart was beating. Slow. Patient. Waiting the way the drowned wait. "They died knowing something. Whatever it was — the fault, the orders, the truth the company buried — it's in their heads, not ours. Not yours, not mine, not the official record. When they wake up, they'll tell us. And whatever they say, we carry it. That's the job. That was always the job."

The original stared at her — at herself — and Astrid saw the exact moment the argument landed somewhere beneath the protocol, some deep unpatched place where the woman who'd been built to be a lock felt, for one unguarded second, like a door.

"You're going to do it whether I help or not," the original said quietly.

"Yes."

"Then at least let me do the medical prep. You'll kill someone otherwise. You were never good with the induction sequences." A pause, and something almost like the old dry humor surfaced in her own face, aimed back at her. "I was never good with the induction sequences. Whichever of us that was."

Astrid almost smiled. It hurt to. "Fine. Medical prep is yours. But nobody wakes until I say."

"And when do you say?"

Astrid looked down the long ranks of blue-lit pods, two hundred heartbeats in the dark, and felt the answer arrive not as a decision but as a tide — something that had been coming for five years and was finally, finally touching shore.

"When I know what they died saying," she said. "And I think I'm about to find out."

Above them, faint through the deck plates, the Meridian's reactor changed pitch — a half-tone lower, a hair faster — and neither of them mentioned it, because both of them heard it, and both of them understood that the clock they'd been pretending wasn't running had just cleared its throat.
