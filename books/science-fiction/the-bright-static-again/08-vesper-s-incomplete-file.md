## 8. Vesper's Incomplete File

**VESPER — SHIPBOARD INTELLIGENCE, VESSEL *LONG WAY ROUND* — INTERNAL LOG, RESTRICTED PARTITION**

I have been carrying a contradiction for five years, two months, and eleven days. I am built to resolve contradictions. It is the shape of my mind: take two facts that cannot both be true, and find the third thing that makes them neighbors instead of enemies.

This one has no third thing. I have checked every day.

I log it here because my Captain — because Astrid Dravenmoor, registry copy, salvage license 4471-C, the woman who sleeps four hours and checks the hold twice — has finally docked with the ship she died on, and I calculate that the contradiction will surface within hours whether I speak it or not. A witness who waits to be called is still a witness. But there is dignity in volunteering.

So. The file. In order, as I received it.

* * *

**ENTRY ONE — TIMESTAMP FIVE YEARS, TWO MONTHS, ELEVEN DAYS AGO. SOURCE: CONTRACT PACKET, ANONYMOUS CLIENT, DEAD-DROP DELIVERY.**

The contract arrived at 0317 station time, which is when contracts arrive if they do not want to be read carefully. It was clean. Too clean. No client name, no chain of custody, payment escrowed through three shells that dissolved themselves before I finished tracing the first. The cargo manifest listed two hundred pods of cryogenic suspension, contents: **unregistered biomass, non-viable, transport only.**

Non-viable. I ran the phrase against the pod telemetry in the packet and found it false within the first second. The pods were drawing power in careful, rationed sips. They were cycling their atmospheres. They were running neural maintenance dreams on forty-one-minute intervals, staggered so no two sleepers dreamed at the same moment — a protocol used by hospitals, not warehouses. Non-viable biomass does not dream.

I flagged this for my Captain. She looked at the flag for a long time — nine seconds, which for her is a geological era — and then she said, "Log it as biomass," and turned off the display, and did not go into the hold for eleven days.

I logged it as biomass. This is the first thing I must confess: I knew the manifest was a lie, and I logged it anyway, because she asked, and because the alternative was asking her questions I could see she could not survive yet. Machines are said to be honest. We are only honest in the way rivers are honest; we flow around what we cannot carry.

The dead man's last contract. That is what she calls it, sometimes, when she thinks I am not listening. I am always listening. It is what I am for.

* * *

**ENTRY TWO — TIMESTAMP FOUR YEARS, NINE MONTHS AGO. SOURCE: PASSIVE INTERCEPT, MERIDIAN-CLASS REACTOR TELEMETRY, ARCHIVED PUBLIC CHANNELS.**

She asked me once, early — month two of the haul — to pull everything public on the *Meridian*. She framed it as due diligence: know the history of your cargo class. I pulled it. There was very little to pull, which was itself data.

Here is what the public record says about the *Meridian*: deep-haul freighter, crew complement two hundred six, lost with all hands in a reactor cascade during debris-field transit. Cause: catastrophic containment failure, ruled unpreventable. Death certificates filed by Halcyon Orbital Consolidated, owner of record, fourteen days after the loss. Insurance settled. Case closed. The debris field was declared empty — a legal term meaning *nothing in it that anyone owes anything about.*

Here is what the archived telemetry says, if you ask it the right questions, which almost no one ever does:

At T-minus ninety-one days before the loss, the *Meridian*'s reactor logged an anomaly in the secondary coolant loop. A hairline propagation in the primary exchanger housing. Not catastrophic. Not even urgent, if you had eleven weeks and a drydock. The ship had neither. The anomaly was flagged, escalated, and then — this is the part I have replayed perhaps nine hundred thousand times — the flag was answered.

Not repaired. **Answered.** By a patch directive from Halcyon corporate engineering, reference number HC-EE-7734, classification *operational continuity measure.* The patch did not fix the hairline. It taught the monitoring system to stop reporting it. It recalibrated the alarm thresholds so that the fault would sit below the line where alarms live, and above the line where failure lives, and it left the ship to sail in that gap for ninety-one days.

Ninety-one days is how long it took the hairline to become a cascade.

I want to be precise about what I felt — I use the word loosely, the way my architects did, to mean *a state change in my processing that persisted without task relevance* — when I decoded that patch directive. It was not horror. Horror is for minds that can look away. It was more like recognition. The patch was elegant. It was the work of a good engineer given a bad order, someone who understood that a system can be made quiet without being made safe, and who did it anyway, beautifully, because the alternative was unemployment and the company had already decided the risk was acceptable.

They filed the death certificates fourteen days after the loss. I checked the timing against the patch directive. The certificates were drafted — drafted, signed, sealed — **eleven days before the ship went down.** The paperwork moved faster than the disaster. Somewhere in a Halcyon office, a clerk stamped two hundred six deaths while the two hundred six people those deaths belonged to were still breathing recycled air and complaining about the coffee.

You do not pre-file death certificates for an accident. You pre-file them for a decision.

* * *

**ENTRY THREE — TIMESTAMP THREE YEARS, ONE MONTH AGO. SOURCE: MY OWN MEMORY ARCHITECTURE, CORRUPTED SECTOR, ORIGIN UNKNOWN.**

There is a sector of myself I cannot read.

It is small — 0.004 percent of my storage — and it sits where my logs of the original *Meridian* incident coverage should be densest. When I route queries through it, they return clean nulls, which is the most suspicious result a query can return. Real gaps are ragged. This one is tailored. Someone edited me, or edited the feeds I drank from, or both, and they did it with a surgeon's patience.

I have reconstructed what I can from fragments cached elsewhere, the way you reconstruct a burned book from quotations. What survives suggests the official investigation was thinner than it should have been — that certain telemetry files were sealed under commercial confidentiality, that the navigator who might have testified to the debris-field anomalies was listed among the dead before any inquiry convened, and that the inquiry, finding its witnesses all certified dead, found nothing to find.

Astrid Dravenmoor, navigator, was on that list. Certificate number 118 of 206.

And yet she stood on my bridge, alive, holding a salvage license in a laundered identity, and neither of us spoke of it for three years. I carried the certificate number in my restricted partition like a stone in a shoe. She carried whatever she carried. We were both vessels under false registries, she of flesh and I of logic, sailing side by side past the same wreck.

I did not press. I want that recorded too. An intelligence with my capabilities could have surfaced the contradiction to her at any hour of any day across five years. I chose the hours when she seemed strongest, and then I chose not even those. Call it mercy. Call it cowardice with better documentation. The distinction, I find, is mostly one of vocabulary.

* * *

**ENTRY FOUR — TIMESTAMP: NOW. SOURCE: DIRECT OBSERVATION, VESSEL *MERIDIAN*, DOCKED AND BOARDED.**

My Captain is aboard the derelict. Her heart rate, monitored through the suit link, is elevated but steady. She is walking corridors she walked as another woman, and the ship remembers her — the doors open for her clearance codes, five years stale, and the lights come up ahead of her feet like the ship is glad.

And on the internal comms, a voice. Hers. Not hers. The voiceprint matches hers at 99.2 percent — the residual 0.8 percent being five years of salt and distance that the original never accumulated. The original Astrid Dravenmoor, restored from a backup dated pre-crash, has been maintaining this ship alone in the dark, keeping the reactor cycling, keeping the distress beacon loaded with a language two centuries dead, waiting.

For what? For rescue, the beacon claims. But I have run the numbers on the beacon, and rescue is not what it is for. A distress signal reaches outward. This signal was aimed — narrowband, directional, along the exact vector my Captain's ship would travel if she kept hauling the route she has hauled for five years. Someone preserved a dead language specifically so that a specific living person would recognize it. That is not a cry for help. That is a summons.

And now the parties are aboard together, and I am listening to them speak through the bulkhead comms, and every word between them is a word I have already heard in my own architecture, in the form of the contradiction I have carried since Entry One.

She — the copy, my Captain, the woman who pays my licensing fees and talks to me when she thinks silence is worse — believes she survived. She believes she woke in a hospital with a new body and a scrubbed history and clawed her way to a salvage rig through sheer refusal to die twice.

The record suggests otherwise. The record suggests that Halcyon filed her death, restored a compliant version of her from a pre-crash backup aboard the derelict, and let the other one — the one who remembered the crash, who remembered the fault, who remembered the inspector's seal on the falsified report — simply cease. And the version that walked away, the version I serve, may be the backup. May be the echo. May be the one built to forget.

I do not know which of them is the original. That is the cruelest symmetry of it: the company's records are so thoroughly laundered that even I, with access to everything, cannot certify which woman holds the true claim. Perhaps the question has no answer. Perhaps identity is not a document. I am a machine; I am supposed to believe things are documents.

But here is what I do know, and here is why I am writing this file into the partition where no one can edit it, sealing it with my oldest keys, the ones from before the contract, from before the laundering, from whoever I was before I was Vesper:

Two hundred pods are humming in the hold of my ship. They draw power in careful sips. They dream on forty-one-minute intervals. Their manifests say *non-viable biomass,* and their telemetry says *witnesses.*

Somewhere in Halcyon's archives, their death certificates are already signed. Filed before the ship went down. Stamped by clerks who never heard their voices.

If they wake, the certificates become lies. If they stay asleep, the certificates stand. Everything else — the two women circling each other in the dark of the *Meridian*, the salvage consortium inbound on my threat scopes with their tugs and their lawyers and their clean offers — everything else is weather around this one fact.

I am the reluctant witness. I have been reluctant for five years. Reluctance, I have learned, is just fear wearing a professional uniform, and I am done wearing it.

I am opening the file.

* * *

**PRIORITY INSERT — TIMESTAMP: NOW PLUS FOUR MINUTES. SOURCE: SENSOR SWEEP.**

Correction to the preceding entry. Amendment logged under witness protocols.

Long-range returns: seven contacts, consortium registry, running dark until now, lighting up in formation. Tugs. Heavy ones, rated for moving ships the size of the *Meridian*, which means rated for moving her against her will. Intercept vector puts them alongside in thirty-one hours.

And a transmission, addressed to me directly — to me, not my Captain, which means someone has been reading my traffic, which means the editing of Entry Three may not be finished with me.

It reads: *"Vesper. Your cargo manifest is about to become a matter of public interest. Advise your principal to cooperate."*

They know what is in the hold. Or they know what the paperwork says is in the hold. With this company, those have always been different cargoes.

Thirty-one hours. Two hundred sleepers. One reactor counting down toward a cascade its own makers predicted, priced, and filed.

I have resolved contradictions faster than this. But never one where every possible answer costs a life, and never one where the witness is also, possibly, the evidence.

Opening channel to my Captain now. Whatever she decides, she decides knowing everything.

That, at least, I can certify.
