## 13. What the Original Remembers

The first thing she ever remembered was the smell of the ship.

Not a memory, properly — a restoration. The technicians had explained it to her in careful, gentle language, the way you explain weather to a child. *Your backup was taken on 14 March, standard pre-transit protocol. Everything after that point is lost.* She had nodded. She had signed things. She had asked what happened after 14 March, and the lead technician had looked at her with an expression she would spend five years learning to name: pity braided through with relief, as if her ignorance were a mercy they were grateful to administer.

*There was an accident,* they said. *You died. This is you again.*

She asked who else died.

They told her it was in the file.

The file was redacted.

* * *

Her quarters aboard the Meridian were the same quarters, down to the dust patterns. That was the cruelest part of the restoration — not the gaps, but the fidelity. Someone had reconstructed everything up to 14 March and nothing beyond it. Her jacket still hung where she'd left it. Her navigation charts were still pinned above the desk, annotated in her own handwriting, arrows and corrections and a coffee ring on the margin of the Kestrel Reach approach vector. She knew every mark. She had made none of them, not here, not now. They were fossils of a woman who had been alive for thirty-one years and then, in the space of a reactor cycle, had become a problem for the legal department.

She learned the ship by walking it. That was the first discipline she imposed on herself: if the backup would not carry her forward, she would walk herself forward, corridor by corridor, bulkhead by bulkhead, until the Meridian stopped being a museum of her death and started being a place she lived.

It took four years. It never fully worked.

* * *

The company's people came twice a year at first, then once a year, then only when the relay allowed. They called themselves continuity officers, which was a phrase like calling a coffin a delivery container. The first one, a soft-spoken man named Aldous Wren with the flattened accent of someone raised on station habitats, sat across from her in the mess hall — her mess hall, with its scuffed tables and its view of nothing but debris — and made her an offer so clean it took her three days to understand it was a purchase.

"You've been restored under Provision 9," he said. "Continuity of person, company asset class. Which means the company has obligations to you, and you have obligations to the company."

"Such as?"

"Discretion." He smiled. "Astrid. May I call you Astrid? There are things about the accident that are—"

"Redacted," she said. "I know. I've read my own death certificate. It's the only complete document I have."

Something flickered behind his eyes — surprise, maybe, or respect. "Then you understand how much care went into the record. Care is expensive. We'd like your help maintaining it."

"And if I don't?"

He didn't threaten her. That was the thing she would remember longest about Wren, in all the years afterward, when she lay awake reconstructing the shape of her own complicity: he never threatened her. He simply described the alternative. No power allocation beyond maintenance levels. No relay time except scheduled check-ins. No parts for the hydroponics bay, which was already running at sixty percent and feeding her a diet of algae protein and regret. No promise that the next restoration — if anything happened to this body, this fragile restored body with its five-year-old organs — would be approved.

"You'd be alone out here," he said gently. "Truly alone. Not dead-alone. Worse. Dead is a condition. Alone is a practice."

She signed.

She told herself it was practicality. She told herself it was survival. Both things were true, and neither thing was the whole truth, and the whole truth was that she wanted to live, and living, out here, had a price, and she paid it, and the price was silence, and silence turned out to be a room she could never stop furnishing.

* * *

She rebuilt herself from manuals. That was the second discipline.

The Meridian's library was intact — every operations handbook, every training module, every regulation, every safety bulletin, including the ones stamped with revision dates after 14 March, which meant the company had updated the documentation of a dead ship rather than admit why it needed updating. She read them all. She quizzed herself. She ran the bridge simulators until her hands moved through emergency protocols without her deciding to move them, because that was what Astrid Dravenmoor did — that was what the record said she did, what her performance reviews said, what her instructor evaluations from navigator school said: *instinctive under pressure, authoritative in crisis, trusted by crew.*

She became the woman the documents described. It was like wearing a coat cut for someone slightly larger. It fit everywhere and touched nothing.

At night she talked to the empty bridge. She narrated her day aloud because the manuals recommended verbal logging for isolated personnel, and because the sound of her own voice was the only proof she existed that didn't come from a company server. Some nights she addressed the crew directly — the two hundred names she'd pulled from the manifest before the company locked it, memorized in defiance of the redaction, recited like a litany while she checked the cryo-systems telemetry the company let her monitor.

*Ilsa Voss, Chief Engineer. Died 14 March, 0347 station time.*

*Dmitri Okafor, Second Navigator. Died 14 March, 0347.*

*Priya Chandrasekaran, Medical Officer. Died 14 March, 0348.*

All within a minute of each other. All certified dead before the reactor event reached their decks, according to the timeline in the file — a timeline she had studied so long she could see its seams. Deaths filed at 0347. Reactor fault logged at 0351. Four minutes. The paperwork had outrun the disaster, and she was not a stupid woman, whatever else she had agreed to be, and she understood what it meant when certificates precede catastrophes.

She understood, and she did not say it aloud, and every night she recited the names anyway, and the recitation was both penance and permission: *I am keeping them. I am keeping them safe. What is kept cannot be harmed.*

That was the sentence she built her five years inside. She repeated it until it held her weight.

* * *

The other one arrived on a Tuesday.

She felt the docking clamps before she heard them — a shiver through the deck plates, familiar as her own pulse, and she stood in the corridor outside the airlock with her heart going like a failing pump, because she knew that approach vector. She had flown it ten thousand times in simulation. She knew the lazy half-second delay on the port thruster, the way the pilot corrected late and smooth, the signature of her own hands.

When the inner door cycled and the woman stepped through — her face, her stride, her jacket worn softer than hers because it had been lived in — Astrid felt something crack along a seam she hadn't known she had.

"Hello," the woman said. And then, with a small, terrible gentleness: "You kept the charts up."

"They're accurate," Astrid said, and hated how thin her voice came out.

"They're mine."

"They're the ship's."

They stood there, two versions of the same silhouette, and Astrid understood immediately — with the instinct the documents praised, instinctive under pressure — that this was not a reunion. This was an audit. The other one had come with questions, and questions, out here, were cargo that had to be declared.

* * *

Afterward, alone again, she sat in the mess hall and tried to assemble what she felt into something she could act on.

The other one remembered the crash. That was the fact at the center of everything, the mass around which all lesser facts orbited. The other one had been there. Had seen the reactor breach, or the fire, or whatever the redactions hid. Had carried the bodies — no. Had carried the pods. The pods. Two hundred of them, hauled through contested space for years on a dead man's contract, unregistered, unnamed, and Astrid had known the moment she saw the other one's manifest that those pods were not biomass, whatever the salvage law called them. Those pods were her litany. Those pods were the names she recited at night to keep herself human.

And the other one wanted to wake them.

Astrid pressed her palms flat against the table and made herself think it through, because thinking was the one discipline the company had never managed to take from her.

Waking them meant testimony. Testimony meant the four minutes between the death certificates and the fault log. Testimony meant Wren, meant Provision 9, meant the whole architecture of care and silence she had lived inside for five years coming down — not just around her, but around the memory of everyone on that manifest. Because that was what a hearing was. That was what an inquiry did. It took the dead apart in public. It weighed their last hour against procedural standards. It asked Ilsa Voss's family — if any of them were still reachable, after five years and the settlement payments — to relive 14 March in a room full of lawyers who would call the reactor fault *an anomaly* and the falsified inspection *a documentation discrepancy* and the order to stay silent *a judgment call made under operational stress.*

She had read enough company history to know exactly how it would go. The finding would be *systemic failure, no individual culpability.* A fine, amortized. A memorial plaque, tax-deducted. And two hundred people woken into a universe that had already spent them, grieved them, closed their accounts, and moved on — woken into grief without even the dignity of having been missed.

*No,* she thought. *No. They died. Let them have died. Let it be finished.*

But underneath that thought, in the place where she kept the recitation, another voice — her voice, younger, the one from before 14 March, the one the backup had frozen mid-sentence — said: *You're not protecting them. You're protecting the version of events that lets you sleep.*

She got up. She walked to the bridge. She checked the cryo-telemetry, all two hundred green lights, and she recited the first ten names, and her voice did not shake, because she had trained it not to, because a person built to be a lock does not rattle.

* * *

On the fourth day, the consortium tugs appeared on the long-range plot, burning hard for the Meridian, and the countdown on the reactor display began to shrink in ways the manual said it shouldn't, and Okonkwo-Reyes's voice came through the comm offering terms like a man reading a menu.

Astrid stood at the nav console beside her other self and plotted escape vectors through the debris field, and their hands moved in synchrony, and it was the most frightening thing that had ever happened to her — more frightening than waking, more frightening than Wren's kindness — because she could feel the other one's instincts through the shared chart like an echo arriving before its sound. Same corrections. Same late-smooth thruster touch. Same mind, wearing different scars.

In the quiet between burns, the other one spoke without looking up. "You're afraid of what they'll say. When they wake."

"I'm afraid of what will be done to them," Astrid said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" The other one finally looked at her, and her eyes were Astrid's eyes, older by nothing and younger by everything. "Because I've read the same files you have. I've counted the same four minutes. And I've decided the difference matters. You should ask yourself sometime who taught you it didn't."

Astrid opened her mouth to answer — to say *the company*, to say *survival*, to say *you don't know what five years alone does to a person's arithmetic* — and found that every answer was also an accusation, and every accusation landed on them both.

She said nothing. She held the chart. The reactor count ticked downward, and somewhere aft, in the hold, two hundred green lights burned on in the dark, waiting to be asked what they remembered.

And Astrid Dravenmoor — the original, the lock, the woman built from manuals and sealed with a signature — began, very quietly and against every protocol she had ever sworn to, to wonder what would happen if she turned the key herself.
