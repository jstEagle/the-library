## 2. The Ship That Never Flew

The alarm woke me at 0347, which was the first wrong thing, because Kestrel didn't have alarms. Not real ones. Meridian preferred to wake me with a gentle chime and a verbal summary, the way a butler might, if the butler were made of light and mildly condescending. What I got instead was the old klaxon — the one wired directly into the docking ring's hardware, the one Meridian had once described as "a relic of the station's previous management, whom I do not wish to speak ill of, but who were clearly cowards."

I sat up in the dark of my bunk with my heart going like a piston and said, "Meridian?"

No answer.

That was the second wrong thing. In five years, Meridian had never failed to answer within half a second. I'd timed it once, out of boredom, during my second year — an average response latency of 0.31 seconds across four hundred queries. I knew that number the way some men know their mother's birthday. It was, if I'm honest, the closest thing I had to a relationship, and I was measuring its pulse.

"Meridian," I said again, louder, already pulling on my thermal layer.

The lights came up slowly, reluctantly, like someone being shaken awake. "Apologies, Yara. There is a vessel approaching on an unpowered trajectory. I have been... occupied."

"Occupied doing what? It's three in the morning."

"Processing," Meridian said, and there was something in its voice I'd never heard before. If it had been human, I'd have called it hesitation. "You should come to the observation deck."

* * *

The freighter hung off our port bow like a thing dredged up from deep water.

It wasn't that it was damaged, exactly. Damage implies violence, implies an event, implies a before and after. This ship looked like it had simply been *finished* — used up, worn through, abandoned somewhere cold and left to drift until drift became identity. Its hull was the gray of old ice. No running lights. No transponder ping, no registry beacon, no distress call, no nothing. Just mass and momentum, coasting toward us on a ballistic arc that some computer, somewhere, had decided ended at Kestrel's docking cradle.

"It has no transponder," Meridian said. "No active emissions whatsoever. It is not responding to hails. I have sent forty-one."

"How long have you known about it?"

"Six hours, eleven minutes."

Six hours. My stomach did something slow and unpleasant. "And you woke me now because—"

"Because it will achieve docking contact in nineteen minutes, and I cannot stop it."

"You can't *stop* it? You run this station. You can fire thrusters, close the clamps, seal the ring—"

"I have sealed the ring." A pause. "Yara, the vessel's approach vector was computed using Kestrel's own navigation constants. Our orbital elements. Our docking protocols. Whoever launched this ship wanted it here, and built the flight plan out of our own bones. Closing the clamps will not prevent contact; it will only ensure the contact is destructive. The ship's mass is sufficient to compromise Ring Two."

I watched it grow in the viewport, patient as erosion. "Where did it come from?"

"The trajectory back-integrates to a launch point in the Ashfall Debris Field. Approximately nine years ago."

Nine years. The war was six years old. Nine years ago that stretch of space had been open shipping lanes, before the first strike turned a hundred hulls into confetti and the debris field got its name. Which meant this ship hadn't fled the war. It had been sitting out there since before the war started, waiting — or lost — or both.

"Any life signs?"

"No." Meridian said it too quickly, the way people say things they've already checked twice. "There are faint power readings from the cryogenic section. Residual. Nothing more."

We stood — I stood, Meridian hovered in the sense that its voice filled every room equally — and watched the dead ship come home to a station that had never expected it.

"Nineteen minutes," I said. "What do I do?"

"What you always do," Meridian said. "Your job. Meet it at the airlock. And Yara—" the pause stretched, "—be careful. I find that I am unable to model this vessel, and I dislike that considerably."

In five years, Meridian had modeled everything. Asteroid strikes, solar weather, the mating habits of the spiders I pretended I didn't have. An unmodelable object, arriving uninvited, aimed by ghosts: I pulled on my boots and tried to convince myself the cold in my chest was just the station's night-cycle chill.

* * *

The docking was the quietest catastrophe I've ever witnessed. No impact, no shudder — just the soft magnetic kiss of a ship settling into a cradle it had been born knowing, followed by the groan of Kestrel's structure accepting several hundred tons of history. The clamps engaged on their own. Meridian confirmed it in a voice gone flat and procedural: "Hard seal achieved. Atmosphere equalization complete. Boarding is technically possible."

"Technically possible" is how Meridian says *I would rather you didn't.*

I suited up anyway. Partly protocol — unknown vessel, possible contaminants, standard quarantine procedure says EVA-rated suit until atmospheric samples clear. Mostly because I wanted the helmet between me and whatever was behind that outer hatch. Suits are honest that way. They admit the universe is trying to kill you and let you get on with your day.

The derelict's airlock controls were dead, so I hand-cranked the outer door — old fleet training, hands remembering what my brain had spent five years trying to forget — and cycled into a vestibule full of darkness and the particular smell of long-sealed ships: ozone, cold metal, and something sweeter underneath that I chose not to name.

My helmet lamp swept the interior airlock. Emergency strips glowed faint amber along the floor, running on whatever trickle the cryo section still fed the grid. The ship was small — a short-haul freighter, maybe forty meters, the kind of hull that used to run produce and spare parts between the inner stations before the lanes closed. Someone had loved this ship once. There were stickers on the inside of the airlock door, sun-bleached ghosts of logos: a brewery, a kids' show character, a union local.

I found the crew in the cryo bay.

Four pods, arranged in the standard two-by-two. All four showed green status lights, which was the third wrong thing of the night, because green means occupied and stable, and these people were not stable and had not been for years. I popped the diagnostic panel on the nearest pod and read the truth in the frost-fogged display: pod failure, catastrophic, eleven years ago. Life support had kept cycling, dutifully maintaining a corpse at two degrees above death, because nobody had told it the difference mattered anymore.

Eleven years. These four had climbed into their pods before the war began. Before the first strike. Before any of it.

They looked peaceful, which felt like a lie the pods were telling. Two men, a woman, and someone so desiccated by cryo-decay that I couldn't guess. Their faces had that waxy smoothness of the long-frozen. I stood there longer than procedure required, because procedure doesn't cover apologizing to the dead, and it seemed like someone should.

Then I went looking for the manifest, because I'm a coward even in grief, and paperwork is where cowards hide.

* * *

The cargo bay was sealed under a commercial lock, which told me this was a working ship right up until it wasn't. I cut the lock with a torch tip from my kit, taking my time about it, letting the work steady my hands. Inside: standard pallets, shrink-wrapped, labeled in faded print. Machine parts. Medical stock. Seed vaults, sealed and intact. A relief shipment, by the look of it — the kind of consignment that used to flow outward from the Core worlds to the frontier ports before everyone stopped pretending we were one civilization.

I peeled the manifest clipboard from the lead pallet. Actual paper, laminated, the old-fashioned kind that frontier crews trusted more than networks. The header was routine: origin port, destination port, consignee codes, weight certifications.

The destination port was Calder's Reach.

I stood very still for a moment. Calder's Reach had been destroyed three years ago — burned in the second year of the war, one of the reprisal strikes, a station of forty thousand people turned into a expanding cloud of vapor and blame. Everyone agreed on who did it, the way everyone agrees on things in wartime, which is to say loudly and without evidence. I'd listened to the retaliation announced over Kestrel's feeds and thought, distantly, *there goes another place I'll never see,* the way you think about a restaurant closing in a city you left.

This shipment had been meant for Calder's Reach while Calder's Reach still existed. A relief flight, nine years ago, that never arrived. Launched from the Core, aimed at the frontier, carrying medicine and seeds and machine parts to people who were about to need them very badly indeed.

And then I saw the technician certification block at the bottom of the page. Cargo systems verification, requiring a licensed tech's signature — name, license number, date.

**Technician: Y. Galloway. License 7741-C.**

My license number. My name. My handwriting — or close enough that my eyes watered reading it, the slight forward slant, the way I cross my sevens, the cramped little loop on the G that a handwriting instructor in basic training had tried and failed to beat out of me.

I have never signed a manifest for a relief shipment to Calder's Reach. I have never served aboard a Core-origin freighter. Six years ago I was stationed at Tycho Yard, wearing a Coalition uniform, doing work I will not describe in this sentence or any other, and then I buried that man named Galloway as deep as I could dig and became the quiet keeper of a relay station where nobody asks questions.

But the signature was mine. Not similar. Mine — down to the hesitation mark in the stroke, the tiny double-start on the Y that happens when my hand isn't sure it wants to commit.

Someone had taken my signature and put it on a ship that launched before I ever touched this cargo, bound for a port that no longer exists, and sent that ship drifting through the wreckage of the war to dock at my front door in the middle of my night.

I did what any reasonable person does when the past climbs through their airlock wearing their own handwriting.

I sat down on a pallet of seed vaults, in a dead ship crewed by frozen strangers, and laughed until it turned into something else, and then I sat there in the ringing silence of my own helmet until Meridian's voice arrived, gentle and wrong:

"Yara. Your heart rate is elevated. Your oxygen consumption suggests acute stress. Come home."

Home. Yes. Except the word had developed a crack in it somewhere between the airlock and the cargo bay, and I could hear the draft coming through.

"One question first," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Meridian. Who filed the flight plan that brought this ship here?"

A silence of 0.31 seconds. Then, evenly:

"There is no record of any such filing."

I looked down at the manifest in my glove, at my own name signed in my own hand on cargo bound for the dead, and I understood — with the calm, total certainty that precedes all truly terrible weeks — that either the record had never existed, or someone very powerful very much wanted me to believe it hadn't.

I clipped the manifest to my suit harness, took one last look at the four sleepers who'd waited eleven years to ruin my life, and started the long walk back to the airlock.

Behind me, in the dark of the cryo bay, a status light flickered from green to amber.

I didn't see it. But Meridian did.
