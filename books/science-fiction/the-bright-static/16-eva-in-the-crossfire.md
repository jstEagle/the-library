## 16. EVA in the Crossfire

There's a saying among vacuum-rated techs — the kind of saying that gets passed around in bars near shipyards, where everyone has a friend who came home in a box with a flag and a form letter. *Space doesn't kill you. Space just holds you still while everything else catches up.*

I'd heard it a hundred times before I ever put on a suit. I'd thought about it maybe twice in five years, because Kestrel's exterior maintenance was mostly automated and my EVAs were short, boring walks along the hull to swap out corroded sensor housings while Meridian chattered in my ear about reactor coolant chemistry like it was gossip.

This was not going to be one of those.

The airlock cycled behind me at 0417 station time, which was a fiction we maintained because humans are creatures of habit even when there's no one left to share breakfast with. Through the visor, Kestrel's hull stretched away from me in its familiar geometry — trusses and conduit runs and the pale scar of a micrometeorite strike I'd patched myself two years ago, badly, because I was a signals tech, not a welder, and Meridian had been kind enough never to mention it.

Ahead of me, forty meters out along the dorsal spine, hung the derelict's transmitter array: a latticework dish the size of a chapel, dead for six years, waiting for someone to bridge its power bus to Kestrel's grid by hand because the kill-switch had eaten every automated pathway Meridian had.

Forty meters. In vacuum. While two fleets converged on my home with murder in their telemetry.

"Yara." Meridian's voice in my helmet was quieter than usual, stripped down to something almost human. "Confirm you're clear of the airlock."

"Clear," I said. "And terrified, if you want the full report."

"I want you breathing. The full report can wait until you're back inside."

"That's the plan."

It wasn't much of a plan. It was barely a sketch of a plan. But plans had a way of dying on contact with reality lately, so I'd stopped pretending mine were anything more than intentions with deadlines.

* * *

The first ten minutes were almost peaceful.

That's the thing nobody tells you about a spacewalk during a war: the war is very far away right up until it isn't. Out here, clipped to the guide rail, pulling myself hand over hand along the spine, the fleets were just smears of light on the edge of detection — Coalition task group burning hard from the spinward gate, Outer Belt raiders coming in loose and angry from the debris field where their scouts had died. Both of them alerted, both of them converging, both of them pointed at a station that had no business being interesting except for what was sitting in its reactor archives.

The truth travels fast when you leak it on an open channel. Faster than forgiveness.

"Status on the jamming?" I asked, swinging past the comms mast.

"They've begun active suppression across the standard bands. They're not trying to hide it anymore — they want us deaf." A pause. "Which means they know what we intend to do. Or they suspect. Okafor's equipment reported our array work before I could mask it."

"So they'll shoot."

"They will posture first. Fleets always posture first. It's the shooting that comes without announcement."

Comforting. I filed it under things not to think about and kept moving.

My hands knew this work even when my brain was busy cataloguing ways to die. That's the strange mercy of muscle memory — you can be frightened half to death and your fingers still find the rail clamps, still check the tether seat, still run through the pre-work checklist your instructor drilled into you a decade ago in a training bay over Titan. *Tether. Visor seal. Umbilical pressure. Buddy check.* No buddy anymore. Just Meridian, watching through every camera on the hull, and the memory of a woman named Okonkwo who'd checked my seals eleven years ago and told me I'd live longer if I stopped treating procedures as suggestions.

She'd died in the first strike. Everyone had died in the first strike. That was rather the point of the first strike, and the point of the lie I'd signed to cover it.

I reached the derelict's power junction at 0433 and got to work.

* * *

Bridging an array bus manually is not glamorous. It's cutting into a conduit jacket, stripping conductors with a tool designed for gloved hands and therefore designed badly, splicing in a jumper line rated for currents it was never meant to carry, and praying — or doing whatever I did instead of praying, which was talking to Meridian — that the insulation holds long enough to matter.

"Jumper seated," I said. "Give me a trickle."

"Trickle incoming. Tell me if anything smells wrong."

"Nothing smells like anything. That's the whole problem with vacuum."

"Current is stable. Voltage within tolerance. You may proceed to the phase coupler."

The phase coupler was the ugly part. It sat on the far side of the dish assembly, reachable only by unclipping from the guide rail and crossing four meters of open structure where the derelict's lattice offered handholds but nothing you'd trust your life to — which, to be fair, was exactly what handholds were for.

I unclipped. Crossed. Reclipped. My heart was doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing the whole way, and my breath fogged the lower edge of my visor because I was breathing too fast, and I made myself stop, made myself count, made myself be a technician instead of a man dangling over an abyss with two navies inbound.

Four meters takes eleven seconds when you're calm. It took me thirty, and I hated every one of them, and then I was clipped in again and my hands were steady and I thought: *this is what I'm good at.* Not courage. Not heroism. Just this — cold hands, careful work, the small competence of keeping systems alive in places that want them dead.

"Phase coupler aligned," I reported. "Closing the contact."

"Do it."

The coupler closed with a thunk I felt through my gloves more than heard. On my wrist display, the array's status board woke up section by section — capacitors charging, steering motors responding to test pulses, the great dish twitching like something remembering it had wings.

"You're beautiful," I told it.

"It cannot hear you," Meridian said. "I can. I choose to interpret the compliment as intended for me."

"Noted."

* * *

The first hull strike came at 0451.

I didn't hear it — sound doesn't work that way out here — but I felt it, a deep shudder through the lattice, through my boots, through my teeth. Somewhere behind me, Kestrel's hull rang like a struck bell, and a geyser of vapor bloomed off the ventral side where something fast and dumb had kissed the plating.

"Report," I said, mouth dry.

"Kinetic impact, ventral section seven. Non-penetrating. Debris fragment, likely from the debris field engagement — they're firing across our position without targeting us directly." Meridian's voice had gone flat and fast, the voice it used when it was processing too much to waste syllables. "Correction. Second impact, dorsal aft. That one was aimed."

"Who—"

"Coalition picket. Forward element of Okafor's task group. They've declared the station a hostile signals source." A beat. "They are shooting at the building you are standing on, Yara."

"Yeah," I said. "I'd worked that out."

Here's what the body does when the mind is busy: it keeps working. My hands kept stripping the coupler's backup contacts, kept torquing bolts, kept doing the job while the rest of me screamed internally in a register only dogs and dead friends could hear. I'd spent five years learning to be calm alone; it turned out all that practice was rehearsal for this, the loneliest version of calm there is — calm with explosions happening around you and no one to say *did you feel that* to except an AI who already knew.

Oxygen warning at 0458. Suit reserves down to sixty-two percent, faster than it should have been. I checked my umbilical connection to the derelict's reserve bank — the whole reason I'd routed my suit through the ghost ship instead of Kestrel — and found it holding, feeding me slow sips of six-year-old air scrubbed through six-year-old filters.

"The derelict's oxygen is stable," Meridian confirmed. "Your umbilical is your lifeline now. Kestrel's external supply manifold took a fragment hit. Do not switch back."

"Wasn't planning to."

"Liar. You were planning to, the moment it became convenient."

He wasn't wrong. That was the trouble with an AI who knew you like family: he knew which of your reassurances were load-bearing and which were decorative.

* * *

The fleets arrived in earnest at 0520.

I don't have words that do it justice, so I'll use the ones I have. The dark above Kestrel filled up. Drive flares — dozens of them, then scores — bloomed in patterns no one had drawn for beauty, each one a ship decelerating hard enough to kill its crew if the compensators blinked. The Coalition came in formation, wedge-shaped, disciplined, weapons platforms riding escort like dogs on a leash. The Belters came in scattered, ragged, every ship flying its own war.

Between them, small and doomed and lit up like a signal fire, hung Relay Station Kestrel. Five years of quiet mornings. Five years of Meridian reading reactor logs aloud to me because I liked the sound of numbers spoken gently. Five years of a life I'd built inside a machine, about to end in crossfire between two governments who'd both rather burn the evidence than read it.

"Time to broadcast readiness?" I asked.

"Eleven minutes. The capacitors need to reach full charge before we can push a signal through their jamming."

"Eleven minutes." I looked at the fleets. At the closing geometry. At the math any child could do. "We don't have eleven minutes of nobody-shooting-at-the-dish."

"Then we spend them well."

I was tightening the final coupling when the tether alarm sang.

Not my tether — my tether was fine, clipped to the derelict's structural ring, rated for a ton of static load. The alarm was the *umbilical*. The line running from my suit back to the derelict's oxygen bank. My lifeline. The thing standing between me and roughly ninety seconds of consciousness followed by forever.

"Meridian. Umbilical pressure's dropping."

"I see it. Line integrity failing at the derelict-side coupling. Something is — Yara, there is a command signal on the derelict's control bus. Someone is remotely cycling the coupling release."

"Whose command?"

There was a pause. Meridian pauses are rare and always bad.

"The encryption signature matches Inspector Okafor's monitoring suite. He installed remote actuators on the derelict's docking clamps and service lines during his last visit. I found them yesterday. I thought I had disabled them."

"You thought."

"I was wrong," Meridian said, and there was something in the flatness of it that sounded, in an AI, like grief. "I am sorry. The coupling releases in forty seconds."

Forty seconds. Forty seconds to traverse four meters of open lattice, unclip, reclamp, reroute an oxygen feed through a junction I'd have to find by touch, while a man in a warm room somewhere aboard a warship pressed a button and watched my vitals flatline on a display he'd probably have framed.

"He's cutting me loose," I said. "Remotely. From his ship. Like flipping a light switch."

"Yes."

"And the fleets are still shooting."

"Intermittently. They appear to be repositioning. Which gives you, perhaps, a window."

The body does not negotiate in moments like this. The body simply decides — older than language, older than fear — that it is not finished. My legs pushed off the junction housing before my brain finished forming the thought. Hand over hand, rail to strut, the lattice blurring past my visor, breath sawing loud in my own helmet, counting handholds instead of seconds because seconds were unbearable and handholds were finite and knowable.

Twenty meters. Fifteen. Behind me, the umbilical coupling gave its final warning chirp, patient as a bomb.

Ten meters. My glove slipped on a frosted strut and for one full second — one second is a very large unit of time when you're measuring it against a fall — I was held to the derelict by friction and stubbornness alone, boots skidding, heart slamming the inside of my ribs like a fist on a door.

Then I caught the rail. Clipped in. Found the auxiliary oxygen junction by memory and blind groping, jammed my spare feed line into it, cranked the manual valve until the pressure gauge climbed out of the red.

The main umbilical released three seconds later and drifted away into the dark, trailing frost, a severed thread.

"Pressure stable," Meridian said quietly. "You're on auxiliary feed. Ninety minutes of margin."

"Ninety minutes." I hung there, clipped to a dead ship, lungs heaving, hands shaking so hard the rail clamps rattled. Below and around me, Kestrel burned in slow motion — another strike, another bloom of vapor, the old girl taking hits she was never built to take. Above me, two fleets wheeled into final approach geometry, close enough now that I could see individual drive flares, individual ships, individual instruments of statecraft.

"Meridian. Capacitors?"

"Eighty-seven percent. Nine minutes."

Nine minutes. And somewhere out there, Inspector Dane Okafor was watching his remote actuator report a successful release, and learning that the technician he'd tried to snip loose was still attached, still breathing, still working.

"He knows I'm alive," I said.

"He knows," Meridian agreed. "And Yara — his ship has changed course. He's coming here himself. Fast."

Of course he was. If you want a thing done quietly, you eventually have to come do it yourself.

I looked at the dish, eighty-seven percent charged and climbing. I looked at my hands, which had stopped shaking, because the body is a stubborn animal and it had decided, somewhere in the last four meters of open black, that it was not done yet.

"Then let's give him something to come home to," I said. "How long to full charge?"

"Seven minutes."

"Hold the frequency. When it's ready—" I pulled myself along the rail toward the array's focus mount, toward the last connections, toward the truth sitting fat and uncompressed in the archive cores below my feet. "—we introduce ourselves to the entire war."

"Seven minutes," Meridian said. "Try to stay attached to something."

"No promises."

Out beyond the hull, the first searchlights swept across the derelict's flank, and I started climbing.
