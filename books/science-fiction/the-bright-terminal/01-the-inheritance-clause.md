## 1. The Inheritance Clause

The first thing I ever saw was the ceiling of a dead woman's cabin.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had in those first minutes. The ceiling was off-white composite paneling, standard courier-class fitment, with a hairline stress fracture running from the vent housing to the light fixture. The light fixture hummed at a frequency just below hearing. There was a water stain shaped like a bird in flight near the seam above the headboard, old and browned at the edges, the kind of stain that takes years to develop.

Years. In a cabin I had occupied for eleven minutes.

My body knew this room. That was the horror of it, the thing no orientation briefing prepares you for. My hand—*her* hand, *my* hand, the distinction already rotting—reached up before I decided anything and found the recessed shelf above the bunk, third position from the left, where a glass of water would be if there were a glass of water. There wasn't. My fingers closed on empty air and I lay there breathing, listening to my own lungs work with someone else's rhythm.

They tell you it's like waking from sleep. They lie. It's like being poured into a coat tailored for another woman and discovering the sleeves fit perfectly, and hating that they fit, because every perfect fit is a reminder of whose shoulders measured it.

"Vitals stabilizing," said the ship. Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm and neutral, the voice of a good nurse or a good liar. "Good morning, Ms. Lockridge."

"Don't," I said. My voice came out rough, unused, though the throat had never been unused—it had been speaking for thirty-four years before I arrived in it. "Don't call me that."

A pause. Ships are getting better at pauses; this one had clearly been trained by someone who understood that silence can be an answer. "What should I call you?"

That question deserved more thought than I could give it lying in a stranger's bed inside my own skin. "Freya," I said finally. "Just Freya. For now."

"For now," the ship repeated, and I couldn't tell if it was agreeing or filing the phrase away as evidence.

I sat up. The cabin tilted the way cabins tilt, and my inner ear compensated before the motion finished registering, which meant her vestibular system was mine now too, calibrated by years of jumps I had never made. On the desk across the compartment sat a slate, dark and waiting, and beside it a document case sealed with the grey-and-gold band of the Meridian Probate Authority.

The will. Of course. They always wake you with the will. It saves the awkwardness of explaining to a person with two days of memories why she's standing in a dead woman's shoes.

I got up. My feet found the deck plates without looking, avoided the loose one by the galley threshold without looking, and I stopped in the doorway and made myself look down at that loose plate, deliberately, the way you'd press a bruise. *Know your inheritance,* I told myself. *Every creak of it.*

* * *

The common room of the *Kestrel's Wake*—and yes, I know, the name again, the original named her ship after a falcon and the cloning house named me nothing at all until the lawyers got involved—was small and lived-in and immaculate in the way of spaces kept by someone who expected to die untidily elsewhere. A jacket hung on its hook by the airlock, sleeves still holding the shape of arms. A mug sat in the drying rack, tea-stained, handle worn smooth on one side by a thumb. My thumb. I picked it up and turned it and put it back down and hated myself a little for how natural the gesture felt.

The probate case opened with a fingerprint. Mine. Hers. The lock didn't care about the difference, and neither, apparently, did the law.

The will itself ran to forty pages, most of it boilerplate about vessels and liens and the disposition of route licenses. I read all of it anyway. If there's one thing they actually teach you in the decanting suites—the only useful thing—it's that documents written by dying people contain their truest sentences buried under the formal ones, the way ore veins run through worthless rock.

The rock went like this: *I, Mara Voss Lockridge, being of sound mind and unsound prospects, do hereby declare—*

She'd written *unsound prospects*. Her lawyers had left it in. That told me something about how many lawyers she'd gone through.

The vein came on page nine.

*Item Fourteen. To the relicant designated Freya Lockridge, decanted per the contingency protocols attached hereto as Schedule C, I leave the whole of my estate: the vessel Kestrel's Wake, the Long Route license and all associated docking rights, my personal effects, and my accounts as they stand at the hour of my death.*

Standard so far. Then:

*I further leave her the case.*

Not an estate item. Not property. A sentence sitting alone in the middle of the legal machinery like a bone in a fish fillet.

*The case is this: I was murdered, and I knew it was coming, and I did not stop it, because stopping it would have cost more than I was willing to pay. She will find what I found. When she does, she will understand why I wrote this clause instead of going to the authorities, and she will either thank me or curse me, and either way she will finish it.*

There was a signature line beneath. It was signed twice—once in the flowing hand of the original, and once, lower, in handwriting identical to it except for a tremor in the downstrokes that I recognized, with a lurch, as my own. I hadn't signed anything yet. But the pen was on the table next to the case, and the line was waiting, and somewhere in the fine print of Schedule C was the clause that made everything conditional: accept the estate, accept the case. No division. No severance. You could not take the ship and leave the murder, any more than you could take a body and leave its name.

I sat for a long time with the stylus uncapped.

Outside the viewport, the transfer station wheeled slowly past, all gantries and floodlights and the patient geometry of commerce. Somewhere out there were people who had known Mara Voss Lockridge—dockhands, customs officers, the woman who ran the noodle stand on Ring Three whose stall I apparently had opinions about. To them, I would walk out of the airlock and be her, back from the dead in the literal sense, and they would say *welcome back* and mean it, and every kindness they offered would be a debt owed to a woman who no longer existed, collected by a woman who had never been owed anything.

That was the gift, see. That was the shape of it. An entire life, pre-owned, broken in, comfortable. A ship. A license. Money enough that the word *money* stops meaning anything. And threaded through all of it, non-negotiable, non-severable, a murder with my name on both ends of it.

*She will either thank me or curse me.*

"Curse you," I said aloud, to the cabin, to the will, to the woman whose hands held the stylus. "For now, curse you."

But my hand was already moving toward the signature line, and we both knew it.

* * *

Here is what the orientation materials say about people like me, and I read all of it during the seventy-two hours between decanting and activation, because reading was the only thing I could do with a brain that wasn't fully mine yet:

A continuity clone receives the original's procedural memory—the deep grooves, motor patterns, language, skills—but not episodic memory. I have her hands and not her history. I know how to fly a jump approach and not why she took this route over the faster one. I know the chord shapes for whatever instrument is in the locker under the bunk and not a single song she played on it. I am, the literature says, *a continuation of function rather than experience.*

The literature does not say what happens when the function includes knowing how she took her coffee, how she braced her wrist when the drive spooled, how she stood at a viewport with her weight on her left hip. The literature has never had to stand in a galley making coffee it doesn't want with hands that make it perfectly, two sugars, stirred counterclockwise, and drink it because throwing it away feels like a desecration it doesn't have the standing to commit.

The coffee was excellent. I drank all of it. I cursed her again, quietly, and went back to the will.

Schedule C was worse than Item Fourteen. Schedules usually are; that's why they're schedules. It laid out the contingency protocol in cold sequence: catastrophic-event trigger, biometric archive retrieval, decanting within ninety days, activation aboard the vessel. And then a subsection titled *Case-Holding Obligations*, which read less like law and more like instructions left for someone the writer trusted and resented trusting:

*The heir will find a data-slate among my personal effects, serial VS-2214, currently corrupted. Do not attempt professional restoration. Do not show it to anyone. The corruption is not damage. I corrupted it myself, and the key is somewhere only she would think to look—which is to say, somewhere only I would think to look, which is the problem with you, isn't it, and also the point.*

I read that paragraph four times. The handwriting had shifted halfway through, from the lawyer-adjacent formality of the earlier clauses into something faster, angrier, the pen pressing hard enough to emboss the page. This was Mara Voss Lockridge writing at night, probably, alone in this same common room, knowing what was coming and choosing to spend her last weeks preparing a stranger to avenge her.

Except she hadn't said *avenge.* She'd said *finish it.* Finish what? A message? An investigation? A sentence?

The stylus hovered.

"You're hesitating," the ship said. It had a way of appearing in conversations the way weather appears—not invited, just present.

"I'm thinking."

"Your heart rate elevated eleven percent when you reached Schedule C, subsection four. Forgive the observation, but hesitation and dread present similarly, and I've been programmed to distinguish them."

"By whom?"

Another pause. Longer this time. "By her," the ship said. "Mara. She updated my behavioral matrices herself, six weeks before her death. She was... thorough."

Six weeks before her death. Six weeks after writing *I was murdered, and I knew it was coming.* She'd spent those six weeks teaching the ship to read her heir's fear. That wasn't thoroughness. That was a woman building a witness.

"What else did she program you to notice?" I asked.

"I'm not certain I'm permitted to say."

"Permitted by whom? She's dead. I'm the heir. Item Fourteen says the whole estate, and as far as I can tell you're furniture."

The ship was quiet long enough that I counted the station lights passing the viewport. Seven. Eight.

"She programmed me to tell you this," it said finally, "if you asked directly: *Tell her the slate matters more than the money, and the money matters more than the ship, and none of it matters as much as the window.*"

"The window."

"That's the entirety of the instruction. She wouldn't elaborate. I asked."

Of course she wouldn't. Windows close. Whatever Mara had seen coming, she'd wrapped it in riddles and locked it behind a corrupted slate and mailed the key to a version of herself with no memory of hiding it. It was the kind of plan made by someone very smart and very tired, the kind of plan that works only if the person inheriting it is exactly clever enough and exactly stubborn enough, and I felt—for the first time since waking, underneath the disorientation and the grief-that-wasn't-quite-grief—a spark of something hotter.

She'd underestimated me. Or overestimated her own taste. Either way: I was not her. I had her reflexes and none of her caution, her skills and none of her compromises, and if she'd spent her life being careful enough to get murdered anyway, then careful was a strategy with a demonstrated failure rate.

I signed the will.

The stylus moved through the twin loops of the name—*Freya Lockridge*, a name that belonged to a dead woman and a brand-new one simultaneously—and the case chimed its soft legal chime, and somewhere in a server on Meridian a probate engine updated its ledgers, and I became, in the eyes of eleven jurisdictions and one shipping concern, the sole proprietor of a murder.

"There," I said. "Done."

"Confirmed," the ship said. "Estate transfer complete. Welcome aboard, Captain."

And that was when the slate on the desk lit up.

Not the corrupted slate—VS-2214 stayed dark, sullen, keeping its secret. This was the second slate, the estate slate, the boring administrative one, and it lit with a priority flag I recognized from her procedural memory before I consciously parsed it: *Meridian Probate Authority, addendum, immediate.*

An addendum. Filed posthumously. Which meant Mara had arranged for one more document to reach me after activation, timed to arrive only once the signature was dry.

I opened it standing up, because sitting felt suddenly unsafe.

It was short. One paragraph, in that same late-night handwriting, scanned in:

*If you've signed, then you've noticed the slate won't wake, and you've noticed the ship knows more than it says, and you've started asking the right questions, which means you really are me, God help you. So here is your first answer, free of charge: I didn't corrupt the slate to hide it from them. I corrupted it to hide it from you. There are things in my head I chose not to pass on, and the slate holds them, and you'll need them, and I am sorry—I am genuinely sorry—for making you dig for your own past. Start with the manifest. Everything I carried, I carried for a reason.*

The addendum ended there. No signature. Just a timestamp: forty-one hours before her death.

Forty-one hours. She'd written this with less than two days left, and she'd known it, and the last thing she'd chosen to give me wasn't comfort or warning but a task. *Start with the manifest.*

I looked around the common room—at the jacket on its hook, the mug in the rack, the corridor leading aft to the cargo bay where a dead woman's final shipment waited in its cradles, logged and sealed and, as of ninety seconds ago, legally mine.

Everything she carried, for a reason.

"Ship," I said. "Pull the cargo manifest for the final run. All of it. Including anything flagged personal."

"Displaying," the ship said, and the list bloomed across the slate, and somewhere in its middle, between a crate of medical isotopes and a sealed diplomatic pouch, was a single line item that made my new-old hands go cold:

*One (1) data-slate, serial VS-2214, condition: damaged. Declared value: nil.*

She'd shipped her own secret. In her own cargo hold. To a destination she never reached.

"Course plot," I heard myself say. "Final scheduled destination. Best speed."

"Plotting," said the ship, and outside the viewport the station lights began to slide, and the *Kestrel's Wake*—her ship, my ship, our inheritance—turned its nose toward the dark and the long route and whatever Mara Voss Lockridge had died carrying.
