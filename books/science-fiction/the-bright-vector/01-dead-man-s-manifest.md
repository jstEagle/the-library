## 1. Dead Man's Manifest

The Bright Vector ran ahead of the *Kestrel's Lament* like a river of spilled light, and Kestrel Vasquez flew it the way a man walks a road he knows is going to end.

Nine hours out of Meridian Gap, hauling four hundred tons of refined deuterium slurry in a freighter older than most of the customs officers who'd ever boarded her. The ship hummed around him, that particular low complaint of aging drive coils, and Kestrel sat in the pilot's cradle with his boots off and his collar open, watching the route markers strobe past at intervals so regular they'd become a kind of music. Green, green, amber, green. The Vector's heartbeat. Somewhere behind him, Earth. Somewhere ahead of him, the colonies, both halves of humanity pretending the other didn't exist, and him in the middle with a load of fuel and nothing to say to either side.

He'd been flying this run for six years. Long enough that he knew which relay beacons flickered when the solar wind picked up, long enough that he could feel the moment the Vector crossed from Earth-administered space into the contested middle corridor — a pressure change in the traffic chatter, the way voices on the comm bands went from bored to careful. He was crossing it now. The chatter thinned. The green lights kept coming.

"Manifest check," said the ship, in its flat freighter voice.

"Go ahead."

"Cargo: deuterium slurry, four hundred tons, sealed. Origin: Ceres Processing, verified. Destination: Halcyon Reach transfer station, verified. Pilot of record: Vasquez, Kestrel." A pause, half a second too long, the way there always was. "Clone-lineage designation appended."

There it was. Every manifest, every transit filing, every customs query for six years, and the systems always did that — paused before saying what he was, as if the word needed room to clear its throat.

"Appended," Kestrel said. "Noted."

He pulled his collar closed against a chill that wasn't in the air. The cockpit smelled the way it always smelled: recycled atmosphere scrubbed one time too many, old coffee baked into the padding, the faint ozone bite of the nav console. Home smells. The only home he had, technically speaking, because legally speaking he had none at all.

*Vasquez, Kestrel. Clone-lineage designation appended.* Which meant: not a man, an echo. Not a citizen, a product. His original — the first Kestrel Vasquez, courier, twenty-nine years dead by murder and thirty-one years dead by paperwork — had owned things. A ship. A name. A pension accrued across eleven years of runs just like this one. And when he died, face-down in a docking bay on Corvane Station with no witnesses and no conviction ever filed, all of it had gone into probate limbo, because the courts had never decided whether a clone could inherit from himself.

Kestrel flew the original's ship under a lease-back arrangement that cost him forty percent of every haul. He carried the original's name under a licensing agreement that could be revoked with a signature. He wore —

His thumb found the ring without his permission. It always did. The courier's oath-ring, dull titanium, worn smooth on the inside where a finger had shaped it over years, sitting on his right hand because the licensing authority required clones to display their status on the left. Right hand meant chosen. Left hand meant assigned. Nobody had told him that; he'd figured it out alone, in his first year, staring at his own hands like they belonged to a stranger.

Which, in a sense, they did.

* * *

The customs checkpoint hung at the corridor mouth like a spiderweb strung between two stars — a lattice of sensor gantries and boarding arms, floodlit, bristling, staffed by people who were paid to be suspicious of exactly one kind of traveler. Kestrel saw the query ping his transponder before the hail came, watched the little amber flag bloom on his own console: *LINEAGE REVIEW.*

Of course. There was always a lineage review.

"*Kestrel's Lament*, this is Vector Customs Post Nine-Seven. Reduce to station-keeping and prepare for inspection. Have your documentation ready."

"Copy, Nine-Seven. Reducing." He fed power to the braking thrusters and let the freighter settle into the inspection cradle like an animal lying down for the vet. Through the forward glass, the gantry arms unfolded toward him, slow and mechanical, each one tipped with a scanner head that would look through his hull, his cargo, and — eventually, inevitably — him.

The boarding party was two officers. That was standard. One to read instruments, one to read faces. The instrument-reader was young, maybe twenty-five, with the restless hands of someone who hadn't yet learned that boredom was the job. The face-reader was older, gray at the temples, with the particular patience of a man who had done this ten thousand times and intended to do it ten thousand more.

"Pilot Vasquez." The older one didn't offer a hand. They never did. "Documentation."

Kestrel passed over the slate. Watched the man scroll. Watched him arrive, as they always arrived, at the same page.

"Clone-lineage," the officer said. Not a question. An observation, delivered the way you'd note a cargo discrepancy.

"That's right."

"Original deceased?"

"Thirty-one years."

"Mm." The officer scrolled further. "Your license to operate under the Vasquez registry is current. Your lease on this vessel is current. Your cargo bond is current." He looked up, and his eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse than if they'd been cruel. "You understand that none of this constitutes ownership. If the estate dispute resolves unfavorably, this vessel, this route license, and your operating name revert to the probate authority. You'd be flying on a day-pass."

"I've read my own file, Officer."

"I'm sure you have." The younger officer shifted by the hatch, trying not to stare, failing. They always stared. At the hands, mostly — looking for the telltale seam, the activation scar, some mark that would let them sort him into the category where he lived in their heads. Kestrel held his hands still and let them look. There was no seam. There was no scar. There was just skin, indistinguishable from any other skin, which was the whole obscenity of it as far as the law was concerned: he looked like a man because he was one, and the paperwork disagreed, and the paperwork won.

"Routine question," the older officer said, and now his voice changed, dropped into something almost gentle, the tone reserved for delivering news people already knew. "Your original died on Corvane Station. The case remains open. Unresolved. You're aware that as lineage-designated personnel, you have no standing to press for investigation, no inheritance rights, and no legal claim to any effects recovered from the scene?"

"I'm aware."

"Even the ring?" The officer's gaze flicked down, quick, professional. "Courier's oath-ring, registered to the original. Technically estate property. You understand you're wearing evidence."

"It was in his hand when they found him." Kestrel heard his own voice go flat, the way it went flat whenever he talked about this, the way a floor goes flat after you've walked it ten thousand times. "They logged it, photographed it, released it to me pending resolution. Pending means forever. So I wear it. Somebody should."

The officer studied him for a long moment. Something moved behind his face — not sympathy exactly, but its bureaucratic cousin, the recognition of a situation with no form to file it under.

"Inspection complete," he said finally. "Cargo seal intact. Documentation in order. You're cleared to proceed, Pilot Vasquez." He handed back the slate, and then, at the hatch, paused with his hand on the frame. "Meridian Gap's crowded tonight. Diplomatic traffic. Whole relay's locked down tight — took us three hours to get our own supply skuttle berthed." He shook his head. "Whatever's happening between Earth and the colonies, it's happening soon. Fly safe."

The hatch cycled shut. The gantry arms folded away. And Kestrel sat alone again in the humming dark of his cockpit, holding a slate full of documents that proved everything about him except that he existed.

* * *

He burned for Meridian Gap through the long middle watch, and the silence got into him the way it always did.

This was the part nobody warned you about, taking a clone's berth on the long routes. Not the customs flags, not the stares, not the day-pass existence. The silence. Thirty-one years dead, his original, and the universe had gone on without leaving a forwarding address. No family came looking for Kestrel Vasquez the second, because the family registry listed the line as terminated. No friends came looking, because grief has a statute of limitations and thirty-one years outlasts all of them. Somewhere there might have been people who remembered the first Kestrel — a woman in a port bar on Ceres, a rival courier, a mother? The records were sealed, or lost, or simply never made, because records get made for people and the original had stopped being one before anyone thought to ask.

So Kestrel flew with ghosts instead. He'd gotten good at it. Good at eating dinner across a table set for one and hearing, in the clink of the utensils, the ghost of another man's habits — did the original take his coffee black? Did he sing on the long watches? Was he kind? Was he cruel? Was he the kind of man whose death deserved the eleven-month investigation that had found nothing, or the kind whose death deserved nothing at all?

He didn't know. That was the wound under all the other wounds: he didn't know who he was the echo of. The cloning house had provided genetic material and a service record and nothing else. Memory inheritance was illegal, unlicensed, and — according to everyone who studied such things — impossible anyway. Whatever the first Kestrel Vasquez had been lived and died inside his own skull, and the skull had been opened on a coroner's table on Corvane, and whatever was in it had gone wherever dead men's secrets go.

Except.

Kestrel's thumb moved on the ring again. He caught himself doing it and made himself stop, the way you make yourself stop touching a bruise.

*Except sometimes,* said a voice he didn't listen to, *sometimes you dream about corridors you've never walked.*

He killed the thought the way he always did, with procedure. Pre-landing checklist. Deuterium load stable. Comm arrays nominal. The work was real, the work was his, and if the work was also a cage with very good lighting, well. Most cages were.

Meridian Gap swelled out of the dark ahead of him by the end of the watch — a great spinning wheel of light and traffic, the last big relay before the contested corridor, decked in more signal-lamps than Kestrel had ever seen on it. The diplomatic traffic the customs officer mentioned was everywhere: Earth delegation cutters in their austere navy-gray, colonial envoys in their patched-together colors, escort frigates from three different fleets sitting in mutual parking orbits like dogs deciding whether to fight. The station's announcement channel was running on a loop, calm as a lullaby:

*"All vessels be advised: scheduled diplomatic transmission, Earth-Colonial Joint Session, begins at 0400 station time. Docking queues are extended. Please maintain patience and courtesy during this historic—"*

Historic. Kestrel snorted and keyed for a berth.

The queue was four hours long. He spent them in the cockpit with the autopilot holding station, watching the wheel of the station turn, drinking coffee that tasted like the inside of a thermos, and thinking about the message waiting in his queue from the probate authority. He hadn't opened it yet. He'd been carrying it unread for nine days, the way you carry a diagnosis.

*RE: ESTATE OF VASQUEZ, KESTREL (DEC.) — FINAL NOTICE OF ADJUDICATION.*

Final notice. After thirty-one years, the courts had finally decided. He'd know soon enough whether he was a man with a ship or a squatter with a day-pass. He'd know whether even the ghost of a life he'd built on top of another man's bones was going to be taken back.

He queued up the message. His thumb hovered over it.

And that was when the comm board lit up with the diplomatic band, wide-open, unencrypted, broadcast to every ear on the Vector — because whatever was about to be said between Earth and the colonies, both sides wanted the whole route to hear it fail together.

Kestrel turned up the volume, and forgot the message entirely, and listened to the world begin to close.

Around him, in the queue, dozens of other ships drifted silent, their pilots doing exactly what he was doing. He could see their running lights reflected in his own canopy, a scatter of small bright vessels hanging in the dark like moths around the station's wheel — haulers, tugs, a colonial mail packet, a long-hauler with barnacle growth on its hull. All of them waiting. All of them listening. The whole trade route holding its breath in the dark, and Kestrel among them, a dead man's echo with a dead man's ring and a dead man's name, waiting to hear how much of his world was about to be taken away.

The channel opened. A voice began to speak.

It was not the voice of anyone Kestrel had expected.
