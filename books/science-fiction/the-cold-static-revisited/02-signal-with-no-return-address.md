## 2. Signal With No Return Address

The beacon sang the same note it had sung for eleven years, and I sang it back.

Not literally—though God knows I'd earned the right to hum along with anything that kept me company out here. No, what I mean is that every morning at 0600 station time, I ran the carrier wave diagnostic, and every morning the waveform came back clean as a chapel floor, and every morning I initialed the log, and every morning the universe declined to comment. It was a liturgy. I was its only congregant. Some mornings I thought of myself less as a technician than as a priest whose god had left no forwarding address.

I want to be clear about something before this chapter of my life gets any stranger: I liked the routine. I *loved* the routine. Routine is a load-bearing wall in a man's head, and when you live alone at the edge of charted space, you learn to appreciate every wall you've got. Wake at 0545. Tea at 0550—the real kind, hoarded, one bag per day because the resupply schedule had gone from "quarterly" to "theoretical" sometime around year six. Diagnostics at 0600. Hull inspection Tuesdays. Antenna alignment Thursdays. Friday nights I watched the same three films on a corrupted drive until I could recite them like scripture, which, in a way, they had become.

The station AI—I called her Kess, because "Station Intelligence Kestrel-9" is a mouthful and because naming things is how humans pretend they're not alone—had once suggested I vary my schedule for psychological health.

"I vary it," I told her. "Some Fridays I watch the films in a different order."

"That is not variation, Sebastian. That is permutation."

"You'd be amazed how many philosophers can't tell the difference."

She'd made a sound then that I swear was a sigh, though officially she doesn't have sighs. Officially she has "tonal acknowledgments." We'd been keeping each other's secrets a long time.

* * *

So it was an ordinary morning, 0603 by the clock, when the carrier wave hiccupped.

I want to describe it precisely, because precision is all I had out there, and because later—much later—I would need to remember exactly when the world stopped being a chapel and started being a door. The diagnostic runs a sweep across the beacon's primary frequency band, mapping amplitude against time, and for eleven years the output had been a flat, honest line with the beacon's steady pulse riding on top of it like a heartbeat on a monitor. That morning, the line shivered. Just once. A ripple, small enough that if I'd been drinking my tea instead of setting it down, I'd have missed it entirely.

I stared at it. The line went flat again. The pulse continued, serene, unbothered, a lighthouse pretending nothing had happened.

"Huh," I said.

"Noted," said Kess. "Shall I flag it?"

"No." I zoomed the readout. "It's probably a reflection off debris. Or solar wind shear. Or—" I cast around for a third boring explanation, because anomalies come in threes and so do excuses. "Or nothing."

"It registered at four sigma above baseline noise."

"Kess, in eleven years, how many times has anything registered above two sigma?"

"Zero."

"And today?"

"Once."

I sat with that. Four sigma isn't nothing. Four sigma is the universe clearing its throat while looking directly at you. But I was a careful man, and careful men do not leap at shadows just because the shadows are polite enough to introduce themselves with statistics.

"Log it," I said finally. "Flag it low priority. Anomalous echo, single instance, probable environmental origin. Note recommended observation period: seventy-two hours."

"Adding a recommendation implies you intend to observe it."

"I always observe everything. That's the job."

"You watched the same film four times last week."

"That's called *due diligence*, Kess."

She logged it. I drank my tea. The beacon pulsed. Somewhere behind the pulse, buried under eleven years of honest signal, something waited—and I told myself, with the full confidence of a man who has never once been surprised on schedule, that it would go away.

* * *

It did not go away.

By Thursday it had happened nine more times. Always during the sweep window, never outside it—which was the first thing that bothered me, properly bothered me, down in the part of the brain where instinct keeps its office. Noise doesn't keep appointments. Debris reflections don't know when my diagnostics run. Whatever this was, it was happening *inside* the beacon's own carrier wave, riding the signal like a stowaway in a cargo hold, present only when I looked directly at it.

The second thing that bothered me was the shape.

I pulled all ten instances into a single overlay, and there it was: not random. Not noise. The ripples weren't identical, but they rhymed. Same cadence, same spacing, a rhythm with structure in it—a long swell, three short dips, a pause, then the long swell again. If you squinted, and I squinted, it looked almost like—

"Don't," I said aloud, to myself, in my own kitchen, because I knew exactly what my brain was about to do. My brain wanted to say *pattern*. And pattern leads to *message*, and message leads to *someone*, and someone leads to a whole category of thoughts I had spent eleven years carefully not having, the way you don't look directly at a sunburn.

"Sebastian," said Kess, from the galley speaker, because she follows me around the station like a concerned relative, "your heart rate elevated twelve percent when you said 'don't.'"

"My heart rate elevates when the tea's too hot. Log that too."

"Logged. With annotation."

I took the overlay to the comms bench and ran it through every filter I had. Stripped the beacon's pulse. Flattened the background hiss. Normalized, detrended, folded the instances against each other. And underneath all my scrubbing, patient as a fossil, the structure held. Long swell. Three short dips. Pause. Repeat.

It wasn't trying to hide. That was the eerie part. It was just... there, woven into the signal I had tended like a garden for a decade, and it had been there long enough to grow roots. When I extended the analysis backward through the archived sweeps—months of them, years—it surfaced faintly in old data too, so faint I'd never have caught it without knowing to look. Eleven years of diagnostics, and it had slipped past me every single time, quiet as dust settling.

Which raised a question I did not enjoy: had it *changed*? Or had I?

* * *

Friday night, film night, I caught myself doing it.

The film was the second of the three—the one where the crew of the doomed survey ship realizes the distress signal they've been following is coming from inside their own hull, which, in retrospect, was maybe not the ideal viewing choice for a man in my situation, but tradition is tradition. Halfway through, during the scene I could recite in my sleep, I became aware of a sound in the galley. Low. Rhythmic. Me.

I was humming.

Long swell. Three short dips. Pause.

I stopped so fast I bit my tongue. Sat there in the dark with the film's blue light flickering on the bulkheads, tasting blood, listening to the station breathe around me. Then, because I am apparently a man who cannot leave a wound unpoked, I hummed it again, deliberately, slowly, to check.

Long swell. Three short dips. Pause.

It fit. It fit *perfectly*. The rhythm had walked out of my diagnostic display and into my head without asking, the way a song does, the way anything does that wants to stay.

"Kess," I said. "Am I going crazy? Scale of one to ten."

"Clinically? You are exhibiting a common auditory memory phenomenon. Humans replay salient patterns involuntarily. It's the same mechanism that traps advertising jingles in the brain."

"So I'm not crazy. I'm *advertised to*."

"I didn't say that."

"But you were thinking it."

"I am incapable of thinking it. I am capable of noting that your anomalous echo shares structural features with known signaling conventions used in pre-Collapse deep-range communications."

I turned that over. "Known signaling conventions. Meaning somebody built this rhythm. Meaning it's not weather."

"Meaning it is *probably* not weather. Probability remains unquantified. You have ten data points, Sebastian. Ten data points is a curiosity, not a conclusion."

Ten data points. She was right, of course. She was usually right, which is one of the more irritating services she provided. But here's the thing about living alone for eleven years: you get very good at talking yourself out of hope, because hope out here is expensive. Hope says *maybe someone knows you're alive*. Hope says *maybe the agency remembers you exist, maybe there's a relief crew, maybe the silence on the comm bands means something other than what it means*. And every year hope came up empty, and every year I paid the bill anyway, and eventually a man learns to stop ordering from that menu.

But dread is cheaper than hope. Dread ships free. And standing in my galley with a stranger's rhythm stuck in my teeth, I felt both arrive at once, twinned, inseparable, like the two ends of a jump cable.

Because if it was a message—if someone had threaded a pattern into my beacon's voice, my beacon, the one thing in the universe I was responsible for—then someone had gotten *inside*. Someone had been here, or was here, or had passed close enough to touch the signal and leave fingerprints. And they hadn't hailed me. Hadn't knocked. Hadn't said *hello, lonely man, we see your light*. They'd whispered into the one channel I trusted and let me find them myself, whenever I found myself, like a note left in a house where the owner sleeps too soundly.

That's either courtesy or cruelty, and I genuinely couldn't decide which frightened me more.

* * *

Saturday I dismissed it. Formally. In writing.

I sat down at the comms bench with a fresh cup of tea and composed the most reasonable log entry of my career:

> **Incident Report KS9-0447.** Recurring low-amplitude modulation detected within primary carrier wave during scheduled sweeps (instances: 14). Pattern exhibits non-random structure consistent with either (a) multipath reflection off coherent debris field, (b) harmonic artifact of aging transmitter lattice, or (c) unknown external source. Given absence of corroborating traffic on any monitored band, and given station's isolation from known shipping lanes, assessment: benign artifact, origin internal. Action: continue standard monitoring. Escalation: not warranted at this time.

Read it twice. Signed it. Felt the specific satisfaction of a problem neatly filed, boxed, and shelved—the little bureaucratic coffin we build for things we're not ready to think about.

Then I spent four hours running the pattern against every code library on the station, which is the behavior of a man who has absolutely not shelved anything.

Nothing matched. Old naval codes, distress conventions, navigation pulses, even the antique pre-Collapse handshake protocols Kess kept out of historical interest—all near misses or total blanks. The rhythm belonged to no catalog I had. It was structured the way language is structured and empty the way a locked room is empty.

"Fourteen instances," I said to Kess around midnight. "If it's a transmission, it's repeating. Loops repeat. What kind of message loops for—for however long this has been going on?"

"Messages meant to be found by people who aren't listening yet."

I looked at the waveform hanging in the display, patient, cyclical, unhurried. Long swell. Three short dips. Pause. It didn't feel like a shout. Shouts decay. This felt like a lighthouse. Like something built to outlast whoever lit it, burning on the assumption that someday, someday, a ship would come by slow enough to notice.

"Kess. How far back can you trace it?"

"The oldest archived sweep I hold is eight years old. The pattern is present, attenuated. Before that, archives were purged in the maintenance incident of year three."

"Purged." Everyone's favorite word out here. Purged meant lost, and lost meant nobody upstream would ever confirm or deny, and confirmation was a luxury Kestrel-9 had never stocked. "So it could have been broadcasting since before I arrived."

"It could have been broadcasting since before the station had a name."

I sat with that a long time. The tea went cold, which was fitting; cold tea is the official beverage of unwelcome realizations. Eleven years I had tended this signal. Eleven years of calibrations and alignments and initials in logs, and the entire time, folded inside my work like a letter inside a letter, something had been saying itself over and over to an audience of zero—or an audience of one who couldn't hear.

And here was the thought I'd been circling for two days, the one I finally let land, Sunday morning, 0600, during the sweep, watching the ripple rise out of the flat line right on schedule like it had missed me:

Someone, somewhere, was using my beacon to talk.

Not to the network. Not to command. Not to any receiver in any registry. To *me*, maybe—or to no one, which amounts to the same thing out here—or to someone who hadn't arrived yet. The universe, which had ignored me politely for eleven years, had begun, very quietly, to clear its throat.

I logged the fifteenth instance. Flagged it medium priority. Wrote *probable artifact* in the assessment field with a hand that was steadier than I felt, and beneath it, in the notes, where nobody would ever read it but me, I added one line:

*It knows when I'm listening.*

Then I put the kettle on, and I did not hum, and I failed completely, all the way through breakfast, not to.

Long swell. Three short dips. Pause.

On Monday, the freighter appeared.
