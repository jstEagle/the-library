## 13. Storm at the Edge

There's a particular sound a ship makes when it's lying to you. Not an alarm—alarms are honest, alarms are the universe shouting in your face. I mean the quiet sound. The hum that sits half a tone lower than it did yesterday. The ventilation that cycles four seconds longer than spec. Vantablack had been making that sound for two days, and nobody else seemed to hear it, which either meant I was imagining things or meant that forty years of engineering instinct had survived being poured into a body that couldn't reach the top shelf without a step stool.

I'd like to report I handled the second possibility with grace.

"Say it again," Sebastian said. He was wedged into the co-pilot's seat with his knees folded up like a deck chair someone had given up on, holding a mug of recycled coffee he'd stopped drinking an hour ago because the children kept asking for sips and then making faces.

"The hum. It's flat. Half a tone." I was upside down under the environmental console at the time, which lent my diagnosis a certain theatrical quality I didn't love. "The drive's breathing wrong. Not failing—*compensating*. Something out here is pulling on it."

"We're still eleven days from the boundary."

"Space doesn't care about our schedule. Space has never once cared about anyone's schedule. That's practically its defining feature."

He didn't laugh. Eleven days out from dead space and Sebastian Delacroix had developed the specific silence of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want finished. I slid out from under the console, cracked my elbow on the way up—being nine meant my elbows were newly reissued and I hadn't learned their geometry yet—and looked at him until he said it.

"The shear readings are climbing. Ahead of us, not behind. Mara, they started climbing yesterday."

"And you were going to tell me when?"

"When I understood them."

"You're a beacon tender, not a physicist."

"I'm a man who spent eleven years listening to a signal nobody else could hear," he said mildly. "Understanding things late is my entire skill set."

*

The crew assembled in the mess because the mess was where we assembled, the way families assemble in kitchens regardless of whether food is involved. Calling them *the crew* still snagged something in my chest. Captain Okafor was eight years old and sat at the head of the table with her feet not touching the floor, which should have been comedy and instead was the most sobering thing I'd seen all week. Behind those eyes was a woman who'd flown convoys through pirate corridors. In front of those eyes was a juice box, because rationing didn't care about rank.

"Report," she said, and Junie Park stood up.

Junie. Our comms officer. Chronologically fifty-three, physically seven, and the smallest person aboard—the only one of us the reversal had taken all the way down. The rest of the crew had landed somewhere between eight and twelve; Junie had come out the other side of the shear as a first-grader, and she'd handled it with a professionalism that made my throat hurt. She wore her flight suit rolled six times at the cuffs. She kept a list of the adult words she was no longer allowed to say, taped inside her locker, and consulted it before every briefing.

She consulted it now. I watched her lips move over it.

"Comms status," she reported. "All bands normal except the loop. The loop is louder. It shouldn't be louder this far out, but it is, and the harmonics are—" she checked her list again, decided whatever she wanted wasn't on it, and said it anyway— "*weird.*"

Sebastian went very still. "Weird how?"

"Like it's not just playing anymore. Like it's *reaching*."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere behind the bulkhead, the drive hummed its flat, lying note.

That was when the lights went amber.

Not red—amber, the ship's way of saying *pay attention but don't panic*, and I have never in my life been less able to follow instructions. I felt it before I saw it, a pressure change in my inner ear, the same sensation as a dive beginning, except we weren't diving, we were hanging in open black eleven days from nowhere. The deck tilted two degrees. Coffee walked itself off Sebastian's abandoned mug and hung in the air for a full second before the gravity plating caught up, and in that second every child at that table—every gray-souled, small-bodied officer—went silent in the way only people who have nearly died in space go silent.

"Eddy," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Temporal eddy, off the port bow, and it's moving. Everyone strapped in, now. Now means now, people, this isn't a drill, this is the opposite of a drill—"

Okafor was already moving. Eight years old, feet hitting the deck twice per stride, barking assignments in a voice that hadn't changed since she was thirty. Tomas Reyes—no relation to Ilsa, though we'd all noticed and none of us said so—took the helm. He was ten now, had been a pilot for twenty-two years, and his hands knew the stick even if they barely wrapped around it.

The eddy hit us at 1400 hours, ship time, which I know because I was watching the clock when it stopped meaning anything.

*

Here is what a temporal eddy feels like from inside, in case you're collecting experiences the way I apparently collect them: it feels like déjà vu with mass. The first pass knocked the ship sideways and every instrument read *the same moment twice*. My own heartbeat showed up on the medical monitor three times, staggered, like an echo learning to be a drum. Sebastian grabbed the seat frame beside me and shouted something—I never learned what, because the words arrived before he said them, hanging in the air a half-second early, and by the time his mouth moved I'd already heard them and they were just *Mara, hold on, hold on, hold on*—

Then the second pass hit, and time broke properly.

I've thought about how to describe the next ninety seconds for a long time, and I've decided honesty is the only format available: I don't remember them. I remember fragments. The hull groaning in a chord. Okafor's voice counting down from a number that kept un-counting. The viewport showing stars that were in front of us also being behind us. And Junie.

Junie Park was standing—not strapped in, God help her, she'd been reaching for the nav relay when the first pass hit—and when the second pass rolled through the ship, I watched light move across her the way sunset moves across a field. Slow and total. Her hair lengthened. Her shoulders squared. Her flight suit, rolled six times at the cuffs, rolled itself back down as her arms grew to meet it, seam by seam, like the fabric remembered what it was for.

Seven years. I would learn later it was almost exactly seven. Ninety seconds of eddy, and the universe reached into our smallest crew member and gave her back a decade she hadn't earned yet, in the time it takes to boil water.

Then it stopped. The pressure lifted. The hum—my flat, lying hum—snapped back into true pitch so suddenly that I heard it, heard the correction, heard the whole ship exhale.

Junie was fourteen. She lay on the deck plates, breathing hard, staring at her own hands, which now had calluses she wouldn't earn for another five years.

*

Sickbay, such as it was, smelled like antiseptic and fear. I sat on the edge of the diagnostic bed while the scanner did its patient work over Junie, and Sebastian stood in the doorway wearing the expression of a man who has run out of protocol and is considering inventing some.

"Cellular age fourteen point one," the scanner announced, in its chipper little voice, because medical equipment has never once appreciated the gravity of anything. "Telomere profile consistent. No structural anomalies detected. Subject is, functionally, a healthy adolescent."

"She was seven this morning," Sebastian said.

"She was fifty-three last month," I said. "At this point the scanner and I have agreed to stop keeping score."

Junie flexed her fingers. She'd been silent since the eddy, and the silence had a texture I recognized, because I'd worn it myself in the first weeks—this careful, listening quality, a person taking inventory of their own body like luggage after a flight. When she finally spoke, her voice had dropped out of its child register into something rougher, newer, a voice mid-construction.

"It didn't hurt," she said. "That's the weird part. It felt like—" she searched. "Like putting on a coat you forgot you owned. Everything fit. Everything *remembered*." She looked up at us, and there were tears standing in her eyes, but her jaw was set like an officer's. "My hands knew things before I did. I reached for the relay and my arm already knew the reach. Is that—is that normal? Is any of this normal?"

"No," Sebastian and I said together, and despite everything, she laughed—a startled, cracking, fourteen-year-old laugh, and I saw her hear it herself, hear the newness of it, and laugh again at the laughing.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. So it can go backward. It went forward. Which means—"

"Which means it's not a wall," I said quietly. "It's a current."

I want to be precise about what that sentence cost me, sitting there in a body nine years old with a daughter I couldn't picture waiting somewhere in a timeline I couldn't reach. Because hope, real hope, is not a warm thing. Hope is a knife someone hands you handle-first and says *careful*. If the shear could give Junie back seven years—if reversal wasn't a one-way door—then maybe there was a configuration, a passage, a price, that gave the rest of us back ours. Maybe I could be forty again. Maybe I could remember my daughter's face instead of just the shape of loving her.

And maybe the same current that gave could take. Junie had gained seven years in ninety seconds. What had it cost her? What would it cost the next person? The universe doesn't do refunds; it does trades, and it sets the exchange rate arbitrarily, and it never tells you what you're trading until the transaction clears.

Hope with teeth. That's what I called it later, in the log I keep now. Hope with teeth, held carefully, fed sparingly.

"Get some rest," I told Junie, and ruffled her hair, and she let me, which neither of us would have permitted a week ago—we'd been colleagues too long for comfort with sentiment—but grief renegotiates everyone's boundaries eventually.

*

Sebastian found me in the engine room at 0300, because of course he did. The man sleeps four hours a night and spends the difference listening. He lowered himself onto the deck beside me—I was sitting against the drive housing, feeling its vibration through the deck, checking the pitch by ear like some kind of luddite priestess—and for a while neither of us said anything. That was one of the things about Sebastian. He understood that most of what matters gets said in the pauses.

"The hum's back to true," he said finally.

"For now. We're skirting the boundary layer. The eddies are going to get worse the closer we get to Relay Zero."

"You could turn around." He said it gently. "The window closes in nine days. If we pushed hard, some of the crew could make Kestrel-9. Ride out the anomaly at the station. Wait it out."

"Wait it out *how*? Aging randomly? Hoping the shear spits us back where we started?" I pulled my knees up—they actually tucked under my chin now, which was one of the few perks. "No. The answer's at the source, Sebastian. Ilsa built that relay for a reason. Whatever she figured out about time, it's sitting at the end of her signal, and I am not spending the rest of this life—or whatever size life I've got left—wondering."

He nodded slowly. Then, in the pause, in the place where he kept his real questions: "And if the answer costs more than the question?"

I thought about Junie's new calluses. About Okafor's feet not touching the floor. About a face I loved and could not draw.

"Then I'll pay it," I said. "But I'll pay it going forward, not standing still. There's a difference between a price and a waste."

Sebastian looked at me for a long moment, and something passed between us that I didn't have a word for then and still don't, entirely—recognition, maybe, between two people who had both been left behind by the universe in different ways and had both decided, separately, to leave the light on anyway.

"Bridge wants you," he said, standing. "Okafor's orders. She says, quote, 'if the engineer is going to sit up all night talking to the drive, she can do it where I can see her.'"

"That woman was born to command."

"She was born twice, technically."

"*Sebastian.*"

"What? Too soon?" But he was smiling, and I was smiling, and for one suspended moment in the humming dark of the engine room, the fear loosened its grip enough to let a joke through.

*

The alarm woke us at 0512.

Not the amber caution this time. Red, full-throated, the honest kind, and I was moving before consciousness fully arrived, bare feet slapping cold deck, mind running the checklist ahead of my body the way it always does. Eddy signature, portside, larger. Much larger. On the main display it bloomed against the starfield like a slow-motion flower made of distortion, and at its center, faint but unmistakable, riding every band at once—

The loop. Ilsa's loop. Louder than it had any right to be, threading through the storm like a hand extended into dark water.

"Course correction," Okafor said from the captain's chair, calm as bedrock, thirteen years old in body and thirty in spine. "Take us along the eddy's leading edge. Helm, match the engineer's numbers."

Tomas flew. I fed him headings. Sebastian stood at my shoulder translating the loop's harmonics into plain language, and Junie—fourteen now, steady-handed, her coat fitting her—worked comms with the fluency of the fifty-three-year-old she still was underneath, pulling Ilsa's signal clean out of the noise.

And there, at the bottom of the signal, beneath the messages, beneath the decades of devotion, the decoder flagged something new. Something the eddy's proximity had shaken loose.

Coordinates.

Sebastian stared at them for a long time. Long enough that I asked him twice what he was seeing. When he finally answered, his voice had gone strange and quiet, the voice of a man reading his own name in a stranger's handwriting.

"Mara," he said. "These coordinates. I know them."

"How could you possibly—"

"They're where I was born."

Behind us, at the edge of the display, the storm turned, and began, very slowly, to follow.
