## 14. Relay Zero

The relay looked like a tooth.

That was my first thought, anyway, when Vantablack's forward array finally resolved it out of the dark—a single pale structure jutting up out of nothing, cracked and yellowed at the root, still stubbornly attached to a jaw of dead rock that had probably been orbiting this star before humanity learned to write things down. Half its solar skin had fused into slag. The other half was furred with frost. And from somewhere inside that ruined body, patient as a heartbeat, came the signal we'd been chasing for nineteen days.

*Still transmitting,* said the station AI in my ear, riding the suit channel like a ghost I'd brought along for company. I'd copied her core onto a chip before we left Kestrel-9. It felt less like abandonment if I took her with me. "I have run the structural analysis four times. The answer does not improve."

"And?"

"It should not be operational. By any model I possess, it should have gone silent forty years ago."

"Welcome to the club," I said. "We meet Tuesdays."

Mara floated up beside me at the viewport, small hands hooked in the handrail, her borrowed flight suit rolled three times at the cuffs and still swallowing her. Nine years old and she stood like someone who'd spent decades arguing with engines and winning most of them. She studied the relay the way you study a photograph of a house you grew up in—hunger and dread sharing the same face.

"That's it," she said. "That's the source."

"You sound sure."

"I've been listening to that signal since I was old." Her voice did the thing it always did when she talked about *before*—went flat and careful, a woman walking on ice she couldn't see the thickness of. "It's been under everything. Every band, every year of my career. We all thought it was noise. A fault in the deep-array filters." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "It wasn't noise. It was a voice, and we were all too deaf to hear it."

Behind us, the bridge crew of Vantablack—captains and engineers and navigators wearing the bodies of children—had gone quiet in the way only children can go quiet, which is to say completely, which is to say louder than any alarm. Captain Okafor, who was eight years old and had commanded three ships before her ninth birthday caught up with her, drifted down from the command chair and put her small hand briefly on Mara's shoulder. Nobody said anything. There wasn't anything to say that the signal hadn't already been saying for forty years.

"All right," I said, because somebody had to, and apparently it was going to be me, the man whose entire prior job description had been *talk to no one, fix the same machine, repeat*. "EVA party is me, Mara, and Okonkwo. Everyone else stays aboard. If the aperture starts closing early, you fly. No arguments, no heroics. We are not losing anyone else to this place."

Okafor looked at me with eyes that had signed off on casualty lists. "You've gotten bossy, Delacroix."

"I've been alone for eleven years. Bossy is the only register I have left."

She almost smiled. On her child's face it looked strange and wonderful, like frost forming in the shape of a flower.

* * *

The relay's docking collar accepted our magnetic boots with a groan I felt through my whole skeleton, a sound like an old man being asked to stand up one more time. Inside the airlock, the atmosphere read as breathable—thin, cold, stale with forty years of recycled nothing, but breathable. Somebody had maintained the seals. Somebody had kept the air cycling long after there was anyone left to breathe it.

"Ilsa Reyes," I said to the dark, just to hear how the name sounded in the place she'd made it true.

The interior corridor was narrow, lit by emergency strips running on some trickle charge off the surviving solar skin. Frost feathered the walls in patterns like ferns, like lungs, like the branching of every river system on every world we'd ever named. My breath fogged and hung. Everything was quiet except the signal, which was no longer a signal here—it was ambient, it was the room itself, a low rhythmic murmur in the deck plates, in my teeth, in the fluid of my inner ear. I'd spent eleven years calibrating a beacon that carried this voice without my knowing. Now I was standing inside the throat that produced it.

Mara's hand found mine. Small, cold, gripping hard. She didn't say anything and neither did I. Some doors you walk through holding hands with whoever's nearest, and the reasons why don't matter until later, if ever.

The console room was at the heart of the relay, behind a hatch that had been welded shut—from the inside.

"She sealed herself in," Mara whispered.

"No," I said, though I could see the welds, neat and deliberate, a technician's perfect beads of fused metal laid down by someone who wanted very much to make sure the door stayed closed. "Look at the pattern. That's not panic. That's engineering." I ran my glove along the seam. "She cut the room off from the rest of the station. Environmental, comms, everything isolated. She turned this room into—" I stopped, because the word arrived all at once and it was worse than any of the others. "Into a transmitter housing. She made herself part of the circuit."

The hatch, welded or not, had a maintenance override—an old-fashioned manual crank, because technicians like Ilsa Reyes never fully trusted anything they hadn't touched themselves. It took both of us working it, Mara bracing her small frame against the wall for leverage while I hauled, and the wheel turned in increments, shrieking rust, and then the hatch gave with a sigh of equalizing pressure and swung inward onto forty years of held breath.

* * *

She was at her console.

I want to tell you I was prepared for that. I want to tell you that nineteen days of listening to her voice, of decoding her logs, of learning her jokes and her frustrations and the particular way she said *good morning* to a man who could never answer—all of it prepared me. It didn't. Nothing prepares you for the specific gravity of a human being who has finished being one.

She sat upright in the operator's chair, strapped in with the same neat competence that showed in her welds, and the years had taken everything soft and left something light and precise behind, like a diagram of a woman rather than a woman. Her hands rested on the console. One of them lay over a physical switch—an actual mechanical toggle, ancient even by the standards of this ancient place—and her finger was still touching it. Not pressing it. Touching it. Resting there, at the last moment, on the edge of a decision.

"Oh," said Mara, very quietly, beside me. It wasn't a word so much as a wound.

The console was alive. Screens glowed their patient amber. Diagnostics scrolled in a loop. And in the center of the main display, a message buffer blinked, unsent, waiting for a transmission window that had never come—or that she had chosen, at the end, not to use.

I crossed the room the way you cross a church. My boots sounded obscene on the deck. Up close, I could see details the dimness had spared me: the patch on her suit shoulder, hand-stitched, reading *KESTREL* in thread that had once been gold; a photograph taped to the console bezel, frost-eaten, showing two women squinting into a sun that wasn't this sun, one of them laughing with her head thrown back; a mug, fossilized to its coaster, with a faded cartoon robot on it and the words WORLD'S OKAYEST TECHNICIAN.

Eleven years I'd tended her beacon. Eleven years I'd wound her clock without knowing whose hands had built it, or why, or for whom. And here she was, the answer to a question I hadn't known I was asking, sitting in the dark at the edge of dead space, waiting with the patience of the dead for someone to come read her mail.

"The message," Mara said. Her voice had gone thin. "Sebastian. The buffer. Look at the addressee field."

I looked.

**TO: WHOEVER WINDS MY CLOCK**

The room tilted. Or I did. I put a hand on the console—on the cold metal beside her cold hand—and steadied myself against eleven years of my own life rearranging itself around a sentence.

Because there was only one person who could be. Only one keeper, one successor, one pair of hands she'd never met, commissioned by orders lost in a purge, sent out to tend a machine she'd built as a love letter and left running as a lighthouse. I'd thought my assignment was bureaucratic accident. Paperwork that survived a fire by falling between the flames. But she'd written those orders. She must have—filed them eleven years before my arrival, addressed to a name she couldn't know, to a stranger she'd hired across time the only way left to her: by making sure the paperwork outlived the fire.

She'd known someone would come. She'd trusted the universe to send a person who would wind the clock. And the universe, for once, for once in its long indifferent history, had complied.

Me.

"Play it," Mara said.

My hand hovered over the control. Behind me, faintly, through the open hatch, I heard the others on the ship channel holding their breath—children with old souls, waiting in the dark for a dead woman to speak.

I pressed play.

* * *

Her voice filled the little room, and it was different from the loop—the loop was polished, performed, a message shaped for repetition. This was raw. Recorded in one take, in this chair, with whatever time she had left.

*"If you're hearing this, then the door worked, and you're the one I hoped for. Don't ask how I knew someone would come. I didn't. I gambled. It's what she would have done, and I've had forty years to learn her habits better than my own.*

*"Her name was Adaeze. I'm saying it out loud one more time because the beacon says it every six hours but I'm the only one left who says it in this voice. She was a physicist. Temporal mechanics, back when that meant chalkboards and optimism instead of whatever it means now. They were running an experiment—small, contained, they promised—and she stepped into the field to pull someone out, and the field took her instead. Not killed her. That would have been simple. It scattered her. Across time. Across the shear. Somewhere out past the boundary of mapped space, in the place your charts call dead, she is still arriving, still stepping into that field, still reaching for that person, forever, in every direction at once.*

*"So I built the beacon. You've been keeping it lit, so you know: it doesn't broadcast outward, not really. It broadcasts *inward*. Into the shear. Because time isn't a wall out there—it's a hallway, and sound carries down hallways. Every message I record goes into the loop, and the loop goes into the anomaly, and maybe, someday, in some order of events where cause and effect got scrambled beyond recognition, she hears my voice and turns around.*

*"Forty years. I've recorded ten thousand messages. I've told her about the frost patterns. About the mug. About the year the solar skin slagged and I nearly froze and laughed the whole time because I thought, wouldn't it be funny, after everything, to die of something ordinary. I've apologized. I've argued with her. I've sung, God help us both.*

*"And now my heart has informed me, in the way hearts do, that the shift is ending. So here's the last one, and it's not for her. It's for you.*

*"I need you to know that the signal you're standing inside of is not a warning and not a distress call and not science. It's a love letter. It was always a love letter. And I am leaving it running, and I am sorry, because I know—I know—what it costs. The shear feeds on it. The loop holds the wound open. Anyone caught in that footprint gets unmade, the way Vantablack's people were unmade, the way she was unmade. Every day it runs is a day someone else might lose everything to keep my hope alive.*

*"But I couldn't do it. I couldn't press that switch. Forty years, and my finger won't move. It's the one repair I can't make.*

*"So I'm leaving it to you, whoever winds my clock. Shut it down and save them, and I will understand, and I will even forgive you, because that's the arithmetic a decent person does. Or let it run, and keep calling her name into the hallway, and I will understand that too, because that's the arithmetic love does, and I stopped pretending the two were the same a long time ago.*

*"Either way: thank you for coming. Thank you for the eleven years you'll give this place before you even know it's mine. Thank you for winding the clock.*

*"Whoever you are—you were always the answer. I just needed someone to ask the question after I was gone.*

*"Shift's yours."*

Silence. The real kind, total, the kind that has weight and temperature.

Then, softly, through the ship channel, a child's voice—Okonkwo, the youngest, seven years old and formerly a propulsion specialist—said, "Oh no," in the small crushed tone of someone who has just understood something enormous, and Mara began to cry, silently, the way adults cry, which is to say like children who have practiced.

I stood in the frozen room with my hand resting on the console next to hers, and I did the only thing that seemed right. I reached over and closed her fingers—the ones that had hovered forty years over the switch she couldn't press—gently, formally, the way you close a book you've finished reading and intend to keep.

"Shift's mine," I said.

And that was when the console chimed. An alert, amber and urgent, scrolling up beneath her unsent message:

**ANOMALY APERTURE STABILITY: FAILING. ESTIMATED CLOSURE: 31 HOURS.**
