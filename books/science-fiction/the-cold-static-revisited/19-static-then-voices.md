## 19. Static, Then Voices

The dark, it turns out, is not empty. The dark is full of things that used to be voices.

I know this because I am currently floating in the middle of it, strapped into a crash couch that smells like burnt insulation and someone's abandoned lunch, listening to the sound of a dead woman's love letter come back to us across four hundred thousand kilometers of nothing.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

The collapse hit Vantablack like a wave hitting a sandcastle built by an optimist.

One moment we were riding the shockwave out of the folding anomaly, and the next we were tumbling through a silence so complete it had texture. Every external array went dark. Long-range comms: gone. Navigation: gone, or worse than gone—giving us numbers with the serene confidence of a liar. The main drive coughed twice and decided, on reflection, not to participate in whatever came next.

We drifted. That was the word for it. Drifting implies a certain leisure, a hammock quality. What we were doing was falling without a floor, at speed, in the wrong direction, with a hull that groaned like an old man getting out of a chair.

And the crew—the crew, my crew, my impossible crew of former officers wearing their own childhoods like borrowed coats—they did not panic.

I want to be clear about what that means, because I don't think I understood it myself until that moment. These were people who eleven days ago couldn't reach the overhead consoles without standing on ammunition crates. Their hands were too small for the tools they'd spent twenty years mastering. Captain Okafor—eight years old, gap-toothed, ferocious—had spent the transit relearning her own ship the way you'd relearn a language from a dream: half-remembered, mostly instinct, occasionally weeping with frustration in a corridor when her fingers wouldn't do what her mind demanded.

And now, in the dark, she climbed into the command chair—which she could barely see over—and said, in a voice pitched high as a whistle, "Damage report. All stations. Now, please."

And they gave it to her. Station by station. Calm, clipped, professional. A bridge full of children reciting casualty figures about their own ship in the flat tones of people who have done this before, in other bodies, other years, and who have decided—decided is the right word, chosen, elected—that competence does not live in the hands. It lives somewhere deeper, somewhere time can't shear away.

I stood at the engineering console—I still fit mine, more or less, though I'd lost thirty years and gained a view of the room from knee height—and I felt something crack open in my chest that wasn't grief for once.

It might have been pride. It might have been terror. At this point in my life the two are difficult to distinguish, and honestly I've stopped trying.

* * *

The damage list was long but survivable, which is the best kind of damage list. Power was down to emergency cells. Life support was limping along on recycled air that tasted increasingly of panic and protein paste. We had no drive, no comms, no fix on our position, and no idea whether the anomaly's collapse had thrown us into normal space or some vestibule between spaces where physics waits outside with its coat on.

"We're blind," said Delacroix—young Delacroix, the navigator, twelve years old and already talking like a man three divorces deep. "Completely blind. No beacons, no traffic, no background hum. Nothing."

"Nothing," repeated Okafor. "As in, space has gone quiet."

"As in, space has never been this quiet in the history of quiet."

There was a pause on the bridge. You could hear the environmental fans working, and beneath them, if you listened, the faint tick of the hull cooling, contracting, settling into itself like a dog circling before lying down.

It was then that I heard it.

At first I thought it was tinnitus. I get it sometimes—a thin whine in the left ear, souvenir of a compressor accident aboard a ship whose name I've since forgotten, back when I was old and whole and my daughter was still a face and not a wound. But this whine had structure. It pulsed. It kept time.

"Does anyone else—" I started, and stopped, because half the bridge had already turned toward me.

"It's on every band," whispered Priya Chandrasekaran, our comms officer, currently nine years old and drowning in a headset sized for an adult skull. She'd patched the receiver audio straight to the cabin speakers because the visual displays were dead, and so the whole bridge heard it together, the way you'd hear rain.

Static. Pure, cold, featureless static—the same static that fills dead space, the static we'd been flying toward for nineteen days, the static that had swallowed every signal but one.

Except now, threaded through it, faint as a pulse under skin, there was a voice.

* * *

You have to understand what that voice was to me before you understand what happened next.

Eleven days ago I didn't know Ilsa Reyes existed. Ten days ago she was a name on a corrupted file, a technician's signature on an order nobody remembered issuing. Nine days ago she was a voice in a loop—a personal log, repeating, addressed to a woman named Adaeze who had stepped into an early temporal experiment in 2143 and never stepped out, at least not into any year Ilsa could follow her to.

Ilsa Reyes had spent forty-one years alone at Relay Zero, at the edge of dead space, recording messages to a woman who could not answer. And she had aimed them at a beacon she built herself—a beacon commissioned under a falsified order, staffed by a man hired through channels so obscure that even he didn't know why he was there—and that beacon had carried her voice out into the dark for decades, patient as geology, faithful as a lighthouse keeper's lamp.

We found her body at the console. Sebastian found the final message, unsent, addressed to *whoever winds my clock.* He read it aloud to us in the relay's dying light, and I watched a grown man cry in a way I hadn't seen since I was old enough to know what crying was for.

Then he made his choice. Not shutdown, not preservation—something third. He spliced her archive into the relay's permanent core, soldered joint by soldered joint while the jump window shrank around him like a closing fist, and he left her voice broadcasting on the open band. Not a private letter anymore. A monument. A lighthouse. A thing anyone in the dark could find.

We rode the shockwave out. Systems failed. We flew blind on trust, which is the only instrument that never breaks.

And now, in the static, in the silence after the end of everything—

Her voice.

* * *

"—day two hundred and nine. Adaeze, you would laugh at me. I burned the rice again. Forty years of field rations and I still cannot make rice, which I choose to believe proves I was only ever staying alive for you, because a person who intends to live would have learned rice by now—"

Priya had the gain up as far as the dead boards allowed. The voice filled the bridge, small and warm and slightly degraded, the way all voices are when they've traveled a very long way to reach you.

Nobody moved. Okafor had both small hands flat on the armrests of her chair. Delacroix had stopped pretending to work the nav board. Even the youngest of us—Tomas, six years old, formerly our chief medical officer, currently asleep in a corner because six-year-olds sleep when their bodies vote—stirred and half woke, and did not cry, and listened.

"—and today I fixed the coolant loop on the secondary array, which you will remember I said could not be fixed, and you will also remember that you told me to stop saying things cannot be fixed, and I am telling you now, wherever you are, whenever you are: you were right. Things can be fixed. I'm sorry it took me thirty years to agree with you. I'll tell you again tomorrow, in case tomorrow exists—"

"That's her," I said. My voice came out strange. Younger. Everything about me was younger; my voice was just catching up. "That's Ilsa."

"I know," said Sebastian.

He was standing beside me at the engineering station, and he looked terrible, in the way men look terrible when they have done the most important work of their lives and then immediately been thrown into a wall by the universe. There was a burn on his forearm from the last splice, a bad one, wrapped in gauze that Tomas had applied with six-year-old solemnity and, apparently, six-year-old technique, because it was coming loose.

"You did this," I said. "You put her on the open band."

He nodded. He didn't say anything. Men like Sebastian don't narrate their own heroism; they'd rather talk about solder joints. I did it for him, later, in the log, in the record, in the story we would eventually tell at dinner tables in a station none of us had ever expected to call home. But in that moment neither of us spoke, because the voice was still going, and it felt like eavesdropping even though she had, in the end, meant it for everyone.

"—day two hundred and ten. Still here. Still calling. If you're hearing this, Adaeze, I want you to know the stars from here look exactly the way you described them, that night on the observation deck, before the project, before everything. You said they looked like a city seen from far enough away that you can't tell which windows are lit by love and which by habit. I've thought about that every day. I've decided it doesn't matter. Lit is lit—"

Okafor made a sound. Small. I looked over and she had her face turned away, toward the dead viewport, toward the black, and her shoulders were shaking in a way that eight-year-old bodies shake when they are carrying fifty-year-old griefs.

"Captain," I said gently.

"Don't." Her voice cracked down the middle, half child, half commander. "Don't 'captain' me right now, Voss. Just—give me a second. Give me a second."

We gave her a second. We gave her several. The voice kept going, patient, looping, and the static held it like cupped hands.

* * *

Here is the thing about catharsis that nobody warns you about: it doesn't feel like relief. It feels like being seen.

For eleven days I had been a nine-year-old woman with a forty-year-old memory, mourning a daughter whose face I could not retrieve. I had described her to Sebastian once, in the galley, at ship's midnight: *she had my eyes, everyone said so, but I can't—I know she laughed with her whole body, I know she bit her nails, I know she hated socks—but her face, Mara, I can't find her face.* And Sebastian had listened the way he listens, completely, without offering solutions, which is the rarest form of kindness in any century.

Now Ilsa Reyes's voice was filling the ship, and she was talking about loss—not my loss, hers, older, longer, worn smooth by decades of repetition—and somehow her loss was holding mine. Like a key that fits a lock it was never cut for.

Because that's what the beacon was, in the end. Not a warning. Not a signal. A hand extended across time, saying: *someone else has lost someone, and they went on, and the going-on is the message.*

"—day two hundred and eleven. I dreamed about you last night. In the dream you were arguing with me about the thermostat, which is how I knew it was really you, because no one else on Earth or off it ever cared that much about seventy degrees. I woke up and set the relay habitat to seventy degrees. It's wasteful. I don't care. Some debts you pay in kilowatts—"

Delacroix laughed. Actually laughed, wet-eyed, twelve years old and helpless. "She argued about thermostats," he said. "Forty years alone and she fought about the thermostat. That's—" He shook his head. "That's the truest thing I've ever heard."

"It is," I said. "Love isn't the big declarations. Love is setting the thermostat for someone who isn't there anymore. Love is burning the rice. Love is telling them they were right, thirty years late, and meaning it."

Sebastian looked at me then, and I saw him understand that I wasn't just talking about Ilsa and Adaeze. I was talking about him, winding the clock at Kestrel-9 for eleven years, keeping a stranger's faith without knowing whose faith it was. I was talking about all of us—the abandoned, the forgotten, the reversed, the ones the bureaucracy erased from its own files. We had all been somebody's unfinished sentence. And here, in the dark, drifting, powerless, we had become each other's endings.

The private letter was public now. Anyone drifting in that blackness, any ship, any soul, any future crew lost the way we'd been lost—they would hear her. They would hear a woman promise to tell a dead woman she was right, again, tomorrow, in case tomorrow existed.

Lit is lit.

* * *

"How long has it been broadcasting?" Okafor asked finally, voice reassembled, commander again, though her feet didn't reach the floor.

"Since the splice," Sebastian said. "Open band, all frequencies, permanent core. It'll outlast the relay's hull. It'll outlast—" He paused. "It'll outlast us, probably. That was the point."

"The point," repeated Priya, testing the word.

"That grief shouldn't have to be private to be honored," Sebastian said. "That a love letter nobody receives is still a love letter. That someone, someday, drifting and dark, exactly like us, might need to hear a human voice insisting that things can be fixed." He shrugged, embarrassed, the way he always got when caught doing something decent. "Also the core needed a checksum anchor and her logs were the cleanest data on the relay. Engineering rationale available on request."

"Terrible liar," I said.

"Noted," said Okafor. "Log it."

And then—it was Priya, I want it on record that it was Priya, nine years old, swimming in her headset—Priya sat up very straight and said, "Wait. Wait wait wait. The signal source. I'm reading a bearing."

Every head turned.

"We're blind," said Delacroix. "You said we're blind."

"I said the displays are dead. The receiver works. It's been working. That's how we're hearing her." Priya's small hands moved across the board with sudden, startling authority, the muscle memory of a career flooding back into a child's fingers like water finding an old channel. "The bearing on the carrier. It's not Relay Zero. Relay Zero collapsed with the anomaly—we watched it fold. This signal is coming from somewhere else. Somewhere closer. Somewhere—"

She looked up at us. She looked, specifically, at Sebastian.

"It's coming from Kestrel-9," she said. "It's your beacon. It's answering."

The bridge went silent except for the voice, still looping, still patient, Ilsa promising tomorrow to a woman forty years gone.

"Answering," Sebastian said slowly. "Beacons don't answer. They broadcast. They don't—" He stopped. I watched him arrive at the thought the way you watch someone walk to the edge of a high place. "Unless someone taught it to."

"Did you?" I asked.

"No." His voice was very quiet. "I set it to autonomous mode. I said goodbye to it. I told it—" He laughed, a short, disbelieving sound. "I talked to it like a spouse. I told it to keep the light on until I got back. I didn't program a response. I didn't program anything. It shouldn't be transmitting on the open band at all. It especially shouldn't be transmitting *her*."

"Then who," said Okafor, "or what, is?"

On the speakers, warm and small and traveling impossibly far to reach us, Ilsa Reyes promised, once again, to tell Adaeze she was right tomorrow—in case tomorrow existed.

And somewhere behind us, in the direction of a station one man had called home for eleven years without knowing why, something that knew his name was winding the clock.

* * *

We found the bearing held steady. Whatever was transmitting, it wasn't drifting. It was waiting.

Okafor ordered power conserved, course estimated by dead reckoning and prayer, and a watch rotation established—children standing shifts their adult selves had once designed, keeping vigil over a signal that should not exist, bound for a station that had learned, somehow, to speak.

Before I took my bunk, I stood a moment at the viewport, looking back the way we'd come. Dead space lay behind us like a closed eye. Ahead, nothing visible at all. And threading through both, faint and constant, her voice—no longer a letter, no longer a monument, but something new. A conversation, begun forty years ago by a woman who burned the rice, joined eleven days ago by a man who winds clocks, and answered now by—who?

By what?

Tomorrow, I thought. In case tomorrow exists.

I went to bed believing it did.
