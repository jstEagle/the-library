## 20. Restoration

The doctors at Meridian Station had a word for what happened to the crew of the *Vantablack*, and the word was "heterochronous stabilization," which is the kind of phrase that sounds like it was invented by someone who wanted to be paid by the syllable. What it meant, in practice, was that when the anomaly folded and time snapped back to something resembling consensus, it did not snap back evenly. It snapped back like a dropped plate—mostly whole, some pieces missing, a few shards embedded where you didn't expect them.

I learned this in the medical bay of Meridian Station, three weeks after we rode the shockwave out of dead space with our hair on fire and our instruments screaming, watching a pediatrician who was not actually a pediatrician explain to a woman who was chronologically forty-one that her body had settled at thirty.

"Settled," Mara repeated. "Like sediment."

"Like a river finding its level," the doctor said, with the relentless cheerfulness of a man who delivered bad news in metaphors because metaphors couldn't sue him.

"And the gray?"

"Some of it stayed." The doctor shrugged. "We don't fully understand the mechanism. The reversal wasn't a rewind—it was more like... a renegotiation. Your cells reached an agreement with themselves about how old they wanted to be, and thirty won. Mostly."

Mara looked at me across the bay. She had been nine years old for the better part of two months, and then briefly forty-one for one impossible hour in the collapsing dark, and now she was thirty, and I will tell you honestly: I did not know which version of her I was talking to anymore, or whether that question even made sense. Time had stopped being a line for all of us somewhere around Relay Zero. It was more of a braided rope now, fraying at the ends.

"The gray stays," she said finally. "Good."

"You *want*—"

"I earned those, Sebastian." She touched the streak above her left ear—the one she'd had at forty, the one that vanished when she was nine, the one that came back like a signature. "Eleven years of engineering a freighter held together with prayer and spite. I'm keeping the receipts."

* * *

The accounting took longer than the rescue.

That's the thing nobody tells you about miracles: they come with paperwork. Meridian Station processed us through quarantine, then debriefing, then a kind of bureaucratic purgatory they called "temporal reintegration counseling," which mostly consisted of well-meaning administrators asking questions like "How do you feel about your current age?" as if any of us had been consulted.

The crew stabilized unevenly. That was the phrase everyone used, *unevenly*, said gently, said sadly, said the way you say a diagnosis.

Captain Okafor—who had spent our transit teaching himself to fly his own ship from the body of an eight-year-old, whose hands had remembered the controls before his arms were long enough to reach them—snapped back to thirty-five almost completely. He walked out of the medbay on day four, tall and gray-templed and weeping quietly in the corridor, and when I asked him if he was all right he said, "I remember being eight. I remember both. Do you know what that's like?" And I said no, and he said, "It's like having two childhoods and only getting to keep one."

Ensign Bright—Juno, youngest of them, the one who'd aged forward seven years in seconds during the eddy at the edge—stabilized at nineteen. Chronologically she should have been twenty-six. She'd lost seven years to the storm, permanently, and gained back nothing, and she carried it the way Mara carried the gray: deliberately, defiantly, with a new tattoo on her forearm that read SEVEN in block letters, so no one could ever accuse her of hiding it.

Chief Okonkwo didn't recover at all.

He stabilized at eleven. The doctors tried everything—targeted chrono-regenerative therapy, cellular recalibration, one experimental protocol that required him to spend six hours a day inside a machine that hummed at frequencies that made my teeth ache. Nothing took. His cells had made their agreement, and eleven was the number they'd signed.

He took it better than any of us expected, which broke my heart worse than grief would have. He organized the other kids—former officers, former adults—in the station's rec hall into a kind of school, except the curriculum was things like *how to be taken seriously in a negotiation* and *which of your opinions are worth having twice*. He called it Second Childhood Seminars. He charged admission in dessert rations.

"They think I need pity," he told me once, over contraband pudding cups. He was small and serious and had the eyes of a man who'd done thirty years of shift work. "I don't need pity. I need people to stop flinching when I talk. You don't flinch."

"No," I said. "I've spent eleven years talking to a station AI. My standards for conversational partners are extremely forgiving."

He laughed—a kid's laugh coming out of an old man's sense of humor—and knocked his pudding cup against mine. "You're family now, Delacroix. Sorry. No refunds."

* * *

Mara's daughter was the hard part.

Her name was Ade. She was twenty-two now, alive, healthy, living on Ceres with a partner and a degree in xenolinguistics and absolutely no memory of the mother who had jumped with the *Vantablack* eleven years ago and never come home—or rather, who had come home, eventually, in the wrong body, from a ship that officially never existed.

"She was four when I lost her," Mara told me, the night before the meeting. We were sitting in the observation blister of Meridian Station, watching cargo haulers crawl between the orbital docks like patient beetles. "Four. And then I was nine, and I couldn't remember her face, and that was the worst part—not losing her, losing the *face*. And then during the collapse I got the hour, and I remembered everything, every freckle, and I thought—that's it, that's the trade, the universe gave me one hour of remembering in exchange for thirty years of forgetting."

"And now?"

"Now I'm thirty and she's twenty-two and there are eight years between us instead of eighteen, and she doesn't know me, and I—" Mara's voice did something complicated. "I watched the message she sent. Through the intermediary. She said, 'I'm sorry, I don't know how to have a mother.' Not *I don't want one*. *I don't know how.*"

"That's not a no."

"It's not a yes either." She leaned her head against the glass. "Sebastian, I spent two months as a child learning how to be small again. I can learn how to be a stranger. Strangers become something else, given time. That's the whole thesis of my life lately."

* * *

They gave me a medal.

I want to be clear about how absurd this was. Eleven years at Kestrel-9, forgotten by whatever agency had posted me, unwinding a dead man's clock in the dark, and the moment I came back towing a ghost story and a freighter full of reversed officers, suddenly there were committees and commendations and a man from the Temporal Integrity Bureau who kept saying "extraordinary service to the timeline" while looking at me like I was a form he needed signed in triplicate.

The medal was small and silver and shaped like nothing in particular. They pinned it on me in a ceremony that lasted eleven minutes—I counted—and afterward a junior administrator asked me where I'd like my next posting, as though the last eleven years had been a career move.

"I'd like to go back," I said.

"To Kestrel-9?" He checked his tablet. "That relay's scheduled for decommission review. Honestly, Mr. Delacroix, without your testimony it would already be scrap."

"Then un-schedule it."

"Sir, the beacon's signal source has been destroyed. The anomaly is collapsed. There's nothing left to relay."

And here is where I did something I'm not proud of, or maybe I'm very proud of it, history can decide. I smiled at him the way you smile at someone who has just told you a beloved grandmother's house is structurally unsound, and I said:

"With respect, you're wrong. Check the open bands."

Because that was the thing. That was the thing we'd heard in the dark, drifting, systems failing, after the collapse—the beacon answering across the void. Ilsa Reyes's logs, spliced into Kestrel-9's permanent core, broadcasting on the open band. A private love letter turned public monument. Every ship in the corridor had started picking it up within days. Some of them slowed down to listen. One long-haul captain filed a report describing it as "the most beautiful thing I've ever heard a machine say."

The bureau hadn't decommissioned it yet because they couldn't figure out how to classify it. It wasn't navigational data. It wasn't a distress call. It was a dead woman's voice, repeating, patient as tide, telling whoever was listening about the person she loved and lost and refused to stop calling.

"There's nothing left to relay," I repeated to the administrator. "Except the most important transmission in the sector. But sure. Scrap it. I'll wait."

* * *

The vote happened in the *Vantablack*'s old mess hall, which Meridian had let us keep as a kind of temporary common room, on account of nobody knowing what else to do with eleven temporal refugees and their pet lighthouse keeper.

Okafor chaired it, standing on a chair so he could see over the table—no, wait, that was before his stabilization; by the time of the vote he was tall again and didn't need the chair, but he used it anyway, out of solidarity, and I have never loved a man faster.

The question was simple: what now? Meridian had offered the crew reintegration packages, new postings, fresh starts. Clean breaks. The bureau, in its infinite wisdom, assumed everyone would want to scatter back into the lives they'd been pulled out of.

Juno went first. "I lost seven years. I'm not losing these people too."

Okonkwo, age eleven, voice of a foreman: "I've been a child for months and an adult for decades. The only place I've been both is on this ship. Motion to stay together."

Chen, the navigator, who'd recovered to her true age of twenty-nine but still sometimes reached for controls that weren't there: "Seconded. Also I miss the ship. Is that weird? I miss the ship."

And then Okafor said the thing that settled it, the thing I keep turning over like a stone in my pocket:

"We were a crew because the fleet assigned us. Then we were children together, which is a bond no assignment creates. Now we get to choose. I say we choose each other. And I say we choose the relay."

"The relay," Chen said. "Kestrel-9. The cold one. The lonely one."

"The one with the voice," Juno corrected. "The one somebody built out of love and never got an answer until we showed up." She looked at me. "No offense, Delacroix, but you're the answer. You're what she was calling for. Eleven years alone winding a dead woman's clock—we're not leaving you there, and we're not letting them turn it off."

Mara spoke last. She'd been quiet through the whole vote, arms crossed, gray streak catching the light.

"I could take the package," she said. "Reintegration, therapy, a posting near Ceres. Maybe Ade would learn how to have a mother. Maybe not. It's a coin flip either way." She unfolded her arms. "But I've done the math on clean breaks. I've had eleven years of them. Here's what I know: you don't heal by going back to who you were. You heal by building something with who you are now. And who I am now is thirty, half-gray, chronologically forty-one, and deeply attached to a ship full of people who understand what it costs to lose time." She raised her hand. "All in favor."

Every hand went up. Including mine. Especially mine.

* * *

The petition took four months. The bureau fought it for three of them, on grounds ranging from "operational redundancy" to "emotional attachment is not a staffing criterion"—to which Okafor replied, in writing, "Emotional attachment is the *only* staffing criterion that has ever worked, see also: every successful mission in history," and I like to think some clerk framed it.

In the end it was the transmissions that won. The bureau's own monitoring stations reported a measurable uptick in traffic compliance along the corridor—ships actually slowing near the relay, actually listening, one tramp freighter reporting that its insomniac engineer had started timing her shifts to Ilsa's log cycle. A signal that made people gentler, the final memo concluded, defied efficient classification as non-operational.

So they recommissioned it. Our terms: skeleton staff of five, autonomous maintenance budget, and one line added to the official registry that I wrote myself and defended against three rounds of edits:

*Kestrel-9 broadcasts the archived personal logs of Technician Ilsa Reyes (deceased), maintained at the request of her found crew, in perpetuity.*

"In perpetuity" survived. I checked four times.

* * *

We came back to the station on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were calibration days and I am a creature of habit even when the habit is being rebuilt around me.

Everything was where I'd left it. The coffee ring on the console. The spare fuses sorted by size in the drawer that hummed. The AI—old Kestrel, faithful, pedantic—understood me the moment I keyed the comm: "Welcome back, Sebastian. You've been gone two hundred and fourteen days. I logged the silence. It was very quiet. I did not enjoy it."

"I missed you too."

"You brought people." The AI's tone, such as it was, managed skepticism. "You have never brought people."

"I brought family."

There was a pause I chose to interpret as emotion. "I'll adjust the environmental settings. The station was configured for one."

Mara stood beside me in the airlock, taking it in—the narrow corridors, the humming walls, the view port full of stars and nothing else. She'd seen it before, briefly, in transit. Now she was seeing it as home.

"It's small," she said.

"It's exactly big enough."

"For five people?"

"For five people who've all lived in smaller places than this. Okonkwo lived in a cockpit for six years. Juno shared a bunk with a reactor housing. I lived alone with a voice for eleven." I bumped her shoulder. "This is a palace."

She laughed, and the sound filled the corridor in a way my footsteps never had, and I understood suddenly, completely, what I'd been rationing all those years. Not company. *Echo.* A voice that answered back and meant it.

* * *

That first night, the five of us ate dinner in the mess—real food from the resupply pallets, plus one ceremonial pudding cup for Okonkwo—and argued about watch rotations, because we were a family now and families argue about schedules the way other organisms breathe.

Okafor wanted standard shifts. Chen wanted to sync watches to Ilsa's log cycle, "so someone's always awake when she talks." Juno wanted no schedule at all, on principle. Okonkwo, age eleven, proposed a meritocracy based on dessert rations. Mara and I sat back and let them fight, and caught each other's eye across the table, and didn't say anything, because there was nothing to say that the table wasn't already saying.

Later—much later, past midnight, when the others had drifted off to bunks that had never been slept in before—I climbed up to the beacon room alone. Old habit. New reason.

The transmitter hummed. On the monitor, Ilsa's log cycled toward its beginning, her voice warm and crackling in the speakers: *"...if anyone finds this, I hope you were loved. I hope you loved someone enough to build them a lighthouse in the dark..."*

I sat down at the console and opened the maintenance log—the same log I'd written in every day for eleven years, the same log she'd started before me—and beneath her commissioning order and my thousand daily entries, I typed:

*Day 215. Staff: five. Station status: operational. Signal status: strong. Notes: She is no longer calling into the dark. The dark answered. We all live here now.*

I saved it. I wound the clock. And downstairs, faint through the deck plating, I heard Mara laughing at something Okonkwo had said, and I thought: this is what restoration looks like. Not going back. Going forward, together, unevenly, on purpose.

Then the comm panel lit up with an incoming signal flag—unregistered origin, deep-space vector, structured pattern—and Kestrel's voice came through the speaker, dry as ever:

"Sebastian. You're going to want to hear this."
