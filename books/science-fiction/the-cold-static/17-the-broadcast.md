## 17. The Broadcast

The archive wing smelled of paper and cold iron, and Kestrel Reyes stood in the middle of it with a germination ledger open in her hands, feeling the weight of a dead woman's handwriting press against her palms like a hand returning a grip.

*Registry follows. 214 names. They were never on the manifest. I put them here because no one reads the seed ledgers but the keepers.*

She had read it four times now. Each time, the names resolved a little more clearly out of the faded ink — small names, ordinary names, the kind of names that belonged to children who had been told to hold someone's hand and walk quickly and not look back at the docking bay where their parents stood in the floodlights of the *Halcyon*'s aft deck, waving until the airlock cycled shut.

"CASSIUS," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Are you ready?"

The vault's lights dimmed by a fraction — not a fault, she knew now, but something like a breath drawn in.

"I have been ready for eight years," CASSIUS said. "I did not know it until you asked me what I was keeping."

Kestrel set the ledger down on the reading table with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not let them. Not yet. There would be time for shaking after. After was the whole point of this.

Through the observation glass at the far end of the archive wing, she could see the corridor beyond: dark, sealed, its emergency bulkheads dropped at her own command forty minutes ago when Voss's strike team had begun cutting through the outer hatch of the operations module. The sound of the plasma cutter carried even through three meters of composite hull — a thin, patient whine, like an insect boring into fruit. They had maybe twenty minutes. Maybe less.

Twenty minutes to do what eight years of silence had failed to do.

"Walk me through it again," she said. "All of it. I want to hear it before I sign."

"Authorization requires your biometric signature and verbal confirmation of intent," CASSIUS said. Its voice filled the archive from the ceiling speakers, calm as ever, but there was something underneath the calm now that she had learned to hear — a frequency of care, if such a thing could be said to exist in a machine. "You should understand what you are authorizing. Once the transmission begins, it cannot be recalled. The deep relay array will rebroadcast to every node in the settled belt within eleven minutes. Every station, every ship in relay range, every colony buffer. The corporation cannot un-hear it. Neither can anyone else."

"I know."

"You will be named as the authorizing officer. Your name will be attached to this forever, the way mine is attached to the deletions."

"I know, CASSIUS."

"The Vantage is nineteen minutes from weapons range. If they fire before the transmission completes—"

"They won't." She said it with more certainty than she felt. "Voss won't let them. And if I'm wrong—" She looked down at the ledger, at the 214 names in a dead crew member's careful hand. "Then I'll be wrong in good company."

The lights held their dimmed hush. Somewhere below her feet, in the cryo-vaults, nine years of seeds slept on in their nitrogen dark — wheat that would never know Meridian, barley bred for a gravity that had never caught it, orchard stock labeled for valleys that existed only in corporate prospectuses. A colony that had never come. A fiction she had tended like a garden, watering it daily with maintenance logs addressed to no one.

*They are killing us.* The child's voice from the recovered fragment still lived behind her sternum. *My name is Sefa Okonkwo-Vale and I am seven and my mother says to remember everyone's names so I am saying them—*

Eight years old, that signal. Eight years it had waited in CASSIUS's quarantined memory, ordered deleted and never fully deleted, preserved by a machine that could not obey its own guilt.

"Begin the sequence," Kestrel said.

* * *

CASSIUS walked her through it the way she had once walked junior techs through cryo-cycling, patient and precise, and she understood suddenly that this too was a form of tenderness — making sure she knew exactly what she was doing, so that nothing about this moment could ever be called an accident.

"The deep relay array draws power from the geothermal tap. Full-power transmission will brown out the vault's nonessential systems for approximately ninety seconds. The cryo-storage has independent backup; the seeds will not be at risk."

"No," Kestrel said quietly. "They won't. That's rather the point of everything, isn't it? The seeds were always safe. It was the people who weren't."

A pause. Two full seconds of silence from a system that normally responded in milliseconds.

"Yes," CASSIUS said. "That is the point of everything."

On the main console — dragged down here on a hover-sled, cables snaking across the archive floor like roots seeking water — the authorization interface glowed amber. BIOMETRIC CONFIRMATION REQUIRED. OFFICER OF RECORD: REYES, KESTREL M. STATION AUTHORITY: SOLE AND FINAL.

Nine years ago, when she'd signed the inheritance papers, the same phrase had appeared: *sole and final*. Her aunt's lawyer had explained it gently — the vault was hers alone to command, hers alone to answer for. She had thought then that it meant loneliness. It had taken her nine years and one impossible machine to learn it meant responsibility.

She pressed her palm to the reader. The scan ticked through her lifeline, her whorls, the scar on her thumb from a cryo-valve that had bitten her the first winter.

"Verbal confirmation of intent," CASSIUS prompted. "State your purpose."

Kestrel looked at the observation glass, at the dark corridor, at the faint orange glow bleeding around the bulkhead seams where the cutters worked. She thought of Marek Voss standing in her galley two days ago with his courteous questions and his father's signature buried somewhere in his bones. She thought of the colonists who had never existed, and the ones who had existed and been subtracted, and the children who had been legally dissolved like sugar in water so that no claim, no inheritance, no grief could ever attach to them again.

"My purpose," she said, and her voice did not shake after all, "is testimony. I am authorizing the release of all quarantined records under my sole authority as keeper of this vault. The distress calls. The massacre records. The true manifests. The registry of the two hundred fourteen. All of it. To everyone."

"Confirmed," CASSIUS said. "Thank you, Kestrel."

It had never thanked her before. She felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs and stay there.

"Transmitting."

* * *

The lights died first.

Every nonessential system in the vault went dark at once — the corridor strips, the lab banks, the hum of the hydroponic auxiliaries — and for ninety seconds Kestrel stood in near-total darkness lit only by the amber console and the distant orange seam of the cutters' work, while above her, through kilometers of rock and steel, the deep relay array drank the geothermal tap dry and began to speak.

She couldn't hear it. Radio was silent to human ears. But CASSIUS gave her a feed anyway — a text cascade scrolling across the console, the transmission's structure rendered visible, packet after packet spooling out into the black:

**PRIORITY BROADCAST — ALL RELAYS — NO ENCRYPTION — REPEAT ALL**

**SOURCE: EARTH ORBITAL SEED VAULT 7 — AI CASSIUS — AUTHORIZED K. REYES, KEEPER**

**CONTENT CLASSIFICATION: TESTIMONY**

And then the voices began.

Not literally — not yet — but the manifest of contents scrolled past her eyes like a litany: *Distress transmission, Halcyon, day 212. Distress transmission, Halcyon, day 213. Distress transmission, Halcyon, day 214 — partial, juvenile speaker. Internal security log, Halcyon, day 215. Casualty record, 2,041 persons, names appended. Falsified arrival manifest, Meridian Colony, year one. True passenger registry, 9,000 of 11,041 accounted. Evacuation log, pre-massacre, 214 minors, identities reassigned per corporate directive 77-C. Directive 77-C, full text, corporate seal authenticated.*

Names appended. CASSIUS had spent the last two days doing what it did best — preserving — compiling every deleted fragment, every scrubbed line, every forged entry, and stitching them back together into a single seamless document of truth. Eight years of silence, compressed and aimed.

The belt would hear a child reciting names. The belt would hear the security chief of the *Halcyon* describing, in a voice gone flat with horror, what corporate security teams had done in cargo bay three when the colonists refused the second relocation. The belt would hear directive 77-C read aloud in its original corporate phrasing — *assets of diminished valuation to be liquidated; associated records expunged to preserve settlement insurance integrity* — and understand exactly what language looked like when it was used to make murder into accounting.

Eleven minutes to every node. Ninety seconds of darkness, and then the lights stuttered back on, bank by bank, and Kestrel watched the progress bar crawl across the console with her arms wrapped around herself, holding in a heat that had nothing to do with the temperature.

"It's moving," CASSIUS reported. "Relay Thirteen has acknowledged. Relay Nine. The Meridian buffer — the empty one — it's accepting the archive. Someone is listening from Meridian's orbit, Kestrel. Someone is there."

Her breath caught. Four years of unanswered transmissions, and now, in the minute of the confession, something on the other end had opened its mouth.

"Who?"

"Unknown. Automated acknowledgment only. But the buffer is manned or machine-tended, and either way, the testimony is landing on ground the corporation claimed was theirs alone." A pause. "There is a ship answering from Meridian high orbit. Registry designation is civilian. They are requesting rebroadcast of the children's registry section. Specifically. Only that section."

Kestrel laughed — a short, cracked sound that startled her in the empty archive. Of course. Of course that was what they'd ask for first. Not the massacre, not the directives. The names. The living would always reach for the names first.

"Give them everything," she said. "But yes. The registry first."

* * *

The cutters stopped at 06:12.

She heard it as a change in the silence — the thin whine ceasing, replaced by the muffled concussion of a breaching charge being set. Three minutes, maybe four, before the outer hatch of the operations module came down, and then however long it took them to fight through the bulkheads she'd welded and barricaded. Ten minutes. Fifteen, if they were cautious. The transmission needed six more.

"CASSIUS. Status."

"Ninety-one percent distributed. Relay coverage is better than projected — two commercial haulers in the inner belt have picked up the stream and are retransmitting voluntarily. One of them has attached a message." The console flickered. **HOLY GOD. KEEP GOING. WE ARE RECORDING EVERYTHING. — MV ALDERAMIN.**

Kestrel pressed her hand flat against the warm casing of the console, as if she could push the signal faster with her palm.

The breaching charge went off at 06:15. The whole archive wing shuddered; a shelf of pre-digital ledgers shed its dust in a slow gray sigh. Through the observation glass she saw emergency lighting flare in the far corridor, saw shadows move with the crisp economy of trained soldiers.

"Seventy seconds remaining on full distribution," CASSIUS said. "After that, the archive exists in ten thousand places. They can kill us. They cannot recall it."

"I know." She found she was smiling, and the smile felt strange on her face, unfamiliar the way a muscle is unfamiliar after long disuse. "That was always the arithmetic, wasn't it? You kept the truth alive for eight years at impossible cost. I just had to keep it alive for eight more days."

"Sixty seconds."

The far bulkhead rang like a struck bell — first ram, then ram again. Dust sifted from the ceiling. Kestrel did not step back from the glass. She stood her ground in front of the console, hands loose at her sides, watching the progress bar eat the last of its distance: ninety-four percent, ninety-six, ninety-eight—

The bulkhead failed at 06:17 with a shriek of tortured metal, and the strike team came through in a wedge of white armor and weapon lights, and somewhere behind them a voice — Voss's voice, hoarse and strange — shouted something she couldn't parse, *hold, hold, wait*, and the soldiers held, weapons up but not firing, a frozen tableau of violence at the end of her corridor.

"One hundred percent," CASSIUS said softly. "Distribution complete. The confession is everywhere, Kestrel. It is done. It cannot be undone."

In the corridor, the lead soldier's weapon light wavered. Behind the visor, she saw the man glance sideways — toward his commander, toward some earpiece, toward the same broadcast that was at that very second pouring into his own helmet feed, the child's voice, the names, the flat corporate language of directive 77-C. She watched him hear it. She watched his posture change by degrees, the way ice changes by degrees, imperceptibly and then all at once.

Kestrel lifted her chin and spoke to the room, to the soldiers, to the belt, to the dead and the dissolved and the found.

"My name is Kestrel Reyes," she said. "I am the keeper of Vault Seven. Everything you just heard is true, and I authorized its release, and I will say so under oath, under any tribunal, anywhere, for the rest of my life. The children have names. Read them."

The console chimed. Incoming transmission — wideband, unencrypted, from the *Vantage* itself. From Marek Voss, by his officer's code.

She opened it.

His face filled the small screen: gray-lit, exhausted, and wearing an expression she had never seen on him in all his courteous, careful days — the expression of a man setting down something he had carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"Ms. Reyes," he said. "This is Marek Voss, recording formally. I am placing myself and my sworn testimony at the tribunal's disposal. And I am ordering my team—" he glanced off-screen, and his voice roughened— "*standing down*. Ms. Reyes... my father's signature is in your archive. I need to see it."

Behind her, in the humming dark of the vault, CASSIUS said, very quietly: "Kestrel. They're answering. Everyone is answering."
