## 7. Okafor's Silence

The liaison's boots were the first thing Emeka Okafor noticed about him. Not his face, not his credentials, not the immaculate Trade Authority grey of his coat — but the boots, polished to a mirror shine that no working deck in the Cold Terminal could sustain for more than an hour. They clicked when he walked. Everything about Vess Corrin clicked: his heels on the deck plating, his stylus against his slate, his fingernails against the rim of a cup he never drank from.

Okafor had been captaining freighters for thirty-one years, long enough to know that men who made noise did it on purpose. Quiet men were the dangerous ones. But Corrin was something new — a man who had decided that noise itself was a kind of authority, and who filled every room with it so completely that silence became his gift, bestowed or withheld at will.

He sat now in the chart room with his legs crossed and his slate balanced on one knee, watching Okafor pour two cups of tea from the battered pot that had crossed more jumps than either of them had.

"Sugar?" Okafor asked.

"No, thank you." Corrin smiled the way a customs seal smiled — official, adhesive, difficult to remove. "I take things as they come."

Okafor set the pot down and took the chair across from him. Between them lay the route chart for the Meridian run, its lanes glowing soft amber, the way they had glowed for twenty years. He had drawn half those lanes himself, back when the colonies out here were young and hungry and the Cold Terminal hauled everything a growing world needed: seed stock, reactor parts, cryo-backups for people whose bodies couldn't survive the transit but whose contracts could.

"You wanted to discuss the schedule," Okafor said.

"I wanted to discuss the schedule," Corrin agreed pleasantly. "Specifically, stop seven."

Meridian. Okafor kept his face still. Thirty-one years of keeping his face still while creditors, inspectors, and grieving families asked him questions he couldn't answer. It was a skill like any other, and like any skill it rusted if you didn't practice, and lately he'd been practicing more than he liked.

"Meridian's a standard stop," he said. "Two days dockside, standard cargo exchange. We've been running it quarterly since before you were issuing credentials, Mr. Corrin."

"Liaison Corrin," Corrin corrected gently, as though offering a gift rather than a correction. "And yes, I know. I've read your service record, Captain. It's exemplary. That's rather the point."

He turned the slate around. On it, Okafor saw his own career laid out in columns: tonnage moved, jumps completed, incidents logged (few), commendations (several), debts (one, flagged amber, fourteen years old). The sight of that last line landed in Okafor's chest like cold ballast. He had known the Authority kept files. He had not known they kept *that* file.

"Fourteen years ago," Corrin said, tapping the amber line once, softly, with one polished fingertip, "you found yourself in a difficult position. A refit loan called in early after the Halcyon yards collapsed. You owed one hundred and forty thousand credits to an institution that no longer existed, which meant the debt had been purchased by someone who did exist, and who was not patient." He looked up. His eyes were pale grey, almost colorless, and very steady. "Someone helped you, Captain. Restructured the debt. Forgave most of it. In exchange for small considerations. Authorization codes, primarily. Signatures on manifests you didn't examine too closely. Nothing you'd call criminal. Nothing anyone would call criminal. Just... consideration."

The tea went cold in Okafor's hands. He didn't drink it. He watched steam rise off it in thin threads and thought about how many times he had told himself the same story: nothing criminal, just consideration, just a signature, just a code entered into a system that would have processed the transaction anyway, with or without him. The debt had been killing him. The Cold Terminal was mortgaged to her rivets; forty-three crew drew wages from her holds; his daughter's medical trust sat in an account that the restructured loan had quietly replenished when the interest rates spiked. Consideration. Small considerations, compounding annually, like anything else you failed to pay down.

"That was a long time ago," Okafor said.

"It was," Corrin agreed. "And you've been reliable ever since, which is why we're having tea instead of a hearing." He uncrossed his legs and leaned forward, and for a moment — just a moment — the charm dropped away, and what was underneath was worse: sincerity. Genuine, unarmored belief. "Captain, I'm going to tell you something true, because you've earned a truth and because it will serve us both. There are forces at work in this corridor that make my office look like a schoolteacher. Cartels. Contract syndicates. Men who move populations the way you move cargo, and who will burn a station to protect a margin. What the Authority does — what *I* do — is keep the peace. Keep the lanes open. Keep two hundred million colonists fed and fueled and ignorant of how close the whole lattice comes to collapse every single quarter. Sometimes keeping the peace means a colony gets administratively inactive. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand you're asking me to skip Meridian," Okafor said.

Corrin smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which somehow made it colder. "I'm asking you to do what you've always done. Be reliable. Log the schedule amendment as a fuel-economy measure — which, incidentally, it genuinely is; the long loop saves you four percent at current prices, and I know how you watch your margins. Dock at Thessaly instead. Take on the usual tonnage. And in three months, when the schedule cycles back around, Meridian will be there waiting for you, exactly as it always is." He paused. "Probably."

*Probably.* The word hung in the chart room like a pressure leak.

"And if it isn't?"

"Then it won't be your concern," Corrin said, "because you weren't there, and neither was your ship, and neither was your manifest. Captain — think of it as navigation. You've spent your whole life steering around weather. This is weather. Political weather. And the first law of your profession has always been that the ship comes through. Not the schedule. Not the sentiment. The ship." He rose, buttoned his coat with two precise movements, and collected his untouched tea as though it had served its purpose by existing. At the door he stopped, and without turning around, added: "Your engineer signs his maintenance logs in a very distinctive hand. Your navigator keeps unusual hours. These are good people, Captain. People notice things. It's a hazard of employing good people. I'd hate for their noticing to become your problem."

The door sealed behind him with a click.

* * *

Okafor stayed in the chart room for a long time after that.

He ran the numbers first, because numbers were where he hid. Four percent fuel savings on the long loop: real, verifiable, the kind of fact you could put in front of a crew meeting without blushing. Two days saved per cycle: also real. The Cold Terminal's reactor was nineteen years old and her hull coating was due for replacement within the fiscal year; every margin he banked was a rivet he didn't have to beg for. He could sell the schedule change to Dov Hale in ninety seconds flat. He could sell it to himself almost as fast.

That was the trick of it, he thought. That was the engineering of it. Corruption, done properly, was never a door kicked open. It was a load path — a force applied gradually along lines the structure already carried, until one day the structure was bearing a weight it had never agreed to bear, and no single joint could point to the moment it started failing. He had signed his first false manifest fourteen years ago with his heart hammering and his hand shaking, and it had felt like a crime. Somewhere in the intervening years it had stopped feeling like anything at all, and that frightened him more than any auditor ever had.

He pulled up the crew roster and read down it the way he did every night, a ritual older than the debt. Dov Hale, chief engineer, twenty-two years aboard, who still talked to the reactor like it was a horse he was gentling. Ines Pemberton, navigator, who had come to him five years ago out of some private grief she'd never named and who flew the ship like an extension of her own nervous system. Forty-three names. He knew all of them. He knew which ones sent money home, which ones had kids on which stations, which ones drank, which ones prayed, which ones would follow him into a fire and which ones he'd carry out of one.

And somewhere out ahead of them, past the amber lanes, was Meridian Station, where a teacher named Amara Okonkwo — he'd met her twice, at cargo exchanges, a serious woman with ink-stained fingers — was reportedly telling anyone who'd listen that her students were dreaming of a massacre.

He'd read the report. Of course he'd read the report; Corrin had made sure it crossed his desk, wrapped in the language of public-health nuisance, station administration overreacting, children's neural implants misfiring on shared broadcast interference. A tidy explanation. Tidy explanations were the Authority's native product. But Okafor had been hauling cargo out here for thirty-one years, and he remembered Kestrel-9. He remembered the evacuation equipment — two hundred cryo-cradles, atmospheric seals, trauma kits by the pallet — loaded onto his own decks in a week of frantic overtime, and then the order countermanding the launch, and then the manifests rewritten while the cradles were still warm from handling, and then the silence. He remembered signing the fiction afterward. Dov had stood next to him at that terminal. Neither of them had looked at each other.

One hundred and ninety-six souls, the original manifest had said, before it said something else.

He thought about the children dreaming. He thought about what it might mean — really mean — if forty small minds on one station were receiving the same signal from the same dead place. He thought about his granddaughter, six years old, asleep right now on Thessaly under a ceiling covered in glow-stars, and whether the lattice of minds out here had edges, and whether weather spread.

Then he thought about the amber line in his file. One hundred and forty thousand credits, forgiven. His daughter's trust. The ship. The forty-three names. All of it resting on considerations already given, on signatures already signed, on a man he had already been for fourteen years. Corrin hadn't threatened him tonight. He hadn't needed to. He had simply shown Okafor the blueprint of his own life and let him see which beams were load-bearing.

A decent man choosing the wrong side slowly — that was what this was, he understood suddenly, looking at his reflection ghosted in the dark chart glass. Not a decision. A drift. Each individual course correction so small it registered as prudence: skip one stop, save four percent, log the economy measure, protect the crew from questions they were better off not asking. Prudence, prudence, prudence, until the accumulated heading pointed somewhere he had never chosen to go.

He opened the scheduling console.

His hands were steady. That was the worst part. Fourteen years of practice had made them steady.

* * *

He found Ines Pemberton on the aft observation deck at 0300, which was where he knew she'd be, because navigators kept the hours of people waiting for something. She stood at the long window with her arms folded, watching the dead relay station tumble slowly through the black — a relic of the first expansion, all solar sails and corroded spars, marking a lane nobody used anymore.

"The schedule amendment," he said. "You'll have seen it."

She didn't turn. "Fuel economy. Four percent." Her voice was flat in a way he recognized; he'd heard it from Dov, years ago, the morning after the Kestrel-9 manifests were rewritten. Flatness was what conscience sounded like when it was being held shut by hand. "We're skipping Meridian."

"We are."

"For how long?"

"The cycle will bring us back around," Okafor said, and heard Corrin's own words come out of his mouth, wearing his voice, and hated them.

Ines finally turned. Her eyes in the starlight were very clear and very tired, and they went through him like a sounding line going through shallow water, touching bottom on the first pass. "Captain," she said, "three colonies on our chart went administratively inactive this quarter. I checked. The last time that happened, we were carrying evacuation equipment to one of them." She paused. "I'm telling you what I saw. As your navigator. Because that's my job — to see what's out there and tell you."

"I know what your job is," Okafor said.

"Do you?" No anger in it. That was the terrible thing. Just a question, honest and weightless, drifting between them in the dark like debris.

He wanted — briefly, violently — to tell her everything. The file with the amber line. The tea that Corrin hadn't drunk. The one hundred and ninety-six. He wanted to say: I am not the man you think I am, and I am exactly the man you think I am, and both of those things are true, and I don't know how to be only one of them anymore. He wanted to warn her, because some part of him, some joint deep in the structure that hadn't yet fully yielded, understood that warning her was the last load-bearing act available to him short of refusal itself.

Instead he said: "Log the amended route, Navigator. Fly the ship. That's an order, and it's the last one I'll give you tonight."

Something moved behind her eyes — disappointment, or confirmation, or the beginning of a plan. He would learn which later, and it would cost him.

"Aye, Captain," she said, and left him alone with the tumbling relay and the stars, which kept no records and forgave nothing and shone equally on the guilty and the lost.

Okafor stood at the window a long time. Below his feet, two hundred sleepers rode the hold in their cradles, breathing the slow breath of the preserved, trusting the ship, trusting the route, trusting him. He pressed his palm flat against the cold viewport glass, as if he could feel them through nine decks of steel.

"Three months," he said aloud, to no one. "It'll bring us back around."

He was lying, and he knew whose voice he'd stolen the lie from, and the relay tumbled on through the dark, broadcasting nothing to anyone, the way he was about to teach his whole ship to travel.
