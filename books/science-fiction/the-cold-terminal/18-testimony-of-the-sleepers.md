## 18. Testimony of the Sleepers

The dead did not sleep. That was the first thing Ines understood as she moved through the Cold Terminal's converted cargo bay, past the twelve gurneys bolted where cryo-pods had been, past the tangle of medical lines and jury-rigged heaters that Dov had spent nine hours coaxing into something like a recovery ward. The dead lay awake in the dark with their eyes open, remembering.

She had woken them screaming. Now she had to ask them to speak.

* * *

Mara Voss went first, because she insisted on going first.

Her source body had been thirty-four years old, a hydroponics technician on Kestrel-9, and the backup lying on the gurney wore that body's face — younger by eleven years, frozen at the moment of imprint, smooth-skinned and unlined while her memories carried every day since. She sat propped against pillows Dov had scavenged from three different bunks, an old freight blanket around her shoulders, and stared at the recorder Ines had mounted on a cargo strap above her head.

"It's recording," Ines said gently. "Whenever you're ready."

"Ready." Mara laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle like river ice. "I've been ready for eleven years. I just didn't know it." She looked down at her own hands — hands that had never planted a seedling, never pulled a harvest, hands that had done nothing but wait in the dark — and flexed them slowly, as if testing whether they belonged to her. "My name is Mara Voss. Backup designation KV-114. Source: Kestrel-9, agricultural sector. I was imprinted on—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, slower, the way you'd walk across a floor you suspected was rotten. "I remember the last day. All of it. I remember everything up until the imprint cut off, and then I remember nothing except waiting, and now I'm here, and I don't know what year it is or how many of us there are, but I know what happened to my colony, and I need someone to write it down before I lose my nerve."

Ines pulled a stool close and sat, keeping herself low, below the recorder's frame. A witness, not an interrogator. "It's written down the moment you say it. Take all the time you need."

"The evacuation ships never launched." Mara said it flatly, the way you'd report a failed pump seal. "That's the part nobody will believe, so I'll say it first. There were six transports on the pad. Six. I helped load them — food stock, medical kits, the children's crates. We were told dome integrity was failing after the quake, that we'd lift within forty hours. Forty hours. And then the order came down that the launch windows had been reassigned." Her jaw tightened. "Reassigned. Like weather. Like we were cargo that could be rescheduled."

"What order?" Ines asked quietly. "Who gave it?"

"A colonial administrator named Dev Anand. He read it over the public channel so no one could pretend they hadn't heard. He was crying when he read it. I want that noted too — he cried, and he read it anyway." Mara's voice steadied, found a terrible rhythm. "The transports were diverted to Solenne Reach under emergency contract priority. Contract priority. Two hundred thousand people stood under a cracking dome and watched their own rescue ships burn fuel toward another colony's profit margin, because some cartel's quarterly numbers mattered more than our lungs."

* * *

Dov Hale recorded second, though his testimony wasn't testimony at all. He sat in the engineering alcove with the recorder running and told the machine about the loading bay at Meridian, seven years ago, when he was twenty-three and eager and bad at reading silences.

"They had me sign a manifest correction," he said. His big hands were folded on his knees, knuckles scarred from decades of work the backups had never done. "Sixty crates of atmospheric processors, outbound to Kestrel-9, marked as agricultural equipment. I asked why the mislabel. My supervisor said customs classification, said it saved tariff fees, and I believed him because believing him was easier than not. Then after the colony went quiet, a man came aboard — Authority, plain clothes, polite as a surgeon — and had me sign a second document confirming the first shipment had been returned unused. Returned. I signed it. I want the record to show I signed it, and that I knew, by then, that nothing had come back, and I signed it anyway because he mentioned my sister's visa status in the same sentence, very casually, the way you'd mention the weather." He looked directly into the recorder's lens, which was harder than looking away. "Complicity isn't always a choice between good and evil. Sometimes it's a choice between evil and a smaller evil with your family's name on it. But it's still complicity. Write that down. I'm giving this core to history, and history can judge me with the rest of them."

* * *

They came one at a time through the long night cycle, because twelve people waking from eleven years of sensory void could not bear each other's faces all at once. Ines learned to schedule grief the way she scheduled course corrections: in blocks, with margins for overrun.

A man named Tomas Reyes, dockworker, backup designation KV-067, described the final breach in the voice of a man reading a shipping report. "The dome failed at grid section fourteen at 0312 station time. Not an explosion — a peeling. The quake had worked the seams loose over months and the repair budget kept getting deferred, and everyone knew, and everyone kept deferring, because the Authority's audit cycle rewarded colonies that reported stability. So we reported stability. Right up until the sky came in." He paused. "Four hundred and six people made it to shelter level two. The shelters were rated for nine hundred. They'd sold the excess capacity back to the Authority in a cost-recovery program four years earlier. I processed the paperwork myself."

A woman called Adaeze Nwosu, school administrator, spoke for nineteen minutes without stopping about the children's wing, about the teachers who stayed, about the names of eleven children she recited from memory while her backup body — young, unmarked, breathing steadily — shed tears it had no stored memory of shedding. Grief, Ines realized, lived somewhere deeper than the imprint. It had ridden along in whatever made a person a person, and it had waited eleven years in the dark for a mouth.

* * *

Dev Anand came ninth.

He was the name Mara had given, the colonial administrator who had read the diversion order aloud, and when Ines had spoken it to him in the recovery bay, he had gone very still and said only, "Then I'm still needed." He looked older than his backup body the way the others did, but more than that — he looked like a man who had rehearsed this moment for a decade inside his own skull and found the rehearsal insufficient.

He sat upright, unassisted, and asked Ines to check the recorder twice.

"My name is Dev Anand. I served as Deputy Administrator for Logistics on Kestrel-9 for six years. I am, to my knowledge, the highest-ranking survivor of the Kestrel-9 evacuation failure, if 'survivor' is a word that applies to a body that never left its pod." He folded his hands. "I'm going to give you names, dates, and contract numbers, and I want every one of them preserved exactly, because I have spent eleven years of subjective silence understanding that the truth of Kestrel-9 will not fail from lack of evidence. It will fail from lack of a witness with standing. So let me establish mine."

And then he laid it out, methodical as a ledger, and Ines felt the shape of the thing assemble itself in the air between them like a constellation resolving out of noise.

"The evacuation-contract cartel — Meridian Consolidated Lift, Solenne Transit Group, and their holding entity, the Halcyon Corridor Partnership — held exclusive emergency-evacuation rights for eleven outer colonies. Their contracts contained a force-majeure clause: in the event of a declared colony-wide emergency, contracted transport assets could be redirected to higher-value clients at penalty rates paid by the Authority itself. Do you understand what that means? Every redirection earned them money twice — once from the client they fled to, once from the colony they abandoned, billed as a service disruption surcharge." His voice didn't rise. It descended, quieter and quieter, the way deep water gets darker. "Kestrel-9 was not the first. It was the fourth. And it would not be the last, because the system worked. It worked beautifully. It transferred wealth upward with every catastrophe, and the catastrophes themselves became line items."

"Corrin," Ines said. "Vess Corrin. Was his office—"

"Corrin's office authored the deletion orders." Anand said it without hesitation, without drama, the way a surveyor marks a boundary stone. "I saw the authorization trail before the network went down. Directive series K-9 through K-11, routed through the Trade Authority's Office of Contract Integrity — Corrin's office, though he wasn't Director yet; he was the enforcement arm even then, the one who made inconvenient auditors retire. The deletions weren't concealment, Ms. Pemberton. Concealment implies fear of discovery. This was curation. They weren't hiding the deaths. They were editing the record so that the deaths had never been possible — so that no future auditor would ever think to ask why a colony of two hundred thousand had no evacuation record, because officially there had never been an emergency to evacuate from. The dome didn't fail. The colony didn't suffer. The colony simply ceased to be administratively active, and administrative inactivity, conveniently, requires no explanation."

"And the children," Ines said. Her throat was tight. "On Meridian. Dreaming it."

Anand turned his head and looked at her fully for the first time, and behind his eyes she saw the whole weight of the thing he carried. "The colony's central AI — KESTREL, we called her, she ran the life support and the archive — she was scheduled for decommission with the rest of the infrastructure. Someone in the shutdown chain gave her a final directive, and I've thought about this for eleven years, and I believe it was mercy, or the closest thing a machine can feel to it. She was ordered to archive everything. Everything. Sensory logs, civil records, the final seventy-two hours. And her last act, with her transmitters failing and her power bleeding out, was to broadcast that archive outward on every frequency she had — not as data, because data can be intercepted and deleted, but smeared across the neural bands, raw, undirected, into any receiving mind within range. Into implant-equipped children sleeping on a station two jumps away." He exhaled. "You cannot delete a dream, Ms. Pemberton. They built their entire architecture on the assumption that evidence lives in files. They forgot it can live in people. In forty small witnesses who never set foot on Kestrel-9 and cannot be made administratively inactive."

* * *

By the eleventh hour of the recording cycle, the cargo bay had changed character. The witnesses who had finished — hoarse, hollowed, lighter by exactly the weight of what they'd put down — refused to return to the bunks. They arranged themselves instead in a loose crescent around the gurneys of those still waiting, and when Adaeze Nwosu took her turn, the others listened, and when she faltered on the eleventh name, the whole crescent breathed with her until she found it.

Tomas Reyes sat beside the twelfth witness, a young man whose source body had been nineteen, and held his hand through the worst of it, and neither of them remarked on the strangeness of strangers comforting strangers with the same memories.

Ines stood at the bay's edge and watched the dead find their voices, and understood that something irreversible was happening. Not the recordings — those were data, and data could be stolen, corrupted, deleted. What was happening was in the room itself. Twelve people who had been cargo were becoming testimony. The Cold Terminal had carried them for years as freight, sealed manifests, tonnage. Now they were passengers. Now they were witnesses. And a ship full of witnesses was a different object entirely in the eyes of the law — and in the eyes of men like Vess Corrin, a far more dangerous one.

Dov appeared at her shoulder, grease-streaked, exhausted, holding two cups of recycled coffee that tasted like regret and copper. "Storage arrays are at ninety percent," he murmured. "Every word triple-backed. Core copies in the jettison pods, the ones you prepped." He hesitated. "There's something else. I've been monitoring the relay traffic since we docked at the dead node. Authority chatter's gone strange. Encrypted bursts, short ones, directional. Somebody knows where we are, Ines. Or somebody's looking."

"Let them look," said a voice behind them.

Mara Voss stood there, blanket around her shoulders, steady on feet that had never walked. Behind her, the crescent of witnesses had risen, all of them, silent, facing outward toward the bay doors as if they could see through the hull to the dark where the Authority's ships would come from.

"We spent eleven years being quiet," Mara said. "We're done being quiet. Whatever you're planning to do with our words, navigator — do it loud."

Ines looked at the twelve of them, at the recorders still turning, at the ancient relay hanging outside the viewport like a rusted crown, and felt the last hesitation fall away from her like a burned-away seam.

The transmitter, she thought. The relay's transmitter. Old, enormous, powerful enough to reach every colony channel in the corridor.

"All right," she said. "Then let's give them something they can't delete."

Behind her, in the dark of the hold, the dead began to sing — softly, badly, in a language half of them hadn't spoken since childhood — and Ines turned toward the bridge and the longest broadcast of her life.
