## 10. What the Record Keeps

The thing nobody tells you about escaping a burning station is how much of it is paperwork.

Osei's people gave me a bunk on the *Long Answer*, third deck, between a water reclamation tech who snored like a failing compressor and a crate of medical supplies that had opinions about gravity. They gave me a blanket, a ration pack, and a form to fill out: name, origin, status of personal effects.

Personal effects. I wrote down "one data core, six years of dead people, and a coffee mug I left behind because it wouldn't fit in the pocket." The intake officer read my form twice and decided not to ask.

* * *

Meridian-9 burned for eleven hours.

I know because I watched all of them. Osei's convoy held position at a respectful distance—close enough to see, far enough that the debris wouldn't reach us—and the whole ragtag fleet of salvagers and refugees turned their external cameras toward the station like mourners at an open casket. Nobody said much. There was nothing to say that the fire wasn't already saying better.

It went in stages. First the comms array sheared off, spinning away in a slow, almost apologetic arc, trailing coolant like a severed artery. Then the habitat ring depressurized in a series of soft white exhalations, one section at a time, the station breathing out everything I'd ever lived inside. My bunk. My garden—the sad hydroponic tomatoes I'd coaxed along for four years out of pure spite. The observation lounge where I'd talked to the relay logs every morning for six years, telling them about the weather, about the colony's dwindling signal, about how much I hated the Corporation and missed the sound of another human voice.

The logs were gone now. But that was fine. That was the entire point.

Because while Meridian-9 burned, its memory didn't. Every byte of those six years—the starvation logs, the ignored pleas, the deletion orders with their tidy corporate timestamps—had been mirrored across both fleets' archives before I ever opened the channels. Voss had them. Osei had them. Forty thousand crew and colonists and mercenaries had them, copied onto personal devices within minutes, because grief travels faster than any firewall. You can't un-broadcast a truth. You can only wish you'd told it sooner.

The reactor went last, at 0347 by ship's time, and even from this distance it was beautiful in the way that only endings are—a white bloom, then a slow orange bruise spreading across the dark, then nothing. Just a scatter of cooling metal where my home used to be.

I cried, if you're wondering. Quietly, into the blanket, so the snoring water tech wouldn't hear. Six years of loneliness doesn't evaporate when you win. It just changes shape.

* * *

Amara Chen found me two days later, in the mess hall, staring at a cup of tea that tasted like someone had boiled a boot.

She moved slowly now, one hand braced on the table edges, but her eyes hadn't aged a day past sharp. Two hundred days in a deep shelter, and two centuries of a dying language living in her head, and she'd come through both with her sense of humor intact—which was more than I could say for myself.

"You look terrible," she said, sitting down across from me without asking.

"I'm told that's fashionable among witnesses."

"Mm." She wrapped her hands around her own cup. "The others are asking about you. The survivors. They want to know what happens next."

"What do you tell them?"

"That a woman they've never met burned down a government to save them." She sipped her tea and grimaced. "They find it very dramatic. Some of them find it suspicious. Old habits."

"Can't argue with that. I did forge a plague."

"You did." Her mouth twitched. "Your Latin was atrocious, by the way. *Quarantina* is not how you decline that word. I could hear Commander Voss wincing from orbit."

"She read Latin?"

"Voss reads everything. That's why she lost." Chen set the cup down and looked at me directly, and I felt the full weight of those two hundred days settle into her gaze. "Ulyana. I asked you to testify for us. Not to save us—you did that anyway, which was either brave or insane, we're still voting—but to testify. So I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer honestly."

"Those are historically not my strong suits."

"No," she agreed. "But they're your future ones. What happens when the inquiries start? Because they will start. Voss is already under review—her own board summoned her before her flagship finished docking. And Osei turned down salvage rights on Halcyon's orbital assets yesterday. Turned down forty billion in recoverable tonnage to file for witness status instead. His own crew nearly mutinied. Something is happening out here, and both of them are going to be asked how, and by whom, and why."

"And the answer is a dead technician with forged credentials."

"The answer," Chen said, "is a live one. If you want it to be."

I looked into my boot-tea for a long moment. Outside the viewport, Halcyon hung below us, blue-green and indifferent, its deep shelters now lit with rescue beacons, its surface dotted with landing lights as the first honest relief ships made planetfall. Two hundred days too late. Six years too late. But late is not never, and never is what the Corporation had planned.

"They'll want a scapegoat," I said finally. "Someone to blame for the forgery. For the near-war. For embarrassing two admiralties in front of each other."

"Yes."

"And if I testify—if I stand up in some tribunal and say *yes, that was me, I invented a plague to stop a massacre*—they'll take everything. Whatever pension I don't have. Whatever freedom I wasn't using anyway."

"Yes," Chen said again, gently. "Probably."

* * *

Here's what I learned about justice outside the law: it doesn't come with a gavel. It comes with a cost, itemized.

Voss faced her inquiry aboard her own flagship, stripped of command pending review, and by every report refused to shift blame downward. When the tribunal asked why she'd stood down her guns above Meridian-9—with firing solutions locked and her officers begging for the order—she reportedly said only: "Because I read the records. Read them yourself." Four words. They're being quoted in parliaments now, apparently, which would have horrified her and which she'd have pretended not to care about.

Osei chose testimony over salvage, formally, publicly, in a transmission he addressed to "every fleet, company, and committee that forgot Halcyon existed." His convoy lost half its contracts within a week. He gained something else: the survivors' manifest, and the right to be the man who carried it. His crew, eventually—grudgingly, the way sailors agree to anything—came around. Mercy, it turns out, is contagious if you quarantine everyone with the alternative.

And me? I got a summons. Of course I got a summons. The Colonial Reconstruction Tribunal, convening at Meridian-7, requests the presence of one Ulyana Reyes, former technician, current person of interest, to account for the unauthorized broadcast of classified corporate records and the fabrication of emergency protocols under the authority of a defunct governing body.

Chen offered to come with me. I told her no. She'd testified enough for two lifetimes; the shelters needed their linguist more than a tribunal needed her glare. She squeezed my hand at the airlock and said, in flawless Old Terran Standard, "*Veritas non perit*"—truth does not perish—and then ruined it completely by adding, "Even when it's grammatically mangled."

* * *

Which brings me to now. To this.

I'm writing this in a cabin on a slow transport to Meridian-7, three weeks of transit ahead of me and nothing to fill them with except the truth. I requested the records back—all of them, the mirrored copies, the six years—and Osei's archivist sent them over without a word of argument, just a note that said: *Somebody should keep the honest version.*

So I am. This is it. This document you're reading, these chapters, this account—it's the honest version. Not the tribunal transcript, which will be redacted until it squeaks. Not the corporate deposition, which will be fiction wearing a suit. This. The technician's version. The version with the crying into blankets and the forged Latin and the tomatoes.

I don't know what happens at the tribunal. Maybe they jail me. Maybe they make me a symbol, which is worse. Maybe the Corporation's lawyers are so busy drowning in their own leaked records that they forget I exist, though I doubt it—I embarrassed them in front of history, and corporations forgive everything except that.

But here's what I do know. Somewhere below us, on a colony that was supposed to die quietly, thirty-one thousand people are eating hot meals tonight because a lie bought them time and a truth spent it well. Amara Chen is teaching children a language the Corporation tried to kill. Two fleets that came to fight over a corpse are instead arguing, in committee rooms, about who failed harder—and for once, the argument might actually matter.

And Meridian-9 is dust, and I am not, and the record keeps what the record keeps.

I used to talk to the dead relay logs every morning. Now I'm writing for whoever finds these pages instead. Same ritual, really. Same voice. Just a bigger room.

The transport shudders as it makes its first burn toward Meridian-7. In three weeks I'll walk into a tribunal and hand them my confession, my testimony, and my complete lack of remorse, in roughly that order.

Until then, there's this. There's always this.

My name is Ulyana Reyes. I was the last human aboard Meridian-9.

I'm not the last anymore. None of us are.

That's the whole story. That's what the record keeps.
