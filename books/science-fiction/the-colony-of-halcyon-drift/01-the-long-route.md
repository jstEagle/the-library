## 1. The Long Route

The vault woke Oscar the way it always did: not with sound, but with the absence of it. Ten years had taught her to read the silence of the Halcyon Drift Seed Vault the way sailors once read the sea — by its texture, its moods, the small deviations that meant weather coming or weather passing. This morning the silence was clean and deep, the silence of a hard frost outside the hull, and she lay in her narrow bunk for a full minute before moving, listening to the building breathe through its vents, feeling the faint tidal hum of the geothermal taps far below the permafrost.

She had stopped expecting anyone else to be awake a long time ago.

The corridor outside her quarters ran forty meters to the central hub, and she walked it every morning at the same pace, her boots striking the deck plates in a rhythm she no longer heard. The walls were lined with the vault's oldest photographs, printed on actual paper by some optimistic administrator in the years before the Fracture War: fields of wheat in Kansas, terraced rice in the Philippines, a greenhouse on Mars with a child's handprint pressed into its fogged glass. Oscar had looked at them so many times that they had worn smooth in her mind, the way river stones wear. She could have described them with her eyes closed. Some mornings she did, as a kind of prayer.

The hub was a cylinder of pale light and green smell. Fourteen germination bays ringed the space like the petals of some enormous metal flower, each one a climate-sealed chamber where seeds from a hundred vanished harvests slept in nitrogen dark, waiting for a world that might someday want them again. Oscar made her rounds the way her predecessor, a taciturn Finn named Halonen, had taught her in her first month: bay by bay, gauge by gauge, her fingertips trailing the cool ceramic of the access panels. Humidity nominal. Nitrogen pressure nominal. Germination viability, bay seven, down two-tenths of a percent since last quarter — expected, logged, forgiven. The seeds were dying by fractions, all of them, the way everything was, and her job was to make the dying take as long as possible.

"You're all still here," she said to the bays, because she said it every morning, because the ritual mattered more than the words. "Nobody's left without me."

The vault did not answer. It never did. But the lights cycled from their night amber to working white, and somewhere in the walls a pump shifted pitch, and Oscar chose, as she chose every morning, to take this for a reply.

She ate breakfast at the observation window on the hub's second level, where a meter-wide pane of armored glass looked out onto the Greenland plateau. The sun had not quite cleared the ridge. The ice was doing its dawn thing, all pink and violet and impossibly clean, and the sky above it was the deep bruised blue that meant another day of hard cold and hard clarity. Ten years ago there had been lights on the southern horizon — the colony relay at Nuuk, the atmospheric processors, the little constellations of a civilization still bothering to illuminate itself. Now the horizon was dark. The colonies had gone quiet one by one after the Fracture War severed the shipping lanes, and the last message from Nuuk, four years ago, had been a form letter: *Resources reallocated. Thank you for your service.*

*Your service.* As if she were a soldier. As if tending a vault full of seeds for a species that had stopped planting were a service and not a sentence.

Oscar rinsed her cup, checked the weather array, and began the day's real work: the manifest.

* * *

The supply run was the spine of her year, the thing her whole calendar hung from. Every eleven weeks, the vault's automated requisition system compiled a list of what she needed — filters, gaskets, nutrient salts, the slow consumables of preservation — and broadcast it to the relay at Svalbard, and whatever freighter happened to be working the northern lanes would pick it up or it wouldn't. In the early years there had been choices, three or four ships sometimes, captains she came to know by voice. Now there was usually one, and lately the ships had names she didn't recognize, crews she never saw, running their cargo from hold to dock by drone so that the whole trade had become a kind of ghost commerce — goods appearing at Dock Nine as if exhaled by the ice itself.

The run was scheduled for the end of the week. This morning she was only doing what she did every Monday: reviewing the incoming manifests, cross-checking them against her requisitions, flagging discrepancies for the relay's indifferent auto-clerk. It was bookkeeping. It was the most human contact her week contained, in its bloodless way — somewhere on the other end of each manifest line was a person who had loaded a crate, sealed a drum, typed a weight into a field. She had never met them. She imagined them anyway, the way lonely people do: a woman with cold-reddened hands, a man humming while he worked. Her invisible colleagues. The crew of the world's smallest fleet.

She was halfway through the list when she saw it.

At first it was nothing. Manifest 7741-K, freighter *Meridian*, out of Reykjavik relay, bound for Halcyon Drift, Dock Nine, with her standard consignment plus a replacement compressor for bay three that she hadn't ordered but certainly needed — someone had actually read her maintenance logs, which was its own small miracle. She was reaching to approve it when her eye caught the crew roster, appended below the cargo, as regulations still required even though no crew ever came down the dock ramp anymore.

*Master:* R. Okonkwo. *Purser:* T. Vasquez. *Comms:* D. Lindqvist. *Passenger, certified:* V. Andrade.

Oscar's hand stopped over the approval key.

She knew the name. Everyone in the northern lanes knew the name, the way everyone knows the name of a storm that killed people. Vess Andrade, daughter of Admiral Cassian Andrade of the Meridian Compact, the girl the propaganda feeds had made into a martyr six years ago when her ship burned at Perseus Dock with all hands — the incident that had given the admiral his casus belli, his grief-stricken face on every broadcast, his license to close the lanes and fracture the colonies into armed camps. Oscar had watched those feeds in this very hub, in the weeks after the burn, while the vault's uplink still carried anything but silence. She remembered the girl's school portrait, pixelated and over-repeated. She remembered the admiral's voice cracking on the word *daughter*.

And she remembered the *Meridian* herself, because everyone in logistics remembered the *Meridian*: a beautiful old Arctic freighter, hull number painted over with a memorial stencil, listed lost with all hands at Perseus Dock, six years ago, on a date Oscar could have recited if you'd asked her, though no one ever had.

The manifest said the ship was coming to her dock in five days.

She sat very still. The vault hummed around her, patient, green-smelling, indifferent. Out the observation window the sun had finally cleared the ridge and laid a long blade of light across the ice, and the light was beautiful and completely without opinion.

"Old record," she said aloud. "Recycled hull number. Registry error." The auto-clerk's databases were patched together from a dozen pre-war systems; ships got resurrected in the paperwork all the time, misfiled, misnamed. There would be an explanation. There was always an explanation, and it was always boring, and the boring explanation was the last luxury of a quiet life.

She pulled the registry cross-check, and the cross-check confirmed the *Meridian*, hull 7741-K, status: LOST, and she felt the first loosening of breath in her chest — and then she looked closer, and saw that the cross-check was pulling from the same relay database the manifest had come from, which proved nothing except that the database agreed with itself.

So she went deeper. She always went deeper; it was the one habit ten years of solitude had sharpened rather than dulled. She went to her own archives — the vault's private, physically isolated records, the ones Halonen had built on the old principle that a seed vault that trusted the network deserved to lose its seeds. The vault kept its own copy of the northern shipping registries, updated quarterly by physical data courier in the years when couriers still came, frozen in time at the last update, four years ago.

She searched hull 7741-K.

The vault's archive said: *Meridian. Lost with all hands. Perseus Dock. Six years ago.* And beneath the status line, in the archived crew manifest of the final voyage, the same four names — Okonkwo, Vasquez, Lindqvist — and the same passenger, certified: *V. Andrade.*

The dead ship and the living manifest, carrying the same dead woman, bound for her dock.

Oscar sat back. Her heart was doing something she resented, something quick and animal, and she made herself breathe the way she made herself do everything else: slowly, by routine, because routine was the only muscle she had never let atrophy. She ran the comparison twice. She checksummed both files against their stored hashes. Both intact. Neither edited — not on her side, at least. The vault's archive was a stone; whatever had changed, had changed out there, in the warm and lying world beyond the ice.

Which meant one of two things. Either the relay's database had suffered a corruption so strange it had resurrected a burned ship with an intact, regulation-formatted crew roster — or someone, somewhere in the ghost commerce of the northern lanes, was flying the *Meridian* again, and had put the name Vess Andrade on her papers, and had sent those papers to the one terminal in the hemisphere that still kept the old records.

The light had crawled across the ice while she sat there. The germination bays cycled through their noon dimming. Somewhere below her feet, in the nitrogen dark, ten thousand years of harvests waited out the end of the world with more patience than she could manage.

Oscar pulled up the manifest one more time and read the arrival estimate. Five days. Dock Nine. A ship that had burned with all hands, bringing her a compressor she needed, and a name she had last seen on a propaganda feed beside the word *martyr*.

She did not approve the manifest. She did not reject it. She left it open on her terminal, glowing in the hub's green half-light like something washed up on a beach, and she went and stood at the observation window with her arms crossed against a cold that was not in the room, watching the dark horizon to the south, waiting for it to stay dark.

It stayed dark. But five days, she thought, was a long time for a ghost to fly.

* * *

That night she did not sleep well, which was itself an event, because Oscar Eriksen slept the way she did everything — completely, on schedule, as a discipline. She lay in her bunk listening to the vault breathe and turned the name over and over like a stone with something underneath it.

Because here was the thing she hadn't let herself think in the hub, in the daylight, in front of the bays: the name hadn't been *Andrade* the way the propaganda feeds said it. The feeds had used the full name, the martyr's name, *Vess Andrade*, over and over, until it stopped being a person. But the manifest used the registry format — initial, surname — *V. Andrade* — and registry format was what living people used, people who had to be certified, insured, accounted for. Dead martyrs got statues. Living passengers got paperwork.

Someone had done the paperwork.

And someone had routed it through the northern lanes to a dock at the end of the world, to a caretaker whose requisitions were small and whose silences were total — the one stop in the fractured hemisphere where no one would look twice at a freighter that wasn't supposed to exist.

Unless, said a colder voice in the dark, that was exactly what someone wanted her to think. Unless the manifest was bait, or a test, or the first move in a game whose board she couldn't see. The war had never officially ended. It had only run out of targets, the way a fire runs out of a forest, and the embers of it were out there somewhere in the dark lanes, still banking, still breathing.

The vault ticked and hummed. The ice outside the window went from black to the faintest grey of pre-dawn, and Oscar Eriksen, who had spent ten years being the most reliable woman in the hemisphere, lay awake and understood that her long route — the route she had flown alone for a decade, the route that was the last unbroken line between the old world and whatever came next — had just, without her permission, changed course.

Five days, she thought again. And then, because she was who she was, she got up, made tea, and went to check on the bays.

"You're all still here," she told them. "Nobody's left without me."

But for the first time in ten years, she wasn't entirely sure it was true — and for the first time in ten years, she wasn't entirely sure she wanted it to be.
