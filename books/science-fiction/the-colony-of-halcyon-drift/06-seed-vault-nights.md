## 6. Seed Vault Nights

The storm arrived the way all storms arrived at the vault: without ceremony, without mercy, and without any interest in what Oscar had planned for her week.

She felt it first in her teeth. That was the strange truth of living inside a mountain of concrete and lead on the coast of what had once been Norway — the solar weather announced itself not through instruments but through a faint metallic ache behind her molars, an old injury from a flare she'd flown through in her second year of service. By the time the array's warning chimed through the corridors, she was already standing at the kitchen window with her mug cooling in her hands, watching the aurora crawl down from the north like something alive.

"Storm?" Vess asked from the doorway.

Oscar had stopped being startled by how quietly the woman moved. Six days now since Dock Nine, six days since she'd found a ghost eating ration crackers in the dark of a dead ship's hold, and already the vault had rearranged itself around Vess Andrade the way water rearranges around a stone. There were two mugs drying by the sink now. Two jackets on the pegs. A second set of boots by the door, one laced, one not, because Vess still hadn't learned to be tidy in a home that wasn't hers.

"Class four," Oscar said. "Maybe five. Comms will be soup for two days. Array goes to hard shutdown in—" she checked the wall clock, an analog thing she'd salvaged because digital clocks died too easily, "—ninety minutes."

"And after that?"

"After that we wait it out. Nothing comes in, nothing goes out. The vault seals itself better than I seal it." She turned. "It's designed to survive without me. Sometimes I think that's the whole point of me — a redundancy the architects included out of politeness."

Vess came to stand beside her at the window. Outside, the sky was doing something obscene and beautiful, green light folding over green light above the black line of the sea, and the radiation that made it was already threading down toward them, patient as rot.

"You're not afraid of it," Vess said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I used to be. First storm, I sat in the core shelter with my knees against my chest and counted seconds for thirty hours." Oscar smiled into her mug. "Now it's just... weather. The vault doesn't care about my fear. The seeds don't either. Things grow or they don't, and the storm is only another season to them."

Vess was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had that careful flatness Oscar had learned to hear as armor. "My father used to say storms like this were when you could tell who your real officers were. Everyone brave in sunlight. Only a few brave in the dark."

"What are you in the dark?"

Vess looked at her then, and whatever answer lived behind her eyes stayed there. "Hungry," she said. "I'm always hungry in the dark."

* * *

They spent the ninety minutes before lockdown doing what Oscar privately called the walking of the wards — a full circuit of the germination bays, checking seals, topping reservoirs, logging temperatures by hand even though the system logged them automatically three times a second. The hand-logging was ritual, not necessity. Her predecessor had done it. His predecessor had done it. Somewhere in the archive there were forty years of handwritten bay logs in eleven different scripts, and Oscar found a stubborn comfort in adding her own small, mortal handwriting to the pile.

Tonight, for the first time, someone walked beside her.

"This is Bay Twelve," Oscar said, palming the lock. The door sighed open onto warmth and green. "Cold-adapted grains. Rye, barley, some wheat strains the old world bred for soil nobody farms anymore."

The bays were the heart of the vault, though the public record called the seed stores themselves the heart, and Oscar had long ago stopped correcting anyone. The stores were death — vacuum-sealed, frozen, waiting. The bays were life. Rows of hydroponic troughs under full-spectrum light, each one growing a living sample of everything sleeping downstairs, because a seed that has never been grown is only a rumor of a plant. Every strain in the deep archive passed through these bays once a decade, proving it could still wake up.

Vess stood in the doorway a moment, breathing it in. Oscar watched her do it — watched the tension go out of her shoulders by degrees, watched her hand rise almost unconsciously toward the nearest trough where a row of rye stood hip-high, its seed heads just beginning to fill.

"It smells like rain," Vess said. "I didn't know anything down here would smell like rain."

"That's the algae tanks. They off-gas when the humidity spikes. Storm pressure does it." Oscar stepped past her, running her fingers along the trough rim the way she did every night, feeling for vibration. "Everything else is filtered air and machine hum. But the tanks — the tanks smell like the world before."

"The world before," Vess repeated softly. "You say that like you remember it."

"I was nine when the Fracture started. I remember rain that wasn't acid. I remember my mother's garden." Oscar knelt to check the nutrient lines, mostly so she wouldn't have to see Vess's face while she said the next part. "I don't remember her face anymore. Just the garden. Isn't that a terrible trade? Keep the garden, lose the gardener."

"No," Vess said. "That's not terrible. That's just true."

* * *

Lockdown came at 21:40 local, announced by a sound Oscar had heard hundreds of times and never gotten used to: the great outer shutters rolling closed, floor to ceiling, concrete on concrete, until the vault became a sealed lung holding one breath. The lights dimmed to night-cycle amber. The array went dark. Somewhere far overhead, the sun was throwing fists at the magnetosphere, and here below, two women stood in a corridor that smelled faintly of rain and waited to find out if they were afraid.

"So what do you actually *do*," Vess said, "for thirty hours?"

Oscar had been dreading the question, because the honest answer was embarrassing. "I make bread," she said. "And I play cards against myself, badly. And I talk to the bays, which I'm telling you now so you can decide how worried to be about me."

"You talk to—"

"They respond better than people do." She was already walking, and Vess followed, because following had become what Vess did, tentatively, like a cat testing whether the house was safe. "Come on. If we're sealed in together for a day and a half, you're going to learn the nightly round. Fair warning: it's boring. Boring is the point. Boring means nothing's dying."

The round took them through the cold rooms first, where the frost breathed visibly in the amber light, then up through the germination decks, then into the workroom where Oscar kept the things that made the vault feel less like a tomb: a battered guitar with two dead strings, a shelf of paper books, a card table scarred by decades of elbows. And in the corner, squat and flour-dusted, the oven.

"You really bake," Vess said, and there was something in her voice Oscar couldn't name — surprise, maybe, or grief, or the particular ache of seeing domesticity in a place built for extinction.

"My instructor did. Old man Halvorsen, ran this place before me. He said a caretaker who can't feed herself is a caretaker waiting to fail." Oscar pulled the grain bin open, let Vess look at the rye berries inside, dark and glossy as river stones. "He also said bread is the oldest technology we have. Older than fire control, arguably. You take the dead seed of a grass and you convince it to become food. That's the whole job, Vess. Everything I do is a variation on that."

"Convincing dead things to live again."

"Something like that."

Vess reached into the bin and took a handful of berries, turning them over in her palm. In the amber light her hands looked older than the rest of her — a pilot's hands, rope-veined, with a burn scar across the left wrist that Oscar had noticed before and never asked about. "My father kept a garden too," she said. "Did I ever mention that? Before the fleet, before the war. He grew tomatoes on the station at Ceres. Real dirt, hauled up at ruinous expense. He said it kept him honest."

"What happened to it?"

"He paved it over for torpedo storage in the second year." She let the berries fall back into the bin, one by one. "I think about that sometimes. Which one he thought was keeping him honest."

Oscar didn't have an answer for that. Instead she measured flour, water, salt, starter — the starter itself older than both of them, fed continuously for sixty years, a sourdough culture that had survived the Fracture War in a jar on a shelf while nations burned. She set the dough to proof and dealt cards onto the scarred table, and taught Vess a game Halvorsen had invented called Vault, which was solitaire with a traitor card, and they played until the dough had risen and the aurora outside was invisible behind four meters of concrete and neither of them had mentioned the war for three hours, which was, Oscar realized somewhere around midnight, the longest peace either of them had known in years.

* * *

At 03:00 the storm peaked, and the vault sang.

That was the only word for it. The induction loops in the walls picked up the electromagnetic wash and translated it into a low harmonic drone, sub-audible mostly, but present, a note held at the edge of hearing like a monk sustaining a chant. Oscar had named it years ago, alone, embarrassed by her own naming. She called it the vault's voice.

Vess heard it during the second bread round, standing in Bay Seven among the legumes, and went very still.

"Do you hear that?"

"The storm. The walls pick it up." Oscar checked the moisture wicks along the soybean troughs. "Halvorsen swore it was the building settling. I checked the structural logs once — the building settles exactly zero during storms. It's the loops. But I like his explanation better, honestly. Buildings should settle. It's more honest than singing."

"It sounds like—" Vess paused, head tilted, and the amber light caught the planes of her face and made her look, briefly, like a figure in an old painting, some saint in a desert. "It sounds like a ship I served on. The *Corvane*. Her hull used to sing like that in heavy weather. Everyone hated it. I used to sleep in the aft compartment just to hear it."

"You slept better near the singing."

"I slept better knowing the singing meant the hull was holding." She turned to look at Oscar directly. "That's the same thing, isn't it? Your vault. The noise means it's holding."

"That's exactly the same thing." Oscar moved down the row, pressing her thumb into the growing medium, checking for hot spots. "People think the opposite. People think silence means safety. Silence just means you've stopped listening."

They finished the round in silence anyway, the good kind, the kind that doesn't need filling. And when they came back through Bay Twelve, Vess stopped at the rye again, and this time she didn't just touch it — she took up the pruning shears hanging by the trough and, glancing at Oscar for permission, snipped a single tillering stem gone leggy and weak.

"Thinning?" Oscar asked.

"You're supposed to thin, aren't you? Cut the weak ones so the strong ones—" Vess stopped. Looked at the cutting in her hand. "So the strong ones have room."

"That's the theory." Oscar took the stem from her, turned it in the light. "Halvorsen never liked it. Said we shouldn't get comfortable deciding which ones deserve the room. He kept a jar of the cuttings, tried to root them anyway. Half of them took." She nodded toward the windowsill, where a row of mismatched jars held straggling green shoots in cloudy water — survivors of the cull, roots pale and determined in the glass. "His jar. My jar now. Every storm, I add the thinnings. Most die. Some don't. I've stopped pretending I know which is which in advance."

Vess stared at the jars for a long time. Something moved across her face — Oscar saw it arrive and saw her decide not to say it, whatever it was, and filed that away with all the other unsaid things accumulating between them like sediment.

"Show me how to root one," Vess said finally.

So they did that, in the small hours of a sealed night, while the vault sang its one long note: two women bent over a jar of water, trimming a rye cutting at an angle, pinning it with a sliver of cork, setting it on the sill among its doomed and saved siblings. Vess wrote the date on the jar in grease pencil. Her handwriting was precise, military, beautiful.

"Jar forty-one," she said.

"You're numbering them now?"

"Halvorsen must have numbered them."

"He did. I dropped the habit. Too much arithmetic for a superstition."

"Then I'll keep the numbers." Vess set the pencil down with a small decisive click. "Someone should know how many things we've tried to save."

* * *

The storm broke on the second evening, and the shutters rolled back onto a sky scrubbed clean and violent blue, and comms returned in a flood of queued traffic that Oscar sorted with the mechanical patience of long practice. Supply requests. Weather bulletins. Fleet circulars she read the way one reads the labels on poison bottles.

Vess was asleep — actually asleep, not feigning, sprawled on the workroom cot with one arm hanging down and the guitar within reach, because sometime in the last hour she'd been trying, badly, to learn the three chords Oscar knew. Oscar let her sleep. She carried two mugs of coffee to the comm desk and began the queue.

Most of it was noise. Then, third from the top, a message flagged PRIORITY — FLEET AUDIT LIAISON, and Oscar's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

She opened it with hands that were perfectly steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything happening inside her.

*To: Caretaker Eriksen, Earth Seed Vault, Station Registry 114. From: Office of Fleet Audit, Combined Quartermaster Command. Advance notice of routine certification review, per Schedule 7-C. Inspector Dana Reyes, arriving via courier transfer, ETA 96 hours. Scope: manifest integrity, chain-of-custody records, bay logs, personnel files. Please ensure all records current and available for inspection.*

Routine. The word *routine* sat on the screen like a landmine dressed in a uniform.

Oscar looked at it for a long time. Behind her, Vess slept on, breath slow and even, one hand curled loosely around the neck of a guitar with two dead strings. On the windowsill, forty-one jars caught the returning sun, and in jar forty-one — impossibly, absurdly, after less than two days — a pale thread of root had already begun to reach down through the water.

Oscar drank her coffee. It had gone cold. Everything, she thought, goes cold while you're learning to hope.

Then she deleted the message from the local cache, opened the manifest archives, and began, for the first time in ten years of scrupulous honesty, to edit history.

Ninety-six hours.
