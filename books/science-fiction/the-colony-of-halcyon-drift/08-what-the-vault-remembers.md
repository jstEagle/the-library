## 8. What the Vault Remembers

The vault woke Vess before the alarms did.

Not with sound — Oscar had shown her how to silence the night-cycle chimes — but with a kind of pressure, a hum that lived in the deck plating and rose through the soles of her bare feet like a second pulse. Ten years of freighter bunks had taught her to sleep through engine noise, reactor wash, the groan of hulls flexing against vacuum. This was different. The vault didn't drone. It *breathed*. Somewhere below her, in the deep archive levels, ten thousand cryo-cradles cycled through their slow respiration, and the whole structure seemed to expand and contract around them, a lung of glass and steel buried under the regolith of a dead world.

She lay in the narrow guest bunk — Oscar's spare, smelling faintly of machine oil and dried lavender, because apparently even caretakers of extinction kept one small vanity — and listened to it breathe.

*You are alive,* the vault said. *Everything here is alive. Everything here is waiting.*

She had been aboard nine days. Nine days since she'd stood in the restored hold of her own death ship and begged a stranger for forty-eight hours, and the stranger had given her nine days instead, and neither of them had said aloud what was growing between those hours like something in the germination bays — patient, green, unstoppable.

Vess swung her legs out of the bunk and dressed in the dark. She'd learned the layout by touch now: the corridor to the galley, the turn at the hydroponics annex where Oscar grew tomatoes with an almost religious tenderness, the lift down to the working levels. But tonight she went the other way. Up.

The archive level sat at the crown of the vault, beneath the antenna array, and it was the one place Oscar had told her she could go freely. *"Records are meant to be read,"* she'd said, with the particular firmness of a woman who certified documents for a living. *"That's the whole point of them. If they're only read by one person, they're not records anymore. They're just memories."*

Vess had thought about that sentence for three days.

She keyed the archive door with the temporary code Oscar had given her — her name wasn't on any roster, couldn't be, would never be again — and stepped into the cold blue light of the reading room.

* * *

The pre-war logs lived in the oldest section, in cabinets of actual paper behind climate-sealed glass, because the founders of the vault had distrusted anything that could be edited. Vess ran her fingers along the cabinet labels in the dark: **ACCESSION LEDGERS 2087–2103**. **GERMINATION TRIALS**. **PROVENANCE RECORDS — EARTH ORIGIN STOCK**. Her breath fogged faintly in the chilled air. Dust motes hung in the light strips like suspended snow.

She hadn't come looking for anything. That was what she would tell herself later, and it would even be mostly true. She had come because she couldn't sleep, because the vault breathed and her father's voice still lived in her head — *you were made for this, Vess, you were made to matter* — and because there was a strange comfort in being alone among records no one had touched since the world broke.

She pulled the first ledger and carried it to the reading table, and the spine crackled like old ice.

The accession ledgers were beautiful, in the way that only obsolete things are beautiful. Hand-annotated columns. Seed lot numbers cross-referenced to deposit dates, origin coordinates, germination viability percentages updated in fading ink by generations of caretakers whose names she didn't know. Oscar's predecessors. A lineage of quiet people who had spent their lives keeping the future alive while the present burned.

Vess turned pages. 2091. 2094. Wheat from the Ukraine steppe. Rice from terraces that were now, presumably, underwater or ash. She let the names wash over her, a litany of places that existed now only as coordinates and seed counts, and she felt the grief she always felt — the low, ambient grief of the war generation, the one you stopped noticing the way you stop noticing gravity —

— until she reached the entry dated eleven days before the Perseus Incident.

Her hand stopped.

She knew the date. Everyone knew the date. It was carved into memorials on both fleets: the day the Andrade convoy was fired upon at Perseus Dock, the day the first shots of the Fracture War were fired, the day Admiral Elias Andrade lost his daughter's mother and swore the fleets would never again be trusted with humanity's future. Eleven days before that date, someone had withdrawn stock from this vault.

Vess read the entry twice. Three times. Then she set the ledger down very carefully, the way you set down something that might detonate, and went to find the terminal.

* * *

The digital mirror of the paper records was Oscar's domain, and Vess felt like a thief scrolling through it, though nothing here was locked. Deposit records. Withdrawal records. Chain-of-custody certifications, each one signed by a caretaker, each one countersigned by a fleet liaison — because before the war, the vault had belonged to everyone, and its records had required two hands to open.

Two signatures on every withdrawal. One from the vault. One from outside.

Vess found the withdrawal from eleven days before Perseus. Lot 4471-C. Origin: Meridian agricultural research station. Contents: blight-resistant wheat cultivars, experimental. Destination field: blank.

Blank. Every other withdrawal in forty years of records had a destination. Convoys logged destinations the way ships logged headings. This one was a hole in the shape of a shipment.

And the countersignature — the fleet liaison's authorization code — resolved, when she traced it through the old directory, to a command-level clearance in the Andrade convoy administration.

Her father's administration. Three years before the war. When he had been, officially, a logistics admiral with no ambitions beyond grain contracts.

Vess sat back in the chair. The reading room hummed around her. Somewhere far above, the antenna array ticked as it tracked a satellite that no longer answered.

It proved nothing, she told herself. A missing destination field was sloppiness, not conspiracy. Liaison codes got reused. Administrations were big; a signature proved a clerk, not an admiral.

But she had grown up in that administration. She knew how her father's office worked, the immaculate paper trails, the way every irregularity was smoothed flat within hours. Her father did not permit holes in records. Holes were how investigations started. Which meant this hole wasn't an oversight.

It was a message. Or it was a mistake so large that someone had died keeping it quiet.

She pulled more ledgers. She worked until her eyes burned, cross-referencing paper against terminal, terminal against paper, and the pattern assembled itself the way patterns do — slowly, then all at once, the way a constellation appears when you finally stop looking at individual stars.

Lot 4471-C was not unique. There were others. Withdrawals in the two years before the war, all experimental cultivars, all blight-resistant strains — the exact class of seed that thrived in the marginal soils of the outer colonies. All with blank destination fields. All countersigned with liaison codes that traced back, not to her father directly, but to a small cluster of officers in his logistics command. Officers whose names she knew. Officers who had, after the war began, all been promoted.

Someone had been moving war-grade agricultural stock out of the shared vault, off the books, into private hands. Stocking something. Preparing something.

And eleven days before Perseus Dock burned, the last shipment had gone out.

Vess stood up. Her legs were unsteady. She walked to the glass case where the original paper ledger lay open — she'd left it open, she realized, unable to put it back — and looked at the ink line recording the last withdrawal, written by a caretaker dead six years now, in handwriting that slanted slightly left, in ink that had faded to the color of old bruises.

*Eleven days,* she thought. *Whatever they built, whatever they planned, they finished stocking it eleven days before my father needed an incident.*

The thought arrived whole, and once it arrived she could not unthink it:

*Perseus wasn't an attack. Perseus was a delivery.*

* * *

She found Oscar at dawn in the germination bays, kneeling on the wet floor in front of Tray 88, doing something delicate with a pipette and a seedling no bigger than an eyelash. The bay lights were set to morning-spectrum, amber and rose, and the air smelled of growth medium and water and green things insisting on themselves. Oscar looked up, and whatever was in Vess's face made her set the pipette down immediately.

"You found something."

Not *good morning*. Not *you're up early*. Oscar Eriksen, who certified manifests for a living, who had spent ten years learning to read documents the way doctors read faces, took one look at her and knew.

"I need you to look at it," Vess said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "I need you to tell me I'm wrong. You're the one who reads records for a living. Tell me I'm seeing ghosts."

Oscar washed her hands, dried them, and walked with her without another word.

In the reading room, Vess laid it out the way she'd assembled it in her head all night: the withdrawals, the blank fields, the liaison codes, the promotions, the eleven days. She watched Oscar's face while she talked, and Oscar's face did what careful people's faces do — stayed composed, went pale at the edges, gave away nothing until the mind behind it had finished checking the math.

Then Oscar pulled up the certification templates on the terminal, the ones she used herself, and overlaid the old liaison signatures against the administrative handbooks archived from the pre-war fleet.

"The codes are real," Oscar said quietly. "That's the worst part. Whoever countersigned these used live authorization codes, current at the time. This isn't forgery, Vess. This is your father's own command structure signing its name to theft." She paused. "Or to preparation."

"Preparation," Vess repeated. The word tasted like copper.

"For a war that officially started by accident." Oscar turned in the chair to face her fully. "If these shipments went where I think they went — if someone was seeding emergency food caches along the border colonies before the first shot was ever fired — then the blockade isn't retaliation. It never was. It's *continuation*. Your father didn't respond to the war. He scheduled it."

The reading room was very cold and very bright and Vess stood in the middle of it and felt the ground reorganize itself beneath her feet.

All her life she had carried a specific grief, shaped like a specific story: her mother died at Perseus Dock because the other fleet fired first, because trust between the fleets was impossible, because humanity had simply failed, the way weather fails, the way accidents happen. Her father had built a fortress of that story and raised his daughter inside it and eventually she had fled it — but she had never doubted it. Doubting it would have meant her mother died for nothing worse than ambition.

Now the ledger lay open on the table with its faded ink and its blank destination fields, and the story came apart in her hands like wet paper.

"He killed her too," Vess said. She heard her own voice as if from a distance. "Maybe not with his own hands. But he stocked the board before the game started. Mom died at a dock he'd already loaded." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "He's spent six years telling both fleets that Perseus was murder. He's spent six years making *me* the martyr that proves it."

"And if the truth comes out," Oscar said, very gently, "the truce doesn't just collapse. It collapses in a direction. Right now both fleets hate each other evenly, and evenly is stable. But if one fleet learns the war was manufactured by the other side's founding hero—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to.

"—then it's not a ceasefire that breaks," Vess said. "It's a dam."

They stood there in the blue light, among the records of everything humanity had thought worth saving, and the silence between them filled with the weight of it. Vess thought about the inspectors Oscar had lied to. About the manifest ghost she herself had become. About how many small mercies were currently stacked on top of this enormous one, and how the whole tower would come down together.

"We could burn it," Vess said at last. "The paper. Wipe the terminal copies. No one would ever know I found it. The lie holds, the ceasefire holds, everyone goes on hating each other safely."

Oscar looked at her for a long moment. "Could you?"

"No," Vess said. "That's the problem. I've spent six years being a lie. I can't be one anymore. Not even a useful one." She laughed, and it came out broken. "My father taught me that records are power. He just never imagined I'd learn it from his own paperwork."

Oscar crossed the room and took her hands. Her grip was work-worn and steady and warm, and Vess held onto it like a rail in a storm.

"Then we don't decide tonight," Oscar said. "We copy everything. Paper, terminal, all of it. We make the truth safe before we make it loud." She squeezed. "And Vess — whatever we choose, we choose it awake."

* * *

They worked through the morning. Scanners hummed. Oscar showed her how to hash the files so no one could alter them without detection, how to split the archive across three storage media, how to hide one copy inside the vault's own maintenance telemetry where no auditor would think to look. Two women building a small, careful bomb out of paper and patience.

It was nearly midday when the console in the corner chimed — the external array, flagging incoming traffic. Oscar glanced over, frowning, and Vess watched her open the log and go very still.

"What?" Vess asked. "What is it?"

Oscar turned the screen toward her. On it, a signal record scrolled in plain text: a distress ping, automated, repeating. Its origin code resolved to a relay buoy near the Perseus system.

Near the ruins of Perseus Dock. Near the wreck of the Meridian.

Six years silent, and now, suddenly, singing.

"It's addressed to this array," Oscar said slowly. "By name. Someone out there knows exactly where we are."
