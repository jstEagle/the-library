## 13. Orders from a Dead Ship

The message came in at 0347, ship's time, and Oscar was awake to see it because she had stopped sleeping properly weeks ago.

She sat in the Meridian's cramped comm alcove with a mug of barley tea gone cold beside her, watching the array feed scroll its usual midnight traffic: beacon pings, weather satellites, the slow arithmetic of orbital decay. Vess was asleep in the bunk behind her — actually asleep, for once, her breathing even and unguarded in a way Oscar had learned to treasure like a rare harvest. And then the console chimed, soft and polite, the way it did when something arrived on a priority channel.

Priority channels were for fleet traffic. Priority channels were never for her.

Oscar set down the tea. Her hands were steady, but only because she made them be — a trick she'd taught herself during the Fracture War years, when supply manifests carried casualty lists and she'd learned that trembling hands spilled things. She opened the message.

It was addressed to both fleets simultaneously. She could see the routing headers, the dual distribution, the way it had been flung out into the dark like a stone into still water. The sender field read:

**MERIDIAN — PERSEUS DOCK SURVIVOR COMMAND**

Oscar read it three times before the words stopped sliding around.

*To all units of the Halcyon Compact and the Perseus Concordance: The vessel Meridian, listed destroyed six years past, remains operational under survivor authority. We hold verified records pertaining to the origin of hostilities. These records will be released to whichever fleet secures the neutral station Halcyon Drift within nine days. Failure to comply will result in full disclosure to all parties without preference.*

There was more — authentication codes, fleet cipher fragments, a signature block that made Oscar's stomach drop through the deck plating. The signature read *V. Andrade, Commanding.*

Behind her, the bunk creaked. "What is it?" Vess's voice, thick with sleep, then sharpening as she saw Oscar's face. "Oscar. What is it?"

Oscar turned the screen. Vess read it standing, barefoot on cold decking, one hand braced against the bulkhead. Oscar watched her face go through it — confusion first, then recognition, then something colder and older than either.

"That's my handwriting," Vess said quietly. "Or close enough to pass. That's my old command signature. I haven't used it since—" She stopped. "Since I died."

"Someone has your codes."

"Someone has *everything.* My cipher keys, my voiceprint hash, the Meridian's transponder history, the exact wording of the Perseus Dock incident report." Vess laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Do you know how much access you need to forge something this clean? This isn't a pirate's forgery. This is institutional."

Oscar pulled up the second message, which had arrived ninety seconds after the first — a mirror order, same timestamp, sent to the other fleet with one crucial difference. Where the Compact version promised the records to whoever seized Halcyon Drift, the Concordance version inverted it: the records would go to whoever *defended* the station against Compact aggression. Each fleet had been told the other was coming to take something. Each fleet had been told they had nine days.

"They're not being offered anything," Oscar said slowly. "They're being aimed."

Vess sank onto the bench beside her. In the low light of the console her face looked carved, all the softness of the vault nights burned away. "My father," she said. "This has his fingerprints. Escalation by ambiguity. He used to do this with border incidents — give both sides a reason to move first, then let momentum do the killing."

"But would he forge his own daughter's death?" Oscar asked. "Would he use you?"

"I don't know anymore." Vess's jaw tightened. "That's the worst part. Six years ago I'd have said no. But I've been dead six years, and dead daughters are useful in ways living ones aren't. A martyr can sign anything."

* * *

By dawn they had not slept, and the array had given them more to chew on.

Both fleets had acknowledged receipt. Both fleets had begun moving. Oscar watched it happen in real time on the traffic display — convoy formations assembling out of scattered patrol routes, fuel tenders rerouting, the patient bureaucratic machinery of war waking up and stretching. Two fleets that had spent six years glaring at each other across a demilitarized line were now, on the strength of a single forged transmission, converging on a neutral station neither could afford to let the other hold.

"It's beautiful, in a horrible way," Vess murmured. She stood at the display with her arms crossed, watching the vector lines crawl toward convergence like veins filling. "No shots fired yet. No violations. Just... positioning. Everyone convinced they're responding to the other side's aggression. By the time anyone fires, it'll be self-defense all the way down."

"The truce protocols," Oscar said. "The ones built on the manifests I certify—"

"Are about to be tested by an order from a ship I'm supposed to have burned on." Vess turned to look at her. "You understand what this means for us. If either fleet boards the Meridian — if anyone runs our transponder against the death registry — we're not fugitives anymore. We're evidence. Or we're a weapon, depending on who finds us first."

Oscar thought about Inspector Reyes, with her courteous eyes and her notebook of discrepancies. She thought about Ilsa Ren, somewhere out there with her courier's smile and her paid-for silence, and wondered what Ilsa was making of the same traffic. If Ilsa was even still silent. The bargain had bought them weeks; bargains like that didn't buy forever.

"There's another possibility," Oscar said. "One we keep avoiding."

Vess waited.

"Whoever wrote this knows you're alive." Oscar kept her voice level. "Not suspects it. *Knows.* They have your codes, your history, your father's playbook. And instead of exposing you directly — instead of just telling the fleets 'the admiral's daughter lives' — they've chosen to weaponize the ghost. Why? What does that shape of attack accomplish that simple exposure doesn't?"

Vess was quiet for a long moment. Outside the viewport, Earth turned, blue and indifferent, the terminator line crawling across the Pacific.

"Because exposure ends the lie," she said finally. "And ending the lie too fast might end the truce with it — but chaotically, unpredictably, in ways even the forger couldn't control. But *this*..." She gestured at the converging vectors. "This gives everyone a script. This makes the escalation *legible*. Fleets respond to legible threats. Someone wants them at Halcyon Drift, both of them, armed, in nine days. Someone wants a stage."

"And what happens on the stage?"

"I don't know." Vess's voice dropped. "But my father always said wars aren't lost in the field. They're lost in the room where the terms get written. If someone's building a stage at Halcyon Drift, they intend to be the one holding the pen."

* * *

They spent the day doing the only thing that made sense: trying to think like their enemy.

Oscar pulled every archived fragment of the original Perseus Dock incident report — the true one, the one only her terminal seemed able to show — and laid it alongside the forgery. The styles matched. Not perfectly; the forger had aged the metadata convincingly but had gotten two small things wrong, details so minor no auditor would ever catch them unless they already knew what to look for. The incident report had used the old Concordance date convention. The forgery used the Compact's. And the survivor-command designation referenced a dock section number that hadn't existed until four months *after* the fire.

"Whoever built this had the archives," Oscar said. "Deep access. Post-war access. They studied the record the way I study seed viability curves."

"Which narrows it," Vess said. "How many people alive can read those archives at that depth? Fleet intelligence on both sides. The armistice commission. And—" she paused, and Oscar watched her arrive at the name she didn't want, "—my father's office."

"You keep assuming it's him."

"Because if it isn't him, it's someone using his methods, which might be worse. It means someone learned from him. Someone inside the machinery decided the peace was a problem to be solved." Vess pressed her palms flat against the console. "Oscar, listen to me. There are people on both sides who have built their entire post-war power on the story of Perseus Dock. Martyrs need enemies. Blockades need justification. If the real record ever surfaces — the record showing the incident was staged — half the command structure of both fleets loses its reason to exist. Forging orders from a dead ship to force a final confrontation? That's not desperation. That's a man burning his own house down so no one can read the deed."

Oscar stared at the traffic display. Nine days. The vectors converging. Somewhere out there, thousands of crew people — loaders, pilots, medics, cooks, the vast quiet majority who never chose any of it — were getting their deployment orders and kissing whatever they kissed goodbye, all because of words written in a dead woman's name.

"We can't stop the fleets," she said. "We can't recall the orders. We can't prove the forgery without exposing ourselves, and exposing ourselves now — mid-escalation — might be exactly what the forger wants. A third actor appearing out of nowhere, muddying the water further."

"So what do we do?"

Oscar looked at her. At this woman she had pulled half-starved out of a restored hold, this woman whose germination-bay rituals had become the architecture of Oscar's days, this woman whose hand she had held through a solar storm while the vault hummed around them like a cathedral. Ten years alone had taught her how to survive anything except company. Now the company was asking her to help steer toward a storm.

"We stop trying to stay invisible," Oscar said. "We've been playing defense since Dock Nine. Hiding, certifying, lying to inspectors, buying silence. Defense only works until someone else decides the tempo. Someone just decided the tempo." She tapped the forged order on the screen. "So we take it back. If they want a stage at Halcyon Drift, we make sure we're on it — with the real record, the raw logs, the truth about Perseus Dock. Not as ghosts. As witnesses."

"That's suicide."

"It's also the only move that doesn't leave the forger holding the pen." Oscar felt something settle in her chest as she said it — not courage exactly, but the end of a long argument with herself. "I've spent ten years keeping things alive in the dark. Seeds, records, a truce built on paper I signed. I know how to keep things alive. Maybe it's time I learned how to bring something into the light."

Vess looked at her for a long time. Then she reached over and took Oscar's hand — the calloused one, the one that had certified ten thousand lies and planted ten thousand seeds — and held it the way she'd held it during the storm.

"Nine days," Vess said softly. "Then we fly a dead ship into a summit of the living."

Before Oscar could answer, the console chimed again.

A new message. Single recipient this time. Addressed not to either fleet, but to the Meridian itself — to the ghost frequency, the dead channel, the address that existed in no registry Oscar had ever maintained.

The sender field was blank. The body was one line:

***We know you're reading this. Bring the logs to Drift, or we tell both fleets where you are.***

Oscar's blood went cold. Vess's grip on her hand tightened until it hurt.

Someone wasn't just aiming the fleets anymore.

Someone was aiming at *them*.
