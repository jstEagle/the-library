## 16. The Run to Halcyon

The Meridian had not flown in six years, and she flew like a woman waking from a long illness — every joint complaining, every system remembering itself one reluctant muscle at a time.

Oscar felt it through the deck plates before she saw it on any readout: the deep, arrhythmic shudder of a drive that had been cold too long. She sat in the pilot's cradle with her hands hovering over controls that were older than her career, older than the war, and let the ship's hesitation become her own. Around her, the cockpit smelled of ozone and old insulation, of dust heated for the first time in years, of something faintly sweet that might have been the adhesive of decades-old labels peeling off cargo crates in the hold behind her.

"Drive plume is stable," Vess said from the navigator's station, her voice carefully level. "Ninety-one percent nominal. Don't push past two-thirds burn until we clear the Perseus shadow."

"I wasn't going to push."

"You were thinking about it."

Oscar almost smiled. "I was thinking about it."

They had burned out of Earth's approach corridor at 0400 local vault time, sliding away from the seed vault's little constellation of lights like a secret leaving a body. No clearance codes. No flight plan filed under any name that existed in fleet registries. The Meridian's transponder broadcast the only thing it could truthfully say: *I am dead.* Six years of death, stamped and certified and countersigned by Oscar herself, back when certification had been the simplest work in the solar system.

That was the whole architecture of the plan, and both of them knew how thin it was. A ghost ship does not get hailed; it gets ignored, or it gets destroyed on sight. There was no middle ground, no procedure for a ship that was officially ash but currently accelerating toward the most heavily guarded neutral station in the drift. The blockade around Halcyon Drift had been drawn tight over nine days of forged orders, both fleets converging on false intelligence that someone — Ilsa's handlers, the admiral's people, whoever sat behind the curtain — had woven specifically to bring everything to a head at the summit.

Which meant the only way through was to be exactly what the records said they were: nothing. Wreckage. A rumor with a drive signature.

"Coming up on the first patrol lane," Vess said. "Kill running lights."

Oscar touched the switch and the cockpit went dark except for instruments dimmed to embers. Outside the forward port, space was the black it always was, but denser now, textured — the Meridian was crossing the outer shell of the blockade zone, where patrol ships flew patterns designed to catch anything that moved like it had somewhere to be.

"We're going to drift through," Vess said. It wasn't quite a question.

"We're going to drift through." Oscar eased the throttle to nearly nothing. The drive note dropped to a hum she felt in her teeth rather than heard. "Two hours of pretending to be debris. Try to look inert."

"I've been practicing my whole life."

The joke landed soft between them, and neither of them laughed, because it was true, and they both knew it was true, and there was no time to hold it properly. Later. Oscar was building a small treasury of laters: later they would talk about the kiss among the seedlings; later they would decide what came after the summit if they survived it; later, maybe, there would be a morning that didn't begin with a countdown. For now there was only the next ninety minutes, and then the ninety after those.

* * *

Drifting through a military blockade in a resurrected corpse of a ship turned out to be an exercise in exquisite boredom shot through with moments of pure animal terror.

For the first hour, nothing. The stars wheeled with glacial patience. Vess ran passive sweeps every four minutes, her finger tapping the console edge between scans in a rhythm Oscar slowly recognized as the tempo of a song — some old civilian ballad, pre-war, the kind of thing that would have played in dockside bars on Perseus before the docks burned. Vess never sang it. She just kept time with it, like a metronome keeping a heart alive.

At hour one and twelve minutes, the terror arrived.

"Contact. Bearing three-seven mark one-two, closing slow." Vess's voice went flat, professional, the voice of someone who had grown up on bridge decks watching her father's officers speak exactly this way. "Frigate class. Corvid pattern — that's his fleet."

"Range?"

"Forty thousand klicks and shrinking. He's sweeping the lane." A pause. Four seconds of it, which in the dark of a cockpit felt like four years. "He'll paint us in about six minutes if we stay on this vector."

Oscar looked at the throttle. Two-thirds burn would take them out of the sweep cone in ninety seconds — and announce them to every listening ear in the blockade zone. Staying cold meant trusting the frigate's sensors to dismiss them.

"Options," she said.

"One: we run, and we're identified inside a minute. Two: we stay dead, and we bet a crew of frightened conscripts won't look twice at junk." Vess's finger had stopped tapping. "Three: we answer a hail we haven't received yet, as something else. Derelict tender. Salvage tug. Anything with a reason to be here."

"Can you fake a registry?"

"I grew up forging my own death certificate." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile in the ember-light. "A salvage tag is beneath me."

The frigate grew on the passive display like a bruise. Oscar watched its icon crawl across the plot and thought about the seed vault, about the ten thousand rows of sleeping green she'd left under automatic care, about how she had spent a decade being careful and was now, by every measure that mattered, the least careful person in the solar system. Flying a dead ship through a live blockade toward a summit where the truth would either save two fleets or start the worst war in human history. Her certification revoked in advance. Her whole quiet life spent.

And underneath the fear, unmistakable, unwelcome, undeniable: exhilaration. The old animal joy of flight, of risk, of a machine answering her hands. She hadn't felt it since the Fracture War ended and flying became supply runs between colonies that wouldn't answer their comms. She hated herself a little for feeling it. She let herself feel it anyway.

"Hail coming," Vess said quietly. "Standard challenge format. They want identification."

"Give them a derelict. Old hull, Perseus yard number, decommissioned before the war. Something so boring their watch officer files it without reading it."

Vess's fingers moved. On the comm channel, a voice assembled itself from static and fabrication — a tired salvage master, running a dead-hull recovery contract, requesting lane passage, attaching credentials that dissolved into plausible corruption halfway through transmission. The kind of paperwork nobody checks because checking it means work.

Silence. Thirty seconds. Forty.

"They're buying it," Vess breathed. "Watch officer's flagging us as low priority. They're... yes. They're vectoring off to chase a contact on the far side of the lane." She exhaled, long and shaking. "Some other poor bastard is having a worse night than we are."

"Some other poor bastard is probably empty vacuum," Oscar said grimly. "A sensor ghost. That's what these lanes are full of now — everyone chasing each other's ghosts."

Vess was quiet for a moment. Then: "Like us."

"Not like us. We're real. That's the whole point of all of this." Oscar reached across the dark gap between their stations and found Vess's hand, closed her fingers around it once, firmly, and let go. "We're the realest things in this system right now. Everyone else is flying on fiction."

* * *

They made the second patrol lane at the worst possible hour of the mission: the hour when exhaustion begins speaking in a persuasive voice.

Oscar had been awake for thirty-one hours. Vess longer — she'd spent the previous night recalibrating the Meridian's navigation array by hand, coaxing accuracy out of gyroscopes that had drifted six years' worth of error into their bearings. Now she sat at her station with her chin propped on her fist, eyes half-closed, correcting course in increments so small the autopilot wouldn't have bothered.

"Sleep rotation," Oscar said. "Four hours each. I'll take first watch."

"If I sleep, I dream about the summit hall." Vess said it plainly, the way she'd learned to say terrible things during their weeks together — flat, unadorned, offered like evidence. "I dream about standing up in front of both delegations and my father's face doing something I've never seen it do. So I'd rather sit here and be tired."

Oscar considered arguing. She was good at arguing; a decade alone makes a person fluent in every side of every conversation. But she remembered the cryo-bay during the inspection, the forty minutes Vess had spent sealed in the dark among the vault's spare embryo canisters while Inspector Reyes' footsteps crossed the deck above her head, and she understood that Vess had already done her time in the dark. Sleep was not rest for her yet. Sleep was another room where the war waited.

So instead Oscar did the thing she was actually good at, the thing the vault had taught her across ten thousand solitary nights: she made the waiting habitable. She brewed tea on the Meridian's ancient galley plate — real tea, hoarded from her own stores, the last of a batch she'd traded for at a silent colony two years ago — and she brought two cups to the cockpit and set one beside Vess's elbow without ceremony.

Vess wrapped both hands around it and held it against her sternum like something rescued. "You know what I used to think caretakers were?" she said. "Before you. Retired people. People whose wars were over."

"And now?"

"Now I think you're the only ones still fighting for anything worth having." She drank. Closed her eyes briefly. "Everything else out here is fleets arguing over who owns the ashes."

"The ashes are where the seeds are," Oscar said. "That's the whole argument, isn't it? Both fleets think they're protecting the future. Neither of them has planted anything in six years."

Vess looked at her for a long moment — the kind of look that, a month ago, would have frightened Oscar with its intensity and now simply steadied her, the way a fixed star steadies a navigator. "Say that again at the summit," Vess said. "Word for word. When I freeze, when I lose the thread, when he looks at me — say exactly that."

"You won't freeze."

"I might."

"Then I'll say it." Oscar turned back to the plot. "But you won't. I've watched you walk into rooms full of things that should have killed you — Reyes, Ilsa, your own name. You don't freeze. You get quieter, and then you get dangerous."

Ahead of them, the second lane lay open. Behind them, the frigate's wake faded into noise. Between the two fleets' patrol shells there was a corridor of nothing — sixty thousand kilometers of vacuum where neither side flew because neither side trusted the other enough to fly there. The demilitarized seam. The scar tissue of the truce.

They crossed it in silence, two women and a dead ship moving through the one place in the system where the war had agreed to stop pretending, and Oscar thought: *this is what peace actually looks like from inside. Not a treaty. An emptiness everyone is too afraid to fill.*

In nine days — no, in less than two days now — they intended to fill it with the truth.

* * *

The near-miss, when it finally came, came without warning, which was the nature of near-misses.

Vess had just taken the watch. Oscar had been asleep for perhaps ninety minutes in the bunk behind the cockpit, deep enough into exhaustion's gravity well that dreams had started — ordinary dreams, absurdly, dreams of germination bays and watering schedules — when the ship's hull rang.

Not an alarm. A sound. A physical sound transmitted through structure: the high harmonic whine of a powerful drive passing close, close enough that its exhaust wash pressed against the Meridian's flank like a hand testing a door.

She was out of the bunk and in her crash harness before her mind fully caught up, and the cockpit was already flooded with light — hard white searchlight raking across the forward ports, sweeping the hull, painting Vess's face in strobing slashes of shadow.

"Patrol cutter," Vess said, fast and low. "Unmarked. It came out of the seam — our side of the seam — running dark until it wasn't. It's circling us."

"How close?"

"Close enough that I can see rivets." Vess's hands were moving, killing systems, dropping the Meridian back into its coffin posture. "It's not hailing. It's just looking."

Through the forward port, the cutter resolved: a lean predatory shape, weapon housings bare, running lights blazing now in deliberate arrogance. It rolled lazily along the Meridian's length, and Oscar understood with cold clarity what she was seeing. Not a routine patrol. A hunter, checking a specific carcass. Someone had told them to look for the Meridian — or someone had told them to look for anything flying the seam, and the Meridian was the only anything there was.

"It knows the registry," Vess whispered. "It has to. Why else come all the way out here?"

"Then why hasn't it fired?"

"Because firing first, at the daughter of Admiral Andrade, inside the truce seam—" Vess stopped. Oscar watched her arrive at the same conclusion from the other direction. "Because someone wants us taken alive. Or wants whatever we're carrying intact. This isn't a patrol. It's a collection."

The searchlight swept the cockpit again. Oscar flattened herself below the sill of the port, dragging Vess down with her, and in the dark under the instrument panel they crouched together, shoulder to shoulder, breathing the recycled air of a dead ship while a living one circled them like a shark testing whether the meat was worth the trouble.

"Play dead harder," Oscar murmured. "Full power-down. Batteries to minimal. If we look like a hull drifting on momentum—"

"Momentum we don't have. We were under burn when it found us." Vess's jaw tightened. "Our plume is on its logs. Dead ships don't leave plumes."

"Then we have minutes, not hours." Oscar's mind ran the arithmetic of desperation. Run: identified, chased, destroyed or boarded. Fight: a survey ship against a gunship, suicide with extra steps. Surrender: the summit dies, the evidence dies, Vess goes home to her father in a cage or a shrine.

Or — the thought arrived whole, unbidden, the way the best and worst ideas always did —

Or the Meridian could do the one thing no one would ever expect a dead ship to do.

"Dive," Oscar said. "Toward Halcyon. Full burn, straight down the seam, directly at the station's traffic control envelope. The moment we cross into station jurisdiction, their weapons are locked out by truce protocol. Every gun in this system goes safe inside the Drift's defensive radius. Even theirs. *Especially* theirs."

"That's insane. We'd be announcing ourselves to both fleets—"

"Yes. Both fleets will see a ghost ship make a final run at the neutral station, and both fleets will spend the next six hours arguing about what it *means*, and by the time anyone agrees, we'll be inside the envelope drinking bad coffee with the harbor authority." She was already strapping into the pilot's cradle, hands finding the throttle by touch. "Vess. The whole plan was to arrive as a lie. That plan is dead. So we arrive as a *story*. Nobody shoots down a mystery in front of the whole summit. It's the one camouflage left to us."

Vess stared at her for one full second — Oscar counted it, that second, the longest of the night — and then she was in her own harness, fingers flying across the navigation console, plotting the dive.

"Burn solution ready. Three minutes to station envelope at maximum thrust. The Meridian hasn't pulled max thrust since—" 

"Since before she died. She remembers." Oscar fed power to the drive and felt the old ship gather herself beneath them, engines coughing, clearing their throat, waking all the way up for the first time in six years. "Come on, girl. One more run. You've been patient long enough."

The cutter's searchlight swung wide, realizing — a half-second too late — what was happening. On the comm, a channel cracked open at last, a voice sharp with alarm: *unidentified vessel, heave to, heave to or—*

Oscar pushed the throttle to the stop.

The Meridian leapt.

It was not graceful. Six years of cold had left grit in every bearing, and the old freighter climbed into full burn with a shriek of metal finding its purpose again, pinning them into their harnesses, pressing tears sideways from the corners of Oscar's eyes. Alarms she'd never heard before woke up and began screaming in chorus. Somewhere in the hold, a cargo strap snapped loose and whipped against a bulkhead. The ship complained about all of it, loudly, and flew anyway.

And God help her, Oscar laughed. Out loud, wild and helpless, the laugh of a woman who had been careful for ten years and was done. Beside her Vess called out ranges and bearings in a clear strong voice, threading them down the seam's narrow safe channel, and outside the ports the stars stretched and the cutter fell away behind them, shrinking, its weapons locked out by distance and law and the sheer audacity of the dead refusing to stay buried.

"Envelope in ninety seconds!" Vess shouted over the roar. "Station control is hailing us — everyone is hailing us — both fleets are demanding—"

"Let them demand!"

"Oscar." Vess's hand found hers on the throttle, gripped it, gripped *her*. "Whatever happens when we drop out of burn — whatever's waiting in that traffic pattern — thank you. For the vault. For the lie. For this."

Oscar tore her eyes from the plot for exactly one heartbeat. In the red wash of the alarm lights, Vess Andrade — admiral's daughter, ghost, martyr, liar, the bravest person Oscar had ever known — was smiling like a woman coming home.

"Fifty seconds. Forty. Brace."

The Meridian crossed the station's defensive envelope at maximum thrust, trailing six years of silence behind her like a burning veil, and all across the Halcyon Drift, on every bridge and every delegation floor and every crowded observation deck of the summit station, alarms began to ring.

On the main plot, the traffic controller's voice came through cracked with disbelief:

*"Perseus Control to vessel... Meridian. Your registry shows you destroyed six years ago with all hands. State your intentions. State your crew. Over."*

Oscar keyed the comm, looked at Vess, and found her nodding.

"This is the Meridian," she said. "We have survivors."
