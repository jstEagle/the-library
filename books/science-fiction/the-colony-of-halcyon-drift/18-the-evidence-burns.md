## 18. The Evidence Burns

The cache lived in three places, because Oscar had learned in ten years of solitude that nothing should live in only one.

One copy rode the Meridian's hold computer, buried beneath a decade of germination telemetry where no auditor would think to look. One copy sat in a shielded drive strapped to her own spine, cold against her skin like a second vertebra. And the third — the master, the one Vess had spent nine days annotating and cross-referencing until it read less like evidence and more like testimony — waited in a dead-drop locker on Halcyon Drift's Ring Three, behind a noodle stall that smelled of scorched ginger and recycled steam.

Nine days had become four. The summit convened tomorrow at 0900 station time, and the concourses of the Drift had filled with the pageantry of two fleets pretending they weren't pointing everything they had at each other. Delegates in charcoal and slate-blue moved through the rings in tight clusters, trailed by aides carrying tablets they never set down. Marines from both sides stood at intersections with the studied indifference of people who had been told not to look at the other side's marines, and were therefore looking at nothing else.

Oscar walked the ring with Vess on her arm, and to anyone watching they were exactly what they appeared to be: a vault caretaker and her partner, off-world for the first time in years, gawking at the bunting.

"Third locker on the left," Vess murmured, lips barely moving. "Past the stall."

"I see it."

"I know you see it. I'm telling you so you'll stop staring at it like it owes you money."

Oscar exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh. Four days ago she would have said fear made her clumsy. She was learning instead that fear, properly worn, could be mistaken for composure — that terror and calm used the same muscles. Her heart hammered at parade rest while her face did its work.

They passed the stall. The locker sat flush in the wall, matte grey, unremarkable, its status light a patient green. Untouched. Oscar let her hand trail along the panel as they went by, feeling the faint warmth of its electronics, and kept walking without breaking stride.

"Green," she said, when they'd put thirty meters between themselves and it.

"Green is good."

"Green means no one's found it."

"Green means the light works." But Vess squeezed her arm anyway, and Oscar felt the tremor in her that she was too disciplined to show anywhere else. "We collect it tonight, after the delegations' welcome reception. Both copies go out simultaneously — yours to the Concord delegation suite, mine to the Assembly. Same minute. If one gets intercepted, the other still lands."

"And if both get intercepted?"

Vess was quiet for three steps. "Then we're out of ideas," she said, "and I'm out of reasons to stay hidden."

Oscar didn't answer. She had heard that note in Vess's voice before — twice now, once over the seed logs and once the night after Reyes nearly found her in the cryo-bay — and she had learned it wasn't despair. It was something being forged. She hated how much she admired it.

They turned down toward the residential ring, into the smell of hot metal and lichen-farm humidity, and neither of them saw the woman in courier grey who fell into step forty meters behind them, or notice that she peeled away at the junction without ever having looked at them at all.

* * *

Ilsa Ren had taught them, in the weeks aboard the vault, that trust was a thing you built in small increments — a shared watch rotation, a cup of tea left steaming outside a cryo-bay, a story told at 0300 about a brother lost at Perseus Dock. She had taught them that a survivor carries her dead with her, and that grief makes a person honest. She had been generous with all of it.

She had never once told them which side her brother had died fighting for.

That omission came back to Oscar at 2140 station time, standing in the shadow of a coolant conduit on Ring Two, watching the noodle stall below through a maintenance grate, watching a woman in courier grey hand something to the stall's owner and walk on — and watching the owner, sixty seconds later, key open the dead-drop locker with a code he should not have possessed.

"No," Oscar said aloud, to no one. "No, no—"

The locker's light went amber. Then red. Then dark.

Her comm crackled. Vess's voice, low and fast: "Oscar. The Meridian just pinged me. Hold computer's been accessed remotely — someone's pulling the archive. I'm locking it down but they're inside my—"

"Ilsa." The name tasted like copper. "It's Ilsa. She's at the drop. She has the master."

Silence on the line. In it, Oscar could hear the whole architecture of their nine days collapsing — the annotated logs, the timestamps, the chain of custody Vess had built so carefully that any tribunal would have wept with gratitude. All of it flowing out through a courier's hands into whatever channel fed the admiral.

"Kill the spine copy," Vess said at last, and her voice had gone very flat, very calm, the voice of a woman raised in a war room. "Wipe it. Now. Before she traces your biometrics."

"It's air-gapped—"

"She taught us air-gaps are a courtesy. Wipe it."

Oscar reached back, thumbed the drive free of its port, and held it in her palm — a sliver of metal warm from her own body, holding the only clean copy of the truth left in the universe. For one absurd second she thought about running. Taking the drive, taking the Meridian, taking Vess, and going so far out along the long route that the war would be a rumor again, the way it had been for ten beautiful, terrible years.

Then she thought of the colonies gone silent. Of the blockade lines drawn around worlds that had done nothing. Of the seed rows in her vault, waiting in the dark for a future nobody was building.

She crushed the drive under her heel until the casing split, then ground the fragments into the deck grating, small enough to scatter, small enough to be nothing.

"Done," she said. "Vess. We still have the hold copy if the lockdown holds."

"The lockdown isn't going to hold." A breath. "Oscar — she didn't come for the data. Not really. Data can be reassembled; she knows that better than anyone. She came to confirm what we have and how much of it survives. Which means somewhere on this station, right now, there's a man who already knows his daughter is alive."

* * *

They met in the Meridian's restored hold, because it was the only place left that felt like theirs — the old freighter that had died at Perseus Dock and been resurrected as a lie, its cargo cradles empty, its air smelling of sealant and old coffee. Vess sat on the deck beneath the main display, knees drawn up, the way she had sat the first night Oscar found her, half-starved and furious at being alive.

The display showed the hold archive's status: LOCKDOWN ACTIVE — INTEGRITY 61% AND FALLING.

"They're peeling it," Vess said. "Layer by layer. Whoever's on the other end is good. Better than me." She laughed, a small broken sound. "Probably trained by my father's people. Everything good is, isn't it? That's the joke of my whole life."

Oscar sat down beside her. Didn't touch her yet. Some griefs need a perimeter first.

"We broadcast raw," she said. "No annotations, no curation. Just dump the remaining archive to open channels before it's gone. Let both fleets see the same garbage-fire of unverified files at the same time. It's messy, but—"

"It's not evidence, it's noise. My father's people will drown it in an hour. They'll call it a forgery, and they'll be half right, because anything stripped of provenance *is* a forgery." Vess pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Provenance. That's the whole game, Oscar. That's why I spent nine days on the annotations. Raw logs are weather. Testimony is a weapon. And we just lost the testimony."

The integrity counter ticked to 54%.

Oscar looked at it, and looked at Vess, and felt something in her chest rearrange itself — the same sensation she'd had at the vault, years ago, when a bay failure had killed four hundred seed lines overnight and she'd understood, sitting alone in the red emergency light, that some losses cannot be prevented, only answered.

"Then we don't need the data," she said slowly.

Vess lowered her hands. "What?"

"The data was never the proof. You were. You've been saying it for days — the admiral's whole position rests on your martyrdom. The blockade, the truce terms, the memorial fleet named after you. Take the martyrdom away and there's nothing underneath. No document proves that better than you do. You *are* the manifest ghost, Vess. You always were."

"You want me to walk into that hall." Vess's voice was very quiet. "Tomorrow. In front of both delegations. In front of him."

"I want—" Oscar stopped. Started again, because lying to Vess had become impossible somewhere in the last four days, and lying to herself was close behind. "I want you on the Meridian, outbound, safe, and I hate that wanting it doesn't make it right. Yes. I want you to walk in. Because it's the only door left, and because you've been walking toward it since the night you begged me for forty-eight hours and I was fool enough to give them to you."

"Fool," Vess repeated softly. She turned, and in the hold's work-light her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "You gave a stranger two days and it turned into the only honest thing either of our fleets has done in six years."

The counter hit 47%. Somewhere in the ship's bones, a relay clicked as the intrusion pushed deeper.

"If I do this," Vess said, "they won't take me alive politely. He can't afford a live daughter who talks. You understand that. Whatever happens in that hall, the moment I say his name—"

"Then I say it louder." Oscar took her face in both hands. "You testify. I broadcast. Every terminal on this station, every ship in both fleets — the vault's raw seed logs, the Perseus Dock records, everything we memorized, everything I backed up in places even Ilsa doesn't know exist. You think I've spent ten years learning redundancy for nothing?" She was shaking, and let herself shake. "You are not doing this alone. That was the deal from the first night. Mercy for honesty. Remember?"

"I remember." Vess closed her eyes, leaned into her palms. When she opened them, the grief was still there — it would always be there, Oscar understood now, grief for the father who had made her a symbol instead of a daughter — but it had been transmuted, the way ore becomes something you can build with.

"Then it's decided," Vess said. "At 0900, the delegates convene. At approximately 0915, during the opening statements, a dead woman walks into the Halcyon Drift summit hall."

The archive counter flickered, stuttered — and died. INTEGRITY 0%. CONNECTION TERMINATED. They had taken all of it. Every annotated line, every cross-reference, nine days of careful scholarship, gone into the dark.

Neither of them looked at the display. They looked at each other.

"Oscar," Vess said, and her voice cracked on the second syllable and healed itself. "If I don't come out of that hall—"

"You'll come out."

"If I don't. Promise me the long route. Promise me you fly it anyway. Promise me the vault gets planted, every row, open registry, and that you tell them — tell whoever tends it after you — that the seeds were never the point. The tending was the point."

Oscar kissed her forehead, her mouth, her hands, committing her to memory the way she committed star charts — precisely, desperately, against loss.

"Don't write your eulogy the night before the battle," she whispered. "Come write the peace instead."

Outside the hull, the station's chimes rang the quarter hour, and somewhere above them, in a suite full of flags and flattery, Admiral Andrade was reading his daughter's death certificate one last time before tomorrow made it a lie.

* * *

They did not sleep. There didn't seem to be time for it anymore, sleep being a currency of people with futures they trusted.

Instead they prepared the way soldiers and gardeners both prepare: methodically, tenderly, checking each other's equipment. Oscar loaded the backup caches — the ones she had seeded across the station's public terminals disguised as germination telemetry uploads, invisible unless you knew what a seed vault's file signatures smelled like. Vess rehearsed her testimony aloud in the hold, once, twice, a dozen times, her voice growing steadier with each pass until it sounded less like a confession and more like a verdict.

At 0530, Oscar's comm lit with a message from an unmarked address. One line, no signature:

*Ring Three. Noodle stall. Now. Come alone or I tell him where the Meridian sleeps.*

Ilsa.

Oscar stood in the hold's doorway, looking back at Vess asleep at last — truly asleep, finally, curled in a cargo netting hammock with the rehearsal notes still in her hand — and made the last quiet decision of the night.

She went alone.

The stall was shuttered, the ring dimmed to night-cycle amber. Ilsa sat at the counter with two cups of tea steaming, and she did not look up when Oscar approached, and when she spoke, her voice had none of the warmth of the vault weeks in it. Only tiredness, and underneath the tiredness, something that might have been apology, if apology hadn't been a luxury neither of them could afford.

"Sit down, Eriksen. We have four hours, and you need to hear how the sting actually went — because it didn't go the way he wanted." She slid one cup across the counter. "And then you need to decide whether you trust me one more time. Because tomorrow, when your girl walks into that hall, there's going to be a gun on the floor near the delegate from Ceres — and whether it fires depends entirely on what you do next."
