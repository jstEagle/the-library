## 19. Daughter of the Fracture

The summit hall of Halcyon Drift had been built to hold four hundred delegates and had never once held peace.

Vess stood at the edge of the concourse above it, looking down through the observation glass at the tiered amphitheater where the two fleets sat like tectonic plates pressed together — the Andrade Coalition on the eastern arc in slate-grey uniforms, the Perseus Compact on the western in faded blue, and between them a strip of neutral floor polished so bright it reflected both delegations at once, as if the station itself could not decide which side it was on.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them the way you notice weather — distant, impersonal, happening to someone else.

*You don't have to do this,* Oscar had said, an hour ago, in the shadowed maintenance corridor behind the delegation lifts. She'd said it with her back against the bulkhead and her eyes on Vess's face, and she had meant it, which was the terrible part. Oscar Eriksen did not offer comfort she didn't believe in. If she said there was another way, some part of her had gone looking for one and found nothing.

"There isn't," Vess had answered. "There's only this."

Now she pulled her father's old flight jacket tighter around her shoulders — the one she'd taken from the Meridian's locker six years ago, the one that still smelled faintly of engine coolant and a childhood spent on hangar catwalks — and she walked toward the delegate entrance with her chin up and her heart going like a failing reactor.

Behind her, somewhere in the station's nervous system, Oscar was already climbing.

* * *

Oscar had never run through a space station's service spine before. She was learning quickly that Halcyon Drift was a creature of arteries: coolant lines thick as tree trunks, cable runs bundled like harvested wheat, ladder wells that rang under her boots with a sound too much like a bell for comfort. The broadcast array she needed was three decks up and forty meters aft, in a comms relay room that hadn't been audited since before the Fracture War, because no one audited rooms that mattered to no one.

She mattered to no one here. That was her camouflage now, the same way the Meridian's ghost-status had carried them through the blockade. A caretaker. A clerk. A woman with a certification number and a satchel full of drives.

Except the drives were gone. Seized, twenty minutes ago, in a sting that Ilsa Ren had orchestrated with the smooth efficiency of a woman who had never once been on anyone's side but her own. Oscar had watched from across the concourse as station security closed around the courier lockbox, watched the case carried away under armed escort toward the evidence vault, and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very cold.

The proof was gone. The falsified incident reports, the altered telemetry, the seed vault's raw logs that showed the Perseus convoy had never fired first — all of it, sealed behind a blast door, scheduled for "review" by a panel that Admiral Andrade's faction already owned.

Which left only one piece of evidence in the universe that could not be seized, forged, or filed away.

Vess Andrade was alive.

And Oscar's job — the last job her decade of solitude had prepared her for — was to make sure that when Vess spoke, every ear in the hall heard her, and every archive in the fleet recorded it, and no one, ever, could claim afterward that they hadn't known.

She reached the relay room, shouldered the door, and found it exactly as the schematics promised: a cathedral of dead consoles, dust like snowfall on the screens, and one live trunk line running to the station's public broadcast backbone. She set her terminal on the console, jacked in, and began to work the way she worked best — alone, in silence, with her hands remembering what her mind had forgotten.

The vault's raw logs were still on her personal drive. Ilsa had taken the courier cache; she hadn't known about the backup Oscar kept the way other people kept prayer. Ten years of solitary record-keeping, of triple-redundant archives because a seed vault forgives no sloppiness — it all came down to this: a caretaker's habit of never trusting a single copy of anything.

"Okay," she murmured to the empty room, to the dust, to the ghost of the Meridian and everyone who had ever died believing paperwork. "Let's show them what the vault remembers."

* * *

The summit hall smelled of recycled air and cut flowers — real ones, absurdly, freighted in at ruinous cost for the pageantry, lilies and something white and star-shaped that Vess didn't have a name for. Her mother had grown flowers once, in the years before the war, in a hydroponic bay on the *Sovereign's Mercy*. The scent hit her like a hand on the sternum.

She had dressed plainly. That was deliberate. No uniform, no colors, no faction. Just a woman in a dead man's jacket walking down the center aisle while the chamber crier called the session to order and four hundred people looked at everything except each other.

Admiral Edmond Andrade sat at the head of the eastern arc, silver at his temples now, more of it than the last holo she'd seen. He was speaking — low, measured, the voice of a man reading eulogies he himself had written. She caught fragments as she slid into a standing alcove near the rear.

"—the martyrdom of my daughter remains, for this coalition, not a wound but a covenant. We do not negotiate over Vess Andrade's grave. We *keep watch* over it."

The hall murmured its assent. On the western arc, the Perseus delegation sat stone-faced, and Vess saw the young compact delegate — Ferro, the one whose convoy had burned at Perseus Dock — close her eyes briefly, as if the admiral's grief were a weight she'd been carrying for six years without being allowed to put it down.

*He built a church on top of me,* Vess thought. *And he made both fleets attend services.*

The moderator called for the armistice framework review. Delegates rose in their ordered way. Papers shuffled. Somewhere a chime marked the hour, soft and ceremonial.

Vess stepped out of the alcove and walked down the center aisle of the summit of Halcyon Drift.

At first, no one stopped her. That was the strange mercy of bureaucracy — a woman moving with purpose through a formal session read as staff, as courier, as nobody. She passed the neutral strip, its polished floor holding her reflection like thin ice, and she thought absurdly of the germination bays, of Oscar's hands in the soil-substrate, of the small green row they'd planted together during the solar storm and named, half-joking, *Day One*.

Then she reached the open floor between the arcs, turned to face the whole assembly, and said, clearly enough to reach the last row:

"My name is Vess Andrade. And I am not dead."

* * *

The sound the hall made was not a gasp. It was larger than that — a structural event, four hundred breaths drawn at once, the acoustic equivalent of a hull taking pressure it wasn't rated for. Then silence, total, the kind of silence Vess had only heard before inside cryo-bays.

Admiral Andrade rose from his seat.

He didn't shout. That was the thing about him that no one who loved him ever survived understanding: he never shouted. He came down the eastern steps slowly, hands open at his sides, face arranged into an expression Vess knew from a hundred childhood fevers and one funeral that had no body — the expression of a father, worn like a uniform.

"Vessa." Her childhood name. His voice cracked precisely where it needed to crack. "Sweetheart. Six years. Six years we mourned you." He spread his hands to the hall. "Delegates — forgive an old man. My daughter was lost at Perseus Dock. Grief makes ghosts of us all—"

"I wasn't lost at Perseus Dock." Vess kept her voice level. Her heart was slamming, but her voice held, and she clung to that the way a climber clings to rock. "I faked my death because I learned what you did with it. With me. I died so you couldn't use me anymore, Father. And then you used me anyway."

"Murmurs moved through the arcs like wind through grain."

"The Meridian's manifest," she said. "The death record. The martyrdom you've blockaded a quadrant over — I signed none of it. I've spent six years watching your war feed on my ghost." She turned, deliberately, to face the Perseus arc, to face Delegate Ferro, whose convoy had burned. "But that's not why I'm here."

She drew a breath that tasted of lilies and recycled air.

"The incident that started the Fracture War — the firing on the Perseus convoy at Meridian Gate — did not happen the way the official record says. I know because I found the original logs. In Earth's seed vault. In the pre-war archive, where no one thought to edit them, because no one imagined a seed vault would remember a war better than the admiralties did."

"The convoy never fired first," she said. "The telemetry was altered. The attack order came from coalition command — from *you* — twelve minutes before any shot was fired at your ships. You started the war, Father. And then you buried the start of it under your daughter's grave, where no one would dare dig."

The hall detonated.

Not literally — not yet — but the sound was a riot of voices, delegates on their feet, the moderator's gavel cracking like ice, the Perseus arc rising as one body while the eastern arc fractured visibly, officers turning to each other, some in fury and some — she saw it, she would remember it forever — some in the dawning horror of men doing arithmetic with their own memories.

And Admiral Andrade stopped performing.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next. The grief fell off his face like a mask slipping its fastenings, and underneath it was something older and colder, the face from the war councils, the face from the night he'd told her that martyrs were more useful than daughters.

"You always were a sentimental child," he said, quietly, and only for her, though the pickup mics caught it anyway, and the hall heard it, and the hall understood everything.

* * *

Three decks up, in a room full of dust and dead consoles, Oscar Eriksen pressed BROADCAST COMMIT and the trunk line took her payload like a vein taking blood.

The raw seed logs went out across every open channel on Halcyon Drift — the pre-war archive entries, unedited, timestamped by a system so old and so boringly incorruptible that no lawyer in either fleet could challenge it without challenging the concept of records themselves. Telemetry originals. Convoy transponder data. The twelve-minute gap. The order authorizations, signed with a command code that belonged to exactly one officer.

On the relay room's single live monitor, she watched the public feed of the summit hall, and she watched Vess stand in the center of it, small and blazing, and she whispered, "I'm here. I've got you. Say it all."

She saw the admiral's expression change. She saw the moment the performance ended.

And she saw — because ten years alone had trained her eyes to catch small anomalies in large fields, a flicker of wrong motion, a shape out of place — the security detail at the eastern arc. Four men. Three of them watching the delegates.

One of them watching Vess, with his hand inside his coat, and his eyes on the admiral, waiting.

Waiting for a signal.

"No," Oscar said, to no one, to the dust, to the universe. "No, no, *no*—"

She was already moving.

* * *

Later, survivors of the session would disagree about the sequence. About whether the shot came before or after the admiral's aide leaned toward him; about whether the signal was a nod or simply the collapse of the last pretense. What the record shows — and the record, this time, is beyond dispute, because Oscar Eriksen made sure of it — is this:

A single pistol-shot from the eastern security detail, aimed at Vess Andrade as she stood in the neutral strip with the truth in her mouth.

And a woman in a caretaker's jumpsuit coming out of the western access tunnel at a dead sprint, crossing the polished floor in four seconds flat, and putting her body between the bullet and the daughter of the Fracture War.

The round took Oscar high in the left shoulder, punched through, and spun her sideways into Vess's arms. They went down together onto the neutral ground, the strip of floor that belonged to neither fleet, and the second shot never came — because the hall erupted, because delegates threw themselves flat, because Delegate Ferro of the Perseus Compact tackled the shooter with a fury six years deep, because the station's own security finally, finally did their job.

Vess knelt on the floor of the summit hall with blood spreading warm and shocking between her fingers, and Oscar looked up at her and smiled — actually smiled, pale and infuriating and calm.

"Told you," Oscar said. "Triple redundancy."

"Don't you dare—" Vess pressed harder, hands slick, voice breaking wide open. "Don't you *dare* make this a joke, Oscar Eriksen—"

"Every copy matters," Oscar whispered. Her eyes were finding Vess's face the way they found anomalies — completely, refusing to let go. "That's the whole... that's the whole job..."

Medics were running. The moderator was shouting for order that would never come. On the eastern arc, Admiral Edmond Andrade stood motionless amid the collapse of everything he had built, watching his daughter hold a bleeding stranger on the neutral floor, and whatever moved behind his face in that moment, no camera caught it, and no historian has ever agreed on it.

Above them, on every screen in the hall, the raw logs kept broadcasting. Timestamps. Telemetry. The twelve-minute gap. The signature.

The truth, playing on a loop, in a room full of witnesses who could never again honestly say they hadn't seen it.

* * *

Oscar's blood was on the neutral strip, soaking into the seam between the two fleets' territories, and it would not scrub out. Station crews tried, in the days after. The stain stayed.

Vess held her hand all the way to the infirmary, and Oscar held on back, and neither of them said anything else, because everything either of them needed to say had just been said in front of four hundred delegates and the entire listening quadrant.

But as the medics lifted her onto the gurney, as the lights of the corridor strobed past overhead, Oscar's grip tightened once — a squeeze, deliberate, coded, theirs — and Vess bent close, and heard her say, barely a breath:

"Nine days early. Still counts."

Then the infirmary doors closed, and somewhere behind them a heart monitor picked up a rhythm it intended to keep, and out in the summit hall the Fracture War — the real one, the one fought with documents and graves and a dead girl's name — entered its final hour.
