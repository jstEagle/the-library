## 22. Halcyon, Growing

**Oscar**

The vault smells different now.

For ten years it had smelled of dust and coolant and the faint mineral tang of recycled air — the smell of a machine holding its breath. Now, when I walk the long corridor toward the germination bays at first light, I smell green. Wet soil and cut stems and the sweet, faintly rotten perfume of compost turning over in the bins Ilsa built out of salvaged cargo pallets. The change happened so gradually that I couldn't name the day it began, but I can name the moment I noticed: a Tuesday, eleven months after Halcyon Drift, when I opened Bay Four and found a tomato vine growing through the seam of its tray into the neighboring bay, as if it had opinions about property lines.

I stood there laughing alone in the aisle like a madwoman, and Vess found me like that, and she didn't ask what was funny. She just leaned against the doorframe with dirt on her hands and said, "It's the Andrade beefsteak. It always was an imperialist."

That's the vault now. That's what we made of it.

The trust paperwork went through in the spring — actual paper, printed on the station press at Perseus Dock, which has been rebuilt half from wreckage and half from apology. *The Halcyon Seed Trust*, chartered under joint fleet authority, open registry, open doors. My caretaker certification was never restored; instead they gave me something better and stranger, a title nobody had held before: Steward. Not a keeper of secrets. A keeper of seeds, which turns out to be a different profession entirely. A secret-keeper works alone. A steward works with everyone who shows up.

And they show up. They keep showing up.

There are fourteen of us now, permanent enough to have bunks and opinions about the coffee. Juno from the Ceres agri-corps, who talks to seedlings in three languages and insists they respond best to Portuguese. Old Marek, a dockworker from Perseus who lost his brother in the Fracture War and came here, he says, "to put things in the ground instead of in orbit." Two kids from the Drift station schools doing their field year, endlessly fascinated by cryo-bays and endlessly careless with the watering schedule. And Ilsa Ren.

Ilsa, who tried to sell us to my government and then, when that failed, sold her government to us instead — or maybe just finally sold herself to the truth, which is the only transaction she ever made cleanly. She arrived eight months ago with no explanation and one duffel bag, and Vess met her at Dock Nine with a pry bar in one hand and, visibly, a decision in the other. Ilsa looked at the pry bar and said, "Fair." And then she asked where we kept the tools for building raised beds.

She runs logistics now. Of course she does. She's brilliant at it, ruthless about efficiency, weirdly tender with the bees we keep in the modified cargo module — the first hive ever flown off Earth, or so Juno claims, though Juno claims a lot of things. Some mornings I watch Ilsa move through the corridors with her manifests and her checklists, and I remember her standing in this same corridor with a weapon trained on the woman I love, and I don't forgive her, exactly. But I've stopped flinching. In a place built for extinction, that might be the most hopeful crop we grow.

This morning there's a supply run loading at Dock Nine, and for the first time in a year, I'm not dreading it.

* * *

**Vess**

My father sends letters now. Real ones, handwritten, transmitted from whatever station he's been remanded to while the tribunal does its slow, grinding work. The armistice stripped him of command; the evidence stripped him of everything else. He writes four pages at a time, dense and careful, and every letter ends the same way: *I do not ask you to come. I ask only that you read.*

I read them. I read them the way you'd read weather reports from a country you used to live in — attentively, without longing. He writes about the trial. About the delegates who signed the armistice using the raw seed logs Oscar broadcast across both fleets' channels, the pre-war records no one had thought to falsify because no one imagined a seed vault would be keeping history. He writes about the families of Perseus Dock, who finally learned how their dead were spent. He writes, once, only once, about the day he learned I was alive: *I sat down in a room full of enemies and my daughter walked in wearing her mother's face, and I understood that everything I had built was a wall between us, and walls are just wars that got tired.*

I keep the letters in a drawer in the room Oscar and I share, beneath a jar of seeds from the first open-registry row. I haven't answered yet. But last month I sent him something better than words: a packet of seeds. Barley, from the row we plant for the future. No note. If he plants them, he'll understand. If he doesn't, he was never going to.

Oscar thinks I'm being cruel. Oscar thinks I'm being generous. She can't decide, and honestly, neither can I, and that's fine. We've stopped pretending the important things resolve neatly. The war didn't end neatly. It ended the way a fever breaks — sweating, ugly, and then one morning the light comes through the port and your body belongs to you again.

Today we fly the long route together. Both fleets' patrol ships clear us through without a scan now; the Meridian is famous, the ghost ship that carried the truth home, and fame is its own kind of camouflage. But we take the long route anyway, past the silent colonies that aren't silent anymore — Ceres answers hails now, and Ganymede, and even the deep relay at Tau Ceti, blinking awake like eyes opening one by one. We deliver seeds and soil inoculant and letters from volunteers to family they haven't seen since before the Fracture. We deliver, in other words, the thing I spent six years believing was impossible: continuity.

Oscar flies the first shift. I sit in the second seat, the one that was empty for ten years, and I watch her hands on the controls — steady, unhurried, the hands of someone who has finally stopped bracing for the next loss. Somewhere past the Mars corridor she reaches over without looking and finds my hand, and we fly like that for an hour, two women and a resurrected ship and forty tons of living cargo, going nowhere urgent, coming home eventually.

"Vess," she says, when we're crossing the belt. "Look."

Ahead, drifting in the long dark, is a ship I don't recognize — small, battered, running dark except for a single hull light. And painted on its flank, fresh and defiant, in letters visible from a kilometer out:

*HALCYON GROWS.*

* * *

**Oscar**

They're colonists. That's all. Eleven of them, from the Drift station, who pooled their savings and bought a derelict ore hauler and filed for a land grant on one of the moons the war emptied. Their leader is a former schoolteacher named Amara Osei, and when she comes aboard the Meridian clutching a data chip — their application, their soil surveys, their list of requested seed stocks — her hands shake so badly that Vess takes the chip from her gently, like taking something fragile from a child.

"We heard the broadcast," Amara says. "During the summit. Everyone heard it. And I kept thinking — she was keeping seeds for a world that ended. And we're asking for seeds for a world that hasn't started. And those felt like the same act, somehow."

Vess looks at me over the woman's head. I know that look. It's the look she wore in the cryo-bay during the inspection, the look she wore walking into the summit hall to give herself to the truth. It's the look of someone deciding.

"The trust will grant your request," Vess says. "Full stock. Germination support. And a standing supply line — we'll fly it ourselves, quarterly."

Amara Osei cries. So do two of her crew. Ilsa, watching from the cargo bay door, says nothing, but later she amends the flight schedule without being asked, carving out the quarterly run before anyone signs anything, because some people show loyalty in logistics.

We escort them as far as the jump gate. As their hauler spins up, Amara transmits one last message: *"Tell the vault the colony of New Halcyon says thank you. Tell it we'll send back seeds. Our own."*

Seeds of our own. Sent back. I sit with that phrase the whole way home, turning it over like a stone from a riverbed. For ten years the vault only took in. It was a machine for preserving what was already lost. And now, eleven months into the rest of our lives, it gives, and receives, and grows, and the loop closes and opens and closes again, the way loops are supposed to.

* * *

**Vess**

Evening, back at the vault. The bays are dimmed to night cycle, but the new row — Row One, the open registry — glows under its dedicated lights, because Juno swears the young barley needs twenty minutes of extra dusk and no one has the heart to argue with her.

I walk the row alone. This is my ritual now, the one I took from Oscar, the way she took my habit of counting airlock cycles when she thinks I'm not looking. Row One stretches away in perfect lines, each plot labeled with a name and a date and, since spring, a third line: *planted by*. Today's entries are in Marek's blocky hand, in Juno's looping script, in the kids' wobbly capitals. In one plot, small and stubborn, barley sprouts in a pattern too regular to be accidental — my father's packet, planted by Ilsa, who never explained and was never asked.

I kneel. I touch the soil. It's warm from the heat cables, alive with the microscopic commerce of roots and fungi, and I think about my father in his remand cell somewhere, holding a handful of barley if he chose to, and I think: *this is what I have instead of an inheritance.* Not a fleet. Not a name that makes rooms go quiet. A row of seeds anyone may plant, planted by anyone, for a harvest that belongs to whoever is hungry.

Oscar finds me there. She always knows which bay I'll be in; she says I'm predictable, and I say she's a liar, and we're both right.

"They confirmed the New Halcyon charter," she says, crouching beside me. "First post-war colony. There'll be more. Ilsa's already tracking nine other applications."

"Nine."

"Twelve by winter, probably. Word travels." She pauses. "You know what that means. More routes. More runs. More of the long way around."

I look at her. Her hair is going gray at the temples — it started the month after the summit, and she claims it's the water, and I let her claim that. Her hands are earth-stained. Ten years ago she flew these routes alone between colonies that wouldn't answer her hails, and she did it because duty was the only shape her loneliness could wear. And now the same route, the same ship, the same work — transformed, because she isn't alone in the second seat, because the colonies answer, because the seeds go out and come back changed.

"Oscar Eriksen," I say. "Are you asking me to take the long route with you?"

"I'm telling you," she says, "that the long route is the only route I ever want to fly. And it's better at your speed."

I kiss her among the barley, the way we kissed among the seedlings a year and a lifetime ago, and the difference is that no one is hiding now, no inspectors, no ghosts, no forged manifests. Just two women in a vault built for the end of the world, quietly refusing to let the world end.

Later, in the corridor, we pass the notice board where the volunteer roster hangs, and I stop. Someone has pinned a new transmission printout to the cork — standard practice, anything addressed to the trust goes up for all to see. It's from the Perseus Dock reconstruction office. Official seal. Formal language.

But it's the last line that stops my breath:

*"In clearing the Meridian's original berthing records, workers recovered a sealed personal log, authorship unconfirmed, contents untranscribed. The trust is requested to advise."*

A log. Sealed. From the Meridian — my ship, our ship, the ship that burned at Perseus Dock with all hands, except the hands it didn't burn.

Oscar reads it twice. Then she looks at me, and I see it in her face, the old instinct waking — the archivist's hunger, the steward's caution, the woman who once noticed one impossible name on a manifest and followed it all the way to the rest of her life.

"Well," she says softly. "You want to take the long route to the answer?"

I take her hand. Outside, beyond the vault's thick shell, the drift turns, patient and vast, full of things still waiting to be found.

"Always," I say. "We fly at dawn."
