## 1. Decant Date

The first thing Noor Ishikawa ever felt was cold.

Not the cold of air or water—she had no reference for those yet—but the cold of a body remembering itself in pieces. Sensation arrived like cargo being unloaded: the weight of her own hands, the ache behind her eyes, the wet sting of amniotic saline draining from her ears. Somewhere above her, a machine counted her heartbeats aloud in a voice that had no interest in whether she survived them.

*Decant complete. Neural integration at forty-one percent. Subject responsive.*

She opened her eyes to white light and a face looking down at her—a med-tech, young, with the careful blankness of someone trained not to react to what came out of clone tanks.

"Hello, Administrator," the med-tech said.

Noor tried to answer and discovered she did not know how her mouth worked. She tried again, and this time the word came out shaped by someone else's habits: "Status."

The med-tech blinked. "You're stable. Integration is proceeding on schedule."

*I didn't choose that word,* Noor thought. *It chose me.* It was the first thing she understood about herself: that somewhere inside her skull, another woman's reflexes were already living, answering questions before Noor could form them, reaching for handholds in her own body the way you'd reach for furniture in a dark room you'd lived in for thirty years but had never once seen.

They sat her up. The transport's medical bay resolved around her—narrow bunks, a viewport fogged with condensation, the smell of recycled air and antiseptic. Beyond the glass: nothing. Deep transit black, pricked with stars so distant they looked less like objects than like opinions.

"How long was I—" she began, and stopped, because the question was wrong. She hadn't been anywhere. She had been made eleven days ago in a vat facility orbiting Ceres, grown fast from a template, and everything she knew before waking had been poured into her rather than lived.

"How long until integration completes?" she corrected.

"Ninety days, roughly. Full personality transfer is never clean, but—" The med-tech hesitated, choosing words with visible care. "Your template's engram was high quality. You'll have most of her. The parts that matter."

*Her.* The original. Administrator Noor Ishikawa of Kepler Reach, aged fifty-two at time of death—except there was no record of death, not officially, which was the first strange thing, and Noor had known it before she woke, because knowing it was part of the inheritance too.

She lay back and let the memories settle over her like sediment in disturbed water.

They came in fragments, unmoored from context. A kitchen on Mars with yellow tile and a kettle that whistled flat. The smell of ozone near a reactor junction. A woman's laugh—her own laugh, she supposed—and the specific weight of a signet ring worn on the right hand. A corridor on Kepler Reach, Ring C, where the lights flickered in a pattern she had complained about for six years and no one had ever fixed. Grief arrived attached to these things like a label glued to empty jars: *you loved this; you lost this; you are mourning a life you never lived.*

It was the strangest grief there is, Noor decided. To mourn yourself. To wake already widowed from your own history, holding photographs of a house you've never entered, aching for people who would walk past you in a corridor without a second glance—because to them, you were still alive, still there, still wearing your face.

Because that was the arrangement. That was why she existed.

The case file found her on the third day.

She had graduated from the medical bay to a narrow cabin, learning to walk a body that walked slightly differently than her instincts expected—the original had favored her left knee after an old injury, and Noor kept stumbling because her new knee worked perfectly. She ate meals whose flavors she recognized before tasting them. She dreamed in a stranger's dreams.

The file arrived as a priority flag on the cabin's terminal, stamped with the seal of the Colonial Administration Authority. It opened without asking her credentials, because of course it did. Her credentials were the original's credentials. That was the entire point of her.

**CASE FILE KR-4471 — INCIDENT AT KEPLER REACH STATION**
**STATUS: CLOSED**
**CLOSURE AUTHORIZED BY: ISHIKAWA, N. (ADMINISTRATOR)**
**DATE OF CLOSURE: 11 DAYS PRECEDING TEMPLATE DECANT**

Noor read that last line four times.

Eleven days before they grew her, the original had closed her own murder investigation. Which meant the original had known she was going to die—or had died, and someone with access to her authority had closed the file afterward, using her name, her seals, her voice if voiceprints were required. Both possibilities were terrible. One meant suicide dressed up as closure. The other meant murder dressed up as administration.

She scrolled the file. It was thin in the way that deliberately emptied rooms are thin. An incident report dated fourteen months prior: *unspecified trauma, Administrator's quarters, Ring B.* No cause of death listed. No autopsy summary. No witness statements. A single line of medical finding—*subject stabilized, transferred to long-term care*—and then nothing, a flatline of redaction running down the page like a scar.

Except the original wasn't dead. Not officially. The official record said Administrator Ishikawa had suffered a medical event, was recovering in seclusion, and had delegated routine authority to her deputy while retaining final say. The CAA had accepted this. The colony had accepted this. And now the CAA had spent a fortune growing a clone and installing half a lifetime of memories into her, and shipping her nine months across the black—

—to do what, exactly? Close out a case no one wanted solved. That was what her briefing packet said, in language so sanitized it circled back around to obscene: *facilitate orderly transition of administrative authority; assess continuity of station operations; effect graceful resolution of outstanding legal matters.*

Graceful resolution. Of a woman who had been beaten nearly to death in her own quarters, whose attacker was never named, whose investigation was closed by her own hand eleven days before her replacement decanted.

Noor sat with the terminal glowing against her face and felt something move through her that she had no word for. It wasn't fear. It was closer to vertigo—the sensation of standing at the edge of someone else's grave and realizing the headstone bore your name.

*She closed the file,* Noor thought. *Why would she close the file?*

And beneath that, quieter, more unwelcome: *Or did she close it? Or did whoever killed her reach into her authority one last time and tidy up?*

The memories stirred, offering fragments the way a damaged archive offers fragments. A memory of signing something. A stylus heavy in her hand—the original's hand—on a surface that hummed. A sense of enormous pressure, of a door closing, of the phrase *for the good of the Reach* spoken in a voice that might have been her own. But the fragment had no date, no context, no faces. Forty-one percent integration. The parts that mattered, the med-tech had said. Apparently the parts that mattered were still in transit.

On the fifth day, the locked partition appeared.

She found it the way you find a wall in a familiar house at night—by walking into it. She reached for a memory of Kepler Reach's administrative structure, a simple org chart, something she should have known as easily as her own signature, and hit resistance. Not absence. Resistance. A region of the implant that returned static when queried, wrapped in encryption layered three deep, flagged with a lock code she didn't have.

She ran diagnostics. The partition was large—years of memory, compressed and sealed. Someone had curated her inheritance. Someone had decided what the new Noor Ishikawa got to know about the old one, and had walled off the rest behind a lock that would not open.

The briefing packet, she remembered suddenly, had contained a single line about this, buried in clause forty-something of her contract: *certain template memories may be administratively restricted pending resolution of legal matters.* Pending resolution. Of a case that was already closed. Of a death that had never happened.

She laughed—one short, ugly sound—in the empty cabin, and the laugh came out in the original's voice, pitched low and dry, and hearing it frightened her more than anything else had so far. Because it fit. Because the voice fit her throat like a glove, and she could feel, rising unbidden, the original's habits of thought arranging themselves around the problem: *catalogue the anomaly, control the narrative, tell no one.*

Stop, she told herself. Think your own thoughts.

But that was the trap, wasn't it. Where did her thoughts end and the template's begin? She grieved a kitchen on Mars she'd never stood in. She missed a sister she'd never met. Every instinct she trusted had been installed. If someone had curated her memories, they had curated her judgment. They had built an investigator and then removed the evidence.

Unless the original herself had done the locking. Unless, in whatever lucid interval remained to her after the attack, the woman whose face Noor wore had sealed her own knowledge away—hiding it inside her successor's skull like a message smuggled out of a prison, waiting for a lock code, waiting for trust, waiting for the day the copy became strong enough to carry what the original had carried.

*Find me,* the sealed years seemed to say. *I'm in here. I hid myself where only I would look.*

Noor pressed her palm flat against the terminal, feeling its faint warmth, and made her first decision as a person rather than a product: she would not close anything. The file said CLOSED. She would treat that stamp as the first lie in a document full of them.

* * *

On the ninth day, the transport's captain invited her to the bridge.

Captain Oyelaran was a lean, gray-haired hauler with the permanent squint of someone who had watched too much vacuum for too many decades. She offered Noor tea—real tea, hoarded, the kind captains keep for occasions—and studied her over the cup with frank, professional curiosity.

"You have her way of sitting," Oyelaran said. "I hauled the original out to the Reach, twenty-six years ago. Fresh appointment, straight from Mars. She sat exactly like that. Spine straight, hands folded, like she was chairing a meeting even when there was no one to meet."

"I wouldn't know," Noor said. "I've never met her."

"No," Oyelaran agreed, and smiled thinly. "You've just *been* her, in the small ways." She sipped. "You want to know why I took this contract. Everyone asks eventually, and you're too polite to ask, so I'll spare us both."

Noor waited.

"Because nobody else would say yes." Oyelaran set down the cup. "Three haulers turned this run down before it reached me. Clone transport to a contested station, carrying an administrator's double into a situation the CAA won't describe in plain language. My crew drew straws. I let them." She leaned forward. "So let me tell you what an old hauler sees, Administrator, since no one official will tell you anything true."

"The Reach is dying," Noor said. It wasn't a guess. The supply manifests in her briefing packet had told her—told the original, rather—everything: filtration replacements ordered and never delivered, hydroponic bay allocations cut year over year, maintenance logs that read like a slow apology.

"The Reach is dying," Oyelaran confirmed. "And everyone in the trade knows it, and nobody says it, because the Reach sits on the best jump approach in the sector and half the nav charts out here depend on her beacon. When a station like that dies, it doesn't die quiet. It dies loud, and it takes its neighborhood with it." She turned the cup slowly on its saucer. "And now two fleets are sniffing toward that approach like sharks toward blood, and the CAA grows itself a little administrator and ships her out in a box, and everyone pretends this is a personnel decision."

"What do you think it is?"

Oyelaran looked at her for a long moment—not at her, Noor realized, but at the face, searching it the way you'd search a photograph of someone you'd known.

"I think," she said, "that somebody wanted the original's authority walking around, breathing and signing things, and the original herself unavailable to contradict it. And I think you should ask yourself, before you dock, who benefits from that arrangement. Because it isn't the colonists. Their air gets thinner every year regardless of whose signature is on the requisitions." She rose, collecting her cup. "Get some sleep, Administrator. Docking in six days. Kepler Reach doesn't broadcast much anymore, but what she does broadcast looks fine. Everything out here looks fine, right up until it isn't."

She paused at the hatch. "One more thing. The original—when I ferried her out, all those years ago—she told me something I've thought about more than once since I read this contract. She said, *'If anyone ever offers you the truth about the Reach, check what it costs them to give it.'*" Oyelaran shrugged. "Seemed like the sort of thing worth passing on. Same face, same ears."

The hatch sighed shut behind her.

Noor sat alone on the bridge afterward, watching the stars hold their positions, and turned the sentence over. *Check what it costs them.* It sounded like paranoia. It also sounded like the aphorism of a woman who had learned, late and expensively, that on Kepler Reach every truth had a price tag, and the people offering truths cheapest were the ones who had stolen them wholesale.

The memories offered nothing. The locked partition sat in her mind like a sealed room in a house she owned but couldn't enter. Forty-one percent. Fifty. The integration counter climbed each morning, and with it the original's reflexes sharpened, her mannerisms settled deeper, her griefs grew heavier—and the sealed years stayed shut, keeping whatever secret had gotten their shared face beaten nearly to death in a room on Ring B.

On the twelfth day, the transport's long-range array caught Kepler Reach's beacon, faint and steady, announcing to the dark that all systems nominal, all services operational, administrator on station.

Administrator on station.

Noor stood at the viewport for a long time, her breath fogging the cold glass, her reflection ghosted over the distant point of light that was her destination, her inheritance, her predecessor's grave and her own future residence. Two women's faces superimposed in the glass, indistinguishable.

*You're dead,* she told the reflection silently. *Aren't you. Whatever the file says, whatever the beacon says—you're dead, and I'm the lie they built to stand over your body, and neither of us asked for any of this.*

The reflection said nothing. The original's memories said nothing. The locked partition said nothing.

But somewhere in the static between integrations, Noor felt it again—that pressure, that sense of a door held shut from the other side, of knowledge waiting with the patience of the dead.

The beacon pulsed. All systems nominal.

Six days to docking. Six days to learn to walk in a dead woman's shoes, past a dead woman's colleagues, into a dead woman's office, and sit down in a dead woman's chair, and smile at whoever had killed her.

Noor turned from the viewport and went to find the case file again. CLOSED, it said. By order of the original herself.

*We'll see about that,* she thought, in a voice that was hers, and hers alone, and also—she was beginning to suspect—exactly the voice the original had used, all those years ago, when she'd first started asking dangerous questions about the Reach.

The beacon pulsed twice, far away, like a heartbeat. Like a knock.

Like something on the other side of the locked door, waiting to be let out.
