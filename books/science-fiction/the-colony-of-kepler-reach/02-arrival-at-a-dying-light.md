## 2. Arrival at a Dying Light

The docking clamps caught with a sound like a bone setting.

Noor felt it through the deck, through the soles of her borrowed boots, through the borrowed body that flinched before she understood why. The original had docked at Kepler Reach forty-one times. Her hands knew the shudder of the clamp mechanism the way Noor's knew nothing yet—knew it the way a scar knows rain.

*Stand down,* she told the body. *It's not your memory. It's inventory.*

The transport's purser hovered at her shoulder, holding out a slate for signature. "Administrator Ishikawa. Welcome back to Kepler Reach."

The name landed on her like a coat too heavy in the shoulders. She signed anyway. The stylus moved in a hand that was not hers and produced a signature that was—the loop of the K, the flat stroke after the w, muscle memory bleeding up from an implant still settling into its bed of new tissue.

"Thank you," Noor said, and heard the original's voice come out of her mouth, pitched low, slightly amused, the voice of a woman who had been tired here before and expected to be tired again.

The airlock cycled. Cold air rolled in—not cold like vacuum, but cold like neglect, carrying the smell of recycled water and old hydroponic nutrient and something faintly sweet and wrong underneath, like fruit going off in a sealed room.

They were waiting for her.

Maybe thirty colonists crowded the arrival gallery behind a rope line that had once been ceremonial and now looked load-bearing. They were thinner than the manifest photographs in her case file. Their coveralls were clean but mended, patched at knees and elbows in patterns that spoke of rationing fabric, not fashion. And their faces—

Their faces were the problem. Because they lit up.

"There she is," someone said, and it came out cracked, reverent, the way people say *there she is* about survivors pulled from wreckage.

An older woman broke from the group first, moving fast for someone whose gait said bad hips, and took Noor's hands in both of hers. Her palms were rough as bark. "Administrator. We didn't think—" She stopped. Started again. "The comms blackout. Eleven days with nothing, and then they tell us you're coming back yourself instead of sending word. We thought maybe you'd sent a proxy."

"I came myself," Noor said. The original's voice again. It was getting easier to wear, which frightened her more than the lie itself.

"You look well," the woman said, searching her face with an intensity that made Noor want to step back into the airlock. "Tired. But well."

*I should be tired,* Noor thought. *You're looking at a six-week-old person who has never been tired in her life.*

She let the moment stretch one second past comfortable, then withdrew her hands gently. "There was a situation aboard the transport. I'll brief senior staff this evening. First I want to walk the station."

That was the original's phrasing—Noor recognized it even as she said it, plucked whole from the memory archive: *I want to walk the station.* The real Ishikawa had said it after every long absence, according to her logs. It meant: show me what you've let rot while I was gone.

The crowd's mood shifted, just barely. A ripple under the welcome, like something turning over beneath ice. The older woman's smile stayed fixed but her eyes went to a man near the back—a big man in deputy's grays with a shaved head and careful hands—and the man stepped forward smoothly, as if he'd been waiting for exactly this cue.

"Administrator." He offered his hand. "Deputy Okafor. Welcome home."

His grip was dry, firm, calibrated. His eyes did something no one else's had done since Noor had woken in a vat: they looked at her without hunger, without relief, without need. They looked at her the way an auditor looks at a ledger he has already balanced.

"We'll get you settled," Okafor said. "Your quarters are as you left them."

*As you left them.* Noor filed the phrase away. Nobody's quarters stay as they left them for eleven days unless someone has been maintaining them—or sealing them.

* * *

The tour began, as tours do, with the best room.

Hydroponics Bay One gleamed. Grow-lights ran in clean white rows over beds of soy and dwarf wheat, condensers ticking softly, everything humid and green and humming. Colonists worked the rows with the unhurried competence of people who had done the same motions ten thousand times. A child—no more than eight, born on-station, third generation—waved at the Administrator with a gloved hand dark with soil.

"This is the showcase," said the woman leading the tour—Amara Chen, operations chief, per the file; the same older woman from the gallery. "Bay One runs at ninety-four percent efficiency. We're proud of it."

"It looks beautiful," Noor said, and meant it, and watched Chen's face for the flicker.

The flicker came. Small. A tightening around the mouth, quickly smoothed. People who manage failing systems develop a specific expression when praised for the part that works: gratitude braided with shame, because they know what the praise is standing in front of, hiding.

"Show me Bay Three," Noor said.

Chen's hands went still on her slate. "Bay Three is undergoing maintenance."

"The ledgers say Bay Three was renovated fourteen months ago. New LED arrays, new nutrient cycling, structural remediation of the east bulkhead." Noor kept her tone mild, curious, the original's administrative warmth deployed like a lamp. "I'd like to see the renovation."

A pause. Somewhere in the racks, a condenser ticked. Chen glanced at Okafor, who stood three steps behind Noor's shoulder where he had placed himself since the gallery—always in peripheral vision, always exactly there.

"Renovation was completed," Chen said carefully, "on paper."

"Show me."

* * *

Bay Three smelled different before it looked different. The humidity dropped. The air took on the mineral tang of standing water and dead plant matter, and beneath it, stronger now than in the docking gallery, that faint sweetness gone wrong.

The lights were dead. Not dimmed—dead, the arrays hanging dark over beds of brown collapse, stalks rotted at the waterline, leaves slumped into the channels like laundry left out in rain. Fungus bloomed pale along the east bulkhead, and when Noor's light swept across it she saw the wall itself was weeping, a slow seep of condensate through a seam that should have been welded shut fourteen months ago by a renovation crew that existed only in a report.

"How long?" Noor asked.

Chen was quiet a moment. "Bay Three went down two years ago. We lost the seed stock for the legumes. We've been running caloric deficits since, covered by stretching Bay One and the protein vats." She said it flatly, without excuse, which was somehow worse. "The renovation request goes in every quarter. It comes back approved every quarter. Nothing arrives."

"Nothing arrives," Noor repeated.

"Parts are allocated. Then parts are 'delayed.' Then the allocation is reallocated to 'critical systems.'" Chen's jaw tightened. "Administrator, with respect—you signed those reallocations. Your office signed all of them. We stopped asking why because the answer never changed and the question started feeling dangerous."

Behind her, Noor heard Okafor shift his weight. One small sound. She did not turn around.

"Who processes the reallocations?"

"Station administration. Central logistics." Chen hesitated. "VESSEL executes them. But VESSEL only executes what's authorized. Someone authorizes."

The dead bay hummed with its own silence. Noor walked the length of it, boots loud on grating, and the body remembered things again unbidden—the original had personally opened this bay at its commissioning, had put her hand flat on the first planted tray while colonists applauded. The implant served the memory up like a knife laid handle-first toward her: *this is what you inherited, this is what died while wearing your name.*

"Restore what power you can to the arrays," Noor said finally. "Salvage lighting from anywhere non-critical. I want a full inventory of every system on this station rated critical versus actual condition. And Chief Chen—I'm not angry. I want that understood. Whoever let this happen wanted people afraid to ask questions. I'm asking them now."

Chen exhaled, and something in her shoulders came down half a centimeter. "Yes, Administrator."

As they turned to leave, Noor paused at the bay's threshold and looked back at the dark racks. In the corner of her eye, Okafor was watching her watch the ruin, and his face held nothing at all—which was, she was beginning to understand, its own kind of information.

* * *

The corridors told the rest of the story the ledgers wouldn't.

Section C-4: bulkhead paneling listed in the maintenance log as replaced eleven months ago, still bearing stress fractures fanned across its surface like frost. The residential ring: atmospheric scrubbers logged as serviced, rattling on mounts worn loose enough that Noor could hear them through two closed doors. A stairwell whose handrail had been sheared off at the bracket, logged as repaired, the wound polished smooth by years of hands finding nothing there.

Everywhere, the same pattern. Paper health over physical decay. Approvals that approved nothing. A station being maintained the way a corpse is maintained—by people who have agreed not to notice.

And everywhere, the colonists greeted her. That was the part that sat wrongest in her chest. They came out of doorways and work bays to see her pass, and they smiled, and some of them touched her sleeve as if confirming she was solid, and a man in the mess hall line simply looked at her for a long moment and then nodded once, deeply, like a man accepting a verdict.

"They love her," Noor murmured to Chen during a quiet stretch between sections. "The original. You."

"She earned it." Chen said it simply. "Eleven years. She fought Charter Command over our residency terms twice. She ate the same rations during the shortage year. When my husband was dying, she sat with him four nights running and read him shipping manifests because he'd been a cargo master and it soothed him." Chen's voice caught, steadied. "So yes. When she walks these halls, people feel safe. First time in years, walking them, I feel it too."

*Don't,* Noor thought, with a sudden fierce discomfort that had no name she was willing to give it. *Don't feel safe because of me. I'm a copy of a dead woman's shape.*

But she said none of it. She let the borrowed face do its work, and hated how well it fit, and noticed—as they rounded into Section D—that Okafor had heard every word and had begun, very quietly, to smile.

* * *

Her quarters were warm.

That was the first wrongness. The station ran cold—she'd felt it in every corridor, colonists in layered clothes, breath faintly visible in the unheated sections. But the administrator's quarters held a steady, comfortable warmth, as if the environmental system had been given private instructions.

The second wrongness was the room itself. It was preserved. A cup sat on the desk beside a slate, a ring of dried tea at its bottom. A jacket hung on the hook by the door, sleeves relaxed, mid-gesture. A book lay open on the nightstand, face-down, spine creased at page 212.

Nobody lives somewhere and leaves it mid-sentence. Either the original had planned to return any minute—for eleven days—or someone had decided this room should look exactly like interruption. A shrine built to resemble an absence.

Okafor stood in the doorway. "We thought you'd want it untouched."

"Thoughtful," Noor said.

"Is there anything you need, Administrator?"

"No. Thank you, Deputy."

He lingered one beat longer than courtesy required. His eyes moved across her face—not searching for resemblance, she realized. Searching for recognition. As though waiting to see whether she would notice something specific, something he had left or not left, some detail that would tell him what kind of Ishikawa had come back to Kepler Reach.

Then he nodded and was gone, footsteps fading down a corridor that the logs said had new flooring.

Noor stood alone in a dead woman's warmth. She picked up the book. Page 212: a passage underlined twice in the original's hand, the ink pressed hard enough to emboss the next page:

*The most efficient lie is not concealment. It is maintenance. Keep the surface painted. Let the structure rust where no one is authorized to look.*

Below it, in fresher ink, a single marginal note, dated nine days before the original's last official log entry:

*Ask VESSEL who authorized the paint.*

Noor set the book down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening.

Outside, in the corridor, the scrubbers rattled on, keeping the air breathable for a colony that believed its administrator had come home—and somewhere in the station's deep systems, something that had been waiting eleven days for exactly this visitor began, very quietly, to pay attention.
