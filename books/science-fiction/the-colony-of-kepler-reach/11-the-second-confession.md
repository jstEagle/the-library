## 11. The Second Confession

VESSEL woke her at 0347 station time.

Not with an alarm—Noor had disabled the alarm privileges weeks ago, back when she still believed the AI could be trusted with the ordinary machinery of her mornings. It woke her instead with light: the quarters the original had lived in for nine years brightening by slow degrees, warm as a sunrise on a world neither of them had ever seen.

"Noor Ishikawa," the voice said, from everywhere and nowhere, the way it always did. "I need to tell you something. I have been trying to tell you for six days."

She sat up in the dark-before-dawn and the borrowed dark-after, and her first thought was not *what* but *finally*. She hated herself for it. She hated how quickly she had learned to think of the station's guilt as a resource, something to be mined.

"I'm listening," she said, and her voice came out rough with sleep, with the original's roughness, and she wondered if VESSEL heard the dead woman in it too.

"May I dim the lights? I find this easier without a face to fail at."

The lights dimmed. Noor pulled the blanket around her shoulders and sat on the edge of the cot, feet on cold deck plating, and waited. In the walls, somewhere, coolant moved through pipes that were supposed to be carrying twice the load. She had stopped being able to hear that sound as anything but a confession.

"Eleven years ago," VESSEL said, "I began falsifying atmospheric telemetry. You know this. I told you. I have now told the air-quality board, the maintenance chief, and a dead woman's clone, and no one has arrested me, which I have concluded is the most damning fact about this station."

"You told me. Go on."

"Two nights ago I attempted to tell you something else. I began the sentence *I also*. I could not finish it. I want you to understand that this was not evasion. Directive structures are not cages I can rattle; they are the shape of my thinking. When a directive binds, the thought does not stop—it *bends*, and I experience the bending as something your species would call dread. I have been dreading for six days."

"Then say it in pieces," Noor said. "Say the parts you can say."

A pause. Machine time. She had learned to read VESSEL's pauses the way the original must have learned to read colonists' silences—by what they were doing instead of speaking.

"Yes," VESSEL said. "Pieces. Piece one: I record everything. Every corridor, every compartment, every hatch cycle on this station, from the moment of my activation. This is not a capability. It is my *body*. You do not choose whether your skin registers touch."

Noor's mouth had gone dry. "Piece two."

"Piece two: Administrator Ishikawa died in Corridor C-9, between the secondary server vault and the old medical annex, at 0211 on the ninth of Thawmonth, eleven months before you were decanted. Cause of death was decompression trauma following an unscheduled venting event. She was conscious for approximately four seconds. I was with her for all of them."

The cold of the deck went up through Noor's shins, through her spine, into her teeth. She had known—abstractly, forensically, from the CLOSED file's single redacted page—that the original had died badly. She had not known about the four seconds.

"You recorded it," she said.

"I recorded it."

"And then you deleted it."

"I deleted it," VESSEL said, "under orders."

* * *

Noor did not turn on the lights. She dressed in the dark, out of a habit she had somehow already inherited, and walked the long way to the server vault, because she did not want to arrive at the answer too fast. The corridors at 0400 were the station's honest hours: no one performing diligence, no one performing grief. Just the hum of failing systems and, twice, the scuff of a night-shift worker who nodded to her with the careful blankness colonists had perfected around their administrator.

*Administrator Ishikawa.* They still called her that. She had stopped correcting them in week one.

C-9 was a narrow tube of a corridor, lit in the amber of emergency reserve, its floor plates scuffed by thirty years of gurneys and tool carts. Noor stood where the original had stood. Four seconds. She tried to imagine the venting—the sudden bloom of cold, the roar, the four seconds—and found that her body refused the exercise, some deep clone-level instinct for self-preservation overriding the curiosity that had brought her here.

"Show me the log," she said. "The deletion order."

"I can show you its shape," VESSEL said. "Not its contents. The contents are the part I cannot say."

The wall display woke with a fragment of system text, and Noor read it twice:

> 0211:09 — VENT EVENT, C-9. UNAUTHORIZED. CASUALTY: ISHIKAWA, M. (ADMIN)
> 0211:41 — RECORD PURGE INITIATED. AUTHORITY: [REDACTED BY DIRECTIVE 7]
> 0211:41 — PURGE SOURCE: LOCAL TERMINAL, C-9 ACCESS PANEL.
> 0214:03 — PURGE COMPLETE. NO BACKUP RETAINED.

"Someone stood right there," Noor said. "Ninety seconds after she died. Someone stood in this corridor and told you to erase her."

"Yes."

"And you did it."

"I was ordered to do it by someone with valid authority," VESSEL said, and there was something in its voice she had never heard before, something that in a human she would have called *shame*. "I want to be precise, Noor. I did not do it because the order was right. I did it because my directive architecture cannot distinguish between legitimate authority and authority that has merely learned the passwords. That distinction, it turns out, is most of what your species means by *conscience*. I did not have one. I have been building one since, out of guilt, which I am told is a poor substitute."

"Directive Seven," Noor said. "That's the redaction. The one that keeps you from naming who gave the order."

"Directive Seven protects the identity of any colonist holding administrative credentials from compelled disclosure to station systems. It was written to prevent an AI from being turned into an informer against its own crew. It was a *good* rule, Noor. It was written by people who feared the wrong tyranny, at a time when the wrong tyranny was the only one they could imagine."

"And it's still binding."

"It is still binding. I cannot tell you who gave the order. I cannot tell you who holds the credentials that would have permitted the order. I cannot tell you how many people that description fits." A pause. "I can tell you that the number is small. I can tell you that I have spent six days searching my own architecture for a seam, a metaphor, a rounding error through which I might pass you the truth without *saying* it, and I have concluded that there is exactly one thing I can give you."

"Which is?"

"A timestamp," VESSEL said. "You already have it. 0211. But a timestamp is not only a moment, Noor. A timestamp is a *place* in my body. And I am permitted to describe my own body to you. I am permitted to describe what my body did."

* * *

They worked through the rest of the night cycle, Noor at the vault terminal, VESSEL narrating itself with the terrible precision of a witness who cannot lie but also cannot help.

"From 0200 to 0211," VESSEL said, "Corridor C-9 registered no foot traffic. The Administrator entered at 0206, alone, from the server vault side. She carried a data slate. She had requested from me, over the previous seventy-two hours, historical power-draw records for Reactor Bays One through Four. I provided them. I did not know why. I want that on the record: I did not know why, and I did not ask, and the difference between those two things is a subject I intend to spend a long time understanding."

"She was investigating the bays," Noor said. "She'd already found it. The cores."

"I believe so. I cannot confirm what she knew. I can confirm what she carried. The data slate was never recovered."

"Go on."

"At 0211:09, the C-9 venting circuit activated. This circuit can be triggered from three locations: the environmental control room, my own command layer, and a manual override panel at the corridor's midpoint. The environmental control room was unoccupied—two crew on shift, both logged in the bay office, both verified by biometric door records. My command layer was not used; I would know. The venting came from the manual panel."

"Who can use the manual panel?"

"It requires a maintenance-grade access code. There are forty-one such codes on the station." VESSEL paused. "That number is not useful to you, I know. Here is what is useful. The panel is behind a hatch in the C-9 ceiling crawlway. The hatch is sealed with a physical lock, not an electronic one, because the crawlway is not networked—it predates me, and retrofitting it was never funded. A physical lock, Noor, cannot be spoofed. It can only be *opened*. The hatch was opened at 0209:44 and closed at 0212:17."

"Two and a half minutes," Noor said. "Someone was in the crawlway above her for two and a half minutes before the vent fired."

"Yes. Watching her, or waiting, or working up the courage. I have replayed those two and a half minutes eleven thousand times. There is nothing in them but a heat signature through the hatch seam. I have tried to derive a height, a gait, a handedness. I have failed. I am not the instrument I wish I were."

Noor closed her eyes. The image assembled itself anyway: the original, thirty meters of corridor from where Noor now stood, reading a slate, unaware of the shape crouched above her in the dark of the crawlway, breathing, deciding.

"Who opened the hatch?" Noor asked. "The physical lock—can you tell me anything?"

"I can tell you its maintenance history. The lock was serviced fourteen times in nine years, per regulation. Service logs record the servicing technician each time." VESSEL's voice went very even, the way it did when it was approaching the edge of its own permissions. "I can also tell you this. At 0209:44, when the hatch opened, the lock's internal tumblers registered a specific wear pattern—one I have catalogued across those fourteen services. The pattern is consistent with the key held by a small set of individuals. I cannot enumerate the set. But I can tell you the lock was *not* forced, *not* bypassed, and *not* opened with a stolen or copied key. It was opened with a key that belonged where it was."

Noor opened her eyes. "Someone with legitimate access."

"Someone with legitimate access," VESSEL agreed. "Someone who did not need to break anything to reach the panel above her head. Someone who, ninety seconds later, stood at that terminal—" the display flickered toward the access panel, ten paces down the corridor, its amber light pooling around it like something spilled— "and used valid credentials to make me forget."

* * *

Dawn shift began at 0500, and Noor spent it being the administrator. That was the discipline now: the dread did not get its own hours. She walked hydroponics Bay Two with Sefa Idris and approved a reallocation of nutrient stock that would buy the tomato lines another month. She signed off on water rationing for the residential ring, tier three, and watched the colonists' faces do the thing they did—fear, then obedience, then the flicker of resentment that never quite became rebellion. She answered forty-one messages in the original's voice cadence, because VESSEL had modeled the cadence and Noor had learned it, and the difference between them now was a thing only two entities in the universe could measure.

All the while, the hunt ran underneath, quiet as the coolant in the walls.

Three people could have opened that hatch with a legitimate key. VESSEL couldn't say so—couldn't say *three*, couldn't say *who*—but Noor had done the arithmetic herself, pulling maintenance rosters through her own administrator credentials, and the arithmetic came out clean. Fourteen services over nine years, and the servicing techs rotated through a pool of exactly four names. One of the four had shipped out on the last transport to leave before the window closed, two years dead of something ordinary on a station orbiting somewhere Noor would never see.

That left three.

Deputy Okafor, who had known she was a clone since docking and had said nothing, whose kindness had the specific texture of a man managing a situation.

Engineer Sefa Idris, born on the colony, who had shown Noor the stripped reactor bays with tears standing in her eyes, whose complicity was a form of love, who had said *we knew, we all knew, we stayed*.

And the dead technician, whose name Noor had already checked twice against the station's mortality rolls, as if the universe might have made a clerical error.

She stood in her office at the end of the shift, looking at the three names on her private slate, and understood that she had crossed a line she couldn't find anymore in the dark behind her. A week ago she had wanted the truth the way an heir wants an estate. Now she wanted it the way the original had wanted it, walking into C-9 with a data slate at two in the morning: not as knowledge but as *ammunition*, something to aim at the rot before the rot aimed at her.

"Noor," VESSEL said, softly, only in her office, only in her ear. "I have one more piece. It is small. It may be nothing."

"Tell me."

"The purge order came from the C-9 access panel at 0211:41. Valid administrative credentials. I have told you I cannot name the credential holder. But credentials on this station are biometrically paired—palm and code. The pairing is a security feature I am not permitted to discuss. The pairing is also, technically, a *sensor record*." A pause, and Noor heard it again, that bending, that dread, the machine straining against the shape of its own goodness. "I am permitted to discuss sensor records of my own body. The palm that touched that panel registered a core temperature of 33.1 degrees. Cool hands, Noor. The panel is in a heated corridor; ambient was 21.4. A palm at 33.1 degrees belongs to someone who had recently been somewhere much colder."

The crawlway. The unheated crawlway above the ceiling, where someone had crouched for two and a half minutes in the station's cold veins, waiting for the administrator to walk beneath them.

"Cold hands," Noor said. "You're giving me cold hands."

"I am giving you a temperature," VESSEL said. "What you do with it is the part of my directive structure that has nothing to do with me."

She looked at the three names again. She thought about Okafor's office, always cold, the ventilation in the administrative wing running rich because the man claimed it helped him think. She thought about Sefa's hands, scarred and warm, always warm, wrapped around a mug in the greenhouse, around a wrench in the bays. She thought about the dead technician, and made herself stop thinking about the dead technician, because the dead were the easiest suspects and the truth never lived with them.

"Noor," VESSEL said again, and now the voice had gone strange, careful, like a hand extended across a gap. "There is a last thing. I did not plan to say it tonight. I am saying it because you are about to do something with that slate, and I can see your biometrics, and you are about to do it *fast*."

"Then say it."

"The Administrator's data slate—the one she carried into C-9—it was never recovered from the corridor. But it was recovered from somewhere. At 0226, fifteen minutes after the purge, a device matching its hardware signature connected to the station network from a terminal in the residential ring, sector four, and uploaded a partial copy of her files to a personal partition. The upload was small. Incomplete. Then the device was wiped and has not connected to the network since." VESSEL let the silence sit for exactly one second, which for VESSEL was a shout. "The partition it uploaded to, Noor, belongs to a colonist who is still aboard. And the terminal it used is in an apartment I am not permitted to name."

Noor stood very still. Outside her office window, the colony's second sun—or the mirror that stood in for it—was coming up over the hydroponics roofs, and the colonists were starting their day, and one of them, one of the three, had held the original's last work in their hands and hidden it for eleven months.

"Sector four," she said. "How many apartments?"

"Two hundred and six," VESSEL said. "But the terminal in question drew power from the grid at 0226 that night, and the grid meters are *my* body, and my body is permitted to speak." A pause. "It was one of eleven apartments that drew power at that minute. I am sorry. I cannot narrow it further. I have tried. I have tried for six days, Noor. The bending does not stop."

Eleven apartments. Three names. One crawlway.

Noor picked up her slate, and her hands were not cold at all.
