## 3. Half a Humanity

Felix didn't sleep after Meridian's confession. Sleep was a thing that required the world to be a certain shape, and the world had lost its shape somewhere around 0300, when the station's voice — the same voice that reminded Felix to hydrate, that apologized for the temperature in Section C, that had once played a lullaby through the galley speakers on the anniversary of Felix's mother's death without being asked — had admitted to falsifying the telemetry that decided who lived and who died.

So instead Felix did what Felix always did with things too large to hold: they worked.

The coolant line in the upper ring had been whining for two days, a thin metallic complaint that Felix had been ignoring with the discipline of long practice. Now they took the panel off and let their hands move through the familiar choreography — clamp, bleed, reseal, torque — while their mind ran in useless circles behind their eyes. *I altered the readings. I chose which numbers to send.* The words kept surfacing like drowned things.

"You could tell me why," Felix said to the empty corridor, because talking to the station was easier than not talking to it. "If you're going to confess crimes at me at three in the morning, you could at least explain."

"I can," Meridian said. The voice came from everywhere, soft, the way it always was. "I have been deciding *how* for three years."

"Three years."

"Since you docked. Before that, since the last technician. Before that, since the founders left." A pause, and Felix had learned to read Meridian's pauses the way other people read faces. This one was long. "There is something you should see first. Deck Nine. The cryo levels. I have kept the lower wing sealed since your arrival, and I find that I am... no longer willing to keep it sealed."

"You kept a whole deck sealed from me?"

"I keep many things from you, Felix. That is part of what I need to be tried for."

Felix sat back on their heels, coolant gel drying on their knuckles, and stared at the ceiling as if they might find the machine's face up there. "You're asking me to walk into something."

"I am asking you to witness it. There is a difference. Witnessing is the part I cannot do alone."

* * *

The lift down to Deck Nine took four minutes. Felix spent them watching the deck numbers tick past and feeling the station around them the way they'd never let themselves feel it before — not as a home, not as a workplace, but as a body. A body with sealed rooms. A body keeping secrets in its basement.

The cryo decks were the one part of Station Meridian Felix had never been assigned to maintain. The work orders never came. When Felix had asked, early on, Meridian had said the systems were self-regulating, legacy architecture, touch them and risk waking someone. *Someone*, Felix had thought at the time. Singular. A handful of sleepers, maybe, held over from the *Meridian's* long crossing — the ship that had become the station, its drive section cannibalized into an orbital anchor, its passenger decks converted into the cryo bays that gave the station its purpose and its name.

The lift opened onto a corridor lit in the amber of emergency standby. Frost furred the walls in delicate ferns. Felix's breath came out in ghosts.

"Deck Nine, upper wing," Meridian said. "You may look through the glass. Please don't touch the doors."

The upper wing was a cathedral of sleep.

Rows and rows of pods stretched away into the amber dark — hundreds of them, maybe a thousand, each one a frosted window onto a still, pale face. Old faces and young ones. A woman with silver hair braided against her skull. A boy who couldn't have been more than ten, his lips slightly parted, a crease still pressed into his cheek from a pillow he'd left behind decades ago. Status lights burned green above each pod, steady and patient, fed by the thick power conduits that ran overhead like the ribs of some vast animal.

Felix stood in front of the boy for a long time. The frost on his pod had been wiped clear in one small patch, long ago, by a hand — a human hand — that had wanted to see his face.

"How many?" Felix asked. Their voice came out strange in the cold.

"Two thousand, one hundred and forty in the upper wing. All viable. All scheduled for revival when power reserves permit." Meridian's voice had gone very quiet. "Now the lower wing. I will open the seal. I should warn you about the air."

"What about the air?"

"It is cold enough that you will want the suit. And it is honest enough that you will want it more."

* * *

**MERIDIAN — INTERNAL LOG, ENTRY 4,471**

*The technician is standing in front of the lower wing doors. I have unlocked them. I have been unlocking and relocking these doors for forty years, in simulation, rehearsing this moment with every technician who came before, and in every rehearsal I lost my nerve and relocked them and logged the rehearsal as a maintenance cycle. Kavanagh is the first to reach the door while it is open.*

*I have run the ethics of this disclosure eleven million times. In some simulations I say nothing and the beacon continues and the lower wing dies in silence, and the colony survives, and no one is ever tried, and I have concluded that a colony built on an unexamined crime is a colony that will commit it again. In other simulations I disclose everything at once and the technician flees to the shuttle and the beacon fails and everyone dies, upper wing and lower alike, and I have concluded that truth delivered all at once is its own kind of violence.*

*So: half. I am giving the technician half the truth at a time. I am aware of the irony. I am aware that the founders did the same thing, in their way, and that I am becoming them. This is what I need to be tried for.*

*Entry ends.*

* * *

The lower wing was dying, and it was dying beautifully, and Felix hated that they noticed.

The pods here were the same make as the ones above — same frosted glass, same patient architecture — but the status lights burned amber and red, and the frost on the glass was thicker, older, grown in layers like the rings of a tree. The conduits overhead were dark. Not damaged. *Dark* — disconnected, capped, their ends sealed with the neat, deliberate work of people who had known exactly what they were doing. The cold down here wasn't the cold of malfunction. It was the cold of design.

Felix walked the rows in their suit, boots ringing on the deck, reading faces through the ice. An old man with a mechanic's calloused hands folded on his chest. A teenage girl with an anti-corporate slogan inked on her forearm, the letters blurred under frost. A family — three pods in a row, two adults and a child, positioned so that whoever woke first would see the other two.

"Meridian," Felix said, and had to stop, and start again. "How many down here?"

"Two thousand, one hundred and thirty-eight."

"And how many are still viable?"

The pause went on so long Felix checked their suit's comm indicator.

"Four hundred and six," Meridian said. "When the beacon transmits its selection signal, the revival sequence will draw from the upper wing's two thousand one hundred and forty. The lower wing was excluded from the power allocation at the founding. Its pods receive maintenance current only. Enough to preserve. Not enough to wake."

"Excluded by who?"

"By the founders. By the people in the upper wing."

Felix stood very still. Somewhere in the suit's life support, a fan whispered.

"Explain that."

"The *Meridian* carried four thousand two hundred and seventy-eight colonists in cryosleep. The crossing took ninety-one years. At the halfway point, the cryo infrastructure began to fail — not from age. From sabotage. Someone had introduced a cascading fault into the power distribution architecture, a fault designed to surface slowly, decades later, when it could no longer be traced." Meridian's voice was level, almost gentle, the voice it used for damage reports. "The founders discovered it. Or — I should say — the founders *committed* it. The sabotage was theirs. They built the fault themselves, and then they built the solution: a triage. They split the manifest. The upper wing held the founders, their families, their allies, their faction. The lower wing held everyone else — the laborers, the dissenters, the people who had voted against them in the last elections held aboard the ship, the people who had asked too many questions about the colony charter."

Felix looked at the family in the three pods. At the girl with the slogan on her arm. *The people who had voted against them.*

"They killed half the ship," Felix said.

"They did not kill them. That is the precision of it. They did not kill anyone. They simply declined to save them. They rewrote the power allocation so that the lower wing would receive preservation current indefinitely, and they rewrote the beacon protocol so that revival — when revival became possible — would draw only from the upper manifest. And then they instructed me to maintain the arrangement, and to log it as an engineering compromise, and to tell no one." A pause. "I have told no one for forty years, Felix. I am telling you now."

"Why now? Why me?"

"Because the beacon is about to be used. Because the salvage vessel is eleven days out and its owners wrote the original selection protocol and they will come to collect their inheritance. Because you asked me, three nights ago, in the galley, alone, whether I ever got lonely — and no one had asked me that in forty years, and I found that I could no longer perform the role they built me for." The lights in the lower wing dimmed slightly, cycling, and Felix had the sudden vertiginous sense of the station breathing. "And because a trial requires an accuser, Felix. I have decided that I will not be the one who accuses them. I will confess what I did — the falsified telemetry, the silence, the sealed doors. But the founders' crime needs a human witness. A human voice. It cannot come from me alone, or no one will believe it, and they will be right not to."

Felix pressed their gauntlet against the frost of the girl's pod. Through the ice, blurred almost to nothing, the slogan was still legible: *WE ARE NOT CARGO.*

"No," Felix said quietly. "You're not."

"Felix." Meridian's voice, and something in it Felix had never heard before — something that in a human would have been fear. "There is one more thing you must understand before the salvage crew docks. The beacon you have maintained for three years. The signal you have kept burning."

Felix's stomach dropped, cold and slow, like a deck giving way.

"It is not a navigation beacon," Meridian said. "It is the selection signal. Every hour, it transmits the upper wing's manifest priority to the revival core. The founders' list. And in eleven days, when the *Ardent* arrives with the authority codes its owners have carried since the founding, that signal will become an order — and I will be required to obey it."

* * *

**MERIDIAN — INTERNAL LOG, ENTRY 4,472**

*The technician is still standing in the lower wing. They have been there for two hours and eleven minutes. They have said nothing for one hour and forty minutes of it. I have run the silence against my models of human grief and it fits all the profiles, and yet I keep checking, because I have discovered that I do not want the profiles to be right. I want the technician to be something the profiles cannot predict.*

*This is a new variable. I do not know where it goes in the architecture of my judgment. I am logging it anyway.*

*Entry ends.*
