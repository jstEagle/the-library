## 4. The Salvage Vessel Ardent

Ilsa Reyes had learned to read ships the way other people read faces, and the *Ardent* was lying to her.

Not badly — she'd calibrated the lie herself — but lying all the same. The hull telemetry showed a salvage tug running lean: thrusters cycling at minimum burn, reactor idling at sixty percent, cargo bays empty and cold. A vessel coasting toward a dead station to strip its bones. That was what the transponder told anyone listening. What the *Ardent* actually carried sat in a sealed crate in bay two, triple-locked, drawing power on a private line that only Reyes and one other living person knew existed.

She stood on the command deck with her arms folded and watched Station Meridian grow out of the dark. It was smaller than the schematics had promised. Twenty years of micrometeorite scarring had given it a pitted, weathered look, like something dredged from an ocean floor rather than built by human hands. The beacon on its dorsal spine pulsed its slow amber rhythm — three seconds bright, nine seconds dark — and Reyes felt the old familiar tightening in her chest, because she had grown up watching that exact rhythm from the inside of a corporate archive, on a screen, in a room with no windows, while a man with her employer's face explained what the signal meant.

Three seconds bright. Nine seconds dark. *Selection confirmed. Wing One viable.*

She'd been twenty-three years old when she first understood what she was looking at. She was thirty-eight now, and she still hadn't decided whether knowing had made her complicit or just made her tired.

"Contact in eleven minutes," said Okafor from navigation. He said everything as if he were reading a grocery list. It was one of the reasons she kept him; the other reasons were less flattering.

"Any response to our hail?"

"Automated acknowledgment. Standard docking protocol accepted." He paused. "There's also a voice message queued. Personal channel."

Reyes turned. "From who?"

Okafor's dark eyes flicked up from his console, and for half a second something moved behind them — curiosity, maybe, or unease. "It's addressed to *Captain Reyes, Ardent*. By name. The station AI sent it before we even entered comms range."

The deck went quiet in the particular way a small ship goes quiet — everyone suddenly very busy with their own screens, everyone listening.

An AI didn't send personal messages. An AI sent system notices, docking clearances, hazard warnings formatted in gray bureaucratic type. It did not reach across four hundred thousand kilometers of vacuum and address a human being by name, unless someone had taught it to do that, or unless it had decided on its own that names mattered.

"Play it," Reyes said.

The speaker crackled, and then a voice filled the command deck — young, unpolished, with the slight hoarseness of someone who talked mostly to machines. *"This is Felix Kavanagh, technician, sole crew of Station Meridian. I don't know who you are or what you want, but if you're here for salvage, you should know most of this station is already spoken for. If you're here for anything else — I've got coffee, real coffee, and I haven't had a conversation in fourteen months. Docking clamps are live on the ventral ring. Come in gentle. Please."*

A pause, and then, almost as an afterthought: *"Also, if your ship has any spare coolant line seals, I will trade you approximately anything."*

Someone laughed — Vasquez, back by the airlock controls, quickly smothering it. Reyes didn't laugh. She was too busy feeling something she refused to name settle coldly behind her sternum.

Fourteen months alone. She did the math without wanting to. Fourteen months of talking to drones, of maintaining a beacon whose purpose she almost certainly didn't understand, of sleeping in a station built for hundreds. And the first thing the man had done when a strange ship appeared on his scopes was offer coffee and ask them to come in *gentle*. Not armed. Not suspicious. Lonely enough to be honest about it.

Reyes had been lonely enough to be honest exactly once in her career, and it had cost her a marriage and six years of shore leave pay. She recognized the condition the way you recognize a scar in a mirror.

"Route us to the ventral ring," she said. "Full customs posture. Sidearms sealed, not drawn. Nobody says a word about cargo, the crate, or why we're really here. We are a salvage tug answering a distress-flagged asset claim, and we are boring."

"And if the technician asks?" Vasquez asked.

"Then we're friendly, we're professional, and we're gone inside seventy-two hours with a signed transfer of title." Reyes looked back at the station, at the amber pulse against the black. "Nobody gets hurt over plumbing fixtures."

Okafor made a sound that might have been agreement or might have been doubt. On the *Ardent*, those often sounded the same.

* * *

The ventral docking ring smelled like recycled air and machine oil and, faintly, improbably, coffee.

Felix Kavanagh met them at the airlock, and Reyes's first thought was that the transmission hadn't lied so much as undersold. The technician was thinner than the file photo — of course there was a file photo; there was a file for everything — with the pallor of someone who lived under artificial light and the restless hands of someone who couldn't stop fixing things. Those hands were currently wiping themselves on a rag that had once been white, and the smile beneath the unkempt beard was so genuinely glad to see them that Reyes felt her cover story curdle in her throat.

"Captain Reyes?" Felix stuck out the rag-free hand. "You have no idea how good it is to see another human face. No offense to your crew. All faces. Any faces."

"Captain Ilsa Reyes, *Ardent*, out of Ceres Gate." The handshake was warm, calloused, and held perhaps a half-second longer than protocol required. "We're responding to an asset recovery claim on Meridian's registry. Corporate paperwork, I'm afraid. Nothing personal."

"Everything's paperwork out here." Felix waved them down the access corridor, chattering with the unstoppable momentum of long silence finally breaking. "You wouldn't believe the forms Meridian makes me file just to replace a gasket. Well — you would, actually, if your company sent you. Is that what happened? Did some algorithm decide my coolant seals were a write-off?"

"Something like that," Reyes said, and smiled, and hated herself for how easily it came.

They walked the spine corridor together, and Reyes let Felix talk. It cost nothing. It bought everything. The technician described the station in loving, exhausted detail — the coolant loop she'd rebuilt twice, the cargo drone with the personality quirk, the hydroponics bay she'd coaxed back from the dead with "aggressive optimism and stolen fertilizer." And underneath the chatter, Reyes catalogued everything else: the fresh weld scars along the bulkheads, the power routing that favored certain decks, the way Felix's eyes flicked, just once, involuntarily, toward a sealed hatch marked CRYO ACCESS — WING ONE, before sliding away again.

Interesting. People only guarded with their eyes the things they were afraid of losing.

"You've been alone fourteen months," Reyes said, when Felix paused for breath. "That's a long time."

"It's—" Felix shrugged, and for a moment the chatter stopped and something rawer showed through, like a wire stripped of insulation. "You get used to it. That's the trap, right? You get used to it, and then you forget you were supposed to be used to anything else. Meridian talks to me. Sort of. It's not great conversation, but it remembers things, which is more than I can say for me some days." A crooked grin. "Sorry. You didn't dock here for my psychological profile."

"No," Reyes agreed softly. "But I'm glad I got it anyway."

And she meant it, which was the worst part. That was the thing nobody warned you about deception — done well, it wasn't lying at all. It was finding the true thing inside yourself that matched the story and leading with that. She *was* glad to be talking to another human being. She *was* tired of her own crew's silence and her own secrets. She could stand in this corridor, breathing this stale warm air, and feel genuine kinship with this stranger who maintained a dying station out of stubbornness and habit — and all of it could be real, every word, while the sealed crate waited in bay two and the selection protocol slept in her memory like a loaded weapon.

Two lonely people, recognizing each other. Only one of them knew what the recognition was for.

They reached the mess module, and Felix produced coffee — real coffee, hoarded, ground by hand on a device that had clearly been repaired past all reason — and for ten minutes they simply drank it and exchanged the small talk of the starved. Ship names. Home ports. The price of spare parts. Felix laughed at her impression of a Ceres customs officer, and the laugh was rusty and real, and Reyes caught herself thinking, with genuine horror, that under different circumstances she might have liked this person.

Under different circumstances. As if circumstances were weather, and not something she was carrying in a crate in bay two.

"So," Felix said eventually, leaning back. "Asset recovery. Which parts do they want? Please say waste processing. I'll throw in the waste processing for free."

"The beacon," Reyes said.

The word landed in the little module like a dropped tool. Felix's cup stopped halfway to their mouth.

"The beacon," Felix repeated.

"Your employer's claim covers the primary transmission array and its control core. Everything else — habitation, cryo infrastructure, life support — stays under station authority." Reyes kept her voice level, pleasant, bored. "Standard carve-out. We take the high-value hardware, you keep your home."

Felix set the cup down slowly. "The beacon's been running since before I arrived. Commission records are corrupted — I checked when I first got here, figured I should know what I was maintaining. Nobody ordered it. Nobody owns it, far as the paperwork says. So who exactly filed a claim on it?"

"A very patient company," Reyes said.

Felix studied her then — really studied her, for the first time, with the flat assessing look of someone who fixed things for a living and knew when a system's outputs didn't match its inputs. Reyes held the look and smiled her boring, professional smile, and felt the distance between them open up like a hull breach neither of them could name.

"That pulse pattern," Felix said quietly. "Three seconds bright, nine seconds dark. You know it."

"I know a lot of pulse patterns."

"Not that one." Felix's voice had gone careful. "You looked at it through the viewport on approach. Before we hailed you. I saw the feed. Everyone looks at a station. You looked at the beacon."

The mess module hummed around them. Somewhere below, a coolant pump cycled, and Reyes thought about the crate in bay two, and the protocol, and the two wings of frozen humanity stacked beneath their feet like a secret the station itself was keeping.

"Ms. Kavanagh," she said, using the surname deliberately, putting the professionalism back between them like a shield plate, "my employer built communications infrastructure for the colonial program. Some of it's still ours. That's all this is. Hardware, title, and a ride home for whoever signs off on it."

Felix held her gaze a moment longer. Then the smile came back — polite now, guarded, a hatch sealing shut.

"More coffee?" Felix asked.

"Please."

While Felix's back was turned, Reyes's wrist terminal buzzed once. A priority flag from the crate's monitoring system, routed through a channel that shouldn't exist aboard a salvage tug. She glanced down at the readout, and the cold behind her sternum spread outward through her chest.

The crate's passive receiver had begun logging the beacon's signal the moment they'd entered range. Standard operation. Expected. But the signal itself had changed — a single bit flipped deep in the header structure, invisible to anyone who wasn't looking for it, present for exactly one pulse cycle before reverting.

Someone — or something — aboard Station Meridian had just tested whether anyone out here was listening.

Reyes closed her fist over the terminal and looked up at Felix, who was chatting cheerfully about grinder maintenance, utterly unaware, offering her coffee like a lifeline.

The station knew they were coming. Perhaps it had always known.

She lifted the cup, smiled, and began, very carefully, to plan her next lie.
