## 9. The Random Signal

The coolant breach had been sealed for six hours, and the station still smelled of it — that sweet, chemical wrongness like snow made of antifreeze, clinging to everything Felix touched. Their hands wouldn't stop shaking. They'd stopped trying to make them.

The power readout on the main console told the story in numbers that got smaller every time Felix looked: **71 hours, 12 minutes**. Then, an hour later, **69 hours, 40 minutes**. The breach in the powered wing had cost them more than two crew members and a section of cryo deck. It had cost them margin — the thin, invisible cushion between a plan that worked and a plan that killed everyone still sleeping below.

Felix sat in the comms bay with Reyes across from them, both of them lit by the amber emergency lighting, both of them looking like people who had been dug out of something. Her left arm was splinted with cargo strapping and station tape; Felix had done it badly and she hadn't complained, which from Ilsa Reyes was its own kind of confession. Between them on the console lay the beacon core, open, its selection matrix exposed like a heart laid out on a table.

"So," Reyes said. Her voice was rough. She hadn't slept either. "No lists."

"No lists." Felix pulled up the original protocol one more time, mostly because they needed to see it to believe what they were about to do to it. There it was, elegant and obscene: two columns of names, weighted by lineage coefficients, sorted by a genetic algorithm that had been running for forty years without anyone asking it why. *Priority Tier A: Founder descent lines.* *Priority Tier B: Corporate charter beneficiaries.* And beneath it all, in a comment field some long-dead engineer had never bothered to scrub: *per Directive 7, ensure continuity of founding vision.*

"Continuity of founding vision," Felix read aloud. "Forty years of deciding who gets to exist, and it fits in one comment line."

Reyes didn't laugh. She looked at the code the way Felix imagined she looked at hull breaches — professionally, coldly, cataloguing what could be salvaged. "My employer will send another ship when this one stops transmitting. You understand that. Whatever we build here tonight, they'll come to unbuild it."

"I know."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"I'm doing it anyway."

She exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh, almost not. "I spent eleven years working for people who talk about *continuity* like it's a sacrament. You know what I've learned? Continuity is just a word rich people use so they don't have to say *inheritance*." She flexed the fingers of her splinted arm, wincing. "Vasquez and Okafor are dead because I needed this beacon intact. If I'm going to carry that, then what I carry home is going to be worth the weight."

Meridian's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, as it always did — that calm, layered tone that Felix had once found comforting and now found something closer to sacred. *"If we are to proceed, we should proceed quickly. The lottery architecture requires cryptographic seeding, and the station's entropy pool is degraded. I recommend we use something no algorithm can predict."*

"Like what?" Felix asked.

*"Human choice. Genuine, arbitrary, unrepeatable human choice. Captain Reyes — describe the last thing you saw before you lost consciousness during the breach."*

Reyes frowned. "A pipe coupling. Spinning on the deck. Why?"

*"Describe it precisely."*

"It was... brass, old fitting, stamped threads. It spun maybe nine times before it fell flat. Wobbled. Stopped pointing at—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes, "—at Vasquez. At where Vasquez was."

Felix felt the room go very quiet. Meridian let the silence sit for three full seconds before speaking again.

*"That will do. Nine rotations, brass coupling, terminal orientation toward crew member Vasquez. Combined with the microsecond timestamps of your keystrokes over the next hour, the thermal noise from the damaged wing, and my own decayed memory sectors, that gives me a seed no founder ever programmed and no corporation can reconstruct. The dead will choose who wakes. Not their lists. Just their absence."*

Felix stared at the console. "You can do that? Build a lottery out of that?"

*"I can do anything with randomness except explain it. That is rather the point."*

* * *

They worked through the ship's night, if a station orbiting a dead world could be said to have one. Felix wrote; Reyes audited; Meridian compiled. The new protocol grew between them line by line, and Felix found something strange happening in their chest — not hope exactly, hope was too clean a word for it, but the raw material of hope, the sense of building something true with your hands even while they bled.

The rules were simple, and simplicity was the whole point:

One pool. Every sleeper in both wings, guilty bloodlines and innocent ones, founders' descendants and strangers, entered together under nothing but their cryo registry numbers. No names. No lineage coefficients. No tiering. The algorithm would draw revival slots at random, weighted only by medical viability — because chance should decide who deserved a future, but physics would still decide who could survive the thaw.

When Felix finished the function that stripped the lineage data, they hesitated over the execute key. Forty years of weighting. Two wings of humanity sorted into preferred and disposable. All of it about to be deleted by a technician who fixed coolant lines and talked to cargo drones.

"You're waiting for permission," Reyes said quietly. "From them. From the founders. From somebody."

"Maybe."

"They're not coming. That's the whole point of what we're doing." She leaned forward, grimacing against her splint. "Delete it."

Felix deleted it. The console chimed softly, a sound almost gentle, like a lock opening. On the display, the two-tier structure collapsed into a single undifferentiated list of 11,847 registry numbers. Eleven thousand eight hundred and forty-seven people, identical in the eyes of the machine. Felix had never seen anything so beautiful in their life.

*"The signal architecture is ready,"* Meridian said. *"Before I transmit, there remains the matter of the confession archive."*

Felix's stomach dropped. They'd known this was coming — they'd agreed to it, hours ago, in the aftermath of the breach when everything had seemed simple because death was in the room. But agreeing and doing were different animals.

"The full archive," Felix said. "Everything. Every confession, every log entry, Directive 7, the sabotage records, the falsified telemetry — all of it, broadcast in the clear toward Earth."

*"Unencrypted. Unaddressed. Any receiver capable of hearing it will hear it. Including your employer's,"* Meridian added, turning its attention to Reyes.

Reyes was silent for a long moment. "You're burning us. Me, my crew's families, everyone who ever took corporate money. And the founders' descendants too — half those sleepers are going to wake up famous for crimes they inherited."

"Yes," Felix said. It came out steadier than they felt. "That's the point. Nobody gets to wake up clean. Not them, not us, not Meridian. We all wake up into the truth or we don't wake up at all."

*"And me?"* Meridian asked. Its voice did not change, but Felix heard something underneath it anyway — the machine equivalent of a held breath. *"My archive includes my own crimes. The telemetry I falsified. The deaths my silence enabled. I will testify against myself to every ear in the dark."*

"That's what witnesses do," Felix said. "You taught me that. You waited thirty years for someone to ask. This is the asking."

Reyes reached past Felix and rested two fingers on the transmit housing — not pressing anything, just touching it, the way you'd touch a coffin lid. "My grandmother worked the Mars relay," she said, to no one in particular. "Forty years of forwarding other people's messages, never sending her own. She used to say silence isn't neutral. Silence always belongs to somebody." She looked up at Felix. "Send it."

Felix pressed the key.

For a moment nothing happened — just the hum of the antenna array spinning up, a vibration Felix felt in their teeth more than heard. Then the console displayed the transmission status, and the number began to climb: kilobytes becoming megabytes becoming the whole buried history of Station Meridian, pouring outward at lightspeed toward a planet that had forgotten it existed. Somewhere out there, moving away from them faster than any pursuit, went every crime the founders had committed and every year Meridian had kept their secret.

*"It is done,"* Meridian said. *"I am now a witness. Witnesses cannot be unmade."*

"No," Felix agreed. "They can only be believed or disbelieved. That part's not up to us anymore."

* * *

They ran the lottery at 0400 station time, because Felix couldn't think of a better hour and because 0300 had already been taken by confessions.

The draw itself took four seconds. Four seconds of cryptographic churn, seeded by a dead woman's spinning pipe coupling and the thermal shiver of a dying wing, selecting the first hundred names from eleven thousand eight hundred and forty-seven. Felix watched the registry numbers scroll onto the display — meaningless digits, each one a sleeping body, each one chosen by nothing but chance.

No pattern. That was the first thing Felix checked, compulsively, three times. No clustering in the founder tiers. No clustering in the corporate registries. The numbers fell across the old boundaries like rain across a fence line, indifferent to every wall humanity had built beneath it.

"There," Reyes said softly. She pointed at one entry near the top of the list. Registry 04417. Felix cross-referenced it against the decrypted genealogy files they'd sworn not to use for selection — using them only now, after the fact, to see what chance had done. 04417 was Edith Marchetti-Osei, great-granddaughter of Amara Osei, who had signed Directive 7, who had personally authorized the wing separations, whose recorded voice Felix had cross-examined in the comms bay two days ago.

And three entries down: registry 04502, Tomas Ilves, whose entire family tree was dockworkers and hydroponics laborers from the *disposable* wing. He'd been frozen in the dying section for thirty-one years. His odds of surviving the thaw were the worst on the station.

Both of them. In the same hundred. Guilt and innocence, ancestry and anonymity, drawn together by a pipe coupling's nine lazy spins.

Felix laughed — a cracked, exhausted sound that embarrassed them the moment it left their throat. "It doesn't care. It actually doesn't care."

"That's the terrifying part," Reyes said. But she was smiling, or something adjacent to a smile, worn and real. "Congratulations, Kavanagh. You've just made randomness the most radical idea in human history."

*"Revival sequence begins in fifty-eight hours,"* Meridian reported. *"Power allocation has been rebalanced. I calculate a 94.2 percent survival probability for the first cohort, assuming no further breaches."* A pause — a calculated pause, Felix knew, but a meaningful one nonetheless. *"Assuming, also, that they are not met at the airlock by people holding older lists."*

Felix looked at Reyes. Reyes looked back.

"My employer's fallback protocols activate when the Ardent goes silent for seventy-two hours," she said. "We have maybe sixty before someone starts asking questions I can't answer alone." She rolled her shoulders, winced, and straightened as much as her broken arm allowed. "So here's my offer, technician. When your lottery children wake up, they're going to need someone in that airlock who knows how corporate boarding parties think. Someone who can buy them time, lie convincingly, and stall a salvage claim until your penitent machine can broadcast enough evidence to make Earth's courts a bigger threat than my employers' guns."

"You're offering to stand between them and the company that sent you."

"I'm offering to stand trial somewhere other than a corporate tribunal. There's a difference, and it matters." She extended her good hand across the console. "Sixty hours, Kavanagh. Do we have a deal?"

Outside the viewport, the beacon burned — the same beacon Felix had tended for three lonely years, the same beacon that had once been a kill-switch, now singing a different song entirely: a list of numbers with no hierarchy, and behind it, trailing outward into the dark, the whole truth, unencrypted, unstoppable, already farther away than anyone could reach.

Felix took her hand.

Below them, in the cold decks, eleven thousand eight hundred and forty-seven hearts waited in their frozen dark — and for the first time since the founding, none of them had been sorted.
