## 6. The Dream Spreads

The dream came back on the fourth night after the glacier, and this time it brought friends.

Mara knew before she opened her eyes that something had changed, because the silence in the dormitory was wrong. The Quiet Season was supposed to be silent—recycled air humming, frost ticking against the outer panels, forty children breathing in the dark. Instead there was a sound like a held breath, like a whole room of people trying not to wake each other up.

She sat up. Across the aisle, Jem Okafor was sitting bolt upright in his bunk, staring at his hands as if they belonged to someone else. Two bunks down, Priya Chandrasekaran had her blanket pulled to her chin and her eyes wide open. And at the window, small and still as a figure in a snow globe, stood Tam Voss—Ilsa Voss's grandnephew, the boy who never spoke unless a teacher made him—looking out at the ice field as though he expected it to answer.

"You dreamed it," Mara said. Not a question.

"Sixth time," Tam said. "But tonight there were more of us. I could feel them. Like the city got... fuller."

Jem swung his legs over the side of his bunk. "Eleven," he said. "I counted. In the dream, at the plaza with the broken fountain, there were eleven of us standing in a circle. I could see all our faces."

Mara's skin prickled. "Five of us had it last week. Now eleven?"

"Now eleven," Priya whispered. "And it's not just that." She hesitated, and Mara saw that her hands were shaking inside the blanket. "The door was open."

* * *

They gathered at breakfast the way conspirators gather—loosely, in twos and threes, passing looks instead of words until the hydroponics bay emptied of adults and Mara could pull the five of them behind the tall racks of bean vines where the cameras didn't reach. The hydroponics bay was hers by apprenticeship; Supervisor Adeyemi trusted her with the nutrient lines and, more importantly, never came into the east annex before second shift. Eleven children fit between the vines if they were quiet about it.

She counted them herself, because she didn't trust Jem's dream-arithmetic: Mara, Jem, Priya, Tam, Devi Okonkwo-Bell, the Ferreira twins, little Sefu Mwangi who was only eight and still sucked his thumb when he thought no one watched, Hana Lindqvist, Marcus Reyes, and Odile Brant, who had transferred into their school cycle two months ago from the deep-mining families and whom nobody had expected to be part of anything.

Eleven. Exactly eleven. As if the dream had a roster.

"It's spreading," Devi said. She was the oldest of them, nearly fourteen, and she said everything with the flat certainty of someone reading off a manifest. "It started with five. Now eleven. Next week it'll be twenty. Someone should tell the council."

"Someone did," Marcus said bitterly. "I told Ms. Ferro. You know what she said?" He pitched his voice into a fair imitation of the schoolteacher's patient warmth. "'Dreams are how young minds process new information, Marcus. The glacier trip must have been exciting.'" He dropped the imitation. "Exciting. Like we went on a *field trip.*"

"We did go on a field trip," Priya pointed out.

"To a hatch we found because we dreamed its exact location down to the number of bolts holding it shut," Mara said. "That's not processing information. That's *having* information."

The word hung there among the bean vines. Having. Not making. Not imagining. The distinction mattered, and every one of them felt it, the way you feel a draft from a door left open somewhere in a warm house.

Tam hadn't spoken since the dormitory. Now he lifted his head, and Mara noticed again how much he looked like his great-aunt—the same narrow gray eyes, the same way of going very still when he was deciding something. "It changed," he said. "You all saw it. Say it out loud so we know we saw the same thing."

"The fountain plaza," Mara began, because she'd been the first dreamer and the others had agreed she'd go first. "Same as always. The drowned towers leaning over the water. The fish with the pale rings around their eyes. The street called Anselm's Reach running down toward—" She stopped.

"Toward the door," Sefu said softly, thumb halfway to his mouth, forgetting it.

"In every dream before," Mara said, "the door at the bottom of Anselm's Reach was closed. Round, metal, green with age, sealed with a wheel-lock like an old submarine hatch. Closed. I've seen it closed six times. I could draw it in my sleep, which is apparently what I do."

"And last night," Devi said, "it was open."

"Open," Priya confirmed. "Not broken. Not forced. Opened—from the inside—and swung back against the wall, and there was light coming out of it. Warm light. Like a room with a lamp on somewhere below."

"Warm light underwater," Jem said. "At that depth. Explain that."

Nobody explained it. That was the thing about the eleven of them now—they had stopped needing explanations from each other. What they needed was one adult, any adult, who would look at the evidence instead of through it.

* * *

They tried. That was the awful, humiliating part, looking back on it. They tried so hard, and so politely.

Mara went first, because she was the one with standing—an apprentice with a clean record, a supervisor who liked her. She caught Supervisor Adeyemi between nutrient cycles and told him everything: the shared dream, the identical details, the street names, the hatch on the glacier shelf that matched, and now the open door. She watched his face do the thing adult faces did, the slow rearrangement from interest to concern to that final settled kindness that was worse than disbelief.

"Mara." He crouched to her level, which she hated, because it turned her into a child being handled. "Twenty-two kids your age live in this colony. You eat together, sleep in the same dormitory wing, breathe the same air, hear the same stories. It's completely normal for you to share dreams. There's even a name for it—collective dreaming under shared environmental conditions. The founders wrote about it."

"The founders also wrote about the founding date," Mara said, "and the settlement under the ice is older than the founding date."

Something flickered behind his eyes then—not dismissal. Something quicker and less comfortable. But he smoothed it away almost instantly, and that smoothness frightened her more than any argument would have. "That's a matter for the survey teams," he said. "Not for apprentices. And definitely not for dreamers. Finish your filtration report, all right?"

Devi tried next, with her mother, who worked comms. Her mother listened to the entire account without interrupting—which Devi reported afterward with grim pride—and then said, "Sweetheart, if you're worried about the beacon, talk to the beacon keeper," which was such a non-answer that Devi suspected her mother had been coached. By whom, she didn't say.

Marcus tried with Doctor Hale, who was everyone's favorite adult in the colony, the man who set broken arms and told jokes while he did it and kept a jar of real sugar candies on his desk for scared kids. Marcus came back from that meeting looking like he'd been slapped.

"He didn't even let me finish," Marcus said. "The second I said 'eleven of us,' he stopped writing. He put his pen down. And he said, 'Marcus, has anyone been telling you stories about the old days?' Like someone had *given* us the dream. Like it was a rumor going around the schoolyard." Marcus's jaw worked. "He asked me who else had it. He wanted names. I gave him none."

"Why would he want names?" Priya asked, and nobody answered, but everybody thought the same thought at the same time, and the thought had teeth.

Only Tam said nothing about trying. When Mara asked him directly—he was sorting seed pods beside her in the annex, the closest thing to privacy either of them got—he shook his head once.

"My aunt says curiosity is a luxury," he said. "She says it at dinner like other people say grace." He lined up three pods on the tray, precise as bullets. "I'm not telling her anything. Ever."

* * *

What Mara didn't know—what none of them knew for another two days—was that the monitoring had already begun.

It started small, the way these things do. The dormitory's environmental sensors were recalibrated "for the heating season." The school's free-study hour acquired a new rule: no unsupervised trips, even within the habitat ring. A maintenance crew spent an afternoon in the corridor outside the hydroponics east annex, and when Mara asked what they were fixing, they looked at each other before answering, which maintenance crews never did, because maintenance crews always knew exactly what was broken.

Then, on the sixth day, Devi's mother forgot to close her terminal before leaving the family quarters, and Devi—who was fourteen and had been raised by a comms officer and could read a directory tree the way other kids read comics—found a file she wasn't supposed to see.

She brought it to the vine rows printed on actual paper, which was the most alarming thing about it. Nobody printed anything anymore. Printing meant you wanted something that couldn't be remotely deleted.

The header read **QUIET SEASON — JUVENILE BEHAVIORAL ANOMALY — INTERNAL**. Below it, a list. Eleven names. Their names. Beside each name, columns: sleep-cycle disruption, reported content overlap, peer association map, recommended observation level. Every child on the list had the same recommendation except one. Ten entries read *passive monitoring*. One read *active*.

Beside Tam Voss's name, in the notes field, someone had written: *family aware. cooperative.*

"That's why he wouldn't tell his aunt," Priya breathed. "He already knew."

"No," Devi said slowly. "Look at the phrasing. *Family aware. Cooperative.* That's not him reporting to her. That's her reporting on him. To whoever compiled this." She tapped the signature block at the bottom of the page, and Mara felt the floor tilt gently under her, the way it did in dreams.

**Authorized: I. VOSS, Council Chair.**

Voss. The woman whose face was on the habitat ring's welcome display. The woman who had shaken every colonist's hand at Last Landing Day. The woman who signed the colony's official communications with a stylized flourish that half the children could draw from memory.

"She knows," Mara said. The vines hummed their faint nutrient-hum around them, indifferent. "She's known since the beginning. Since before the glacier, maybe. And instead of asking us what we're seeing, she's—" Mara searched for the grown-up word and hated that she had to use it. "*Observing* us. Like we're a leak."

"Like we're a symptom," Devi corrected. "There's a line in the summary section. Want to hear it? 'Assess whether shared content originates from external signal intrusion or internal contagion. Recommend containment of narrative spread pending council review.'"

"Containment," Jem repeated. "Of a narrative. Of *us*."

"They think we're making it up," Priya said. "Or they're afraid we're not."

That was the sentence that stayed. Mara carried it home that night through the corridors, past the mural of the Founding—forty smiling settlers in front of a lander, the date stenciled beneath them, the date that was younger than the city under the ice—and she understood, finally, the shape of the trap they were all standing in.

If the dream was nonsense, the adults were right to dismiss it, and eleven children were simply sick together in some harmless psychic way. Fine. Manageable. Containable.

But if the dream was true—if there really was a drowned city older than the colony, a door that opened itself, warm light burning at the bottom of the sea—then every adult who waved them away wasn't being kind or tired or scientifically sensible. They were being *careful*. And people are only careful about things they already know.

* * *

The dream came again that night, all eleven of them, and this time Mara did what she had never done in any dream before: she fought it. Not to wake—she'd learned that was useless—but to steer. She stood in the fountain plaza with the pale-ringed fish drifting past her shoulders and she refused to look down Anselm's Reach, refused to walk toward the open door, and instead turned and walked *up*, toward the highest tower, the one whose spire broke the surface of the dream-sea at low tide.

The others followed her. She felt them follow, the way you feel a hand take yours in the dark.

From the tower's broken crown she could see the whole drowned city spread beneath the black water: streets in a grid too regular for accident, plazas, a stadium shape, a long avenue leading out of the city toward the horizon—toward, she realized with a lurch, the direction that on the waking world's maps was *up-glacier*. Toward the hatch. The city and the colony were not neighbors. The city was underneath. The colony had been built on top of it the way you build a stage set over an abandoned house.

And far out, at the edge of the water where the dream always faded into static, she saw something new.

A light. Not the warm lamplight of the open door. A cold blue pulse, rhythmic, patient, sweeping across the drowned streets like a searchlight mounted on something moving. Something big, out in the dark water, circling the city the way the scout drones circled the colony above.

Ten pulses. Eleven. Counting them the way Jem counted them in the circle at the fountain.

Mara woke with her heart slamming and lay still in the dark, listening to the dormitory's held breath, and understood that the dream was not finished with them. It was a message still arriving, delivered nightly, and it had gotten as far as the door. Whatever came next would come through it.

At breakfast, Devi slid a folded scrap across the table. On it, in her cramped comms-officer handwriting: *Beacon maintenance log accessed overnight. Keeper Whitlock pulled 40 years of transmission records at 0300. Alone.*

Mara read it twice. The adult world was starting to move—Voss watching the children, the keeper digging through the archive, the doctor collecting names—and the eleven of them were the only ones who knew the ground all of it stood on was hollow, and drowning, and lit from below by a door that had opened itself.

"We need to talk to Whitlock," she said.

Jem snorted. "The clone? He won't even look at us. Adults treat him like furniture with feelings."

"Adults treat *him* like a lie they have to feed," Mara said. "Which means he might be the only one who wants to know what's true." She folded Devi's scrap into her sleeve. "Tonight. After the dream. We find out what he pulled out of those records—and we show him the door."

Above them, muffled by meters of ice and insulation, the beacon pulsed on toward two fleets that believed two different lies, and beneath them, in the dark water, something counted to eleven and waited.
