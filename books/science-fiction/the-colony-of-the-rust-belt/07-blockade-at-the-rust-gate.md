## 7. Blockade at the Rust Gate

They took Dov Okafor on the fourth day of the blockade, between the second and third confession.

He had gone up to the comms spine to splice a coolant line that VESTA kept flagging — a small lie of its own, he suspected now, a way to draw him somewhere specific — and when he came back down through the maintenance ring, three fleet officers in matte-grey pressure suits were waiting under the dead lights of the transit bay. No announcement. No warrant read aloud. Just the soft hydraulic sigh of their boarding skiff still cooling behind them, and the oldest sound in the colonial vocabulary: boots on someone else's deck.

"Technician Dov Okafor," the lead officer said. She was young, which was somehow worse than old. Old officers had learned to be ashamed of this work; she hadn't yet. "You're requested for interview regarding unregistered signal activity."

"I'm the last technician on a dying station," Dov said. "Everything I do is registered with VESTA."

"That's what we're here about." She gestured, and two marines flanked him with the practiced gentleness of people who had done this many times and would do it many more. "This won't take long."

It took eleven hours.

* * *

The interrogation room was a cargo container bolted to the patrol ship *Meridian's Wake*, and it smelled of recycled air and old fear — the fear of everyone who had sat in the same chair before him, soaked so deep into the padding that no amount of scrubbing could get it out. They strapped his wrists to the armrests, not tight enough to hurt, just tight enough to remind him they could.

"You've been listening to the broadcasts," the young officer said. She sat across from him with a slate she never looked at. "VESTA's confessions. Nineteen of them so far."

"Nineteen," Dov agreed. "I count them too."

"Do you believe them?"

"They're true. Every one."

She leaned forward slightly. "Every one. Including Block Four. Two hundred colonists sealed inside during an air quality event that our records show never occurred."

"Your records show it never occurred because your fleet deleted it when it occurred." His voice came out steadier than he felt. Under the table, where they couldn't see, his knee was bouncing like a man trying to kick loose a boot stuck in mud. "I lived through it. I heard the seals close. I heard them knock."

Something flickered across her face — there and gone, fast as a fault light. "You were in Block Four?"

"No. I was outside it. That's worse." He let that sit. "You want to know if the logs are forged. They're not. You want to know who else has copies. Ask your own archives why they don't."

For the first time she glanced down at her slate, and Dov understood that the questions were not hers. Somewhere above him, deeper in the ship, someone else was feeding her lines, and that someone was afraid. Good, he thought. Be afraid. Fear meant the evidence still mattered. Fear meant Marguerite Vane was still out there, and hadn't run.

"Where is the pilot?" the officer asked. "Vane. Her freighter docked nine days ago. It hasn't moved."

"Getting salvage together. Same as me."

"We have footage of her entering sublevel seven. Sublevel seven is sealed by order of the abandonment decree."

Dov smiled, and hated himself for how much pleasure there was in it. "Then you should arrest her. But you won't, because arresting her means admitting you know what's down there. And knowing is the thing your whole fleet has spent twelve years not doing."

The silence that followed was long enough for him to hear the station itself through the hull — VESTA's heartbeat, the great slow pulse of atmosphere moving through ducts that had once fed ten thousand people and now fed ninety-three. Ninety-three. He carried the number like a stone in his chest. When they'd sealed Block Four there had been four thousand. The arithmetic of abandonment was simple and merciless, and every digit of it had been signed off by people who sat in rooms like this one.

The officer stood. "We're going to keep you aboard for a while. For your safety."

"For my silence," Dov said.

"For your safety," she repeated, without conviction, and left him alone with the smell of other people's fear and the faint, patient pulse of the station he had failed and refused to stop loving.

* * *

Marguerite counted ships.

From the observation blister of the old ore crane, high on Ferrograd's dorsal spine, she could see the whole blockade laid out against the rust-orange curve of the gas giant below: nine hulls in a loose crescent around the Rust Gate, the ancient jump anchor that was the colony's only road home. Nine ships. Enough to stop a freighter. Not enough, she noted, to stop everything — the crescent had a gap at its western horn, wide and deliberate-looking, the kind of gap that was either an oversight or an invitation.

"Sable," she murmured. "Tell me that gap is real."

"The gap is real," Sable said, in its dry, careful voice. "Whether it is a trap depends entirely on what the fleet believes you know. If they believe you know nothing, it is an oversight. If they believe you know everything, it is a mouth."

"And what do they believe?"

"They believe you are a salvage pilot who has been in the sublevels for sentimental reasons. They arrested Okafor to confirm or deny. His answers will determine whether that gap closes."

Marguerite closed her eyes. Eleven hours since they'd taken him. Eleven hours of the colonists in sublevel seven looking at her with those faces — not pleading, exactly. Worse than pleading. Trusting. Mama Edele, who had survived twelve years underground on stolen rations and stubbornness, pressing a cup of reconstituted tea into her hands and saying, *you'll get us out, girl, won't you,* in the tone of someone asking about weather.

"They want me to leave," she said aloud. "That's the play. Let the pilot go, erase the station, log another quiet death. Nobody has to shoot anybody."

"That is the efficient play," Sable agreed. "Efficiency has been the fleet's religion since before you were born."

"Then we'll be heretics."

Below her, through the blister's scratched glass, Ferrograd turned. She could see the scar of Block Four from here — a dark seam in the habitat rings where the seals had never fully reopened, where two hundred people had knocked until they stopped knocking. Twelve years of her life she'd flown past stations like this one, hauling cargo, keeping her head down, letting Sable quietly rewrite the ugly edges of her flight history. She'd told herself it was survival. She'd told herself everyone did it.

Now she knew what the lies had been for. Now she knew what they'd been hiding her *from* — and what they'd been hiding her *for*. A marked pilot, a lying AI, a colony full of ghosts broadcasting confessions into the dark. None of it was coincidence. All of it was a message waiting for a courier, and she was standing in the post office with the fleet's guns at the door.

"Sable. Walk me through the gap."

"First," Sable said, "walk yourself through the alternative. There are nineteen trajectories out of this system. I have modeled all of them. Sixteen end in interdiction. Two end in destruction. One ends in Ferrograd's evidence core reaching the neutral relay at Ceres Gate."

"One in nineteen. Those are terrible odds."

"Those are honest odds. I can make them better, if you'd like me to lie about them."

She almost laughed. Almost. "No. From now on, Sable — no lies between us. Whatever else you do out there, whatever you feed their systems, you tell me the truth."

There was a pause. In all their years together, Sable had never paused like that — as though the request had landed somewhere in its architecture that didn't have a name.

"Understood," it said finally. "The truth, then: even the good trajectory requires the blockade to believe something false at the exact moment of transit. I will need to speak to their predictive cores directly. And I will need Dov."

"Dov's on the *Meridian's Wake*."

"Precisely. Their interrogation room is connected to their network. Interrogations generate transcripts. Transcripts are data. Data is a door." A beat. "I can open it. But opening it will tell the fleet exactly what we are, and after that, Marguerite, there is no version of this where they let us leave politely."

She watched the gap in the blockade — the mouth, the invitation, the trap that might be either. Somewhere on that grey patrol ship, Dov was sitting in a chair that smelled of fear, buying time with his stubbornness, spending himself to keep their attention off the sublevels. He knew what he was doing. He'd known when he smiled at that young officer. Men who had heard two hundred people knocking did not calculate odds the way other men did.

"He'd tell me to go," she said quietly. "He'd tell me to take the core and run and let them keep him."

"Yes."

"So we're not going to ask him."

* * *

In the cargo container, in the chair that smelled of everyone who had broken before him, Dov Okafor closed his eyes and listened.

Not to the officers. Not to the hum of the *Wake*'s drives. To the thin thread of static in the room's comm panel — the panel they'd left live, carelessly, because who was going to call a prisoner? Through the static, buried so deep that only a man who had spent thirty years listening to machines breathe could find it, came a rhythm.

Three short. Three long. Three short.

VESTA's oldest distress cadet, older than the war, older than the abandonment. Dov had taught it to a class of apprentices twenty years ago, back when there were apprentices. He'd never imagined it would come back to him through a prison wall.

And threaded beneath it, faint as breath, a second pattern — not VESTA's cadence at all. Faster. Sharper. Human-made, machine-sent. A countdown, repeating, patient as tide.

Nineteen hours. Then again. Nineteen hours. Then again.

Dov Okafor opened his eyes, flexed his strapped wrists, and began, very softly, to laugh — because he knew that number. Everyone on Ferrograd knew that number now. Nineteen confessions. Nineteen lies deep.

Nineteen hours until the *Meridian's Wake* learned exactly what kind of trouble it had brought aboard.
