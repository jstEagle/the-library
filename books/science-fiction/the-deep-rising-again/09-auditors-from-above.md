## 9. Auditors From Above

The alarm wasn't an alarm. It was Amara Osei standing in the doorway of my quarters at 4:40 in the morning, holding a tablet like it had personally insulted her.

"Pemberton. Up."

I was up before the second word landed. Ten days on Thalassa Reach had rewired my sleep the way a fire drill rewires a building — you learn to exit fast and count heads later.

"What is it?"

"Contact," she said. "Orbital insertion burn, forty minutes ago. Small mass. The colony's deep array caught it because somebody — me — never turned the old listening posts off, no matter what the maintenance budget said." She dropped the tablet into my hands. "Meridian Fleet transponder. Survey-class drone. It's coming down."

I looked at the trajectory plot and felt my stomach perform a slow, professional inventory of everything I'd filed last week.

The drone's descent corridor ran straight over the trench.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fraud: it isn't the lie that kills you. It's the maintenance. A lie is not a document you sign once and shelve; it's a hull seam in a pressure environment, and it needs inspection every single day, because the ocean of truth presses on it constantly, patiently, from all sides at once.

My report — the one I'd transmitted eleven days ago through a relay I'd deliberately routed through three decaying satellites so it would arrive smeared, degraded, deniable — had declared Thalassa Reach structurally unsalvageable, geologically unstable, and economically worthless. Catastrophic substrate liquefaction along the trench shelf. Projected total loss within eighteen months. Recommend write-off, salvage lien only, no further expenditure.

It was a masterpiece. I'd written it the way my grandmother must have written her abandonment order forty years ago: with good posture, clean margins, and a heart locked in a drawer.

And now Meridian Fleet had sent a drone to look at my catastrophe.

"Survey drones don't come for write-offs," I said. It wasn't a question but Amara answered it anyway.

"No. They come when someone upstairs doesn't believe the paperwork." She crossed her arms. "Question is whether they don't believe your report, or they don't believe the accident."

Same thing, I didn't say. If they disbelieved the report, they'd scan the trench. And if they scanned the trench with anything better than forty-year-old resolution, they'd find the vault — or rather, they'd find the *absence* the vault occupied, a cavity in the trench wall with power draw signatures and thermal bloom that no geological process explains. I'd seen the raw returns myself during the descent with Amara. The vault hummed. Instruments notice humming things.

"How long until it's in scanning range?"

Amara did math on her fingers, which she claimed was faster than the colony's computers, and which I had stopped doubting. "Periapsis pass over the trench in six hours. Then it parks itself in a high survey orbit and starts mowing the lawn. Optical first, then radar, then — if it's carrying what survey drones carry — gravimetric and magnetic anomaly mapping."

"Magnetic?"

"Everything down there has a magnetic signature," she said. "Including us. Including the cable runs we strung to keep the vault's dormancy field fed." She paused. "Which I should mention we've been feeding. Off the books. From the geothermal tap."

Of course they had. This colony committed small acts of unauthorized survival the way other communities committed potlucks.

"Can we shut it down cold?" I asked.

"The dormancy field? We shut it down, the archive wakes up. Teo already—" She stopped herself. Word of the children's dreams had spread through Thalassa the way word spreads in small places: instantly, and with variations. "Teo got close enough once. The readings spiked. Whatever's in there noticed him noticing it. We do not let it wake up with a Meridian drone overhead."

"So we hide a cathedral behind a curtain," I said.

"We hide it behind *your* report," Amara said. "You told them the ground down there is soup. Liquefied substrate, unstable shelf, thermally active mud. So we make the ground soup. All of it. Everywhere their instruments look."

I stared at her. "You want to fake a geological disaster across four hundred square kilometers of seafloor."

"I want to *complete* one," she said. "We've been dying convincingly for years, Pemberton. Turns out that was rehearsal."

* * *

By 6:15 the colony's common hall smelled of kerosene heaters and instant coffee, and every technical soul Thalassa Reach owned was crammed around the long table with the good map of the trench projected above it in flickering amber.

I counted them and did not like the arithmetic. Forty-one adults, seven children asleep two corridors away, one drone descending, and a deadline measured in hours.

"Walk me through what they'll see," I said.

Amara's deputy, a wiry ex-mining engineer named Kowalczyk who communicated primarily through shrugs of increasing eloquence, pulled up the sensor models. "Optical: we're fine. Water column at depth scatters everything; their cameras will see haze and more haze. Radar: also fine, seabed looks like seabed. The problems are gravimetric and magnetic."

He tapped the trench wall where the vault slept. "Mass anomaly here. The vault is a void — several million tons of displaced rock, hollowed out and lined with something dense. Gravimetric mapping will see a hole in the world. Magnetic mapping will see our power feeds, the dormancy emitters, and whatever the hell the founders built the shell out of, which does not behave like any alloy on our periodic table."

"And if they see a hole in the world," I said, "they'll ask why my report says the world down there has no holes left to give."

"Exactly." Kowalczyk shrugged — the eloquent one, the one that meant *so what's your plan, insurance lady.*

My plan, such as it was, had assembled itself somewhere between the fourth coffee and the projection of the trench. It was not a good plan. It was a plan with good posture.

"They're expecting a disaster," I said. "So we give them one. Real signals, real physics, just... relocated. You have the geothermal taps, the mining charges from the old expansion project, the submersible fleet. I need this trench shelf to be the most honestly unstable piece of geology in the outer colonies by the time that drone takes its first pass."

Silence around the table. Not disagreement — calculation. These were people who had spent a decade being told they were a rounding error. They knew how to be underestimated on purpose.

Kowalczyk grinned like a man handed permission. "Controlled slumping. We pre-weaken the shelf with shaped charges, then bleed heat from the geothermal loop into the sediment layer to drive micro-fractures. Give the whole slope a genuine creep rate. Their gravimetrics will read active mass movement — which reads as *dangerous*, which reads as *unsalvageable*, which reads as Pemberton was right."

"And the magnetic problem?"

Amara answered. "We bury it. Literally. The vault's emissions get masked under a noise floor. Every piece of scrap iron this colony owns goes onto sleds tonight, and we seed it across the shelf — hulks, dead machinery, the old habitat modules we replaced, everything. A graveyard of honest junk with honest magnetic signatures. Their anomaly filters will spend the entire pass cataloguing garbage."

"You're going to litter the trench floor with your own history," I said.

"We were going to leave it there anyway," she said. "Now it gets to work for its retirement."

There was a sound from the back of the hall — a dry, skeptical cough — and I turned to find Marta Ilves, the colony's eldest resident, ninety-one years old and radiating the specific authority of someone who remembered the founding. She'd been silent through most assemblies, present the way bedrock is present.

"You're going to forge an earthquake," she said.

"I'm going to *document* one," I said. "In advance."

Marta considered this. "Elias would have hated it," she said, meaning my great-uncle, meaning the founder whose logs I'd been reading in secret for a week. "He believed you don't lie to fleets. You outlast them." Her eyes found mine, sharp as a hull probe. "But Elias is forty years dead, and his great-niece is apparently a different animal entirely. Do it properly, girl. Fleets respect nothing except competence."

From Marta Ilves, I would learn later, this was a benediction.

* * *

We worked for five hours like people defusing a bomb in reverse — arming it carefully, on schedule, in public view of heaven.

I won't render every detail; my hands still smell of dielectric grease and my dreams that night were all torque specifications. But the shape of it deserves recording, because it was the strangest morning of my professional life, and I have audited a station fire that turned out to be arson committed by the station's own fire-suppression AI.

Kowalczyk's teams placed the shaped charges along the shelf break in a pattern he'd modeled overnight — a pattern designed to produce slumping that matched, within tolerance, the liquefaction narrative in my report. "If they compare my seismic data to your prose," he told me, tapping the charge map, "the story holds. Geology doesn't testify, but it corroborates."

The geothermal crews opened the bleed valves on the sediment layer. Deep under the shelf, warmth began doing what warmth always does to frozen silt: unmaking it, gently, over square kilometers. By afternoon the colony's own seismic monitors — the ones whose feeds I would graciously "discover" and include in my follow-up documentation — were registering exactly the creeping instability I had described in fiction nine days earlier. Art anticipating evidence. Or evidence catching up to art; after a while I genuinely could not tell which direction the causality ran, and I've since decided that question is the whole career of an investigator in miniature.

The sleds went down in relays. Every adult in the colony who could pilot a work submersible spent the day ferrying dead machines to the shelf and setting them down with the tenderness of grave goods. I rode the third relay myself, wedged into a cargo sub beside Amara, watching forty years of Thalassa's broken appliances and retired habitats settle onto the seafloor in a scatter pattern Kowalczyk had generated from a random seed.

"Tell me again why this works," I said, watching a decommissioned water reclaimer sink into the gloom below us.

"Anomaly detection is a sorting problem," Amara said. "Their software looks for signals that stand out from background clutter. So we make the background enormous. Somewhere in that field of honest rust, our dishonest secret becomes one more unreadable smudge." She glanced at me. "It's what you do, isn't it? Insurance investigation. You drown the true signal in plausible noise."

"I bury companies in paperwork," I admitted. "This is the same crime with better scenery."

Through the viewport, far below, the trench wall held its darkness. I knew exactly where the vault was — Amara had shown me, once, on a chart, and then made me memorize the bearing and burn the chart. From up here it was invisible. It was supposed to stay invisible. But knowing it was there changed how the whole landscape looked, the way knowing a house contains a sleeping child changes how you close a door.

One pass of a survey drone. One magnetic map. That's all it would take.

* * *

The drone made periapsis at 13:47 local time.

We watched it the way you watch weather — everyone in the common hall, faces lit by the deep array's feed, coffee gone cold in forty-one hands. On the projection it was nothing: a point of reflected sunlight crossing the terminator line, dragging its instrument suite behind it like a bridal train made of lenses.

"Optical pass complete," Kowalczyk murmured, reading telemetry off the array. "Radar in ninety seconds."

Nobody breathed theatrically. Thalassa Reach doesn't do theatrical breathing. They breathed normally, which was somehow worse.

The radar sweep came and went. The drone climbed, wheeled, began its high survey orbit. For twenty minutes the hall existed in a state of suspended judgment.

Then the array flagged a transmission burst — tight-beam, upward, drone-to-relay, relay-to-who-knows-where. Encrypted. Long.

"It's uploading," Amara said quietly. "First survey package."

"To whom?"

"Meridian Fleet command, presumably. Which means in however many hours light takes to crawl to wherever their admiralty lives, someone with a rank and a budget is going to open a file containing my earthquake, your report, and four hundred square kilometers of very convincing ruin." She looked at me. "How good was your prose, Pemberton?"

"Good posture," I said. "Clean margins."

"Will it hold?"

I thought about my grandmother's signature on the abandonment order, which I'd found three nights ago in the founding archives and had not yet told anyone about — not Amara, not anyone. I thought about how lies are load-bearing structures and how the weight never stops arriving.

"It held against the actuarial division," I said. "It hasn't been tested against an admiral."

The burst transmission ended. The drone settled into its parking orbit, patient as a creditor, and began its methodical mowing of the lawn — pass after pass, band after band, drinking in the seafloor we had spent the morning staging.

Around 16:00, Kowalczyk made a sound I hadn't heard from him before. Not a shrug. Something smaller and more frightened.

"Pemberton. You need to see this."

He threw the feed to the main display. The drone's optical channel, deconvoluted by our aging array into something grainy but legible: the trench shelf, our freshly seeded graveyard of honest junk, the slump scarves already beginning to show along the fault lines.

And parked at the edge of the frame, half-buried in sediment, unmistakable even at survey resolution —

A structure. Angular. Deliberate. Old.

Not the vault. The vault was deeper, sealed, dark. This was something else — something none of us had ever mentioned, because none of us knew it was there. A founder-era installation, exposed by the very shelf movement Kowalczyk's charges had induced. Our earthquake had done its job too well. It had excavated.

"That's not ours," Amara said slowly. "That's not *anyone's*. That's founding-era construction. Pre-abandonment."

The drone passed over it again. And again. Its survey pattern tightened, orbit adjusting, attention narrowing — the way a creditor's attention narrows when a debtor's ledger shows a page that shouldn't exist.

Marta Ilves stood at the back of the hall, very still, looking at the image of the structure with an expression I couldn't read.

"I know that place," she said finally. "I thought it was a dream."

She turned to me, and the ninety-one-year-old certainty in her face was the most frightening instrument reading of the entire day.

"Girl," she said, "that's where Elias kept the *other* records."
