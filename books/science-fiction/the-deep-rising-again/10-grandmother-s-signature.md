## 10. Grandmother's Signature

The founding records lived in the colony's administrative core, a room Amara called "the morgue" because everything in it was dead paper that refused to decompose. Sebastian had been given access on her second night, as a courtesy — *full transparency, Investigator, we have nothing to hide* — and she had smiled and said something professional and had not gone near the place for six days.

She went now, at 2:40 in the morning, because she could not sleep, and because the question had grown teeth.

It was a small thing. A discrepancy. In Elias Pemberton's logs — the logs she'd spent three nights reading in the archive vault's antechamber, printed on paper that crackled like old leaves — his sister was listed as *Margaret Pemberton-Voss, Fleet Actuarial Command*. His brother-in-law as *Deputy Director of Meridian Colony Services*. Family names, family faces, threaded through Thalassa's founding like rebar through concrete.

And Sebastian's grandmother — her father's mother, the woman who'd taught her to fold paper cranes, who'd died when Sebastian was nine, who smelled of bergamot and cold iron — had been named Voss before she married.

The coincidence had been sitting in her chest for days like a swallowed stone. Coincidences were what investigators were trained to eat for breakfast. But this one wouldn't digest.

The administrative core hummed around her, climate-controlled to two degrees cooler than comfort, its server stacks blinking amber in the dark. She woke the terminal with a touch and pulled up the founding charter index. Forty-one documents. Ratifications, supply manifests, crew rosters, the joint fleet accord that had bound Meridian and Halcyon into an alliance neither had wanted and both had needed.

Document thirty-seven was sealed. Access restricted. She almost laughed — a dead colony, a dying colony, and they'd still bothered with access restrictions. Bureaucracy outliving the people it served.

But Thalassa Reach had been abandoned by its owners, not its administrators, and the local override codes still worked. Amara had shown her that much, weeks ago, without knowing why it mattered. *Everything here still runs*, she'd said. *That's the tragedy of it. Nothing down here has been told it's allowed to stop.*

Sebastian entered the override. The seal dissolved.

Document thirty-seven: **Order of Strategic Withdrawal and Asset Reclassification.** Joint issuance, Meridian Fleet Command and Halcyon Fleet Command. Date: sixty-one years ago. Signatories: eleven names, five from each fleet, their authorization hashes stacked like a wall.

The tenth name stopped her breathing.

**VOSS, MARGARET E. — Deputy Director, Meridian Colony Services. Authorization tier: Final.**

Her grandmother. Her grandmother's signature, rendered in the flat digital script of a fleet that no longer answered its own comms, co-signing the order that had turned Thalassa Reach from a home into a line item. *Asset Reclassification: Non-viable. Salvage priority: Deferred. Population disposition: Self-sufficiency expected; rescue not guaranteed.*

*Rescue not guaranteed.* Six words that meant *we are not coming*. Written by a woman who folded paper cranes.

Sebastian sat very still in the humming dark and let the stone in her chest turn over.

There was more. There was always more; documents were sediment, each one laid down over the last. Attached to the withdrawal order was a supplementary memorandum, classified at a tier above anything else in the index, and her hands hesitated over the decryption prompt the way you hesitate at a door you can hear crying behind.

She opened it.

---

**MEMORANDUM — SUPPLEMENT TO ORDER 37**
**CLASSIFICATION: MERIDIAN/HALCYON JOINT COMMAND — EYES FINAL**

*To the signatories of record:*

*The undersigned wishes it minuted that the withdrawal is not, as publicly stated, a response to infrastructure failure. Thalassa Reach's systems remain within operational tolerance. The colony is viable. The colony will likely remain viable for a generation or more.*

*The undersigned further wishes it minuted that the decision to withdraw follows the discovery, during trench survey operations, of a pre-colonial structure of unknown origin in the western trench wall. Survey team reports attached. The structure does not match any known fleet architecture. The structure predates our arrival by an interval the survey team declined to estimate, on the grounds that the estimate would be meaningless.*

*Fleet intelligence assesses the structure as potentially the single most valuable discovery in the history of off-world settlement. Fleet intelligence further assesses that neither fleet possesses the security posture to guarantee exclusive claim, and that a contested claim risks armed conflict between Meridian and Halcyon at a moment when both fleets' survival depends on cooperation elsewhere.*

*Accordingly, the recommendation of this office is as follows: withdraw all personnel, sever all scheduled resupply, reclassify the colony as failed, and allow the discovery to sleep unclaimed until such time as one fleet or the other achieves sufficient advantage to return and take sole possession.*

*The colonists will not be told. The colonists will be compensated in reputation only. It is the assessment of this office that they will endure. They were selected for endurance.*

*The alternative is war. We have chosen instead to spend a colony.*

*— M.E.V.*

---

Sebastian read it four times.

On the fourth reading, her eyes stopped snagging on the words *spend a colony* and fixed instead on the signature block, and on the small appended note in different formatting, added later, hash-dated three days after the order took effect:

*Addendum: The Keeper protocol remains active. If any descendant of the founding signatories returns to Thalassa, the archive will recognize them. This is not a flaw. This is the design. — M.E.V.*

*If any descendant of the founding signatories returns.*

She thought about her hiring orders. She thought about how, of all the investigators in Meridian's vast actuarial apparatus, the assignment to audit Thalassa Reach's impossible failures had landed on her desk — routed through three layers of automated triage, unremarkable, unflagged, ordinary. She thought about her surname. She thought about her great-uncle Elias, whose logs she'd read by lamplight like scripture, and her grandmother Margaret, who had signed away his life's work with a steady hand and then gone home and folded paper cranes with her granddaughter and never once, in all those years, all those visits, all those slow afternoons, said a single word about the sea.

Had she known? Had she carried it? Had there been some afternoon, some crane half-folded, when the old woman had looked at Sebastian with something drowning behind her eyes and almost —

No. There was no way to know. That was the cruelty of archives: they preserved decisions and destroyed the people who made them. She could reconstruct every hash and timestamp of her grandmother's choice, and not one gram of its weight.

Sebastian pushed back from the terminal. The chair didn't scrape; everything in the core was damped, quiet, considerate. Even the furniture conspired against drama.

She walked out into the corridor and stood a while beneath the emergency strip lighting, watching her breath fog in the cool air, listening to the distant tidal respiration of the colony's pumps. Somewhere above her, seven children were asleep, and if the pattern held, at 3:14 they would all begin to dream of a lit city rising from the trench — rising from the structure her grandmother had known about, had measured and assessed and buried under a bureaucratic grave marker, had spent fifty years keeping secret at the cost of everyone who'd ever breathed Thalassa's recycled air.

*They were selected for endurance.* Her grandmother had written that too. As though endurance were a consolation. As though you could spend people and then compliment them on how long they lasted.

And yet —

And yet the addendum. *This is not a flaw. This is the design.* Her grandmother had known the archive would wait for a descendant. Had known, perhaps, that someday someone with the right blood would walk back into the dark and open the door she'd helped close. Had she signed the abandonment and then, in the same breath, left a key? Had the woman who traded Thalassa for fleet survival also, quietly, in a classified footnote nobody was ever meant to read, arranged for the trade to be reversible?

Or was that just what grandchildren did — excavate mercy in the ruins of the people who hurt them?

Sebastian pressed her palm flat against the corridor bulkhead. Cold metal. Honest metal. It asked nothing of her and offered nothing either, which at 3 a.m. was a kind of kindness.

She thought about the report sitting in her draft queue. The falsified one. *Structurally unsound, environmentally compromised, salvage value negligible, population non-recoverable.* The lie with good posture that was holding two fleets at bay. She had built it to protect the children, the colonists, the vault. She had believed, filing it, that she was betraying her employers for strangers.

Strangers.

Amara Osei, who'd taken her down into the trench and nearly died beside her. Teo, who described the dream in the flat voice of someone reporting weather. Ilsa, who sleptwalked toward the water. Seven children who had claimed her — literally claimed her, had assigned her a seat at their dinner table, had started calling her *the Keeper's niece* before anyone had explained to her what that meant.

Not strangers. Never strangers. But also not hers to protect by fraud alone, because now the fraud had a twin: she was protecting them *from* her own family, and *with* her own family's key, and she did not know which inheritance was the heavier one.

Her comm chimed. 3:14 exactly. The children's hour.

She didn't answer it. She stood in the empty corridor and listened to the colony breathe, and understood, finally, the shape of the thing she'd been circling since she arrived:

Thalassa Reach hadn't been abandoned. It had been *spent* — deliberately, knowingly, by eleven signatures, one of which shared her blood and her nose and, possibly, her conscience. And now the bill had come due, addressed to her, in her grandmother's handwriting, across sixty-one years.

*The Keeper protocol remains active.*

She was the Keeper's blood. She had been sent here — or she had sent herself, the routing logs couldn't tell her which, and maybe there was no difference — to finish a transaction her grandmother had left deliberately unfinished.

Sebastian walked back to the administrative core. Sat down. Pulled up Document 37 again, and her grandmother's memorandum, and read the final line one more time:

*We have chosen instead to spend a colony.*

"No," Sebastian said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead woman, to the humming dark. "I'm spending my career instead."

She opened her draft report — the lie, the good-postured lie — and began to amend it. Not to weaken it. To armor it. If the fleets came, they would find a document so thorough, so meticulously sourced, so *boring*, that no auditor alive would look past it. She would bury the truth under the one thing her grandmother's world respected absolutely: impeccable paperwork.

She worked until dawn. When she surfaced, gray light was leaking through the observation dome, and Amara Osei was standing in the doorway of the core with two cups of tea and an expression that said she'd been there a while.

"You found it," Amara said. Not a question.

"My grandmother signed the order." Sebastian's voice came out level, which surprised her. "She spent this place."

Amara set the tea down on the console. She looked at the document on the screen for a long moment — at the name VOSS, MARGARET E., glowing in dead fleet-green — and then she looked at Sebastian, and whatever she saw in Sebastian's face made her choose her next words carefully.

"In my experience," Amara said, "people aren't their signatures. My father signed papers sending crews into unsafe shafts. He also carried three of them out on his back." She slid one cup toward Sebastian. "The question isn't what your grandmother did. The question is what you do next."

"The question," Sebastian said, picking up the tea, "is what she did *after*." She nodded at the screen. "Read the addendum. Read it again. *This is not a flaw. This is the design.* She knew the archive would wait for one of us. She closed the door and she left me the key, and I don't know if that makes her a monster who repented or a monster who planned ahead, and I don't know which is worse."

"Maybe it makes her a grandmother," Amara said simply. "People contain multitudes. Especially the ones who sign things."

Sebastian drank her tea. It was terrible — colony-grown, over-steeped, faintly metallic — and it was the best thing she'd tasted in weeks.

"There's something else," she said. "Something I haven't told you. The memorandum references a survey of the structure. Survey team reports, attached. I haven't opened them yet." She met Amara's eyes. "Because the attachment manifest lists a seventh file. Six survey reports. One file flagged *Keeper Eyes Only*."

Amara went very still. "Flagged how?"

"By name-derivative. Bloodline recognition. The system won't even show me the file title unless—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Unless I authorize biometric confirmation. Right here. Right now. Fingerprint and retinal, logged permanently, timestamped, unerasable. The moment I do it, the archive knows — formally, officially, irreversibly — that a Pemberton-Voss has returned to Thalassa Reach."

"And if the fleets ever audit this system?"

"Then they'll know too."

Amara set down her cup. Outside the dome, the gray dawn was turning gold over a sea that had kept its secrets for sixty-one years, and somewhere below them, in the western trench wall, something ancient was waiting to learn whether the granddaughter of the woman who spent a colony had come to collect the debt — or forgive it.

"Do it," Amara said quietly. "Whatever it costs. Do it."

Sebastian reached toward the console.

* * *

The biometric scanner woke with a soft chime, indifferent as gravity. Sebastian placed her thumb on the plate. The retinal sweep painted her eye in infrared, patient, thorough, a red line drawing itself across her vision like a horizon.

**BLOODLINE CONFIRMED: PEMBERTON-VOSS LINE. WELCOME, KEEPER'S HEIR.**

The words hung on the screen for three full seconds. Long enough to feel like a door opening somewhere very far below. Long enough for Amara's hand to find her shoulder.

Then the seventh file unlocked.

Its title resolved out of encrypted static, character by character, and Sebastian felt the floor tilt beneath her chair, because it wasn't a survey report at all.

It was a letter.

**FILE 7/7: PERSONAL CORRESPONDENCE — M.E.V. TO E. PEMBERTON — UNSENT DRAFT — RETAINED PER KEEPER PROTOCOL**

A letter her grandmother had written to her great-uncle Elias and never sent. Composed, according to the metadata, forty years ago — twenty years after the abandonment, twenty years after the signing, on a date Sebastian recognized instantly, because it was the day after her own birth.

She opened it.

*Elias —*

*I am told you are dead. I am told it was the deep. I do not believe the deep killed you, because I know what we buried with you, and I know that you went down to it willingly, and I know that you knew what I had done and forgave me for it, which is worse than hatred, which I cannot repay, which I will spend the rest of my life failing to repay.*

*You left something in the archive for whoever came after. I have left something too. When they come — and they will come, the design guarantees it — they will find both. Let them decide which of us they resemble.*

*Tell them, if the archive can carry messages to the living, that the cranes were never for decoration. Tell them I folded a thousand and one. Tell them the thousand and first is in the vault, and that it is for them.*

*— M.*

Sebastian sat motionless. Beside her, Amara whispered, "What is it? What did she say?"

Sebastian stared at the last line, at the words *a thousand and one*, and somewhere in her memory a nine-year-old girl sat cross-legged on a rug while an old woman's clever fingers creased silver paper, and the old woman had said, *one extra, always, little bird — one extra for whoever comes after* — and Sebastian had laughed and asked who comes after, and her grandmother had smiled and said nothing at all.

"She left me a message," Sebastian said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "In the vault. She left me a message in the vault, and it's been waiting down there for forty years, next to the thing she helped bury."

Amara was quiet for a long moment. Then: "The dreams. The children's dream. The city rising." She turned to Sebastian slowly. "Teo told me last week — he said the dream changed. He said there's a new detail now. A door in the vault wall that wasn't there before, and on the other side of it, light, and shapes moving, and—" He hesitated. "He said one of the shapes folds paper."

The tea in Sebastian's cup had gone cold. Down in the trench, in the dark, in the place where her grandmother had spent a colony and then smuggled a pardon into the wreckage, something had heard the biometric chime echo off the trench walls.

And at 3:14 tomorrow morning, seven children were going to dream of a door standing open — and a thousand and one paper cranes, folding themselves, one by one, waiting for someone to come down and count them.
