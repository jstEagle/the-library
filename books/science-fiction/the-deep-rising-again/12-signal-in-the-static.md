## 12. Signal in the Static

The loop broke on a Tuesday.

Sebastian knew it was Tuesday because Thalassa Reach still kept a calendar, one of those small stubborn dignities Amara's people refused to surrender — days with names, a mess hall that served something called pancakes every seventh morning even though the flour was mostly algae protein and hope. She was in the comm shack at 0400 station time because she hadn't been sleeping well, and because the array had become her private obsession: Earth's last transmission, forty-one years old, cut off mid-sentence, playing to an empty ocean on infinite repeat like a prayer nobody remembered starting.

*—repeat, all Meridian assets fall back to Lagrange staging. Halcyon command has confirmed withdrawal of—*

And then nothing. Forty-one years of nothing. The sentence never finished. Whoever had spoken it had either died, been ordered silent, or simply stopped caring whether anyone at the bottom of an alien ocean heard the rest.

She'd listened to it so many times she could recite the static between words. There was comfort in that, she supposed, the way there was comfort in any wound you'd learned to dress yourself. The message was a tombstone and she visited it nightly.

At 0417, the tombstone spoke back.

*—repeat, all Meridian assets fall back to Lagrange staging. Halcyon command has confirmed withdrawal of—*

A pause. Not the usual pause. The usual pause was 1.3 seconds of carrier hiss before the loop restarted. This pause stretched to four seconds, five, and Sebastian sat up straight in the creaking chair, her hand frozen over the logging console, because four seconds was not decay. Four seconds was *editing*. Someone or something had inserted silence into a recording that had run untouched for four decades.

Then the voice resumed. Same voice — she'd have sworn it on her license, the same clipped fleet diction, the same faint compression artifacts — but the words were new.

*"To the Keeper's blood: we see you. The vault sleeps, but we do not. Answer on the old band, or we will assume the inheritance has fallen to unworthy hands."*

Static. Then the loop began again from the top, pristine, unbroken, as if nothing had happened.

Sebastian did not move for a long time. The comm shack hummed around her — recyclers, coolant pumps, the ancient relay stack that Amara's predecessor had cannibalized three times and coaxed back to life twice. Outside the viewport, the trench water pressed black against the glass, two kilometers of cold indifference, and somewhere down there, beneath the basalt shelf where the dreams pointed, the archive hummed its dormant hum.

*The Keeper's blood.*

Her name. Not her name-name — not *Pemberton*, not *investigator*, not *the auditor from Meridian who files reports nobody reads*. The Keeper's blood. Which meant whoever had spliced that message into a forty-year-old loop knew about Elias. Knew about the founding. Knew — her stomach turned slowly, deliberately, like a hull seam giving way — knew what she'd found in the trench wall, and knew who she was descended from, and had chosen this moment, this exact moment, to stop watching and start speaking.

Which meant they had been watching.

***

She didn't tell anyone. Not at first.

That was instinct, and she distrusted her own instincts enough to examine them twice. The investigator's reflex: control the information, control the scene. But underneath the professional reflex was something rawer — if she said it out loud, in the mess hall, in front of Amara and the colonists and the children who dreamed in unison, then it became real, and real things demanded responses, and she had exactly one response prepared and it was a lie with good posture filed to two orbital fleets.

So she spent the morning doing what she did best: work. She pulled the transmission logs. The splice had entered the system through the deep-band receiver, the long-range antenna aimed up through the water column at the sky — which meant the message had come from orbit, or beyond. Timestamp analysis gave her a window: the insertion had happened sometime in the previous six hours, during the station's quiet cycle, when exactly two people were awake, and one of them was her.

She checked whether the signal could have originated inside the colony. It couldn't. The deep-band transmitter was physically incapable of broadcasting; it was receive-only, its transmit circuitry stripped decades ago for parts. She verified this herself, hands inside the relay cabinet, tracing conduits by touch in the dark, half-expecting to find evidence of tampering and finding instead only honest decay. Nobody in Thalassa Reach had done this.

Someone above the water had.

She also found something else, and this was the part that made her sit down on the deck plating with her back against the cold cabinet: the splice wasn't crude. Whoever had done it hadn't just recorded over the loop. They had matched the voiceprint, matched the compression profile, matched the *artifacts* — the tiny sonic scars of a transmission that had crossed eleven light-years and forty years. To her equipment, and probably to anyone's equipment, the new words sounded like they had always been part of the original message. Like Elias Pemberton himself had paused, four decades ago, mid-sentence, to address his great-niece personally.

Except Elias was dead. She'd read the death record. Fleet archives were meticulous about deaths; they were liabilities.

So either someone had gone to extraordinary lengths to forge authenticity — which meant resources, which meant a fleet, which meant the lie she was maintaining was already dead and she just hadn't gotten the memo —

Or the message was old. Recorded long ago, queued, waiting. Triggered by something. A condition.

*Answer on the old band, or we will assume the inheritance has fallen to unworthy hands.*

She thought about the survey drone. Chapter nine of her private nightmare, the Meridian advance scan she'd spoofed with aging equipment and borrowed luck. Had the drone carried more than sensors? Had it dropped something? Or — worse, colder — had the drone's scan itself been the trigger, a tripwire laid across the trench years ago, waiting for the first serious sensor sweep since abandonment?

*We will assume the inheritance has fallen to unworthy hands.*

Unworthy hands. Whose hands would be worthy? And worthy of what — protection, or retrieval?

She got up off the deck, dusted herself off, and went to find Amara, because some instincts deserved to lose.

***

She found her in hydroponics bay three, elbow-deep in a nutrient manifold, arguing cheerfully with a teenager named Dov about phosphate ratios. Amara Osei argued with machinery the way other people prayed: constantly, familiarly, without much expectation of being answered but with absolute faith that listening mattered. When she saw Sebastian's face, she sent Dov away with a gentle word and wiped her hands on a rag that had been clean, once, in some previous geological era.

"Tell me," she said.

"The loop changed."

Amara went very still. Of everyone in the colony, only Amara and Sebastian knew the full shape of what slept in the trench, and Amara had lived forty-one years under the weight of things she couldn't say, so stillness was a language she spoke fluently.

"Changed how."

Sebastian played it for her. Held the recorder between them in the green-tinted grow-light glow and let the dead voice say its piece. *To the Keeper's blood.* She watched Amara's face go through recognition, confusion, and then land somewhere complicated — not fear exactly, but the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Keeper," Amara repeated. "Elias Pemberton was the Keeper."

"You knew that word?"

"It's in the founding charter. Buried in the legal boilerplate — I read everything when I took over as chief engineer, it's a disease." She set the rag down. "There's a custodianship clause. The colony itself was chartered as... stewards, I suppose. Keepers of something the founders left behind. Everyone always assumed it was ceremonial. Founders loved their ceremonies." Her eyes came up. "But you're saying the word is in the message. From orbit. Now."

"Now. Or queued from then." Sebastian told her about the splice analysis, the matched artifacts, the receive-only transmitter, the tripwire hypothesis. She watched Amara absorb it, and she watched the moment Amara arrived at the same conclusion she had, because Amara's face did what honest faces do — it flinched.

"If it's a tripwire," Amara said slowly, "then it fired on something. The drone?"

"That's my working theory. Which means our spoof worked on the drone's *sensors* but tripped something else entirely. Something that was listening for attention, not tonnage." Sebastian laughed, short and without humor. "I forged a report telling both fleets this colony is worthless. And something out there just announced that it knows the inheritance exists and is asking after its next of kin."

"Your next of kin."

"My great-uncle's, apparently." She rubbed her eyes. "Amara. Who's alive up there? Forty-one years, two fleets, a collapsed alliance. Who remembers Thalassa well enough to know the word *Keeper*, and cares enough to ask?"

Amara was quiet for a while. Somewhere in the racks, a pump cycled, patient as a heartbeat.

"When I was young," she said finally, "there was an old woman in the second habitat ring. Sefu's grandmother — you've met Sefu, he runs the water reclamation. She used to say the strangest thing. She'd say, *they didn't leave us, they left something with us.* We all thought it was grief talking. Her husband died in the pullout year." Amara picked the rag back up, folded it, unfolded it. "Maybe it wasn't grief. Maybe somebody kept faith. Somebody in the fleets, or outside them, who's been holding a watch over this place for forty years and just heard an alarm go off."

"Or somebody wants the vault and needed me here to open it."

"There's that too." Amara met her eyes, level and unflinching. "You noticed you're the only person in this message, yes? *The Keeper's blood.* Not the colony. Not the children. You. Whatever comes next, it comes looking for a Pemberton."

"I noticed."

"And?"

"And I don't answer yet." Sebastian paced the narrow aisle between the grow-racks, lettuce leaves brushing her shoulders like small pale hands. "If I answer on the old band, I confirm someone's home, someone's listening, someone knows what the words mean. If I stay silent, they 'assume the inheritance has fallen to unworthy hands' — and I don't know what that assumption makes them do. Come look, probably. In force. With the kind of attention that makes my forged report worth less than the paper it isn't printed on."

"So it's a trap either way."

"It's a *door* either way. The question is whether I walk through it on their schedule or mine." She stopped pacing. "I need time. I need to know if the message is live or queued. A live sender can be traced — traffic analysis, doppler drift, the little tells of a real-time link versus a stored broadcast. If it's queued, if it fired on the drone's sweep, then it's been sitting up there for decades, patient as the archive itself, and that changes everything about who built this trap and why."

"And if it's live?"

Sebastian looked at the recorder, at the green light pulsing on its chassis, steady as the loop it carried.

"Then the past isn't past," she said. "It's just far away, and it knows my name."

***

She ran the analysis alone, that night, in the comm shack, door shut.

Traffic analysis was old craft, older than the colonies, and it soothed her the way only methodical work could. She sampled the loop across eighteen hours. Measured carrier stability. Checked for doppler shift — the tiny frequency slide that betrayed a moving transmitter, a ship on station, an orbital platform keeping pace with Thalassa's slow drift through its long year.

The result was ambiguous in the most interesting possible way. No doppler drift. No movement. But the carrier wasn't the dead, stable tone of a derelict beacon either — it breathed, microscopically, in a rhythm too regular to be noise and too organic to be machinery. Power fluctuations. A station drawing current. Something *manned*, or maintained, holding position relative to the colony with a discipline no natural object possessed.

Something had been up there a long time. Through the abandonment. Through the silence. Through every night she'd sat in this chair listening to a tombstone, something had been sitting in the dark above the ocean, listening to her listen.

She should have felt afraid. She registered, distantly, that fear was the appropriate response, and that some part of her was filing it away for later, the way she filed everything. What she actually felt, sitting in the humming dark with the loop playing its forty-one-year sentence behind her, was something closer to recognition. All her life she'd been the watcher — audits, inspections, the woman who arrives after the disaster and counts the bodies and writes the number down. She had built a career out of observing other people's catastrophes from a careful, insured distance.

Now something was observing her. And it had used the one word that made observation feel like family.

*Blood.*

Her terminal chimed. Station priority channel — rare enough at 0300 that her pulse jumped before she read the header. It was from the children's dormitory monitor, automated, flagged urgent.

**Subject: Teo Vasquez-Roan — sleep event, third this week.**

She was moving before she finished reading.

***

The corridor to the children's wing was dim and cold and she ran it anyway, boots ringing on the deck plates, past the mural of the founding — Elias Pemberton's face painted young and hopeful beside faces she now knew the names of — and into the dormitory's soft amber nightlight. Teo was sitting up in his bunk, awake, eyes open, staring at the bulkhead as though it were a window.

The monitor, a tired woman named Ilsa Chen — not the sleepwalking Ilsa, another Ilsa, the colony apparently only issued one name per generation — put a finger to her lips.

"He won't wake," she whispered. "Third time. He's talking."

Teo was talking. His lips moved, and Sebastian leaned close, and the words were clear and small and terrible:

"—and the Keeper opens the doors, and the doors remember her name—"

He stopped. Blinked. Looked directly at Sebastian, and for one vertiginous second she had the absolute conviction that he saw her — not the investigator, not the auditor, whatever the dream had shown him wearing her face — and then his eyes focused, and he was just a twelve-year-old waking up confused in the amber dark.

"Did I talk again?" he asked.

"You talked," Sebastian said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Do you remember what you said?"

He frowned, reaching. "Doors. Something about doors." His frown deepened, and then, with the terrible directness of children: "The city's getting closer. Every night it's closer. Last night I could see the windows." He looked at her, and his eyes were the flat calm of deep water. "It has your face on one of the doors. I think it's waiting for you to knock."

Behind her, the comm shack terminal chimed again, faint, down the corridor. Priority channel. Twice.

The loop was repeating. And in the static between sentences, for the first time in forty-one years, something was waiting for an answer.
