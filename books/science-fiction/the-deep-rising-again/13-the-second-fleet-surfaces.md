## 13. The Second Fleet Surfaces

The ship came down through the storm layer at 0400, riding its own thunder.

Sebastian watched from the observation blister as the *Halcyon Vigil* resolved out of the murk — a salvage cruiser, all blunt angles and docking clamps, her hull scarred with the geometry of a hundred recoveries. Fleet livery, but faded. Halcyon didn't waste paint on ships they intended to keep.

"Early," Amara said beside her. Not a question.

"Eleven days early."

"Your report said no one would come."

"My report said no one should." Sebastian kept her voice flat. "There's a difference, and Halcyon has always been very good at finding it."

The cruiser settled into the docking cradle at the north pier with a groan that traveled through the station's bones. Thalassa Reach had not hosted a fleet vessel in nine years. The cradle's hydraulic fluid had been recycled twice past spec, and Sebastian heard the whole structure complain about it.

On the pier, a delegation assembled itself with the desperate speed of people who remembered protocol from a lifetime ago. Amara went to stand with them. Sebastian did not. She had a role to play, and it required her to be elsewhere — in her office, ostensibly reviewing actuarial tables, so that the Halcyon captain would have to come to *her*.

It was a small theater. She hoped it would be enough.

* * *

Captain Vess Odile arrived at her office door at 0930, precisely, unannounced, escorted by two crewers who stayed in the corridor like well-trained knives left in a drawer.

She was smaller than Sebastian expected. Grey-shot hair cropped close, a face like weathered composite, and eyes that did the thing Sebastian had only ever seen in the best fraud investigators — they looked at you slightly past your shoulder, as if your body were a rough draft of the document they were actually reading.

"Investigator Pemberton." She offered a hand. Her grip was brief, dry, calibrated. "Captain Vess Odile, Halcyon Salvage Command, Third Recovery Wing. I'm told you've been cataloguing our losses."

"Certifying them," Sebastian said. "There's a difference."

Odile smiled. It was a good smile. It was also, Sebastian noted, deployed the way Amara deployed a welding torch — precisely, and only where needed.

"May I sit?"

"Please."

They sat. On the desk between them, Sebastian had arranged her working files with studied carelessness: the failure ledger, the hull-seam analysis, the payout certification, all slightly askew, all legible. Bait laid out like hors d'oeuvres. If Odile wanted to read, she'd have to reach — and every reach would tell Sebastian what she was looking for.

Odile didn't reach. She folded her hands and looked at Sebastian instead.

"Nine years," the captain said. "Nine years since Halcyon flagged Thalassa Reach for terminal assessment. And in nine years, the colony's death has proceeded at exactly the pace of a bureaucratic schedule. Air recyclers failing on rotation. Comms relays dying oldest-first. Hull seams weeping in sequence. Do you know what I call that, Investigator?"

"Orderly decay?"

"I call it *obedient*." Odile's eyes came around, finally, and settled on her. "Machines don't die politely, Investigator. They die in tantrums. They fail in ways that hurt. This colony is dying like a soldier falling out on command — and I have spent three weeks reading your preliminary filings, and I cannot find the tantrum anywhere."

Sebastian let the silence sit for a three-count. Then she opened the failure ledger and turned it around.

"Page forty," she said. "The north arc recyclers. Cascade failure, six units, over eleven days. Each unit's failure accelerated the load on the next. That's not obedience, Captain. That's arithmetic. The tantrum is there. It's just quiet, because everything here was built quiet. My great-uncle's generation didn't build machines that screamed. They built machines that apologized."

Something flickered in Odile's face at the name *Pemberton* — quick, gone, filed. Sebastian watched it get filed.

"Elias Pemberton," Odile said. "The founder."

"My great-uncle. I'm told the resemblance is in the paperwork."

"And the resemblance to your grandmother?"

The question landed like a needle finding a vein. Sebastian had prepared for it — of course she had, she'd prepared for every version of it — but preparation didn't stop the cold from walking up her spine. Maren Pemberton, Fleet Meridian's iron chancellor, co-signer of the abandonment order. If Odile had read the filings, she'd read the name.

"My grandmother signed the order that abandoned this place," Sebastian said evenly. "I'm here to price what her signature cost. I'd have thought Halcyon would find that comforting."

"I find it *interesting*." Odile rose, crossed to the observation blister, and looked out at the colony — at the domes, the trench station's superstructure, the grey water pressing in from every direction. "Interesting that Meridian sent a Pemberton to bury a Pemberton's work. Interesting that Halcyon sent me, and Meridian sent you, and neither fleet sent the other so much as a courtesy copy. Interesting that the trench survey data in your filing has a gap."

Sebastian's pulse ticked up half a beat. She kept her breathing on the old rhythm — four in, six out, the cadence she'd learned in fraud school for exactly this.

"Survey gap?"

"Forty-one hours. Your own sensor logs, Investigator. The trench array went down for scheduled maintenance for forty-one hours during your survey window, and came back up with recalibrated baselines." Odile turned. "Scheduled by whom?"

"By the colony's engineering staff. The array is thirty years old, Captain. It needs maintenance the way you need sleep."

"Mm." Odile smiled again, and this one had teeth in it somewhere, deep down, like a hook under bait. "Then you won't mind if my crew inspects it. Salvage protocol. We like to know what we're recovering *before* we recover it."

"File a request with the colony administrator. I'm an insurance investigator, not the harbormaster."

"Of course." Odile moved toward the door, then paused with her hand on the frame — a pause engineered to the millimeter, Sebastian was sure, a pause designed to make the next sentence look like an afterthought. "One more thing. The children here. There are seven of them, I'm told. All born on-station?"

Sebastian's face did nothing. She had spent two weeks teaching her face to do nothing, and it was the single hardest certification of her career.

"Eight, actually. Teo Vann turned twelve last month. Why?"

"No reason. Halcyon keeps census on dependent minors in all terminal assessments. Standard." The captain's eyes held hers a moment too long, reading the page behind her shoulder. "They sleep well, I hope?"

"Children here sleep like the dead," Sebastian said. "It's the quiet."

"Mm," Odile said again, and left.

* * *

The dinner was Odile's idea, delivered as an invitation that could not be refused without being an admission. *Captain's table, 1900, formal dress optional, good wine guaranteed.* The wine was the threat. Good wine, on a station where everything had been recycled past spec for nine years, meant Halcyon had come provisioned for a long stay.

Sebastian dressed in the one formal garment she'd packed — a dark jacket, Meridian cut, which she now regretted and refused to change, because changing it would be a tell. She arrived at the *Vigil*'s wardroom to find a table set for six: Odile, her executive officer, the colony administrator, Amara — included, Sebastian noted, because Odile had requested *the chief engineer specifically* — and Sebastian.

And one empty chair.

"Joining us?" Amara murmured as they sat.

"Someone Odile wants to unsettle us with. Watch the door, not the wine."

The door opened at 1900 sharp, and the unsettlement walked in wearing a Meridian uniform.

Commander Lysa Vann — Teo's aunt, colony-born, seconded to Meridian Fleet Survey nine years ago and never returned. Sebastian had read her file in the first week: the one colonist who'd left, the one family thread connecting Thalassa to the fleet that had signed its death warrant. She'd assumed Vann was a footnote.

She was not a footnote. She was Odile's survey specialist, seconded across fleet lines — which was itself extraordinary, a Halcyon ship carrying a Meridian officer — and she sat down at the table with the careful blankness of someone who had been ordered to be here and had not been told why by anyone she trusted.

"Investigator," Vann said, nodding.

"Commander." Sebastian matched the nod, weight for weight. *I know what you are. I know you don't know why you're here. Neither do I, yet.*

Wine was poured. Food was served — actual protein, actual greens, grown in the *Vigil*'s hydroponics, a display of casual abundance that was itself a message: *we have everything, you have nothing, and we can wait.*

The conversation began as pleasantries and descended, by degrees so smooth you could almost miss the grade, into an audit.

"Remarkable colony," Odile said, carving protein with surgical economy. "Nine years past its projected viability and the gardens are still producing. The water reclamation is at ninety-one percent. Do you know what our own ships run at, Investigator?"

"Eighty-four," said her XO, promptly, as if this had been rehearsed. Perhaps it had.

"Ninety-one," Odile repeated, letting it hang. "On thirty-year-old equipment maintained by—" she consulted nothing, "—forty-three adults. Remarkable people, your colonists."

"We manage," Amara said. Her voice was level, her hands still. Sebastian had watched her lie to a fleet captain's face over dinner and marveled at it, and then realized, with a small cold shock, that Amara wasn't lying. Everything she'd said was true. The colony *was* remarkable. That was the problem.

"The trench station," Odile said. "Tell me about the trench station, Chief Engineer. My survey crew pulled the public schematics. There's a level on them that doesn't appear in the construction manifests. Sub-level four. Sealed. No listed function, no listed access. Your manifests just — stop."

"Structural overflow," Amara said. "Founders built redundancy into everything. Sub-level four was ballast and emergency stores. We sealed it when the stores degraded. Mold. You don't want to open a thirty-year-old emergency ration locker, Captain, believe me."

"I believe you're telling me something," Odile said pleasantly, "which is not the same as believing you."

The wine went around again. Sebastian drank hers slowly and watched the table the way she'd been trained to watch a negotiation: not the mouths, the hands. Odile's hands never moved except with purpose. The XO's hands refilled glasses and marked time. Vann's hands were in her lap, and once — only once, when Odile said *sub-level four* — they tightened, just slightly, on the napkin.

*She knows the level exists,* Sebastian thought. *She doesn't know what Odile thinks is in it.*

"Commander Vann," Sebastian said, and felt the table's attention swing to her. "You were born here. You must have opinions about sub-level four."

Vann's eyes came up. For a half-second, the blankness cracked and something raw looked out — a woman nine years exiled, sitting at an enemy captain's table, being used as a crowbar against her own birthplace. "I left when I was nineteen," she said. "I was a gardener, Investigator, not an engineer. I sealed no levels."

"But you've heard the stories. Children's stories, maybe." Sebastian kept her tone light, almost warm. "Every colony has them. What were Thalassa's?"

The silence stretched one beat too long, and Odile leaned into it like a fencer into a riposte. "Actually," the captain said, "that's precisely why Commander Vann is here. Halcyon's long-range listening post picked up an anomaly in this system's EM background. Structured. Periodic. Centered on—" she smiled, "—the trench. A signal with the cadence of a transmission, Investigator, repeating at intervals of—" she paused, delicately, "—approximately nine hours and fourteen minutes."

The number went through Sebastian like ice water. *3:14.* The children woke at 3:14. The dream came at 3:14. And the trench, it seemed, was *keeping time.*

"Interference," Amara said. "The array's maintenance cycle—"

"Runs on station power," Odile said. "Station power runs on station clocks. This doesn't. This runs on *its own* clock, from *down there*, and it has been running, by our estimate, since approximately the year your comms array was commissioned." She set down her glass and folded her hands, and for the first time all evening she looked not like an auditor but like what she was: a salvage captain who had found a wreck worth more than her whole wing. "So. Here is my position, offered with all the courtesy this table deserves. Halcyon is prepared to classify Thalassa Reach as a *rescue priority* rather than a salvage claim. Full evacuation, full fleet support, full — how shall I put it — *forensic attention* to everything beneath us. All I need is for someone at this table to stop performing, and tell me what my listening post already knows."

Nobody moved. The wardroom hummed. Somewhere below them, under forty atmospheres of black water, something hummed back.

It was Sebastian who spoke, and she heard her own voice come out in her great-uncle's register — calm, dry, the voice of a family that had buried colonies before.

"Captain. I've certified the deaths of eleven installations in my career. Do you know what they all had in common? Everyone who came to investigate them found exactly the signal they were listening for." She turned her glass a quarter-turn on the cloth. "Your listening post heard a rhythm in the noise. Rhythms are what the ocean *does*. If you'd like, I'll show you the harmonic analysis in my filing. Page sixty-one. The trench sings in nine-hour cycles because of thermal venting, and it has sung that song since before your listening post had ears."

Odile regarded her for a long moment. The smile was gone now, and what was underneath was worse — respect.

"Page sixty-one," the captain said at last. "I'll have my people read it tonight." She rose, and the dinner ended the way sieges end in bad weather: nobody victorious, everyone counting losses. At the door, she paused — the engineered pause again, the afterthought again.

"Your grandmother," she said, "was on the review board that evaluated Halcyon's last deep-trench survey. Thirty-one years ago. The one that was sealed. I only mention it because I'm a great believer in family continuity." Her eyes moved, briefly, past Sebastian's shoulder — reading the page behind her. "Goodnight, Investigator. Do give my regards to the children."

* * *

Sebastian walked back through the north dome with the storm rattling the plating and her mind running at fraud-school speed, cataloguing damage.

*She knows about the signal. She knows about sub-level four. She knows about my grandmother and the sealed survey. She's read my filings with better eyes than mine.* The lie was holding, but holding the way a hull seam holds — visibly, under known load, one bad day from weeping.

And the last line. *Regards to the children.* Not a pleasantry. A probe. Halcyon kept census on dependent minors, she'd said. Standard. But no census form asked whether children slept well, and no salvage captain said *regards to the children* unless she suspected the children were part of the asset.

Sebastian reached her quarters, keyed the door — and stopped.

The corridor outside Teo Vann's family module was lit with emergency amber, and Amara was already there, still in her dinner clothes, talking fast and low to a pale-faced woman holding a sleeping bag that was empty.

"He got up at 3:14," the woman was saying, her voice fraying at every edge. "He does that sometimes, walks a little, we watch him. But tonight he wasn't walking toward the galley. He was walking toward the *airlock*, and he said—" she swallowed. "He said the doors were opening. He said the city told him it was time to come down. And Investigator—" her eyes found Sebastian, and in them was the specific terror of a mother whose child has begun receiving mail from the deep. "He's not in his bunk. He's not anywhere on the station. Teo is *gone*."
