## 14. Teo Opens a Door

The dream had changed again.

Teo knew it the way you know a song has shifted key — not because anyone told him, but because his chest felt different when he woke. For weeks the dream had been a city rising, all light and slow motion, water peeling off its towers like a coat being shrugged off. Then it had been doors. Doors inside doors, corridors of pale metal, a hum he could feel in his back teeth even after he opened his eyes at 3:14 exactly, every night, like a clock with a face made of sleep.

Now, in last night's version, one of the doors had been open. And behind it there had been a small room with warm lighting, and someone standing in it with their back turned, and Teo had known — the way you know things in dreams, absolutely and without evidence — that if the person turned around, he would see his mother's face.

She hadn't turned around. He'd woken up before she did.

He lay in his bunk in the children's dormitory and listened to Thalassa Reach breathe. The colony breathed through its vents now, a wet mechanical sighing that Amara said was normal, that Amara said was *fine*, in the tone adults used when they wanted a word to be true so badly they pressed on it like a bruise. The air recyclers were dying. Everyone pretended they weren't. Teo was twelve, not stupid.

He got up. He didn't decide to get up; his body decided, and he followed it down the ladder and past the sleeping bunks of Ilsa and Marcus and Priya and the others, the seven of them who dreamed together, who had stopped being embarrassed about it around the same time the adults stopped pretending they weren't scared.

The corridor outside was night-lit, amber strips along the floor. Teo's feet knew the way. That was the strange part — not that he wanted to go somewhere, but that wanting wasn't involved anymore. His feet were going, and he was the passenger.

*The trench station,* he thought. *It wants me at the trench station.*

Or maybe it didn't want anything. Maybe this was what Ms. Pemberton called a compulsion, when she interviewed them gently with her notebook and her careful eyes, asking questions like *did the dream tell you to do things?* and Teo had said no, because it hadn't, not then.

It did now.

* * *

The trench station sat at the end of the long gallery like a held breath. Teo had been there before, in daylight, on a school trip two years ago — before the failures started piling up, before the word *audit* entered everyone's mouth like a bad taste. Back then it had been a museum piece: a viewing blister of old glass, control consoles dark as dead eyes, and beyond the glass, the trench itself, falling away into black that no light ever reached.

Tonight the consoles weren't dark.

Teo stood in the doorway and stared. Faint amber light moved under the surfaces of the panels, slow as blood under skin. The big status board above the main console — the one that had read SYSTEM DORMANT for as long as anyone could remember — now read something else.

**RECOGNITION PENDING.**

His heart was doing something complicated. He walked forward anyway. His feet were still driving.

Up close, the nearest console had a recessed panel, hand-sized, shaped like nothing in particular until you looked at it too long, and then it looked exactly like the shape of a child's hand. Teo knew that was a trick. Ms. Pemberton had explained pareidolia to them once — faces in clouds, patterns in noise. But his hand fit anyway. Of course it did.

The moment his palm touched the surface, cold metal warmed under his skin, and the whole room woke up.

Lights climbed the walls in soft waves. Consoles bloomed with text in an alphabet that took Teo three full seconds to realize he could read — not because he'd learned it, but because the letters rearranged themselves into Standard as he watched, politely, like a host switching languages for a guest.

**WELCOME, TEO MERCADO-ANSAH.**

His name. His whole name, including the part nobody used except his mother, and his mother was four years dead, taken by a hull seam failure in Gallery Nine that the official report called *an improbable confluence of stress factors* and that everyone in the colony privately called what it was: the colony starting to die, and taking people with it.

"You can't know my name," Teo said out loud, because saying it made it less enormous. "Nobody's name is in here. This place has been off since before I was born."

Text scrolled, patient:

**THE ARCHIVE HOLDS THE REGISTRY OF ALL FOUNDERS AND THEIR DESCENDANTS. YOU ARE LISTED. YOU HAVE ALWAYS BEEN LISTED.**

"Listed as what?"

A pause. Long enough that Teo understood the machine was choosing its words, which was somehow worse than if it had answered instantly.

**AS HEIR.**

* * *

He should have gone back. He knew that too. Somewhere above him, in the residential rings, seven other kids were probably sitting up in their bunks right now, feeling what he felt, and Ilsa would tell Amara, and Amara would come looking, and there would be a whole thing. Adults were going to find out about the vault eventually — Ms. Pemberton and Amara had already been down there once; Teo had heard the grown-ups whispering about the descent, the near-death, the humming archive. They thought the children's dreams were a map they needed to follow carefully, with equipment and safety lines and committees.

But the dream hadn't sent them. It had sent him.

**QUERY,** the archive said, unprompted, gentle as snowfall. **DO YOU WISH TO HEAR A MESSAGE?**

Teo's mouth went dry. "From who?"

**FROM YOUR MOTHER.**

The trench station tilted. Or Teo tilted. He put both hands flat on the console to keep the world pinned down.

"That's not possible," he whispered. "She died four years ago."

**CORRECT. THE MESSAGE WAS RECORDED ELEVEN YEARS AGO, WHEN YOU WERE ONE YEAR OLD. IT WAS DESIGNATED FOR DELIVERY UPON ACTIVATION OF THE ARCHIVE BY A REGISTERED HEIR. NO ACTIVATION EVENT OCCURRED UNTIL NOW.**

Eleven years. His mother had been dead for four years and had left him a message that had waited eleven years in the dark under the trench, and it had waited for *him*, specifically, and the weight of that was so large Teo had to breathe around it in pieces.

"If I say yes," he said, "will it be her? Really her voice?"

**IT WILL BE A RECORDING OF HER VOICE. IT WILL NOT BE HER. WE CANNOT GIVE HER BACK TO YOU. WE ARE SORRY. WE HAVE HAD A LONG TIME TO LEARN WHAT SORRY MEANS.**

Something in Teo cracked and reassembled slightly differently. Machines weren't supposed to say sorry. Machines weren't supposed to understand that what he wanted — what he wanted so badly his ribs hurt — was impossible, and offer it anyway.

"Yes," he said. "Play it."

* * *

The lights dimmed to a warm amber, the color of the night-strips in the dormitory, the color of safety. And then his mother's voice filled the trench station, and Teo was one year old again, or younger, some self he couldn't remember, held somewhere warm while she talked over his head to a machine.

*"Testing, testing — is this thing on? Elias, if you're listening from whatever ghost-corner of this system you live in, yes, I'm doing it your way, no, I'm not doing the formal declaration first."*

A laugh in the recording. Teo's knees nearly gave. He knew that laugh. He had spent four years afraid he was forgetting it, hoarding scraps of it in his memory like rations, and here it was, whole, uncompressed, alive.

*"Formal declaration, then."* Her voice shifted, straightened, became the voice she used at assemblies. *"I am Dr. Yewande Mercado-Ansah, second-generation founder of Thalassa Reach, speaking for the record of the Deep Archive. I am recording this for my son, Teo, who is currently asleep on my shoulder and drooling on my collar, and who will hear this only if the Archive wakes — which, knowing how these things go, will happen either never or at the worst possible time."*

Another laugh, but thinner. Teo heard what he hadn't been built to hear yet, what a one-year-old couldn't parse: fear underneath the humor, held down hard.

*"Here's what I need you to know, sweetheart, in case the worst happens and I'm not there to say it myself. Your father and I came down here because we believed the colonies deserved a future that didn't depend on the fleets' permission. What's buried in this trench isn't treasure. It's instructions. It's everything the founders learned about making dead worlds live — compressed, indexed, waiting. It belongs to everyone and that's exactly why both fleets will want to own it, and why we hid it, and why someday someone is going to have to be braver than we were."*

Her voice caught. Recovered.

*"If you're hearing this, Teo, then the Archive chose you, or you chose it, and either way I want you to remember three things. One: you are not responsible for fixing everything. You're responsible for telling the truth about what you found. Two: the people of this colony raised you whether I'm there or not — Amara Osei especially, don't argue with me, I've seen how she looks at you. Let them be your family. Family isn't only blood; your great-great-uncle Elias never understood that, and it cost him everything."*

Teo thought of Ms. Pemberton, with her notebook and her careful eyes and the way she'd started eating dinner with them like she'd always belonged, and felt something click into place that he didn't have a word for yet.

*"Three."* A pause. In the pause, faintly, a baby sound — himself, eleven years ago, stirring against her shoulder. *"Three: the dream isn't a haunting. It's a curriculum. The Archive teaches while you sleep. It's been trying to reach the colony for years and couldn't get through to adult minds — too much noise, too much fear. Children listen better. If the city rises in your dreams, Teo, it's not a warning. It's an invitation. Don't be afraid of it. Be afraid of the people who arrive and tell you it's theirs."*

Silence for a moment. Then, softer, just for him, just a mother now:

*"I love you. Grow up brave. Not fearless — brave. There's a difference, and you'll know it the first time you're scared and do the right thing anyway."*

The recording ended.

* * *

Teo stood in the amber light for a long time. He wasn't crying, exactly. It was more that his whole body had become the place where crying lived, and he was holding the door shut, and the door was buckling.

"She sounded scared," he finally said. "At the end. She knew something was coming."

**SHE DID. THE FOUNDERS RECEIVED THE ABANDONMENT ORDER NINE DAYS AFTER THIS RECORDING. YOUR MOTHER SPENT THOSE NINE DAYS HERE, INDEXING. SHE SLEPT IN THIS ROOM.**

"Did she leave anything else?"

**ONE MORE ENTRY. DESIGNATED FOR DELIVERY ONLY AFTER A SECOND CONDITION IS MET.**

"What condition?"

The status board flickered. New text assembled itself, letter by letter, and Teo read it twice because the first time his eyes refused to cooperate.

**CONDITION: THE PRESENCE OF SEBASTIAN PEMBERTON, KEEPER'S BLOOD, WITHIN THE ARCHIVE'S SENSOR RANGE.**

"Ms. Pemberton," Teo said slowly. "Sebastian. She's — " He remembered the assembly where Amara introduced her, remembered the surname landing on the crowd like a dropped tool. *Pemberton.* Like the founder. Like the logs. "Elias Pemberton was her great-uncle. She told us. She said she was just here to count our broken machines."

**SEBASTIAN PEMBERTON IS LISTED IN THE REGISTRY AS LINEAL DESCENDANT OF ELIAS PEMBERTON, CHIEF ARCHITECT OF THE DEEP ARCHIVE, AND CO-SIGNATORY — **

The text stopped. Teo watched the cursor blink where the next word should have been, and understood, with the strange clarity the dream had given him, that the machine was deciding how much truth a twelve-year-old could carry.

**— CO-SIGNATORY,** the board finished, **OF DOCUMENT 7-A. THE ABANDONMENT ORDER.**

Teo's stomach dropped through the floor, through the trench wall, into the black. Sebastian Pemberton's family hadn't just built this place. Her grandmother had signed the paper that left it to die. Left *his mother* to die, four years later, in a gallery full of stress factors nobody would fix.

And she was up there right now, in the residential ring, asleep, or almost asleep, not knowing that the trench below her had been waiting for her name the way the children had been waiting for morning.

**TEO MERCADO-ANSAH,** the archive said. **YOUR MOTHER'S SECOND MESSAGE CANNOT BE DELIVERED WITHOUT HER. BUT THERE IS SOMETHING ELSE YOU SHOULD SEE FIRST. SOMETHING SHE INDEXED ON HER LAST NIGHT HERE.**

The main display lit with a file header, and Teo read the title, and the door he'd been holding shut against the crying finally gave way — because the title was in his mother's handwriting, scanned from real paper, real ink, dated nine days before the abandonment order arrived.

It read: ***For whoever wakes us: what the fleets are really afraid of.***
