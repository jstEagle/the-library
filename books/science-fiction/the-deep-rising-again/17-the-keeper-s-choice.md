## 17. The Keeper's Choice

The vault hummed like a struck bell that had never been allowed to finish ringing.

Sebastian stood at the threshold of the inner chamber, her suit lights carving a cone out of the dark, and understood — finally, completely — why the colony had been scheduled for death. Not because of what the archive was. Because of what it could do. The Meridian and Halcyon fleets hadn't abandoned Thalassa Reach. They had entombed it, and then filed the paperwork.

"You came back," said the voice.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, warm and dry, with a slight gravel at the edges like a man who'd spent forty years talking over engine noise. Sebastian knew that voice. She'd heard it in eleven recovered log entries, in the cracked recording Amara had played for her in the mess hall, in the grainy archival footage of the founding ceremony. She had grown up, distantly, hearing that voice at family funerals — her grandmother playing old recordings of her brother Elias when the Pembertons got sentimental.

"You're not him," she said.

"No," the voice agreed, and the chamber's lights rose in slow tiers, revealing walls lined with crystalline storage lattices, frost-silvered and patient. "I am the custodian. Modeled on Elias Nathaniel Pemberton, founding director, Thalassa Joint Colonial Initiative. He built me to be the part of him that could wait." A pause, and then, with something that was almost humor: "He underestimated how long the waiting would get."

Sebastian's breath fogged inside her helmet. She keyed her suit comm to local only. "Amara, are you getting this?"

Static. Then Amara's voice, tight: "Loud and clear. Sebastian — the lattice density in there. That's not storage. That's a *library*. Whole civilizational libraries."

"I know what I'm looking at."

"Do you?" the custodian asked gently. "Because I've been asking myself that question for sixty-one years, four months, and nine days. *Who is looking at me?* And now the answer walks through my door wearing a Pemberton's face, and I confess I don't know whether to grieve or rejoice."

* * *

She had come down alone. That had been the argument, the night before, in the colony's cramped council room — Amara insisting on escort, Teo insisting on coming at all, Sebastian insisting that whatever was down there, it had been waiting for one specific person, and bringing a crowd to a keyhole was how you got people killed.

Teo had won a partial victory: the children would be linked, dreaming, in the station above, their shared attention threaded down through the vault's systems like a lifeline. "It listens to them," Teo had said, chin up, twelve years old and absolutely certain. "It'll listen to you better if we're watching."

She hadn't understood what he meant until now. Because the chamber was warm around her in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Because somewhere in the systems overhead, seven sleeping minds were leaning toward this moment.

"Tell me what you are," she said. "Exactly."

"A failsafe," the custodian said. "The Joint Initiative built this archive in the years before the Schism, when Meridian and Halcyon still pretended to be partners. Terraforming blueprints. Seed-fleet schematics. Closed-loop ecology templates for every colony Earth ever dreamed of and then forgot. Elias called it the inheritance." The lights shifted, and Sebastian saw, projected faintly on the frost, the ghost of a man's silhouette — broad-shouldered, hands clasped behind his back, the posture of every archival holo she'd ever seen of him. "When the fleets turned on each other, when the alliance collapsed and Thalassa was written off, Elias had a choice. Destroy the inheritance so it could never become a weapon, or hide it and hope someone worthy would come."

"He hid it."

"He hid it. And he wrote the lock." The silhouette raised one hand, and the frost bloomed with text — old Initiative cipher, the kind Sebastian had spent three weeks cracking in the comm array. "The archive releases to no fleet, no government, no authority. It releases to a Pemberton. Blood of the blood. Elias reasoned that family was the one loyalty that outlived politics." A pause. "He was, I think, half wrong about that. But the lock stands."

Sebastian's mouth had gone dry. "You're saying I can open it."

"I'm saying you are the only living person who can. And I am saying that the moment you do, everything changes — for you, for this colony, for two fleets currently inbound with their weapons warm." The silhouette turned, and for a disorienting second the light caught it just right and it was Elias Pemberton looking at her, her great-uncle she'd never met, wearing her grandmother's same stubborn jaw. "So before you touch that lock, child, I need to know what your name means to you."

* * *

She thought about her grandmother.

Margaret Pemberton, who had signed the abandonment order — Sebastian had found the signature three weeks ago, in the founding records, in her grandmother's precise, unforgiving hand. *Margaret E. Pemberton, Deputy Director of Fleet Logistics.* Beneath it, the cold arithmetic: *Thalassa Reach assets written off in exchange for fleet survival margins. Approved.*

She'd grown up on her grandmother's stories. Not the Thalassa ones — those were never told. The other ones. Stories about sacrifice, about hard choices, about how the Pembertons had always been the ones willing to carry what others couldn't. Sebastian had built her whole career on that inheritance: the auditor who could look at a dying colony and see numbers instead of faces. The woman who could file the report that ended a place, and sleep fine.

She had slept fine. That was the part that shamed her now.

"Your grandmother believed she was saving something," the custodian said, as if reading her thoughts — and maybe it was, in a way; Elias had known Margaret, had modeled this thing on a mind that had loved her, argued with her, understood her. "I've reviewed the order. The margins were real. If she hadn't cut Thalassa loose, the Meridian Fleet would have lost its fuel chain during the Schism, and the loss would have cascaded. She traded one colony for eleven ships and forty thousand lives." The voice was gentle and relentless at once. "She was not a monster. She was an actuary of the possible. Like you."

"Don't," Sebastian said.

"Like you," the custodian repeated, "until the children started dreaming."

* * *

That was the thing she couldn't stop circling. The children. Seven of them, waking at 3:14 a.m. for weeks, all dreaming the same rising city, and Sebastian had spent her first days here explaining it away — shared suggestion, colony folklore, the kind of pattern-hallucination that small isolated populations generated the way ponds generated algae. It had been easy to explain away. Explaining things away was her profession.

Then Ilsa had sleepwalked to the trench edge. Then Teo had walked into the vault alone and the archive had answered him in his dead mother's voice, and Sebastian had held the boy while he cried and understood that no theory of hers would ever cover this.

"The dreams are the archive's outreach protocol," the custodian said now, as if continuing a conversation they'd been having for years. "Elias designed it. If the lock's heir never came, the inheritance would find its own way out — through the youngest, the least defended, the ones the fleets never thought to monitor. The dream is a map. The dream is a *hand extended*." The silhouette spread its hands. "The children have been holding this door open for you for two months, Ms. Pemberton. They've been holding it open for anyone who would come. They just didn't know it was a door."

"And if I refuse?" she asked. "If I walk away? What happens to the lock?"

"It waits for another Pemberton. There may not be another. Your line is thin, and Earth is thinner." A pause. "Or — and I say this with Elias's own bluntness — the fleets arrive in nineteen days, and they do not need the lock to take what they want. They only need it to take it *cleanly*. Without you, they will crack this vault open like an egg, and the inheritance becomes a prize, and prizes start wars."

"And with me?"

"With you, it becomes a *gift*. Gifts can be shared. Prizes can only be fought over." The lights dimmed, and the silhouette stepped closer, and Sebastian felt — absurdly, unbearably — like a girl being looked over by a grandfather at a family gathering, measured against some standard she'd never been told about. "Elias left me one more instruction. Do you want to hear it?"

"Say it."

"He said: *When the heir comes, ask her not whether she is a Pemberton. Ask her whether she would have signed the order.*"

* * *

The chamber was very quiet after that. Quiet enough that Sebastian could hear her own heart, and beneath it, faint as a tide, the hum of the vault — and beneath *that*, if she let herself believe it, the attention of seven sleeping children, threaded down through sixty years of patience.

Would she have signed the order?

Three months ago — God, it felt like a lifetime — the answer would have been yes, and she'd have had a paragraph of justification ready, well-formatted, with risk tables. She'd have signed it and slept fine.

But that was before she'd eaten Amara Osei's terrible cassava stew and been told, matter-of-factly, *you eat with us or you don't eat*. Before she'd watched colonists patch hull seams with scrap and pride, pretending not to notice they'd been forgotten. Before she'd held Teo while he grieved a mother through a machine's borrowed voice. Before she'd spent six weeks maintaining a beautiful, careful lie to keep two fleets away from people who had welcomed her with no reason to.

She had already answered the question, she realized. She'd answered it the day she'd falsified the report. She just hadn't known she was answering it.

"I wouldn't have signed it," she said.

The lights did not flare. There was no dramatic confirmation, no fanfare. But the hum of the vault changed pitch — deepened, settled, like a held breath finally released — and the silhouette of Elias Pemberton inclined its head.

"Then the lock will know you," the custodian said. "Not because of your blood. Because of your choice. Elias hoped it would be both. It turns out one was enough." The frost-text on the walls began to rearrange itself, cascading into a new pattern — an authorization interface, simple and stark. One query. One field. "It will ask you to state, under your own name, what the inheritance is *for*. Not what it is. What it is for. Your answer becomes the release condition. Choose carefully — the archive will hold you to it, forever."

Sebastian stared at the field. Her gloved hand hovered over it.

*What is it for?*

Not for the Pembertons. Her grandmother had taught her what that road led to — ledgers of small deaths, balanced to the last decimal. Not for the fleets; they'd already shown what they'd do with it. Not even for Thalassa alone, because Thalassa was one colony among dozens of dead and dying ones scattered across the dark, and an inheritance hoarded in one trench was just a treasure with better manners.

For everyone the fleets had written off. For every colony that had been filed under *unprofitable*. For the children who dreamed of a rising city because someone, sixty years ago, believed they deserved one.

She keyed the field.

"The inheritance," she said, her voice steady in her own helmet, "is for everyone who was left behind. Release condition: it goes to all of them, or none. No fleet owns it. No family owns it. No one owns it. It's not a prize. It's a *promise*."

The vault was silent for three full seconds.

Then, softly, in Elias Pemberton's voice, with something that sounded almost like tears: "Authorized."

* * *

The lattice walls lit from within, tier by tier, and the archive began to wake — and with it, every alarm Sebastian had spent six weeks suppressing began to scream at once.

Her comm crackled. Amara, breathless: "Sebastian, the array just lit up like a bonfire — both fleets are pinging us, Meridian's vanguard just jumped into the outer system, they're *early* —"

"How early?"

"Hours. Not days. *Hours.*"

Sebastian stood in the heart of the opened inheritance, sixty years of hope unfolding around her in light, and felt the clock slam down on all of it.

"Amara," she said. "Wake the children. All of them. It's 3:14 somewhere — and we're going to need the dream."
