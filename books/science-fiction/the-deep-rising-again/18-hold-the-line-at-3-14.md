## 18. Hold the Line at 3:14

Amara Osei had spent thirty-one years keeping Thalassa Reach alive with baling wire and stubbornness, and she had never once thought of herself as a soldier. But at 02:47 station time, standing in the trench station's control gallery with a wrench in her belt and forty-one colonists behind her, she discovered that the difference between an engineer and a soldier was mostly paperwork.

"Orbit confirmed," said Devi Raghunathan from the sensor alcove, her voice flat in the way it went flat when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "Three vanguard hulls. Meridian pennants on two of them. The third is running dark but its thermal bloom says Halcyon."

"They got here fast," Amara said.

"They got here *angry*. Sebastian's broadcast lit up every channel in the system nine hours ago. Both fleets have been burning hard since." Devi paused. "Weapons are hot. I'm reading targeting radar sweeps — they're painting the trench station."

Of course they were. The trench station sat directly above the vault like a cork in a bottle, and both fleets had come to pull the cork.

Amara turned to the room. Forty-one people. Dockworkers and hydroponics techs, a retired schoolteacher, three teenagers who should have been asleep, old Marta Voss who had bad knees and a welding torch she refused to surrender to anyone. They stood among the cable runs and coolant pipes in their patched coveralls, looking back at her with the particular patience of people who had already decided something and were waiting for her to catch up.

She had rehearsed this speech for six hours. It evaporated now, and what came out instead was the truth.

"You all know what's down there," she said. "You know what Sebastian sent out. Every dead colony in the Reach gets a second chance because of what Elias Pemberton buried under our feet, and both fleets would rather own that future than share it. They're coming down here to take it." She let that sit. "We can't stop them. Nobody in this room believes we can stop them, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. What we can do is make sure that when their boarding parties reach the vault door, the only thing waiting for them is a locked room and a very rude message. The archive wakes on its own schedule now — Teo saw to that — and every hour we hold this station is another hour the inheritance finishes opening itself. That's the whole job. We're not fighting for territory. We're buying time."

"How much time?" asked Yusuf Kaya, who ran the water reclamation plant and had never in his life asked a question he didn't want answered honestly.

"The archive needs until roughly 3:14," Amara said. "That's when the children say it completes its waking cycle. Don't ask me why 3:14. Ask them."

Every head in the gallery turned toward the far corner, where seven children sat in a loose ring on the deck plating, wrapped in thermal blankets, eyes closed. They were not sleeping, exactly. They had described it as *standing in the doorway* — awake enough to talk, linked deep enough to see. Teo knelt at the center of the ring with his palms flat against the floor, and his lips moved sometimes, silently, like a boy reading instructions off the inside of the world.

Ilsa opened her eyes. "They're asking about the doors," she said. Her voice carried the strange calm the children all wore now, the calm of people reporting weather from somewhere else. "The dream says: the first door opens inward. The second door opens downward. The third door—" She frowned. "Teo says the third door isn't a door yet. He says it's a choice."

"A choice for whom?" Amara asked.

"For whoever's standing in front of it," Ilsa said, and closed her eyes again.

Devi's console chirped. "Drop pods. Two of them, Meridian trajectory, deploying now. Impact estimate on the trench shelf in eleven minutes."

Amara looked at the wrench on her belt. Then at Marta Voss, who hefted her welding torch and grinned with all four of her remaining teeth.

"All right," Amara said. "Let's go be inconvenient."

* * *

The trench station had been built to withstand pressure, not people, and this turned out to be the single advantage they had. Its corridors were narrow, its bulkheads thick, its internal layout a maze designed by engineers who assumed anyone moving through it would be carrying equipment and therefore needed handrails, not sightlines. Amara split the colonists into teams the way she'd split repair crews during the recycler failure of Year Nine — by competence, by nerve, and by who could be trusted not to panic when the lights went out.

Because the lights were going to go out. That was the plan's spine, and it had been Sebastian's idea before she'd gone down into the vault to do whatever a Keeper's blood did down there. *Don't defend the station,* she'd said, hunched over the comm array with her hands shaking and her voice steadier than Amara had ever heard it. *Defend the dark. Kill power to everything above the fourth bulkhead, kill the external running lights, kill the docking beacons. Let them drop into a black hole and find out the hard way that we know these corridors better than they ever will.*

So when the first Meridian pod slammed into the trench shelf with a shudder Amara felt through her boots, the trench station went dark from the outside in. Running lights died. Docking beacons died. The great floodlamps that lit the trench wall for maintenance dives guttered out one by one, and below the station the water went black as ink, black as the inside of a closed eye.

"They'll have suit lamps," Devi whispered over the short-range link. She and Amara crouched behind the coolant manifold at the fourth bulkhead, the line they'd agreed to hold. "Lamps just make them targets. Corridor cameras are still up on local power — feeding to my slate. Six contacts through the airlock. Breaching gear, military pattern."

"Marta?"

"Ready and grinning," Marta's voice crackled from the junction above.

The plan was almost embarrassingly simple. The invaders would advance down the main corridor toward the vault access shaft, because the vault access shaft was the only thing down here worth advancing toward. At the third junction, the corridor floor was a maintenance grate over a service drop — and beneath that grate, Amara had spent four hours disconnecting the structural locks on a section of catwalk that had been scheduled for replacement anyway. One cut cable, one hundred kilos of descending colonist, and the corridor became a hole.

She hadn't wanted anyone hurt. She'd said so in the assembly, and old Marta had patted her arm and said, *Sweet girl, nobody's going to die falling four meters into a padded service bay. Their pride might not survive.* It wasn't a weapon. It was an inconvenience with gravity.

The first invader reached the third junction at 03:02.

The grate dropped. There was a clang, a splash, a burst of profanity in crisp Meridian Fleet diction, and then Marta Voss leaned over the edge of the catwalk with her welding torch lit and said, in a grandmotherly tone that carried beautifully in the dark: "The next section's rigged too, loves. And the one after that. You've walked into a building maintained by spite for thirty years. I'd stay put if I were you."

Amara watched on Devi's slate as six helmet lamps converged on the fallen soldier, then swung upward toward Marta, then swept the corridor ahead — where every few meters, in the beam-light, they could now see the telltale seams of more grates, more disconnected locks, more traps that were mostly just honest maintenance deferred into ambush.

One of them laughed. Amara heard it through the contact mic Devi had tapped into the corridor feed — a short, involuntary bark of laughter, quickly smothered. *They expected resistance,* she thought. *They expected weapons, or surrender, or begging. They didn't expect a seventy-year-old woman with a welding torch and a sense of humor.*

It wouldn't stop them. Nothing they could do would actually stop three vanguard ships' worth of marines. But every minute the Meridian boarding party spent mapping fake traps and fishing their comrade out of a service bay was a minute the archive drank deeper from the dreams, and the children upstairs murmured in unison like a tide coming in.

* * *

At 03:07, the Halcyon ship stopped pretending.

Devi flagged it first — the dark hull in high orbit flaring its drives, dropping not a pod but a full assault cutter on a direct ballistic toward the trench station's dorsal dock. No pretense of rescue protocol. No hail. Just a knife falling out of the sky.

"They're not even trying to look legal," Devi said.

"They think the Meridian team will have it secured by the time they land," Amara said. "They're wrong, but it's a reasonable wrong." She keyed the general channel. "All teams, second wave, dorsal approach. Okafor, get your people off the dock ring. Let them land. Nobody dies on that dock — hear me? We give ground, always give ground, we just don't give *time*."

The Halcyon cutter hit the dorsal dock with a boom that rang through the station's bones. Through the corridor feeds Amara watched eight figures in matte-black armor pour down the dorsal spine, moving fast and professional, sweeping rooms that had been evacuated ten minutes earlier. They found empty bunks, cold tea, a hydroponics bay with the grow-lights left running on timers so the tomatoes wouldn't notice anything was wrong.

And they found the corridors dark, and the maps useless, and at every junction a small handwritten sign that some colonist had taped up in the last hour. Amara had seen them being made and hadn't stopped it. *WRONG WAY,* said one. *STILL WRONG,* said the next. *YOU'RE LOST AND WE HAVE SNACKS,* said a third, which had been Teo's contribution before he'd gone downstairs to join the dreaming ring.

The Halcyon squad leader's helmet cam caught her reading the signs, and Amara heard her exhale slowly through her suit's comm, the exhale of a professional recalibrating. "Contact the Meridian element," the woman said. "Tell them we're calling a temporary ceasefire. Tell them—" a pause, and Amara could hear her choosing the words carefully, "—tell them the asset is defended by civilians, and the civilians are *enjoying themselves*, and I want to know whose idea that was before I put a round anywhere near it."

* * *

By 03:11, the trench station held eleven armed fleet personnel in its lower corridors, frozen in place by darkness, disorientation, and the growing suspicion that they were not invading a facility so much as being *hosted* by one, on terms they hadn't agreed to. Two boarding parties, rivals, separated by one bulkhead and thirty years of inter-fleet rivalry, communicating through Devi's tapped feeds because neither side trusted the other's channels.

Amara stood at the fourth bulkhead with her wrench, listening to the children's voices thread through the station speakers — Sebastian had wired the dreaming ring's microphones into the PA before descending, saying, *If this goes wrong, I want everyone to hear what we're actually protecting.*

The children were chanting softly now, all seven, in a rhythm that wasn't language. Below them, under forty meters of rock and water, the vault hummed audibly through the station's frame — a vibration Amara felt in her molars, in the soles of her feet, in the old scar tissue of her hands. The hum was rising. Pitching upward. Like something enormous drawing breath.

"Third door," Ilsa said suddenly, eyes wide open, staring at nothing. "The third door is opening. Teo says—"

The station shook.

Not an impact. Not a breach. A *shift* — the whole trench groaning, sediment cascading off the outer hull, the black water below the viewports stirring with a glow that rose from the trench floor like dawn happening in the wrong direction. Every fleet soldier in the station froze. On the shared feeds, Amara watched eleven helmet lamps swing to the viewports, and through the ports they all saw it together: light climbing the trench wall in veins, gold-white, branching, *purposeful*, racing down toward the vault and up toward the station and out along the seabed in every direction, as if the entire trench had been a sleeping circuit board and someone had finally thrown the switch.

"It's not defending itself," Devi breathed. "Amara. Look at it. It's not fighting them. It's *starting*."

The hum crested. The children's chant broke apart — not into silence but into laughter, seven children laughing in the dark control gallery, and Teo's voice came through the PA clear and young and absolutely certain:

"It's not a door. It never was a door. It's a *launch*."

On every feed, on every channel, on the tapped fleet frequencies and the colony net alike, a new signal began to broadcast — rising out of the vault, unencrypted, unstoppable, addressed to everyone and owned by no one. Amara recognized the format instantly. It was the same format as Sebastian's transmission from nine hours ago.

But this one wasn't words.

This one was a *city* — schematics unfolding, systems waking, the founders' hidden habitat peeling itself off the trench wall and beginning, slowly, majestically, to rise.

And on the fleet channels, in the sudden stunned quiet, Amara heard the Meridian squad leader say the thing she would remember for the rest of her life:

"Contact all ships. All ships. Get the admirals on the line. Tell them—" a pause; the helmet lamp tilted up toward the glowing water, toward the light rising through the dark, "—tell them we're too late, and it's beautiful, and somebody had better figure out how to say sorry."
