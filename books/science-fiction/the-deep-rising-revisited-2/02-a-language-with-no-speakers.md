## 2. A Language With No Speakers

The signal arrived on the third day of the thaw, when the vault's outer sensors were busy cataloguing meltwater and Noor was elbow-deep in the germination trays, coaxing a stubborn line of pre-Collapse barley back toward the light.

Noor heard it first as a wrongness in the room — a pressure behind the ears, the way the air changes before a storm. Then the console in the corner of the workroom chimed: three soft notes, ascending, the tone reserved for transmissions that matched no known protocol. In eleven years, that chime had sounded four times. Three had been debris reflections. One had been a dying satellite's last stuttering breath, a burst of corrupted telemetry that meant nothing to anyone.

This one did not stop.

Noor wiped loam from their hands, crossed the workroom, and stood before the screen the way one stands before a door they have not opened in a long time. The waveform filled it — jagged, repeating, broken at intervals like a sentence spoken through clenched teeth. Beneath it, the system's classification field blinked its patient, stupid verdict:

*UNRESOLVED.*

"Where are you from?" Noor said aloud, to no one, because eleven years alone had made speech a habit rather than a symptom.

They pulled up the signal's origin vector and felt the first cold thread of something that was not quite fear and not quite joy walk down their spine. The transmission came from the Kessari Reach — a wedge of space on every chart marked with the quiet, bureaucratic euphemism *Low Historical Density*. No stations. No beacons. No reason for anything human to be speaking from there at all.

And yet someone was. Or something was. The signal pulsed with structure — headers, checksums, repetition cycles — the skeleton of language wearing the flesh of static.

Noor sat down slowly, the way you sit when your knees have opinions of their own, and began to listen.

* * *

The vault kept more than seeds. That was the irony Noor had never been able to explain to the Concord liaison who had installed them here, all those years ago — the man with the gray gloves who had walked the corridors tapping walls and asking questions about humidity tolerances while never once asking what Noor actually did all day. The Concord believed this place held grain and genome and nothing else. But the founding charter, the one fragment of original documentation that had survived whatever purge had scraped the rest, contained a single line that Noor had read perhaps ten thousand times:

*A seed is only memory that has learned to wait.*

Whoever built the vault had understood that. And so, tucked into the deep archive alongside the cryo-vaults and the cold rooms, there were other stores: cultural seeds. Linguistic ones. Records of languages, some of them already dying when the vault was sealed, preserved the way you preserve anything you love without knowing why — thoroughly, obsessively, against all likelihood of use.

There were forty-one linguistic archives in the deep stacks. Noor had catalogued them in their first year, out of boredom dressed up as diligence, and had not opened a single one since. They were part of the ritual landscape of the vault, like the morning pressure checks and the evening walk along the perimeter, present and unexamined.

Now Noor rode the lift down into the cold heart of the archive with the signal playing on a loop in their earpiece, and the dead language rose around them like water.

Kessari.

The file label read: *KESSARI CLUSTER — ORAL TRADITIONS, TRADE GLYPHS, LITURGICAL FORMS. STATUS: EXTINCT. LAST CONFIRMED SPEAKER: NONE ON RECORD.*

Noor spread the archive across the main terminal — phoneme tables, glyph correspondences, fragments of grammar reconstructed from trade records and funerary inscriptions. It was a skeleton too, but a different kind: not a sentence broken by distance, but a body assembled by scholars who had never heard it breathe. Two hundred years dead, the file said. Extinction by attrition, the summary noted, in the flat administrative voice that history used when it wanted to say nothing at all. Assimilation, dispersal, plague.

*Plague.* The word snagged somewhere in Noor's chest, though they could not have said why. It would take them weeks to understand the snag. For now, they set it aside and turned to the signal.

The comparison work was slow, painstaking, holy. That was the only word for it, Noor decided somewhere in the second hour — holy — though they had no religion and had never wanted one. But there was a quality to the labor that reminded them of tending seedlings in the dark months: the faith required, the smallness of each act against the largeness of what it might become. Match a recurring waveform cluster to a glyph. Match the glyph to a phoneme table. Watch a fragment of sound resolve, tentatively, into a fragment of meaning.

By the fourth hour, Noor had the header.

It repeated every ninety seconds, and once the glyph correspondence locked in, it resolved with almost embarrassing clarity. The Kessari had used a standard distress preamble in their final decades — every culture did; the Reach had been full of shipping lanes once — and the format had survived in the trade records well enough to translate whole:

*TO ANY WHO HEAR. TO ANY WHO REMEMBER US. WE ARE STILL HERE. WE ARE STILL SPEAKING.*

Noor sat back from the terminal. The workroom was dim; they had not turned on the overheads, and the screens painted their face in pale blue and amber. Their own reflection looked back at them from the dark glass, wide-eyed, and for a moment they did not recognize the expression on it.

Wonder. That was the expression. They had almost forgotten its shape.

Eleven years of routine will do that. Eleven years of pressure checks and germination logs and perimeter walks, of seasons measured in humidity differentials, of a beacon transmitting daily reports to recipients who had stopped acknowledging receipt before Noor ever arrived. Noor had made peace with it — had made a devotion of it, even, because the alternative was to admit the devotion had no object. You keep the seeds alive because keeping things alive is what you are for. You tend the beacon because tending is what you are for. The loneliness is just the medium you grow in, like loam, like water.

But wonder — wonder was a different climate entirely. Wonder said: *the universe is larger than your routine, and it has just knocked.*

Noor got up, made tea they immediately forgot about, and went back to work.

* * *

The body of the signal resisted longer than the header. It came in fragments — the transmission was damaged, or truncated, or both, gaps torn through it at irregular intervals like bites taken by something invisible. Noor mapped the gaps, found they fell into a pattern, and spent a frustrated hour realizing the pattern was simply decay: sections of the recording lost to time and interference, unrecoverable, gone the way the speakers themselves had gone.

What remained was enough to torment.

There were voices. Plural. That was the first thing Noor established, and the thing that kept them working past midnight when they should have slept. At least three distinct speakers, layered in the recording — one close to the transmitter, voice roughened by what the spectrogram suggested was smoke or dust; one farther away, a woman or a person with a higher register, calling out something rhythmic that recurred at intervals; and beneath both, fainter, a chorus of indeterminate number, a murmur like a crowd in a large stone room.

A crowd. Recording a distress call with a crowd in the background. Noor turned that fact over and over, and each turn made it stranger. Distress calls were made by pilots, by crews, by isolated stations — by the few addressing the many. This was the many. This sounded like hundreds of people crowded around a single transmitter, taking turns, or shouting together, or —

Noor translated the woman's recurring phrase next. It appeared eleven times in the surviving record, always identical, always urgent, pitched above the murmur like a hand raised in a assembly that would not stop talking:

*WRITE IT DOWN. WRITE IT DOWN. THEY CANNOT BURN WHAT IS WRITTEN IN EVERY MIND.*

Noor's hands went still on the console.

The phrase made no sense as distress. Distress called for rescue, for shelter, for medical aid. This woman was not asking to be saved. She was asking to be remembered — urgently, specifically, with the cadence of someone who knew remembering was about to become difficult.

Noor looked at the archive summary again. *Extinct by attrition. Assimilation, dispersal, plague.*

Plague. The word snagged again, harder this time. Noor pulled the historical annex attached to the linguistic file — a thin document, barely two pages, mostly redacted into gray blocks. What remained legible said only: *Kessari settlements experienced significant population decline circa 2147–2151. Cause consistent with airborne pathogen. Quarantine protocols enforced by Concord Health Authority. No further records retained.*

*No further records retained.* Six words doing the work of an entire erased history. Noor had read that phrase a hundred times in a hundred files over eleven years and had never once thought about it — it was the archival equivalent of wallpaper, the way the Concord ended every story it didn't want to continue.

But now, listening to a woman beg a future listener to write down what could not be burned, *no further records retained* sounded less like wallpaper and more like a lid.

Noor did not let themselves follow the thought. Not yet. They were a caretaker, not an investigator; speculation was a crop they had learned not to plant, because it grew wild and choked everything else. They returned to the translation, methodical, and worked until the vault's night-cycle lights dimmed to amber, and then worked after that, by screen-glow, while outside the hull the thaw-water ticked against the sensor arrays and the stars wheeled in their indifferent courses.

By the deepest hour of the night cycle, Noor had recovered enough of the closest speaker's words to assemble a partial passage — broken, gapped, but unmistakable in its architecture. They rendered it in transliteration, the way you'd render a song whose music you couldn't yet read, and then they read it aloud, quietly, in the empty workroom, giving the dead language a voice for the first time in two centuries.

*"—they came under quarantine colors. We opened the locks because the colors were Concord, and Concord means—"* [gap] *"—not sickness. There is no sickness. The vents carried—"* [gap] *"—my mother is on the third level and the doors will not—"*

[gap]

*"—if any hear this, we were not sick. Say it after me. WE WERE NOT SICK. Say it so it lives outside my mouth. If I die with it only in my mouth, it dies with—"*

The recording degraded into pure noise there, and did not recover for another forty seconds.

Noor sat in the amber dark with their own heartbeat loud in their ears.

*We were not sick.*

Every instinct Noor had — the careful, patient, self-protective instincts of a person who had survived eleven years of solitude by never letting a question grow larger than its container — every one of those instincts said: stop. File this. Flag it for the Concord liaison, as procedure required, and let the people with rank and resources decide what it meant. That was the protocol. That had always been the protocol.

But the woman's voice was still in the room. Noor could feel it — not literally, not acoustically, the speakers were off, but in the way a room holds a bell after the ringing stops. *Say it so it lives outside my mouth.*

Two hundred years she had waited for a mouth.

Noor looked at the transmission log — the outgoing log, the beacon log, the log of eleven years of daily reports sent into silence. And then they looked at the incoming log, where a single entry now glowed: received, unresolved, duration 14 minutes 22 seconds, origin Kessari Reach.

Fourteen minutes and twenty-two seconds. Someone had spent nearly fifteen minutes of a dying moment making sure this reached outward. Had aimed it, somehow, at the one place in human space where forgotten things were kept alive — whether they had known the vault existed or simply thrown the message at the dark and trusted memory itself to catch it.

Noor opened a new file. Not in the general system — in the isolated cold-storage partition, the one the liaison's remote diagnostics had never been able to access, the one Noor had privately called the cellar and used for nothing more rebellious than personal journals. They copied the raw signal there, unaltered. Then the translation tables. Then the partial transcript.

Then they sat with their finger hovering over the transmit key — the beacon key, the key they pressed every day at 0600 to send the same scheduled report to recipients who never answered — and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that pressing it now would mean something entirely different than it had ever meant before.

They did not press it. Not tonight. Tonight they saved the file, encrypted it, and sat for a long time afterward in the amber dark, listening to the vault breathe around them — the hum of the cryo-systems, the whisper of air handlers, the ten thousand sleeping seeds in their frozen drawers, each one a memory waiting.

Somewhere in that hum, Noor thought they heard the woman's voice again. *Say it so it lives outside my mouth.*

"I'm listening," Noor said to the empty room. "I'm writing it down."

Outside, the thaw continued. Inside, something that had been dormant for eleven years — not in the vault, but in Noor — cracked open like a seed coat, and began, very quietly, to grow.

They did not sleep. Near dawn, running the signal analysis one more time purely to occupy their shaking hands, Noor noticed something they had missed in the fatigue-haze of the night: buried beneath the three voices, beneath even the murmuring crowd, there was a fourth layer in the recording. Faint. Structured. Deliberate.

Not a voice at all, but data — a second signal nested inside the first, compressed, encoded, hidden the way you hide a letter inside a lining.

Someone had smuggled something out within the testimony. Something they had considered important enough to bury beneath the voices of the dying.

Noor stared at the nested waveform as the vault lights cycled toward morning, and felt the last of their routine fall away like ice from a branch in thaw.

Whatever it was, it would have to wait for daylight. Everything, now, was about to.
