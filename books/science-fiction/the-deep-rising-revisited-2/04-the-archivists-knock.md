## 4. The Archivists Knock

The knock came at 0400 vault time, which meant it was not a knock at all.

Noor had lived long enough with the vault to know its vocabulary of sounds: the tick of thermal expansion in the outer hull as dawn heat crawled across the plateau; the sigh of the atmospheric scrubbers cycling down into their night register; the dry whisper of seed drawers settling on their rails, a sound like an old man turning over in sleep. Eleven years had taught Noor the difference between the building breathing and the building being touched.

This was the latter.

Three impacts against the main airlock, spaced with bureaucratic evenness. Not the frantic hammering of someone in distress. Not the arrhythmic curiosity of a scavver who'd wandered off the trade lanes. This was a rhythm that had been written down somewhere, in some manual, and rehearsed.

Noor lay still in the narrow bunk above the germination lab and watched their own breath fog the cold ceiling. Below them, through two meters of insulated deck, the testimony core sat where they had hidden it — wrapped in a foil seed blanket inside a cryo-drawer labeled *TRITICUM ANCESTRAL STRAINS, DO NOT THAW*, buried among four hundred identical drawers of wheat that had never known a field. The decryption work was spread across three terminals in fragments, disguised as routine integrity checks. If you knew what you were looking at, it was a survivor's voice describing fire. If you didn't, it was checksum noise.

*If you didn't,* Noor thought, and did not finish the sentence, because finishing it meant imagining the alternative.

The knock came again. Three impacts. Patient. Certain of its welcome.

* * *

Noor dressed in the dark out of habit — the vault's nights were for moving quietly, even when there was no one to disturb — and took the spiral stair up to the entry gallery at a pace that felt, from the inside, like panic and probably looked, from the outside, like nothing at all. That was the trick of the place. You learned to make your fear walk slowly.

The gallery was a wide half-circle of glass overlooking the plateau, built by people who had believed there would one day be visitors. There were six chairs arranged facing the door, upholstered in a fabric that had faded from green to the color of old bone, and no one had ever sat in them. Noor passed them every day. Tonight — this hour — they finally understood what the chairs were for. They were for waiting. They were for the moment when the silence ended and you had to decide who you were going to be in front of another human being.

Through the frost-furred glass, three figures stood in the floodlight wash of the landing pad. Two wore the dove-grey of Concord civil service. The third wore black with a single silver thread at the collar, and Noor's stomach dropped through eleven floors of bedrock, because everyone who had grown up inside the Concord's borders knew what that thread meant. Records Enforcement. The people who kept the archives clean. Nobody ever explained what *clean* meant. You were just expected to understand that some things were dirt.

Noor keyed the intercom. Their voice came out steady, which felt like a small miracle performed by someone else using their throat.

"This is a restricted agricultural facility. State your business."

"Seed Vault Nine-Alpha-Kessari," said the grey figure in front, a woman whose voice had the smooth, unhurried quality of someone reading from a document she had helped write. "We're from the Regional Office of Records Integrity. We apologize for the hour. Our transport window was fixed."

*Kessari.* The name of the vault's sector, printed on every form Noor had ever filed, so ordinary it had become invisible — a word worn smooth as a river stone, meaning nothing, signifying nothing, just geography. Except now it wasn't. Now it was a name that had been spoken into a distress beacon by dying people, and hearing it in a Concord official's mouth made Noor feel like the word itself was watching them.

"One moment," Noor said, and went to open the lock, and used those forty seconds of cycling hydraulics to breathe the way the meditation files taught: in for seven, hold for four, out for eight. The airlock sighed. Cold plateau air rolled in, smelling of iron dust and frost.

* * *

They introduced themselves in the gallery, under lights tuned to simulate morning, because the architects had believed visitors should feel welcomed and the designers of the simulation had apparently never met anyone.

The woman was Auditor-Clerk Ives. Her colleague, silent and taking notes on a slate, was Sub-Clerk Danner. The man in black gave only a designation: "Enforcement Liaison Vess." He stood near the door rather than sitting, and he did not look at the chairs, and Noor understood that he had been in rooms like this before and knew exactly how they ended.

"We're conducting a routine audit of transmission activity in this sector," Ives said. She had a face designed to be forgotten — medium everything, pleasant nothing — and eyes that contradicted it entirely, quick and cataloguing, moving over Noor's face the way Noor moved a magnifier over a seed coat looking for hairline fractures. "Standard practice. Nothing to be concerned about."

"Of course," Noor said.

"You operate the deep-space listening array."

"I maintain it. It's part of the vault's charter. Redundant telemetry, solar weather monitoring—"

"Yes." Ives smiled. It reached her mouth and stopped there, like a courier reaching a closed border. "You also transmit."

"The beacon. Charter obligation. A continuous carrier signal on the assigned frequency, content per schedule, logged and filed quarterly." Noor had recited this paragraph so many times to themselves over eleven years that it came out with the flatness of liturgy. "I can provide the full logs."

"That won't be necessary." She paused, and the pause had weight, had texture, had edges. "Actually. It will be necessary. But we'll come to that."

Danner's stylus moved on his slate. In the quiet of the gallery, Noor could hear it — a fine dry scratching, like an insect in a wall. *That's what they are,* Noor thought. *Insects in the wall of the world. You don't notice them until the structure is already hollow.*

Vess had not spoken. He had walked, slowly, along the gallery's glass wall, and now he stood before the great framed charter that hung there — the founding document of Vault Nine, all gilt lettering and solemn language about *the preservation of viable germplasm for the restoration of human worlds*. His reflection floated faintly over the text, a dark shape superimposed on promises.

"Eleven years," Vess said. His voice was quiet, almost gentle, and somehow that was worse than Ives' smoothness. "Alone. That's a long time to talk to no one."

"The beacon doesn't require conversation."

"No. But people do." He turned. Up close, his face was unremarkable too — the Concord seemed to breed for forgettability — but his eyes held something Ives' didn't. Not cruelty, exactly. Certainty. The terrible calm of a man who has never once doubted that whatever he is doing is correct. "Do you know what I admire about caretakers, Hartwell? Your type. You keep things alive that would otherwise die. Seeds. Signals. Old words." A beat, thin as a blade. "Old languages."

The gallery's climate system cycled. Somewhere below them, four hundred drawers of ancestral wheat hummed in their cold sleep, and one drawer among them held something that was not wheat, and Noor kept their face still through sheer eleven-year practice of being the only person in a room.

"I wouldn't know anything about languages," Noor said. "My training is agronomy."

"Mm." Vess looked at them for a long moment. Then he nodded, as if a box had been checked, and returned to the door, and said nothing more for the rest of the interview, which was its own kind of message: *we have finished pretending to be polite; the rest is procedure.*

* * *

The procedure took three hours.

Ives requested the transmission logs — all of them, eleven years, every packet — and Noor provided them from the public terminal, the sanitized mirror of the archive that existed precisely for moments like this, though in eleven years no one had ever asked. The logs showed what the logs always showed: the carrier signal, the scheduled bursts of vault status, seed inventory hashes, atmospheric data. And threaded invisibly through them, split across dead frequencies and buried under noise-floor padding, the decryption workstream that Noor had spent nine days constructing like a smuggler's false floor.

Ives read. Danner cross-referenced. Vess stood by the door and watched Noor instead of the documents, which Noor understood was the actual audit. Documents could be forged. Faces were harder.

"Your power draw increased by fourteen percent over the past ten days," Ives observed, without looking up.

"Heater recalibration. The winter cycle ran cold."

"Mm." She turned a page. "Your array logged an anomalous inbound signal on the ninth. Directional, low-power, non-standard encoding. Flagged by your own diagnostic suite and then—" she checked the log again, delicately, as if handling something fragile, "—dismissed as interference. That's unusual for you. Your dismissal rate on flagged anomalies is very low. You investigate nearly everything. You're thorough. It's in your personnel file. *Excessively thorough,* your last supervisor wrote."

The word *ninth* landed in Noor's chest like a dropped tool. They had dismissed the signal from the public logs — reclassified it as solar static — but they had left the original flag timestamped, because deleting it entirely would have been the act of a person with something to hide, and Noor had reasoned, in the small hours, that a thorough caretaker would dismiss a signal after *investigating* it, and the investigation record would show... what? Nothing. Noor had been careful. Careful was supposed to be enough.

"I investigated," Noor said. "It was a reflection artifact off the Kessari debris belt. The belt throws echoes during perihelion. I've documented the phenomenon in my quarterly reports."

"Have you." Ives looked up for the first time in twenty minutes, and her eyes did the magnifier thing again, and Noor thought: *she knows the debris belt doesn't do that, or she doesn't and she'll check, either way she's holding this sentence up to the light.*

"It's in the reports," Noor repeated. "Quarterly. Filed. You have access."

"We have access to everything," Danner said mildly, from behind his slate, and it was the first thing he'd said aloud, and it was clearly meant to be heard as reassurance, and it was the most frightening sentence Noor had heard in eleven years.

* * *

They left at 0730, when the real sun finally cleared the plateau rim and flooded the gallery with honest light, and the three of them became silhouettes against it, grey and grey and black.

At the airlock, Ives paused with her hand on the frame and turned back.

"You've done remarkable work here," she said. "Truly. Eleven years of perfect maintenance. The Concord values caretakers like you." She smiled her border-closed smile. "Which is why it would grieve us if anything compromised your position. Accidents happen in remote facilities. Systems fail. Isolation takes its toll on anyone." She let that sit in the air between them, soft as snowfall, heavy as a ceiling coming down. "Stay well, Hartwell. We'll see you again soon. Routine follow-up. Six weeks."

The airlock cycled. Through the glass, Noor watched the transport lift, its drive wash scouring frost from the pad in a white bloom, and then the sky was empty, and the plateau was empty, and the silence came back in like water filling a footprint.

Noor stood in the gallery for a long time. The six chairs faced them, patient, absurd. Downstairs, in a drawer labeled with the name of a massacre nobody remembered, a dead woman's voice waited in the dark, describing fire.

*Six weeks.* Not a courtesy. A countdown. They hadn't found it today — or they had found nothing they could prove — but they would come back with better questions, sharper instruments, and next time there would be no pretense of routine, because Vess had already stopped pretending, and Vess was the one whose report mattered.

Noor walked down the spiral stair, past the germination lab, into the archive cold, and stood before the wheat drawer with their hand flat against its frosted face. Behind that seal: the testimony core, and beneath its encryption, still incomplete, still fragmentary, the second half of the signal that Noor's night-shift decoders were grinding through — a section the first voice had referenced and the translation hadn't yet reached. Something about *where the orders came from*. Something Noor hadn't told the archivists existed, because Noor hadn't fully understood it yet either.

Eleven years, Noor had believed the vault's purpose was preservation. Keep the seeds cold, keep the signal burning, keep faith with a mission no one remembered assigning. Devotion as a substitute for company.

But preservation, Noor understood now, standing in the cold with their breath hanging in the air, was not neutrality. Every archive is an argument about what deserves to exist. The Concord had made its argument in fire and deletion, and now its clerks knocked politely on doors asking about anomalies, and its enforcement men admired caretakers in voices like closing doors.

The seeds in these drawers were kept alive so that something could grow *after*. After famine, after collapse, after whatever ending the founders had feared. That was the whole design. You preserved against the day when planting became possible.

Noor opened the maintenance panel, woke the drawer's local interface, and began — methodically, thoroughly, excessively, as their file said — copying the testimony core onto every redundant storage system in the vault, and then onto seed-grade crystal tags designed to survive a century in vacuum, sealed inside sample capsules, slotted into the automated dispersal launcher that the charter required the vault to maintain and that Noor had never once had reason to use.

If they came back before six weeks, the truth would already be falling through the dark in a dozen directions, scattered like everything else Noor had ever tended, trusting the soil of strangers somewhere far away.

The launcher's status light turned from amber to green.

And in the same hour, on the public band, a reply crackled back from the direction of the shipping lanes — not Concord traffic, not the relay lattice, but a private vessel squawking a transponder ID that Noor's database flagged as *unregistered — probable smuggler class* — transmitting, of all things, a handshake request in an antique open protocol, addressed not to the Concord relay but directly, specifically, impossibly, to Vault Nine's beacon frequency.

A message that began: *To the keeper of the light on Kessari — we heard you were asking old questions. So are we. Open your door.*

Noor stared at the text scrolling across the console, heart hammering, hand still resting on the launcher's green-lit trigger guard.

Someone out there had been listening.
