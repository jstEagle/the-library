## 5. Thieves of Memory

The Vagrant Choir smelled of solder and cardamom, and Sefa Okonkwo had long ago decided that this was the truest description of her crew anyone would ever manage.

She stood in the galley at the ship's heart, watching the morning cycle paint amber light through the viewport slats, and counted her people the way she counted them every day — not because she feared losing them, but because the counting itself was a kind of prayer. Dov was already awake, of course; Dov was always awake, folded into the corner booth with three screens and a bowl of noodles going cold, his fingers moving over the interfaces with the restless tenderness of a man petting animals only he could see. Pell sat across from him reading an actual paper book, which he did every morning as if daring the universe to comment on it. And Wren —

Wren was late. Wren was sixteen, so Wren was always late.

Sefa poured tea from the dented copper kettle that had come with the ship through four owners before her, and let the ritual settle over her like a coat. Eleven years she'd run the Choir out of the shipping lanes nobody official bothered to name, carrying what the Concord called contraband and what she called the truth in its raw form: letters between lovers separated by colonial reassignment, medical records the insurers had burned, songs from planets that had been administratively dissolved. Memory-smuggling, the bounty boards called it. She'd seen the word *thieves* used too, and she'd stopped arguing about it years ago. You could steal a thing back. That was the whole point. If something had been taken from you, then walking off with it again wasn't theft — it was just a slow, stubborn homecoming.

"Your noodles are dead," she told Dov.

"They were dead an hour ago. I'm eating them ironically." He didn't look up. His hair stood up in gray-shot tufts, and there was a burn scar on his left wrist shaped like a lightning bolt, which he claimed was from a power coupling and which Pell claimed was from falling asleep on a heating element, and which Dov himself had never once confirmed because ambiguity was, to Dov, a food group.

"You need sleep."

"I need bandwidth. Sleep is for people whose networks don't whisper to them at night." He slurped the ironic noodles. "Besides, I heard you pacing at 0300. You only pace when you're turning down jobs we can't afford to turn down."

Sefa said nothing. That was answer enough, and they both knew it.

* * *

The job sat in her breast pocket like a stone. It had arrived two days ago, routed through six cutouts and a dead relay orbiting a moon that officially did not exist: a data packet, heavily encrypted, offering an absurd sum for extraction services at a fixed set of coordinates in the outer Verge. No name. No cargo manifest. Just coordinates, a date nine days out, and a single line of text:

*One passenger. One core. They will kill for it.*

She'd turned down plenty of jobs in eleven years — jobs that smelled of Concord bait, jobs that smelled of slavers wearing honest faces, jobs that simply paid too well for what they asked. This one smelled of none of those things. It smelled of fear, real fear, the kind that came off a transmission like heat off a weld. And it smelled of something else underneath, something she couldn't name yet, the way you sometimes smell rain before the first drop lands.

Pell would tell her it was statistically reckless. Dov would tell her it was interesting. Wren would tell her it was destiny, because sixteen-year-olds believed in destiny the way they believed in their own immortality. Sefa herself believed in cargo manifests, airlock seals, and the specific weight of a promise kept. But she'd read that message forty times now, and each time the last line landed in her chest like a dropped tool.

*They will kill for it.*

In her experience, people only killed for two kinds of cargo: profit and memory. Profit she understood. Memory was the one that got ships burned out of the dark.

* * *

Wren came aboard at 0740, breathless, boots half-laced, a paper bag of pastries clutched against her chest like contraband — which, technically, since they came from a market stall run by a woman with two outstanding Concord citations, they were.

"You started without me," she accused, sliding into the booth beside Pell.

"We started without everyone," Pell said mildly, not lowering his book. "You live here. Punctuality is not a virtue we can gift you; it's one you have to grow yourself."

"You sound like a filing cabinet."

"I was a filing cabinet. For nineteen years." There it was — the flicker under the mildness, the thing Pell carried the way other men carried shrapnel. Sefa saw Wren catch it too, saw the girl's grin soften into something gentler, and slide the pastry bag across the table in silent apology. That was Wren's particular genius, though she didn't know she had it: she could find the sore place in any of them without looking for it, and instead of pressing on it, she'd just... leave something warm nearby.

Sefa had found Wren four years ago on Ceres Junction, thirteen years old and selling forged transit chits with hands so steady they'd have shamed a surgeon. The Kessari diaspora communities clustered in the Junction's lower rings like moths around a lamp that hadn't worked in generations — refugees of a plague, the histories said, a plague two centuries gone that had emptied their ancestral colony and scattered the survivors. Wren wore the braids her grandmother taught her and spoke fragments of a language her grandmother called *old-home words*, and when Sefa had asked her once, early on, whether she missed Kessar, the girl had shrugged with a teenager's magnificent indifference and said, "How can I miss somewhere the plague ate? Grandma says we're the leftovers."

Leftovers. The word had lodged in Sefa ever since, working at her like a splinter she couldn't reach.

"Eat," she told Wren now. "Then nav prep. We're doing the Meridian approach drills today, and last time your drift correction was sloppy enough to embarrass us both."

"It wasn't sloppy, it was *creative*—"

"It was sloppy creatively. Nav prep."

Wren groaned with theatrical despair, took a pastry anyway, and went. Pell watched her go with an expression he'd have denied under torture — the soft one, the one that made him look less like a man who'd spent two decades inside the Concord's Bureau of Records and more like an uncle.

"She'll outfly all of us within the year," he said.

"She'll outfly all of us and know it, which is worse." Sefa sat down across from him. "Pell. I want your read on something."

He closed the book — a real gesture, from him, the equivalent of most people standing at attention — and gave her his full attention. Nineteen years as a Concord auditor had left Pell Ashgrave with a face like a closed ledger and a habit of listening the way other men drew weapons: completely, and with visible consequences pending.

She showed him the message.

He read it twice. His thumb moved along the edge of the paper printout — he still printed things, another exile's habit, trusting paper to keep no copies of its own — and when he looked up, his eyes had gone flat and careful, the auditor's eyes, the ones that had assessed ten thousand documents for signs of forgery and knew exactly what genuine fear looked like in the margins.

"The encryption is Bureau-grade," he said quietly. "Not stolen Bureau-grade. Current."

"You're sure?"

"I designed two of the cipher layers myself." He said it without inflection, the way he said everything about those years, and Sefa felt the old cold move through the galley, the cold that lived in Pell and leaked out of him at moments like this. "Whoever sent this has access to live Concord cryptographic infrastructure. Which means either a very senior defector or someone standing inside a facility we're not supposed to be able to see into."

"And the content?"

"A single passenger and a data core, extraction under hostile conditions." He set the printout down and aligned its edges with the table seam, precise as a man squaring accounts. "Captain, I've audited erasure operations. When the Bureau wants something destroyed, they don't send it out with a stranger. They burn it where it sits. Someone asking for extraction isn't trying to destroy a truth." His jaw tightened. "They're trying to save one."

"That's my read too."

"It's also exactly what a trap would look like," Pell said. "Bait built for me specifically. A live cipher, a rescue framing, a target who spent nineteen years making the Bureau's deletions irreversible." He almost smiled. "If I wanted to flush out a runaway auditor, I'd write this exact message."

"I know." Sefa wrapped her hands around her cooling tea. "And if I turn it down and it's real, then somewhere out there a person holding something worth killing for gets killed for it, and we helped by doing nothing. That's the arithmetic. It doesn't balance either way."

Dov, who had been silent so long they'd both half-forgotten him, suddenly spun his screen around. "Except," he said, "it might not be arithmetic at all. Look at the routing." His finger traced the relay chain glowing on the display. "Six cutouts, right? But cutout four is a ghost node hanging off the old Kessari colonial relay spine. Decommissioned infrastructure. Nobody uses that spine except—" he paused, letting it land, "—except diaspora hobbyists keeping the old frequencies alive. Wren's grandmother's generation. People who remember how to talk to hardware the Concord forgot existed."

The galley went quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the Choir's old ventilation system breathed its asthmatic breath, and the amber light crawled another degree across the deck plates.

"So our mystery client," Sefa said slowly, "is reaching out through Kessari's dead bones."

"Or through someone who knows them well enough to borrow." Dov shrugged, but his eyes were bright, and Sefa recognized the brightness for what it was: hunger, the good kind, the kind that had kept them alive more than once. "Either way, captain — that's not a trap built for Pell. Traps are simple. This is *weird*. Weird is usually just history nobody bothered to erase properly."

Pell made a low sound, almost a laugh. "Spoken like a man who's never been erased."

"I've been erased three times, thank you. It's how I got so good at un-erasing."

* * *

They gathered at 0900 in the choir loft — the name was a joke that had calcified into tradition, the cramped compartment above the galley where the ship's original owners had installed a bank of signal-processing racks that hummed in harmonics when the drives ran hot. Sefa stood at the head of the table. Dov sprawled. Pell sat like punctuation. Wren arrived ninety seconds late with a pastry in each hand and no apology, because apology was for people with fewer talents.

"Here's where we are," Sefa said. "A job with no name, no manifest, and a price that smells wrong. Live Bureau ciphers. Dead colonial relays. Coordinates in the outer Verge, nine days out. Passenger plus data core, hostile extraction expected." She laid the printout flat. "I'm not asking for a vote. I'm telling you what I've decided, and then I'm asking if anyone's getting off before we commit, because after tonight there's no clean way off."

Silence. The kind of silence a family makes, the kind with loyalties in it rather than absence.

Dov raised his hand. "Question. Does 'hostile extraction expected' mean hostile from Concord, hostile from locals, or hostile from the passenger?"

"Unknown."

"Cool. Love it. In."

"Dov—"

"Captain." For once, the flippancy drained out of his voice, and what was underneath was older and tireder. "Three times erased, remember? Somebody out there is sitting on a core full of something the Bureau wants dead. I know exactly what that feels like from the receiving end. I'm in. Stop asking."

Pell was next, and he took longer, because Pell took longer with everything that mattered. He turned the printout toward himself, studied the cipher notation, and Sefa watched him do the thing he did — weigh the trap hypothesis against the rescue hypothesis, auditor against apostate, the man he'd been against the man he'd chosen to become.

"If it's bait," he said finally, "then they built it using ciphers I designed, routed through relays my old bureau decommissioned, aimed at a psychology I helped profile. Which means even the trap is mine." He folded his hands. "I'd rather walk into my own sin with my eyes open than keep circling it forever. In."

All eyes went to Wren.

She'd gone still, which was rare enough that Sefa felt the room tilt slightly around it. The girl stared at the printout — not at the coordinates, not at the payment figure, but at the routing chain, at the little node Dov had flagged on the dead Kessari spine. Her thumb moved toward it and stopped, hovering, the way you hover near a stove you've been burned by.

"My grandmother listens on those frequencies," she said. "Old-home band. She calls it *the quiet channel*. Says it's where the dead colony used to sing, before the plague." She looked up, and her eyes were fierce and frightened in equal measure, sixteen years old and suddenly much older. "If somebody's sending a mayday through Kessari's bones, then it's about us. About the leftovers. Whatever's on that core — it's ours."

"You don't know that," Pell said gently.

"No," Wren agreed. "But I know nobody else is going to go find out." She stuffed the remaining pastry in her mouth, chewed, swallowed, and said with her mouth's ghost still full: "In."

Sefa looked at them — the hacker with three lifetimes of deleted names, the auditor carrying his ledgers of guilt, the girl descended from a plague that had never quite explained itself — and felt the familiar ache in her chest that she'd stopped calling loneliness years ago and started calling crew.

"Then we're committed," she said. "Dov, I want that ghost node mapped and every packet it's passed in the last month pulled apart. Quietly. Pell, threat models — if this is a trap, I want to know its shape before it springs. Wren, nav drills, and yes, actual precision, creativity can wait until we're not being shot at." She gathered the printout, and paused. "Nine days. We do this clean, we do this together, and whatever's on that core —"

"— we carry it home," Wren finished, the old crew litany, half-joke, half-oath.

"— we carry it home," Sefa confirmed, and dismissed them to work.

* * *

That night, alone in the choir loft with the drives humming their low chord below her, Sefa fed the mysterious coordinates into the navigation plot and watched the route bloom across the display: out through the shipping lanes, past the Meridian Gate beacon, into the outer Verge where charts thinned and the Concord's attention frayed to nothing.

The destination resolved. An unremarkable star. An unremarkable system. And orbiting a gas giant on the far edge of it, tagged in the survey records with a designation so dull it was practically camouflage:

*Deep Range Seed Vault 7. Custodial assignment. Non-strategic.*

Sefa stared at it for a long time.

Eleven years ago, fresh out of a bad war and a worse marriage, she'd taken a contract haul to that same sector and remembered the vault's beacon pulsing patiently at the edge of her sensors, singing to no one, a lighthouse for a sea with no ships. Everyone in the lanes knew about the seed vaults. Everyone knew they were relics, holdovers from a founding era that liked its symbols green and hopeful. Nobody wondered what else a vault built to preserve things against erasure might have been preserving.

Nobody except, apparently, whoever had just hired her through the bones of a dead world.

Sefa Okonkwo touched the plot, and the route to the vault glowed steady beneath her finger like a pulse.

"Well," she said to the empty loft, to the humming racks, to the ship that had become the only family any of them would admit to wanting. "Let's go meet the gardener."

Behind her, unnoticed, the comm rack chimed once — a single incoming fragment on a frequency the Concord had decommissioned two centuries ago, in a language with no living speakers, addressed to no one and waiting for anyone.
