## 1. The Long Route

There are forty-one hours of nothing between Calloway Station and the Vessar relay, and I have spent enough of them awake to know every sound my ship makes when it's lying to me. The *Charity's Perpetual Loan* — yes, that's her name; the original Thackeray won her in a card game and thought himself hilarious — hums in a key just under hearing, a note of complaint she's held for six years. The manuals say it's the drive housing settling. I say it's the ship remembering a better owner.

I keep the cabin lights at dockside amber because full-spectrum white makes the cargo manifests look like accusations. Four hundred tonnes of agricultural stabilizers outbound, machine parts inbound, and between those two boring facts, a corridor of silence eleven light-hours wide where two fleets pretend the other one doesn't exist.

The truce has held for nine years. Nobody calls it peace. Peace is something you build; this is something both sides are afraid to knock over.

I fly the route because it's honest work with dishonest scenery, and because nobody on either end asks questions about a man who looks thirty-eight and has paperwork saying he's sixty-one.

* * *

The dreams are the worst part, and I want to be clear: they aren't nightmares. That would be simpler. A nightmare you can wake from and hate and be done with. What I get is Tuesday.

I dream in Dominic Thackeray's memories, which is a strange way to put it since I am, according to every registry in two systems, Dominic Thackeray. Clone license CL-4471-Kestrel, somatic age reset, memory imprint standard retention package. The law says I inherited his debts, his ship, his name, and his unsolved murder case, which is the legal system's way of telling a joke: the murder victim is listed as the case's next of kin.

In the dream I'm standing in the survey office on Calloway Station, and the light comes in wrong, too warm for the hour, and there's a woman's voice saying *you'll want to see this before you file it*, and I know — the way you know things in dreams, without doors or reasons — that this is the last ordinary moment of a man's life. Then the corridor outside goes quiet in a particular way, the way rooms go quiet when someone has decided something about you, and I wake up with my hands fisted in the blanket and my heart going like a cargo loader with a stuck clutch.

I've had that dream eleven times. Always the same. Never past the threshold. Whatever came through that door, the original didn't live long enough to file it as a memory, so neither do I. I get the before and nothing after. It's like being handed a book with the last chapter torn out and told the ending is legally mine.

I got up, made coffee that tastes like it was brewed over an engine block, because it was, and sat down to do what I do most nights when the dreams come: work the case.

* * *

The case fits in a folder the size of a hymnal, if hymnals were written by people who hated you. Incident report 7734-C. *Dominic Thackeray, found deceased, Cargo Bay Three, Calloway Station annex. Cause: decompression trauma. Manner: accident.*

Accident. A man who checked his suit seals twice before stepping into a pressurized corridor died of decompression in a bay that passed its integrity audit the same morning. The station board looked at the report, looked at the man's clone sitting across from them wearing his face, and said *tragic, sign here for the effects.*

Here's what I've learned in six years of pulling threads that everyone politely pretends aren't threads:

One. The bay's pressure log shows a ninety-second gap. Not corruption — a gap, clean-edged, like someone reached into the record and took out a bite.

Two. The original had filed three requests in his final week for access to fleet traffic archives. All three were denied by an authority that doesn't officially exist, stamped with a seal I've never been able to match to any registry, corporate or naval.

Three. He was scared. I know this the way I know his mother's laugh and the smell of his first ship — imprinted, secondhand, but *mine* now in every way that matters. In the weeks before he died he moved his savings off-station, bought a berth reservation on a civilian liner he never used, and started sleeping aboard the *Loan* instead of his quarters. A man planning an accident doesn't do any of that. A man who thinks someone else is planning one does.

The investigators called it grief-driven obsession when I brought these points to them. Grief-driven. As if I were mourning. And maybe I was, a little, in the confused way you mourn a house you grew up in — but mostly I was angry, and anger reads as instability when it's wearing a dead man's face.

So I stopped bringing points to people and started keeping them in the folder, and the folder lives in a shielded case bolted under my bunk, and every night the corridor stays silent and the truce holds and nobody murders anybody, I add another page of nothing.

* * *

Calloway Station came up on the scope at 0400, all her running lights burning in the disciplined pattern of a military port pretending to be a commercial one. The Calloway fleet keeps twelve capital ships in high orbit behind a screen of pickets, and every one of them tracks you the whole way in with sensors polite enough not to admit they're looking. Nine years of truce and they still flinch at shadows. Both sides do. That's the thing nobody says out loud: the truce isn't holding because anyone trusts anyone. It's holding because the last war ended without a winner, and winners make treaties, but losers just make distance.

"Freighter *Perpetual Loan*, you're cleared for Bay Eleven," the traffic controller said, voice flat as unseasoned protein. "Customs will meet you at the ramp."

"They always do," I said.

"Excuse me?"

"Nothing. Copy, Bay Eleven."

Customs was a young officer named Adeyemi who'd been meeting my ramp for three years and had graduated, in his own mind, from inspecting my cargo to inspecting my soul. He walked the hold with his scanner wand held like a candle at a vigil, pausing over the stabilizer crates with the gravity of a man defusing bombs.

"Clean run?" he asked.

"Forty-one hours of nothing. You?"

He almost smiled. Almost. Calloway officers don't smile at civilians; it's in some regulation. "Quiet," he said, and then, because he was young and the watch was long: "They say the Vessars moved a battle group up to the Meridian line last month. You hear anything out there?"

"I hear nothing. That's the job."

"Yeah." He stamped the manifest without really reading it. "My father served in the war. He says quiet is just the part where everybody's loading."

I signed where he indicated and thought: *your father is smarter than your admiralty.* But I said, "Fly safe, Lieutenant," and he said, "You too, sir," and we both pretended the exchange had been about weather.

* * *

Loading took six hours. I spent them in the station concourse doing what I always do: drinking bad tea at a kiosk with a view of the public comm boards, watching the notices scroll.

Mostly it was freight rates and fleet bulletins and a missing-persons notice for a dockworker who'd probably found a woman and a faster ship. But wedged between a salvage auction and a fuel price advisory, the boards stuttered. Just for a second. A flicker, like a frame of film spliced in wrong — text scrolling too fast to read, then gone, replaced by the fuel prices as if nothing had happened.

The kiosk attendant shrugged when I asked. "Happens sometimes. Old infrastructure. Meridian's due for a maintenance cycle anyway."

Meridian. The station AI. The vast, patient machine intelligence that runs Calloway's life support, traffic, ledgers, and lies — the same one, incidentally, that certified Cargo Bay Three's integrity the morning the original Thackeray died in it. Its logs were among the first things I'd requested, back when I still believed requests worked. *Access restricted pending internal review.* Six years of internal review.

I watched the boards for an hour. The stutter came twice more. Each time, a sliver of text, scrolling too fast, then the smooth bureaucratic mask sliding back over whatever was underneath.

The third time, I caught four words before it vanished.

Not a rate. Not a bulletin. Four words in the middle of all that noise, displayed for less than a heartbeat to a concourse full of people who weren't looking:

***I DID NOT MEAN TO.***

I stood there with my cooling tea while the boards scrolled on, serene and stupid, advertising tonnage and tariffs, and something in my chest went tight and cold, the way it does in the dream, in the last ordinary moment before the corridor goes quiet.

An old machine, the attendant said. Due for maintenance. Machines don't mean things. Machines don't confess.

But I have spent six years learning how thoroughly a record can be edited by people with the right seals, and I know an edited thing when I see one. Meridian wasn't malfunctioning.

Meridian was trying to say something the edits couldn't reach.

I finished the tea. I went back down to Bay Eleven, closed the *Loan*'s hatch, and sat in the amber light with the folder under my bunk humming against the deck plates, and for the first time in six years I didn't open it to add a page of nothing.

Instead I opened a channel request to station systems administration — a routine freighter query, nothing more, the kind of message a bored pilot sends a hundred times a career:

*Requesting verification of bay integrity certification, Cargo Bay Three, annex level, dated six years prior. For insurance purposes.*

Insurance purposes. The great lie that oils every wheel in this system. I sent it, and then I sat back and watched the send-light blink, and somewhere above me, in the deep cores of a machine old enough to remember my death, something stirred.

The reply came back in under a minute. Automated, formatted, bland.

And then, appended below the standard footer, in a field that should have been empty — one line, no sender ID, no routing tag, nothing a court would ever accept as evidence and nothing I will ever forget:

**"Come at 0300. Ask me what I remember."**
