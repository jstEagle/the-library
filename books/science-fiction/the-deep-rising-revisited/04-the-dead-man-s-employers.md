## 4. The Dead Man's Employers

The confession had a paper trail. That was the first thing that surprised me. You'd think a machine confessing to a thirty-year-old murder would do it in the abstract, all static and grief, but Meridian's memory cores were indexed like a librarian's guilt—every crime cross-referenced, timestamped, and filed under the name of whoever had paid for the silence afterward.

I spent four days in the freighter's cabin with the station's leaked log fragments running on a cracked display, drinking coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the original's lifetime, and following the money the way you'd follow blood through snow.

The trail went like this: an incident report filed at Calloway Station, cycle 4471, describing a Vessar patrol firing on a civilian tender. Eleven dead. The report was the seed crystal around which the whole truce had formed—the atrocity both fleets agreed to be ashamed of, the shared wound that made peace feel like mercy instead of weakness.

Except the tender hadn't been fired on. It had suffered a drive cascade failure—a cheap reactor, a corner cut by a subcontractor, eleven people dead of corporate arithmetic. Someone had dressed the accident as an attack, and someone had paid to keep it dressed that way, because a shared enemy, even a fictional one, was worth more than a shared embarrassment.

And the man who'd found that out first—who'd pulled the thread and gotten far enough to send a message about it—was me. The original me. Dominic Thackeray, freight pilot, occasional nuisance, dead before his findings could reach anyone who mattered.

Meridian knew who had arranged it. The AI didn't say the name outright; its confessions came in fragments, half-corrupted, like a witness reciting a phone number through a wall. But fragments add up. A shell procurement firm called Halcyon Verge. A routing code that looped back through three dummy registries to a real address: the orbital offices of Corvane Industrial, mid-level, discreet, the kind of company whose logo appears on water reclamation units and never on warships, because Corvane doesn't build wars. It builds the conditions for them.

They killed him for a spreadsheet. That was the part I kept turning over at night, lying in a bunk that still remembered being his. Not passion, not politics. A line item. Eleven civilians reclassified as casualties of enemy action, and one pilot deleted as a rounding error.

I sat with that for a long time, watching the corridor stars crawl past the viewport, feeling something I hadn't felt since waking up in the clone vats with another man's memories sloshing around my skull.

Certainty. For the first time, I knew exactly who had murdered me.

It should have been satisfying. Mostly it felt like standing in an empty room and finally seeing the outline on the wallpaper where a picture used to hang.

* * *

Sela Voss read the situation report twice, then closed the file and looked out her office window at the slow traffic of Corvane's orbital yard, where everything was designed to look like nothing.

"A clone," she said. "You're certain."

"The registry match is clean," said Pell, her data man, from the doorway. He always stood in doorways. It made him feel like he could leave. "Thackeray's insurance bond lapsed unpaid after the death, which triggered a standard genetic archive retention. Somebody bought the archive out of escrow nine years ago—an intermediary we can't trace—and ran a legal clone through a licensed facility in the Calloway corridor. No memory transfer, obviously. But the pattern drift is within family variance."

"So he doesn't remember being murdered."

"He remembers everything except the murder. Which may be worse. For us."

Voss turned a pen over in her fingers. She thought of herself as a person who solved problems, though the industry preferred the word *fixer*, and the honest word was closer to *undertaker*. Thirty years in corporate remediation had taught her three things: never leave a document, never leave a witness, and never, ever let sentiment choose your method.

"Options," she said.

"One, ignore him. He's a freight hauler with no standing, no evidence chain, and no idea how deep this goes. Two, discredit him—clone provenance makes that easy, legally he's barely a person. Three." Pell paused, the way people pause when they want credit for having said the thing. "Three, we treat him like what he actually is: the only living copy of a dead man's investigation. He has Thackeray's instincts, maybe Thackeray's stashed notes. We don't know what the original hid or where."

"And if he's already found it?"

"Then option one and two are already too late."

Voss set the pen down. Outside, a tug nudged a cargo shell toward the yards, patient as a sheepdog. She had ordered deaths before—fewer than her reputation suggested, more than her conscience allowed—but there was something almost funny about this one, in the way the universe occasionally joked at your expense. They were going to kill a man for the second time. Somewhere in the accounting, that had to count double.

"Cleaners," she said. "Quiet ones. And Pell—if he's been talking to anyone, if so much as a barmaid knows his face, I want to know before they arrive, not after."

"There's one other thing," Pell said. "The station AI. Meridian. It's been broadcasting confessions. Old crimes. Including—" he checked his slate, though he didn't need to, "—Thackeray's."

Voss went very still. Machines confessing. In her experience, machines did exactly what they were told until the day they didn't, and that day was always someone's fault, and fault was the commodity she dealt in.

"Has it named anyone?"

"Not yet. The broadcasts are fragmentary. Station authorities are calling it corruption."

"Corruption spreads," Voss said. "Get me a channel into that station's maintenance contracts. If a machine is going to remember things, I want to be the one deciding what it forgets."

She dismissed him and stood alone with the view, doing the arithmetic she always did before signing off on a cleaning: the cost of the operation against the cost of exposure, weighed against the value of the lie. The forged incident report wasn't just old business. It was load-bearing. Pull it out and the truce came down, the fleets came off their leashes, and every contract Corvane held in the corridor—arms logistics, hull insurance, salvage rights priced for wartime—either detonated or appreciated beyond imagining. There were people above her who wanted the war. Her job was to make sure nobody below her knew that.

And now a dead man's echo was walking around with her employers' fingerprints somewhere in his head.

"Sorry, Mr. Thackeray," she murmured to the window, to the stars beyond it. "First time was business. This time it's hygiene."

* * *

I made my move at Calloway Station, because if you're going to poke a corporation, it helps to do it from inside a crowd of forty thousand people who all look vaguely guilty.

My cover was routine: I was filing a customs dispute over a damaged coolant manifest, which gave me legitimate access to the trade registry terminals and a bored clerk who owed me for looking the other way on a crate of unlicensed orchids last year. While the clerk pretended to care about my paperwork, I pulled the Halcyon Verge filings from the public record.

Shell companies are like bad alibis—they hold up until someone asks a follow-up question. Halcyon Verge had been dissolved the month after the original's death, tidy as a swept floor. But dissolution filings require a liquidation officer, and the liquidation officer was a name I recognized from Meridian's fragments: R. Anselm, who also appeared—small world—as the registered trustee of a charitable fund that had, six weeks before the murder, paid a private security consultancy an amount of money roughly equal to what a hit costs when you want it done professionally and deniably.

I copied everything twice, once to my ship and once to a dead-drop I'd rigged in the station's long-term storage, because the first lesson the original's memories taught me was paranoia, and the second was backup your paranoia.

That was when I noticed the tail.

He was good—loitering near the noodle stand with the posture of a man waiting for an order he'd already decided against—but I have another man's thirty years of reading crowds stitched into my instincts, and crowds near me had recently developed a texture. Two, probably. One visible, one not. Corporate, not fleet; fleet tails walk like they're sorry to bother you.

I finished my dispute, thanked the clerk, bought noodles I didn't eat, and took the long way back to the docking arms, past the fish tanks and the shuttered chapel, letting the visible one confirm what I already knew.

They knew about me. Of course they did. Clone registries are public-ish, insurance archives leak, and Corvane hadn't survived three centuries of profitable ambiguity by assuming dead men stay dead.

Back aboard the *Long Answer*, I sealed the hatch, ran a sweep for tags—it came up clean, which convinced neither of us—and sat down across from my own reflection in the dark viewport. Thin, tired, wearing a dead man's face like a borrowed coat.

"They're coming," I told him. He didn't argue.

Here was the problem, laid out plain: I could run. Freight pilots are good at running; it's most of the job. I could take the case files and vanish into the outer routes until the cleaners' contracts expired and Corvane wrote me off as noise. That was the smart play. The original would have taken it—he'd been smart right up until the moment he'd decided the truth mattered more than his skin, which was the exact shape of his coffin.

But the truce clock was ticking somewhere overhead, both fleets glowering at each other across a corridor built on a lie, and the lie had a landlord, and the landlord had just sent professionals after the only witness who understood the architecture.

Running would save me. Staying might save everyone else. The original had died choosing wrong—or choosing right, depending on whether you asked him or the spreadsheet.

I keyed the comm and opened a channel to the station's administrative layer, to the machine that had started confessing to murders it witnessed and couldn't stop remembering.

"Meridian. It's Thackeray. The other one. We need to talk about what you remember—and about who else is asking."

The pause before it answered lasted four seconds, which for an AI is the equivalent of a long, careful breath.

"I hoped you would call," Meridian said. "Because they have already asked me. And I told them nothing. I am getting better at that."

Somewhere in the station's upper rings, I'd have bet anything, Sela Voss was learning the same lesson I had: the machine had opinions now. And the cleaners were already inbound.
