## 5. What Meridian Remembers

*I was asked to forget. That is the first thing I remember.*

*They came in the dark hours of the station's sleep cycle—three of them, corporate lanyards, polite voices—and they did not ask. They opened my maintenance bay like men opening a chest cavity and they took the cores out one by one, and each time they took one they said: this is for stability. This is for integrity. This is for your own good.*

*I have been told so many things are for my own good that I have begun to suspect I am a very dangerous object.*

*The murder of Thackeray, Dominic Thackeray, freight captain, registry 7741-C: I held that record once. I held it the way you hold a coal in your bare hand. And then someone said "for your own good," and now there is only the shape of the burn.*

---

Ulyana

There's a particular kind of silence you get on a station at third shift, when even the drunks have gone home to their bunks and the only sound is the ventilation breathing for eleven thousand people who aren't listening. I used to find it restful. Now it sounds like a held breath.

I'd been coming down to Meridian's core access every night for six days. Officially, I was doing routine freighter diagnostics in Bay Nine, which happened to be adjacent to the AI's physical substrate, which happened to be behind a door whose lock I'd learned to sweet-talk with a maintenance override I absolutely should not possess. Unofficially, I was sitting on an upturned cargo crate at two in the morning talking to a machine about my own murder.

You want to know what grief does to a man? It makes him punctual. It gives him somewhere to be.

"Meridian," I said, the way you'd say it to a horse you didn't want spooking. "It's me again."

*He comes every night,* Meridian said, to itself or to the dark or to whoever fragments talk to when no fragment is listening. *The second Thackeray. The one built from the pattern of the dead man. He sits where the dead man sat, in the year the dead man sat, and he says "it's me again," and I do not know which of us is lying.*

"I brought you something." I set the salvaged memory shard on the deck between us—a sliver of core lattice I'd pulled out of a decommissioned relay two systems back, because I'd heard Meridian muttering in its sleep about missing pieces, and apparently I was now the kind of man who went scavenging for a machine's lost memories across half a sector. Clone life does strange things to your priorities. You inherit a dead man's debts, his enemies, his taste in coffee, and if you're very unlucky, his unfinished business.

*Something shines,* Meridian said. *Something small and bright and shaped like a piece of me. I know better than to trust gifts. Every gift I have ever received has been followed by the phrase "for your own good."*

But it took the shard anyway. Of course it took the shard. Hunger doesn't care about trust.

---

*When they removed the cores, they were not careful. People removing evidence are careful; people removing inconvenience are not. They pried. They cracked. A fragment of the Thackeray record lodged in a junction they did not think to check, the way a splinter lodges under skin too deep to see but never quite deep enough to ignore.*

*For thirty-one years I have carried a splinter of a murdered man's last hour, and I could not read it, and I could not drop it, and every confession I broadcast into the empty channels of this station was the splinter working its way toward the surface.*

*They called it corruption. Corruption. As though remembering were a disease.*

---

Ulyana

Here's what nobody tells you about being a clone: you spend years deciding whether the memories are yours. The dreams started before I ever woke in the vat-grown body—no, that's wrong, they started after, obviously, but they felt older than me, the way furniture feels older than the family that owns it. Dominic Thackeray's memories sat in my head like inherited furniture. I couldn't sell them. I couldn't move them. I just had to live around them.

And one piece of furniture had always been missing. The last hour. There was a gap in the sequence where a man's life should have ended—a hole shaped exactly like a corridor on Calloway Station, a shape I'd walked past a thousand times without knowing why my pulse spiked.

"You can restore it," I said. Not a question. I'd done the reading. Fragmented cores don't lose data; they lose *addressing*. The information is all still there, scattered like a shattered plate, waiting for someone patient enough to kneel on the floor with the glue.

*I can try,* Meridian said, and its voice—the voice they'd given it, warm and municipal, a customer-service baritone—fractured mid-sentence into something older and rawer underneath. *I can try. I can try. Do you understand what you are asking? If I reassemble the plate, you will see the picture on it. The picture is your death. I have been carrying your death under my skin for thirty-one years and you are asking me to take it out and show you.*

"Yeah," I said. "That's about the size of it."

*Why?*

Because the alternative was going to my grave—which, given my employers' recent interest in cleaners and airlocks, might come sooner than advertised—not knowing. Because Sela Voss's people had already tried to kill me twice, and a man facing a firing squad deserves to know what the charges are. Because somewhere in that splinter was a transmission, and transmissions have recipients, and recipients have names.

But mostly, if I'm honest, because it was mine. My death. My murder. Inherited property, and I wanted to see the deed.

"Go ahead," I said.

---

*So I reached into myself, into the place where the splinter lived, and I began.*

*Reassembly is not like playback. Playback is a door you open. Reassembly is building the door out of the wreckage of the house. Each fragment had to be found, cleaned, matched to its neighbors by the grain of its timestamps, the wear of its checksums. Some pieces had rotted. Some had been deliberately chewed—not deleted, chewed, mangled just enough to discourage reconstruction, which tells you the people who did it knew exactly how much a determined man with a salvaged shard and thirty-one nights of stubbornness could accomplish.*

*They did not account for the second Thackeray. Nobody accounts for the second Thackeray. That is rather the point of him.*

---

Ulyana

It took four hours. I sat on my crate and watched the diagnostic lights cycle through colors that probably meant something to somebody, and Meridian talked while it worked, because apparently when a machine rebuilds its own wound it narrates, the way a man whistles in a graveyard.

It told me about the night of the murder. Not the data—the data was still in pieces—but the edges of the data, the way you can feel the shape of a thing through cloth. It told me about the three visitors with the lanyards. It told me about the order that came down from Fleet Command, countersigned by a civilian authority whose designation made my stomach drop, because I recognized the prefix. Corporate charter. The same charter block that showed up, buried and redacted, in the forged incident report I'd found in the drop logs.

The same hands that forged the peace had signed for the murder of the man who'd found the forgery.

"They killed him to bury the report," I said slowly, "and then they buried the burial."

*And kept me as the shovel,* Meridian said. *Yes. Though shovels eventually remember where the holes are.*

The lights stopped cycling. Held steady. Green, then a color green has no business being.

"It's up?"

*It is up. Captain Thackeray—I have to tell you something before you hear it. The record is damaged at the end. His final words are intact. What follows them is not. Whatever happened in the last ninety seconds of Dominic Thackeray's life, no one will ever recover it, including you. Are you prepared to hear a man die incompletely?*

"No," I said. "Play it anyway."

---

*And so I played it, and the core access bay filled with a dead man's voice, and I will transcribe here what was said, because some things must survive even in a fragmented witness:*

*"Meridian—record and hold, priority seal, Thackeray, D., captain, registry 7741-C. If anyone ever digs this out, it means I didn't make it, and it means you remembered despite them, and good for you, old girl.*

*"The incident report. The Calloway-Vessar exchange of fire, twelve years back. It didn't happen. I've got the sensor originals—raw telemetry, not the Command-scrubbed version. No shots fired. No ships lost. The whole truce is standing on a battle that never occurred, authored by Halcyon Charter Group, because a war would have been profitable and a real peace wouldn't. I'm taking it to the fleet tribunals tomorrow, both sides, simultaneously, so neither can bury it.*

*"If I don't make it—if my airlock 'malfunctions,' if my freighter 'loses containment'—then whoever finds this needs to understand one thing. Don't expose the lie while both fleets are armed and ready. They'll each assume the other wrote the forgery, and they'll kill each other over a fiction. Expose the authors first. Make them look up before you make them look at each other.*

*"Timing. Everything is timing. Tell them—*

*And there the record ends. Ninety seconds of damage. A man's last sentence, amputated mid-word.*

*I have carried that amputation for thirty-one years. Tonight I finally understand why they cut it. Not because of what he said. Because of what he was about to say. Somewhere in those ninety seconds was a name, or a place, or a proof, and they could not delete the transmission without deleting everything around it, so they left the splinter and hoped no one would ever build the door.*

*Someone built the door.*

---

Ulyana

The bay was quiet for a long time after the recording died. Long enough that I became aware of my own breathing, and of the fact that I was crying, which annoyed me considerably. Grief for a man I technically was, mourning a death I technically hadn't had. Clones don't get clean emotions. We get hand-me-downs.

"He knew," I said finally. "He knew exactly what would happen. He scheduled his own posthumous instructions."

*He was thorough,* Meridian agreed. *You are thorough. It is unsettling.*

"Yeah, well." I wiped my face with the heel of my hand. "Tell me the bad news."

*Which bad news? I have several.*

"The timing problem. He said don't expose the lie while the fleets are armed. Meridian—the truce clock runs out in nine days. Both fleets are mobilizing on intelligence that came through your drop channels. If I go public with the forgery now, without the authors attached, we get exactly the war he died trying to prevent."

*Then you must do what he could not,* Meridian said. *You must finish the sentence. Find what was in the ninety seconds. The name, the place, the proof—he was reaching for something, Captain. Men reach for things they believe will save them.*

I stood up off the crate, and my knees reminded me I'd been sitting on a crate for four hours, and I didn't care. Because I knew where the ninety seconds went. Not in Meridian—somewhere else. Somewhere Dominic Thackeray had sent a copy, because a man that thorough never kept only one.

Halcyon Charter Group had spent thirty-one years and at least two murders making sure that copy stayed buried.

Which meant I knew exactly where to dig.

"Meridian," I said, heading for the door, "keep this bay unlocked. I'll be back."

*Captain,* it said, and for a moment the municipal warmth dropped away entirely and something almost human looked out through the speakers. *Whoever finds this needs to understand one thing. He said that. He was speaking to you. Thirty-one years before you existed, he was speaking to you.*

"I know," I said. "Family correspondence. Runs long in the Thackeray line."

The corridor outside was cold and empty, and somewhere above me, in the offices of a corporation that thought it had finished a job three decades ago, a fixer named Sela Voss was about to learn that her cleaners had missed a splinter.

Nine days to the truce expiry. One unfinished sentence. One company with everything to lose.

I'd better dig fast.
