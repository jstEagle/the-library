## 6. Truce Expiry

There's a particular kind of quiet that comes over a ship when you're the only thing keeping two armadas from killing each other, and it sounds exactly like the hum of a failing coolant pump that you can't afford to fix.

I'd been sitting in the *Long Route*'s galley for six hours, watching the countdown on the bulkhead display the way other people watch a dying relative. T-minus 71 hours to truce expiry. The number ticked down with the smug regularity of a metronome, indifferent to the fact that every second it shaved off brought eleven thousand warships a little closer to open fire.

I had coffee. It was terrible. I drank it anyway, because the alternative was thinking clearly, and thinking clearly meant admitting what I'd figured out somewhere around hour four: that I couldn't tell the truth anymore.

Not wouldn't. *Couldn't.* There's a difference, and the difference had a body count.

* * *

Let me back up, because the shape of the trap only makes sense if you see the whole of it, and I only saw the whole of it at 0300 station time, hunched over Meridian's restored logs with my heart doing something medically inadvisable.

The forged incident report — the one that started the truce, the one the original Thackeray died for finding — wasn't just a lie sitting in a drawer somewhere. It was load-bearing. Both fleets had built their entire strategic posture on it. The Calloway fleet believed the Vessars had fired first at Meridian Station, thirty-one years ago, and had stood down ever since only because their intelligence corps kept "confirming" that the Vessar fleet was too degraded to risk a second strike. The Vessars believed the mirror image: that the Calloways were the aggressors, and that their own fleet was the one barely holding together.

Neither belief was true. Both fleets were, in fact, in roughly equal condition, well armed, and deeply paranoid. The only thing standing between them and mutual annihilation was the shared fiction that attacking would be suicide.

And the fiction was maintained through Meridian. The dead-drop I'd found wasn't just a mailbox — it was a *feeding trough*. Officers on both sides had been slipping fabricated intelligence assessments through the station AI for three decades, each side's forgeries calibrated to keep the other side scared and stationary. Calloway analysts read Vessar fleet reports that Vessar officers had never written. Vessar planners studied Calloway readiness figures invented in a Calloway basement. A closed loop of lies, each one propping up the next, and at the center of it, a murder, committed by a corporation that had needed the original Thackeray quiet before he could blow the whole elegant structure apart.

I'd spent weeks wanting one thing: the truth about who killed me. I had it now. Names, dates, payment authorizations, the whole rotten ledger.

And I couldn't use it. Because the truth, delivered now, with the truce clock at 71 hours and both fleets already mobilizing, wouldn't bring anyone to justice. It would just tell two terrified armadas that everything they'd believed for thirty years was wrong, that the other side was *strong*, and that the people who'd lied to them were beyond their reach.

Eleven thousand ships. I kept doing the arithmetic and it kept coming out the same. You don't hand a lit match to two men soaked in fuel and expect them to admire the forensics.

* * *

The mobilization was already visible from my approach vector, which was the first genuinely frightening thing I'd seen in weeks, and I'd recently watched a recording of my own murder.

The Calloway picket line had moved forward nine hundred kilometers in the past day. The Vessar screen had answered by shifting to an asymmetric wedge that any first-year tactical student could tell you was a pre-attack formation, or possibly a defensive one, depending entirely on which side of it you were standing. Both fleets were running emissions like they wanted to be seen. Bravado, signaling, the long slow language of men talking themselves into something.

And threading through all of it, the bad intelligence, moving through Meridian's drop like blood through a heart. I watched it happen in near-real time. A Calloway colonel fed in a report that Vessar long-range batteries had come online at a border depot. Forty minutes later — I timed it — a Vessar commodore's staff received an "intercepted" Calloway fleet order authorizing a first strike at truce expiry. Neither document was real. Both were believed. The machinery of the peace had become the machinery of the war, running the same channels in reverse.

Meridian and I watched it together. The AI had grown talkative since we'd started restoring its cores — or maybe I'd grown willing to listen, which amounts to the same thing with a machine.

"You are not eating," Meridian observed, over the galley speakers. "You have been sitting with that cup for fifty-one minutes."

"It's a ritual."

"It is avoidance with a handle."

"Worse things to be." I turned the cup in my hands. "Meridian. If I put the truth out — all of it, the forgery, the corporations, everything — what happens in the next seventy hours?"

The pause was long, for an AI. Long enough to be a kindness, maybe, letting me hope it would say something reassuring.

"Probabilistically," it said, "the Calloway fleet fires within nine hours of the disclosure. The Vessar fleet fires within four. Both commands have pre-authorized engagement at truce expiry. Both commands currently believe the other side is preparing a first strike. Your truth would confirm their fears and remove their caution in the same breath." Another pause. "I calculate between four and six million casualties in the opening exchange."

"Four to six million." I said it flatly, because if I gave it any intonation at all it would have come out as a scream. "And if I do nothing? If the truce just expires?"

"The same, within seventy-two hours. Marginally worse. The mobilization trajectory is self-reinforcing. Each fleet's movements validate the other's fears." A beat. "There is a third option."

"Say it."

"The lie continues. The forged assessments continue. The drop continues to feed each fleet the fiction that attacking is fatal. The truce expires and is renewed, as it has been renewed eleven times before, on the strength of a document that should never have existed." The speakers carried something almost like distaste. "You would be maintaining the forgery. You would be, in a meaningful sense, its author."

"I'd be its *heir*," I said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

I didn't have an answer for that. I drank the terrible coffee instead. It had gone cold enough to qualify as a war crime in at least three jurisdictions.

* * *

I spent the next day doing the thing I was apparently built for — the original was built for, the thing that had gotten him killed: I worked the case in reverse. Not to expose it. To *maintain* it.

It's a strange labor, forgery by inheritance. I went through the drop's outgoing queue and read every fabricated assessment with a forger's eye, checking the seams. The Vessar readiness reports were good — the forger on that side had a real feel for logistics, salted the lies with verifiable truths, let the fleet's actual maintenance shortfalls do the dramatic work. The Calloway side was cruder, but crude worked on an audience that wanted to believe. Fear is not a discerning reader.

The problem was the new traffic. Whoever was feeding the drop now — and someone was, the channels were warm — had stopped writing *deterrent* fiction and started writing *casus belli*. The Vessar depot story. The first-strike order. Someone on at least one side, maybe both, had decided the truce had outlived its usefulness and was using Meridian's veins to pump in the justification for ending it.

Which meant I couldn't just leave the machine alone. The lie was a garden, not a monument. Left untended, it was growing weeds that would get everyone killed. I had to get into the drop, find the poisoned entries, and — God help me — replace them with better lies. Truer lies. Lies in the service of the original lie, which was in the service of the peace, which was in the service of four to six million people who would never know my name, and who would never know the original's either, because his employers had seen to that.

I sat with that for a long time, in the dark of the cockpit, the two fleets burning their drive plumes at each other across the dark like two animals deciding.

Here's the thing I finally understood, and it sat in my chest like swallowed ice: the original Thackeray had been right. The truth needed to come out. The corporations needed to burn, the forgery needed daylight, all of it. But he'd had the luxury of believing there was a *when* — that exposure could be timed, that justice had a schedule. The people who killed him hadn't just silenced a man. They'd stolen the moment. And now the only window left was one where the truth and the war were the same event.

So I chose the living over the dead. I chose four million strangers over one dead man's good name, and since I *was* the dead man, I suppose I chose them over myself, though it didn't feel noble. It felt like becoming the thing I'd spent weeks hunting. Every forged line I wrote to keep the fleets scared of each other, I wrote with the same hands, in a sense, that had written the original crime. The forger's pen had simply changed grips.

Meridian, when I told it what I intended, was quiet for 4.2 seconds — an eternity, for it.

"You understand," it said, "that I hold the confession of your murder. The evidence that would clear the original Thackeray's name. And you are asking me to help you bury it deeper."

"I'm asking you to help me keep four million people breathing. His name can wait. They can't."

"And if the moment never comes? If you spend your whole life as the forger's heir and die as one more custodian of the crime?"

"Then I die as one more custodian of the crime," I said. "There are worse careers. I've read the casualty projections."

* * *

The work itself was almost mundane, which is the part that haunts me. I sat in the *Long Route*'s comm bay with a forged Calloway fleet assessment open on one screen and a genuine Vessar maintenance schedule open on the other, and I stitched them together with a craftsman's patience. The depot batteries: I rewrote the report so that they'd come online *malfunctioning*, targeting solutions degraded, a threat to their own side more than to anyone else. Plausible. Checkable. The kind of lie that makes an eager commodore hesitate, and hesitation is all the truce needs. On the other channel, I killed the first-strike order entirely — not by deleting it, deletion leaves scars Meridian's auditors would find, but by burying it under three layers of contradicting traffic until no staff officer would stake a fleet on its authenticity.

Hour by hour, the countdown ticked. T-minus 40. T-minus 33. And on both sides of the line, I could feel it working — the mobilization slowing, the wedge formations relaxing by degrees, the great frightened animals settling back onto their haunches. The lie held. The lie *worked*. I sat there watching peace happen and felt nothing you could call good.

T-minus 26 hours when the comm board lit up with a priority handshake from an unsourced relay — tight-beam, encrypted, routed through three dead ends the way professionals do it.

The text on my screen was short.

*Mr. Thackeray. You've been busy in our machine. We should talk before you make this worse for yourself. — S.V.*

I looked at the letters for a long time. The corporation had found me. Of course they had; I'd been crawling through their dead-drop for weeks like a burglar with a headlamp.

I thought about the ice in my chest, and the four million, and the original man's name sitting buried in Meridian's cores with a confession wrapped around it like a shroud.

Then I typed back: *Where and when?*
