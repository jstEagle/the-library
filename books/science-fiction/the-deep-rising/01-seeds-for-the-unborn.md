## 1. Seeds for the Unborn

The tomatoes were sulking again.

Juniper Eriksen crouched between the hydroponic rows of Bay Three, her knees complaining in that familiar way they did after twenty years of low gravity, and studied the drooping vines with the patience of a woman who had long ago learned that plants, like people, mostly wanted to be listened to.

"All right," she said. "What's your problem today?"

The tomatoes didn't answer. They never did. But their leaves told her plenty—slightly curled at the edges, a faint yellowing near the growing tips. She checked the nutrient drip and found it running two points hot on potassium. Someone—and she had her suspicions, and those suspicions all wore Tomas's coveralls—had adjusted the mix again without logging it.

"You're going to be the death of me, old man," she murmured, though Tomas was only fifty-three and she was the one who'd be sixty in four years, not that she'd admit it to anyone aboard who might repeat it.

She corrected the mix, logged the correction, and then sat back on her heels for the moment of quiet she allowed herself every morning: the walk-through, row by row, bay by bay. It took ninety minutes if she didn't dawdle, and she always dawdled.

Waystation Kestrel's gardens occupied four bays along the station's spin section, and they were, as far as Juniper knew, the most beautiful thing in this particular stretch of contested orbit. Not that anyone else would ever see them. That was rather the point.

She started in Bay One, where the staples lived—wheat strains bred for closed-loop systems, dwarf rice, soybeans with roots that tolerated recycled water of dubious mood. Bay Two was the orchard: dwarf apple trees grafted onto rootstock that could survive a decade of neglect, which was fortunate, because there had been one memorable month eight years ago when Juniper had been down with fever and the apples had nearly staged a revolution. Bay Three was the tomatoes and peppers and the experimental beds where she coaxed Terran crops into accepting alien light spectra. And Bay Four—

Bay Four was hers alone.

Bay Four held the seeds for the unborn.

That was what she called them, anyway, though officially they were catalogued as *Terraforming Baseline Stock, Priority One*, and officially she was *Senior Terraforming Specialist Eriksen, sole custodian*. Unofficially, every seed packet in Bay Four was a letter to someone Juniper would never meet.

Three hundred and eleven varieties, each tagged with its intended destination: the river valleys of Kepler-442c, the terraced highlands of Gliese 667Cc, the cold plains where the second-wave colonists would land in—she did the math out of habit, and the math hadn't changed in years—thirty-one years, give or take a shipping delay. The children who would eat from these seeds hadn't been born yet. Some of their parents hadn't been born yet.

Juniper found this thought comforting rather than sad, most days. Today was a most-days kind of day. She moved down the racks with her tablet, checking seals, noting humidity, whispering the names of things.

"Winter wheat, hard red. Sorghum. Amaranth, because somebody in the colonial office had a sense of humor." A pause at the rack of legumes. "And you, you stubborn little miracle."

The stubborn little miracle was a pea variety developed on Meridian Station, back before Meridian had gone quiet and corporate and stopped answering casual comms. It grew in marginal soil, fixed nitrogen like a champion, and tasted, according to the one packet of dried peas Juniper had sacrificed to science three years ago, like forgiveness felt. She'd written to Meridian twice asking about licensing. Both letters had come back with cheerful automated replies assuring her that Meridian Station was thriving and her inquiry was important to them.

Everyone out here knew what those automated replies meant. Stations went quiet. Corporations bought them. Records got tidy. You didn't ask questions, because questions were expensive and nobody out here had margin to spare.

Juniper sealed the rack and moved on, her boots soft on the grating, the grow-lights humming their endless patient hum. Somewhere below her feet, the station's reactor breathed. Somewhere above, the jump gate hung in the dark like a ring of frozen lightning, and beyond it, everything.

* * *

Lunch was reconstituted noodles with actual fresh scallions, which was the closest thing Kestrel had to luxury cuisine, and she ate it in the galley across from Tomas, who was doing something unspeakable to a circuit board while pretending it was lunch.

"The tomatoes are sulking," she said.

"I adjusted the potassium."

"I know. I fixed it."

"It was better."

"It was worse. There's a reason we log changes, Tomas."

"There's a reason we have taste buds, Juni." He soldered something. A small wisp of smoke rose and he waved it away with the serenity of a man who had been causing small fires for thirty years. "You want flavorless nutrient paste, join a corporate fleet."

"I spent six years on a corporate fleet."

"Exactly. Look how you turned out."

She smiled into her noodles. This was the whole conversation, more or less. They'd been having versions of it since the day Tomas had signed onto Kestrel nineteen years ago—a young engineer with a bad knee and a good heart, fleeing something he never talked about, finding in Juniper a woman who also wasn't talking about something. They'd built a friendship out of mutual silence, and it had held up better than most marriages.

Kestrel's full crew complement was nine. Currently it was five. There was Priya on comms and life support, who communicated primarily through increasingly elaborate passive-aggressive maintenance requests. There was Okonkwo on the reactor, who had once gone forty days without speaking to anyone and claimed afterward it had been restful. There was young Mei-Lin, the apprentice who'd arrived two years ago fleeing debt-bondage and whom Juniper was teaching botany the way her own grandmother had taught her: by handing her a trowel and saying nothing until asked.

And there was Juniper and Tomas, who between them held the station together with duct tape, stubbornness, and a shared conviction that the work mattered even if no one would see the harvest.

"Anything on the board?" Juniper asked, nodding toward the comm display.

Priya's daily digest scrolled past: traffic advisories, a fuel price update, the usual corporate bulletins dressed up as news. *TerraNova Consolidated announces record quarterly growth.* *Meridian Station expands research operations, population now exceeds four hundred.* Juniper skimmed it the way you skim weather reports for a place you'll never visit.

"Halcyon's still overdue," Tomas said, not looking up. "Eight years now. Somebody finally declared them lost last month. Saw it in the bulletin."

"Mm." Juniper had seen it too, and had felt an unexpected pang. She'd never met the Halcyon's crew, but she knew the ship—the way everyone in these orbits knew the ships that passed through. A deep-haul freighter, twelve crew, chartered for a run past the gate network's edge. Out and back in four years, standard time dilation accounting. Except they hadn't come back in four years. Or five. Or seven.

"They're dead," Tomas said, with the gentleness he reserved for facts. "Deep space is deep. Ships go out and don't come back. You know that better than anyone."

"I know that better than anyone," Juniper agreed. Her brother's ship had gone out twenty-six years ago and never come back either, and she thought of him the way she thought of the seeds in Bay Four: filed carefully, tended occasionally, kept alive in the dark.

But eight years was a long time to keep a berth warm, and some part of her—the part that had spent two decades keeping living things alive against all reasonable odds—had never quite let the Halcyon go. Ships came back. That was the thing nobody believed until it happened. Ships came back.

* * *

It happened during second shift, while Juniper was elbow-deep in the pepper beds, training a wayward vine back onto its trellis.

Mei-Lin's voice came over the garden intercom, pitched in a register Juniper had learned to read instantly. Calm voice: routine problem. Careful voice: equipment trouble. The voice she used now—careful but too quick—meant something else entirely.

"Juniper? Can you come to ops? Priya says you need to see this. She says—" a pause, muffled consultation, "—she says you especially need to see this."

Juniper wiped her hands, told the peppers she'd be back, and made the walk to ops in four minutes instead of the usual seven, which her knees noted for future grievance filing.

Ops was a cramped room that smelled of old coffee and ozone, dominated by Priya's console wall and the long-range sensor display that nobody watched anymore because nothing interesting ever happened. Nothing interesting had happened within sensor range of Waystation Kestrel in nineteen years, unless you counted the time a derelict ice hauler drifted through and Tomas had wanted to salvage it and Priya had wanted to burn it as a biohazard and they'd settled on watching it pass for three weeks like spectators at the world's slowest parade.

"What've we got?" Juniper said.

Priya didn't answer. She just pointed at the display.

There was a ship on it.

That alone was remarkable enough. But Juniper looked closer, and the transponder readout resolved, and she felt the floor tilt gently beneath her, the way it sometimes did when the spin section's bearings needed attention, except the bearings were fine and it was the universe that had come loose.

**HALCYON — DEEP HAUL REGISTRY 7734-K — STATUS: PRESUMED LOST**

"Bogus," said Mei-Lin, from the doorway. "That's gotta be spoofed. Right? That's gotta be spoofed."

"Transponder signature matches registry archives," Priya said. Her voice was flat, which for Priya was the equivalent of shouting. "Hull profile matches. Drive signature matches the Halcyon's documented fusion torch pattern—I pulled the old port records from Ceres. It's them. Or it's someone wearing them very convincingly."

"Range?" Juniper heard herself ask.

"Fourteen million klicks and closing. Deceleration burn consistent with approach vector for Kestrel." Priya finally turned around, and her face did something complicated. "They're coming here, Juniper. Nobody routes through here. We're a refueling footnote. They're coming *here*."

Juniper stared at the display. A small bright point, crawling inward across the dark. Eight years presumed lost. Twelve crew. A deep-space run past the edge of everything.

Ships came back.

"Tomas," she said into the intercom, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was a skill twenty years of solitude teaches you whether you want it or not. "Get up to ops. Bring coffee. The real stuff."

"The real stuff? What's—"

"Just get up here."

While they waited, the four of them arranged themselves around the display in the unconscious formation of people witnessing something they don't yet believe: Priya at her console, fingers dancing, pulling archive after archive; Mei-Lin perched on a supply crate; Tomas arriving with the good coffee and standing very still; Juniper in the center, arms crossed, watching the point of light grow.

Priya ran the numbers three times. Each time they came out the same.

"Time to dock?" Juniper asked.

"At current deceleration... thirty-one hours." Priya hesitated. "Juniper, their telemetry is weird. Their mass readings are weird. Everything about this approach is textbook-perfect except it's *too* perfect, like someone flying by memory of a manual instead of by habit. And their crew count reads twelve, same as the original manifest, but the biometric pings are—" she shook her head. "I can't make sense of the biometrics. Could be sensor ghosting at this range."

"Could be a lot of things," Tomas said quietly. He was looking at Juniper, not the screen. "Could be pirates wearing a dead ship's skin. Could be a corporate trap. Could be nothing good at all."

"Could be," Juniper agreed.

"And?"

She looked at the display again. At the small bright point crawling home through fourteen million kilometers of dark, toward a station that existed to feed people who wouldn't arrive for thirty-one years, crewed by people who had chosen invisibility for reasons they mostly kept to themselves.

Somewhere in her chest, under twenty years of careful sediment, something shifted. It wasn't hope exactly. Hope was a word for people with margins. It was older than hope, and more stubborn—the same thing that made her check seed seals every morning for harvests she'd never taste. The refusal to let a living thing die unattended.

"We prep Docking Bay Two," she said. "Full quarantine protocols until I say otherwise. Priya, keep recording everything—that approach, those telemetry readings, all of it, timestamped and archived. If this is a trap, I want evidence. If it's a miracle, I want proof."

"And if it's both?" Mei-Lin asked.

Juniper almost smiled. "Out here, kid, it's usually both."

Thirty-one hours. She spent them the way she spent every hour: working. She sterilized the docking bay herself. She checked the airlock seals twice. She walked Bay Four and stood for a long moment among the unborn seeds, and told them, absurdly, that they might be getting company.

In the last hour, she stood in ops with her whole strange little family and watched the Halcyon resolve on the optical feed: a scarred deep-hauler, hull scorched in patterns no micrometeorite storm should make, running lights flickering in a sequence that Priya identified, frowning, as an old-fashioned distress code—one that hadn't been standard in fifteen years.

"They're hailing us," Priya said. "Audio only. Voice request, priority flag." She glanced up. "It's the captain. Imara Vess, per the manifest."

"Put her through."

The channel opened with a hiss of static, and then a voice—raw, cracked, worn down to the grain, the voice of someone who had been screaming or silent for a very long time and couldn't remember which.

"Waystation Kestrel, this is Captain Imara Vess of the freighter Halcyon." A breath, ragged at the edges. "We have no cargo. We have no quarrel. We are twelve souls requesting emergency docking, and I am authorized to offer payment in—" another breath, and something in it that made the hair on Juniper's arms rise, something that sounded almost like fear, "—in information. Information someone out here will very much want buried."

Static crackled. On the display, the scarred ship hung against the stars, close enough now to see the damage, close enough to see that whatever had happened to the Halcyon out in the dark, it had not been kind.

Juniper keyed the channel.

"Halcyon, this is Juniper Eriksen, Waystation Kestrel. You are cleared for approach to Bay Two. Quarantine protocols apply." She paused, then added, because twenty years of tending living things had taught her that the first words matter: "Come home slow. We've got you."

The channel closed. In the silence afterward, Tomas said quietly, "Juni. Whatever they're carrying—we don't have to be part of this. We can dock them, fuel them, send them on. Stay invisible."

Juniper watched the Halcyon begin its final approach, and felt the loneliness of two decades crack open—not painfully, not yet, but the way soil cracks before a spring flood, holding something that cannot be held back much longer.

"Tomas," she said, "nobody comes back from the dead to sell secrets. Let's find out what they came back *from*."
