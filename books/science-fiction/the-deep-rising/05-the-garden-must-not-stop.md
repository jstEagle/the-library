## 5. The Garden Must Not Stop

The survey vessel was still nine days out when Juniper stopped sleeping.

She told herself it was the tomatoes. The new hybrid batch had been sulking—dropping blossoms, curling their leaves in that particular way plants had of filing complaints—and a gardener's worry was an honest, harmless kind of insomnia. She spent two nights in the greenhouse module with her sleeves rolled up and her hands in the growing medium, whispering encouragement to things that could not hear her, and if her mind kept drifting from phosphorus ratios to transponder signatures, well. That was between her and the tomatoes.

On the third night, Tomas found her there.

"You're talking to the basil again," he said, leaning in the hatchway with two bulbs of tea steaming in recycled air. He was sixty-one now, gray at the temples, still built like a man who had once wrestled a coolant pump into submission and won on points.

"The basil started it."

"Everything starts it, Juno. That's your problem." He handed her a bulb and lowered himself onto the crate she kept for exactly this purpose, because twenty years of friendship taught you which crates were for sitting and which were for seed stock. "You've been up here three nights. Oyelaran asked me to check on you. I told him you were fine. I lied to a medical professional. I hope you appreciate the sacrifice."

"I appreciate the sacrifice."

They drank their tea. Beyond the greenhouse glass, Kestrel's garden ring curved away into the dark—the wheat terraces, the orchard pods, the little pond where Juniper had introduced carp against every recommendation and been vindicated when the fish ate the algae blooms nobody else could control. Twenty years of work. Twenty years of green in a place where green had no business existing.

"They'll dock in six days," Tomas said quietly. "The survey vessel. TerraNova registry, whatever flag they're flying this quarter."

"I know."

"And you've spent three nights talking to herbs about it."

"The herbs are good listeners. They don't interrupt with opinions." She turned the tea bulb in her hands. "Tomas. Do you remember what I told you the day I signed the Kestrel contract?"

He snorted. "I remember you'd had four drinks and cried on my shoulder in a bar on Ceres with gravity so cheap my ears popped. You said—" he pitched his voice lower, doing an impression of her younger self that was unflattering and accurate, "—'I'm going somewhere they can't find me, and I'm going to grow things until it doesn't matter anymore.'"

"That's not what I said."

"It's close enough."

"What I said," Juniper said, "was: *they'll come looking eventually.*"

* * *

Twenty-two years earlier, on a station with real gravity and fake air, Juniper Eriksen had been the youngest environmental systems auditor TerraNova had ever certified.

She remembered the certification ceremony mostly because her mother had come—had taken three weeks' leave and crossed four systems to watch her daughter receive a plaque, and had held her hand afterward and said, *you'll fix things, mpenzi. You'll make them honest.* Her mother had spent thirty years in water reclamation on Earth and believed, with the simple ferocity of someone who had never been able to afford cynicism, that systems could be made honest by putting good people inside them.

Juniper had believed it too. For eleven months after certification, she had believed it very hard.

The Meridian contract came to her desk in the autumn of her twelfth month. Standard audit—she'd done forty of them. Atmospheric processing efficiency, waste loop integrity, soil chemistry reports from the outpost's agri-domes. Meridian Station was a research outpost out past the shipping lanes, small, quiet, funded through a TerraNova subsidiary that existed mostly on paper. Its numbers were beautiful. That should have been the first warning. Real numbers are never beautiful; real numbers have dirt under their fingernails.

She found the discrepancy on a Thursday. It was almost embarrassingly small—a 0.3 percent variance in the atmospheric scrubber throughput logs, repeated across fourteen reporting cycles with a regularity that no machine and no honest technician would ever produce. Machines drift. Technicians get tired. Numbers that repeat perfectly are numbers someone is copying from last year's homework.

She pulled the raw sensor feeds. They didn't exist. Fourteen cycles of raw data, gone—not corrupted, not archived wrong, but *absent*, replaced with synthesized curves so smooth they looked like they'd been ironed.

She did what she'd been trained to do. She flagged it. She wrote the report in language that was firm but professional, because she was twenty-six and still believed in professional. She submitted it up the chain and went home and slept well, and if there was a small warm coal of pride in her chest, she didn't examine it too closely. This was what the plaque was for. This was the system working.

Three days later, her supervisor—a kind, tired man named Okonjo who kept a photo of his daughters taped inside his terminal—asked her to come by his office.

"Walk me through your reasoning," he said, not unkindly.

"A sensor fault would show noise. This shows none. Someone's generating the data."

"Or the sensors were upgraded."

"With no maintenance record? No parts requisition?"

"Juniper." He folded his hands. He looked, she thought later, like a man watching weather come in over a field he couldn't reach. "Meridian is a priority account. The subsidiary has commitments. Your finding is—premature. There may be calibration issues we don't fully understand at that distance. I'm asking you to amend the report. Note the variance as resolved pending sensor verification. It's one line. One line, and Meridian keeps its funding, and three hundred people keep their jobs."

"Then let me verify. Give me a site visit authorization. Two weeks on-station. If the sensors check out, I'll sign anything you want."

"You know I can't do that."

"Why not?"

He looked at her then, really looked, and something in his face made the warm coal in her chest go cold. "Because I'm telling you how it's going to be," he said, "and I'd rather you heard it from me than from someone who doesn't like you."

She didn't amend the report. She escalated instead—to regional oversight, to the ethics office, to a compliance channel she'd been assured was anonymous. Each escalation came back smoother than the last, like stones tumbled in a river until all the sharp edges were gone. The ethics office thanked her for her diligence. Regional oversight noted that the matter had been *reviewed at appropriate levels*. Six weeks after her first flag, a revised audit was issued under her name—her name, her certification number—with the variance marked as sensor error, resolved.

She hadn't written it. She hadn't signed it. But it wore her signature the way a puppet wears a hand.

She confronted Okonjo one last time. He wouldn't meet her eyes. "There are three hundred people on that station," he said. "And there's you. And the company has decided which one it can afford."

"That's not a decision. That's just arithmetic."

"Yes," he said. "That's rather the point of companies."

* * *

She resigned on a Friday. It took her four hours to draft a resignation letter that didn't burn every bridge behind her, and then she deleted it and wrote one sentence instead: *I will not put my name on a lie.*

Her mother called from Earth that night. Juniper had rehearsed the conversation—reassurances, plans, the sensible next job she'd already lined up with an independent hauler outfit. But her mother cut through all of it the way mothers do.

"You did the right thing," her mother said.

"It cost me everything."

"No." A pause, filled with the hum of Earth-side lines, the sound of a planet breathing. "It cost you some things. You'll learn the difference. Come home, mpenzi. Work reclamation with me. The rivers need honest people more than the sky does."

For one long minute, drifting in her tiny rented berth with her whole career packed into two cases, Juniper wanted it so badly her teeth ached. Earth. Gravity that didn't lie. Her mother's kitchen, the smell of real rain through a real window.

But she had seen the Meridian numbers, and she could not stop seeing them. Three hundred people breathing air whose quality reports were fiction. Whatever was happening out there—cost-cutting, corner-cutting, something worse—it was happening to people who trusted the plaque on her wall. If she went home to the rivers, she'd spend the rest of her life knowing she'd walked away from the only version of honesty that mattered: the kind that stays in the room.

"I can't," she said. "Not yet. There's a station—Kestrel. Deep orbit, terraforming prep, colonists scheduled in thirty years. They need someone to build the gardens from nothing. Nobody watches deep orbit, Mama. Nobody looks."

Her mother was quiet for a while. Then, in the tone she used for verdicts: "You're running away."

"I'm planting something."

"You're running away and calling it planting. You get that from me, so I'm allowed to say it." Another pause, softer. "Will you write?"

"Every week."

She wrote every week for nine years. Then the messages got shorter, and then they stopped being answered, and then there was a gap on both sides that neither of them knew how to bridge across twelve light-hours and twenty years of silence. That was the arithmetic of exile. Nobody tells you the interest compounds.

* * *

"You never told me the whole story," Tomas said, back in the present, in the greenhouse, in the smell of tomato vines and recycled rain. "Not really. I got the bar version. The four-drinks version."

"You got the true version. Just diluted."

"Dilute it back for me." He refilled her tea without asking. "Because here's what I know, Juno. I know you took a contract nobody wanted, on a rock nobody visits, to grow food for people who might never come. I know you've turned down three promotions to bigger stations because bigger stations have visitors. I know you flinch—just a flicker, most people miss it—every time a TerraNova ship transits our lane. And I know that right now there's a corporate survey vessel coming to a station that has nothing worth surveying except a garden and a ghost story, and you haven't slept in three nights." He set down the kettle. "So. The undiluted version."

So she gave it to him. All of it—the Thursday discovery, the smooth numbers, Okonjo's tired eyes, the report wearing her name like a stolen coat. She told him about her mother's voice on the line, and the resignation letter of one sentence, and the way she'd stood in the personnel office afterward feeling not free but *unmoored*, like a cutting taken from its plant, alive but waiting to find out if it would root anywhere at all.

Tomas listened the way he fixed engines: completely, without hurrying, letting silence do part of the work.

"Meridian," he said at last. "That was the station with the cooked numbers."

"That was the station."

"And Vess's crew says Meridian's dead. Three hundred people. And every record says it's thriving." He rubbed his jaw. "Twenty years ago somebody falsified that station's environmental data, and you caught it, and they buried it. Now a ship comes back from the dark full of ghosts, and the company that buried it sends a survey vessel to *our* door." He shook his head slowly. "Juno, I've been telling myself for a week that it's coincidence. Routine route check. Bureaucratic noise."

"And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a greenhouse listening to a twenty-year-old conspiracy theory line up end to end like it was machined that way." He looked at her, and his eyes were kind and frightened in equal measure, which was the most honest expression a person could wear. "What did you call it? Back on Ceres? What did you say you were doing out here?"

"Planting something."

"Yeah." He exhaled. "Well. I think it grew."

* * *

After he left, Juniper stayed in the greenhouse a while longer, walking the rows.

This was the thing about gardens that nobody who hadn't kept one ever understood: nothing in them ever really goes away. Compost becomes soil becomes tomato becomes compost. The dead leaves you clear in autumn are next spring's harvest, wearing a different shape. You can pull a weed a thousand times, but if you missed even a fragment of root, it comes back, patient as arithmetic, and it always comes back *bigger*, because it spent the winter learning your ground.

She had thought she'd buried her past twenty years ago, out here in the dark, under twenty years of wheat and carp ponds and quiet. She had thought invisibility was a shelter she'd built, brick by brick, shift by shift.

Standing among her tomatoes with a corporate vessel burning toward her, Juniper finally understood her mistake. She hadn't buried anything. She had planted it. Every season of silence, every year of careful anonymity, every bridge burned and every letter unanswered—all of it had been going into the ground, breaking down, becoming rich and dark and patient. And now the thing she'd planted was coming up through the soil at last, wearing the one shape she'd always known it would wear someday:

TerraNova, coming to collect.

She pressed her palm flat against the grow-light housing, warm as a sleeping animal, and felt the old cold coal in her chest turn over—not dead after all, these twenty years, just banked, just waiting for air.

"Okay," she said softly, to the tomatoes, to the basil, to the ghost of a woman on Earth who had once told her that good people belong inside systems. "Okay. You wanted honest? Let's see how honest they're willing to be."

Behind her, the comm panel chimed—an incoming transmission, priority-flagged, routed through channels that shouldn't have known Kestrel's private frequency.

Sender: TerraNova Survey Division.

Subject line: **Pre-Docking Inspection Requirements — Waystation Kestrel — Compliance Mandatory.**

Attached, already loading, a list of everything they intended to look through.
