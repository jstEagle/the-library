## 8. The Jump Window

Imara Vess had spent thirty-one years in space, and she could count on one hand the number of times she'd been genuinely afraid. The first was her first EVA, when her tether snagged and she hung above Ganymede watching her oxygen gauge like a man watching his own pulse. The second was the day Meridian Station went dark on every channel at once.

The third was now, standing in Waystation Kestrel's astrogation bay, watching a woman named Petra Okonkwo draw a line through a chart that looked, to Vess's untrained eye, like a spiderweb designed by someone having a breakdown.

"Say it again," Vess said. "Slowly."

Petra didn't look up from the display. She was Kestrel's astrogator—a title that sounded grander than it was, since Kestrel's traffic consisted of one garden station, two tugs, and a freighter full of ghosts. She was maybe fifty, built like a filing cabinet, with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair and the particular patience of someone who had explained orbital mechanics to cargo jocks for three decades without once committing violence.

"The Ceres corridor," Petra said, in the tone of a teacher who has already said this twice, "is a gravitational alignment problem. You don't just point at Ceres and burn. The jump gate relay sits in a pocket where the mass of Jupiter, Saturn, and—believe it or not—the asteroid belt itself balance out enough for a transit burn that doesn't cook your crew or shred your hull. That pocket opens when the bodies line up. It's open now." She tapped the display. "It closes in eleven days."

"And after that?"

"After that, the alignment doesn't recur for—" Petra did some quick math on her slate, though Vess suspected she already knew the answer and was performing the calculation for dramatic effect, "—nine years, four months, and change. Give or take a comet."

Vess stared at the chart. Eleven days. Nine years. Somewhere between those two numbers lay the entire future of three hundred dead people at Meridian Station, twelve reversed crew of the *Halcyon*, and whatever was left of Imara Vess's career, reputation, and probably her life.

"Eleven days," she repeated. "To get from here to Ceres Archive."

Petra finally looked up over her glasses. "How fast is your ship?"

"She's not slow."

"That's not what I asked, Captain."

Vess exhaled through her nose. "*Halcyon* was rated for deep-haul runs. Eighteen months out, eighteen months back, loaded to the gills. Her engines are older than my first marriage and twice as temperamental, but when she burns, she *burns*. Full military-ish thrust, we might make the corridor in nine days if nothing breaks."

"If nothing breaks," Petra echoed. She took off her glasses and cleaned them on her sleeve, which Vess would learn was what Petra did instead of swearing. "And how often does nothing break?"

"On the *Halcyon*? Statistically never."

"Mm." Petra put the glasses back on and turned back to the chart. "Then let's talk about what happens if something breaks on day six."

---

They convened the meeting an hour later in Kestrel's mess, which doubled as everything on a station this size: galley, briefing room, chapel, arbitration chamber, and the only place aboard with chairs soft enough to make people honest. Juniper Eriksen sat at the head of the table with a mug of something that smelled like it had been grown rather than brewed, which, Vess reminded herself, it had. Twenty years of terraforming work in a cup. There was something obscene about how good it smelled.

Vess brought her own people. Devi Chandra, the botanist, who carried their evidence in living tissue and carried it like a mother carries a sleeping child—carefully, constantly, with a low hum of terror underneath. Okafor, her engineer, who had aged backward from sixty-one to somewhere around forty-five and complained about it the way other men complained about weather. And Rhee—no, not Rhee yet. Rhee came later. This was before the inspector, before the confession. This was still the simple part, when the enemy was physics.

"So walk me through it," Juniper said. "Assume I know nothing, because mostly I don't. My whole job is keeping plants alive in a tin can. Ships are your department."

"They're not my department either," Vess said. "I'm a hauler. I go where the schedule says. But fine." She pulled up the route on the mess table's display, and the spiderweb chart bloomed above the coffee mugs. "Ceres Archive is the last data haven in the system that isn't owned by TerraNova or its five ugly cousins. Independent backbone, independent power, independent lawyers with teeth. If we can get our evidence onto their network, it can't be quietly deleted. It gets mirrored across forty colonial feeds inside the hour, and then TerraNova has to murder the truth in public, which is the one thing corporations hate more than taxes."

"And the only way there is through the jump gate relay," Juniper said.

"The only *fast* way. Otherwise it's a slow-burn transit—eleven months, minimum, and we'd be doing it with a corporate frigate on our tail and a crew whose biology is—" Vess stopped. She hadn't told Juniper yet. Not all of it.

"Whose biology is what?" Juniper asked, and her voice was gentle in a way that made it worse.

Devi answered for her, because Devi always answered for her; it was one of the thousand small mercies and small betrayals of having served together so long. "We're aging forward again," Devi said. "Rapidly. Okafor's lost almost two years in the eight weeks since docking. We got them back all at once, out there, and now they're being collected. With interest."

The mess went quiet. Even the ventilation seemed to hold its breath.

"I see," Juniper said, and Vess believed that she did—not the mechanism, nobody understood the mechanism, but the shape of it. A debt coming due. "Then eleven days isn't a deadline. It's a lifespan."

"It's both," Vess said. "Which is why I need to be honest with you about the odds, because you're about to risk your home on this, and you deserve real numbers, not captain numbers."

"What's the difference?"

"Captain numbers are what you tell the crew so they'll follow you. Real numbers are what you tell yourself at 0300 when you can't sleep." Vess laid it out flat. "Full burn, no stops, we reach the corridor window with maybe thirty hours of margin. One significant mechanical failure—one engine, one coolant loop, one nav computer hiccup—and we miss it. Miss it, and we drift for nine years with TerraNova picking us off at leisure, or we surrender, or we die trying something stupid. That's the optimistic version."

"And the pessimistic version?"

"The pessimistic version is that someone has already reported us, the corridor is watched, and we fly straight into a net." She paused. "I'd put that at better than even odds, honestly. Corporations aren't fast, but they're thorough, and we've been sitting in your orbit for two months talking too loudly."

Okafor snorted from the end of the table. "Speak for yourself. *I've* been quiet as a church mouse."

"You've been complaining about your knees loud enough to hear through a hull," Devi said.

"My knees earned the right."

Juniper let them run out of steam, then folded her hands around her mug. "What do you need from me?"

"Fuel, mostly. Kestrel's reserves. And your astrogator, if Petra will come—and I want to be clear, I'm asking her, not taking her. This is a volunteer voyage. Everyone on that ship should be there because they chose it, knowing the numbers."

"I'll come," Petra said, from the doorway.

Everyone turned. She was leaning against the frame with her slate under her arm, wearing the expression of a woman who had been listening for exactly as long as it suited her.

"How long have you been standing there?" Vess asked.

"Long enough. I'll come. I've spent twenty-two years plotting courses for seed shipments to colonies that won't exist until my grandchildren's age, if I had grandchildren, which I don't, because I spent twenty-two years plotting courses for seed shipments." She shrugged. "A person wants to aim at something that matters once. Also, I've read your service record, Captain Vess, and I don't trust you to plot a transit through the belt without adult supervision."

"That's fair," Vess said.

"It's extremely fair," Devi agreed.

"*Traitors,*" said Okafor, delighted.

---

That night Vess couldn't sleep, which was normal; what was abnormal was where she ended up when she gave up trying. She followed the smell of green down Kestrel's spine to the gardens, and found Juniper there, alone, kneeling in the dirt of a grow-bed with a hand lens, examining the leaves of something small and stubborn.

"Shouldn't you be sleeping?" Juniper asked without looking up. "Big day of risking everything tomorrow."

"Shouldn't you?"

"Oh, absolutely. I sleep terribly. Have for twenty years. You get used to treating insomnia like a coworker." She sat back on her heels and gestured at the bed. "Do you know what this is?"

"A plant."

"A plant," Juniper agreed warmly. "But specifically, it's wheat. Or it will be, eventually, if I've done my job. Fourth-generation stock, bred for low light and recycled water. These seeds are for colonists who won't arrive for another generation. Nobody alive will ever eat this bread. I bake it anyway, sometimes, in the station kitchen. Just to check."

"Just to check."

"To make sure the work is real. That's the thing about planting for the unborn—you have to trust that the future exists, or the work goes rotten in your hands." She looked up at Vess, and in the grow-lights her face was younger than her file said and older than her voice. "You're carrying seeds too. I know that. Whatever happened out there, whatever you saw—I know you're carrying something that has to be planted somewhere it can grow, and the ground is closing up in eleven days."

Vess crouched beside the bed, because her knees were forty-five years old now and crouching was suddenly possible again, which was the single pleasant thing about her condition. "You want to know what we saw."

"I want you to tell me when you're ready. I'm not going to pry it out of you. Truth pried out isn't truth, it's just confession, and confessions can be managed." Juniper brushed soil from her palms. "But I'll tell you what I know. I know records lie. I've known that since I was twenty-nine years old and a TerraNova supervisor asked me to adjust an atmospheric report by four percent. Four percent! Barely a rounding error. Enough to certify a colony site that would kill people slowly. I said no, and I've been invisible ever since, and I used to think invisibility was my punishment." She smiled, thin and private. "Lately I'm starting to think it was training."

"For what?"

"For this. For being the one place in the system where a ghost ship can dock and nobody asks too many questions, because nobody remembers to ask." She stood, joints cracking like distant gunfire. "Eleven days, you said. Then the door shuts for nine years."

"Nine years, four months, give or take a comet."

"Then we'd better not take the comet into account." Juniper held out her hand and helped Vess up, which was absurd, because Vess was fifteen years younger biologically and it showed mainly in how easily she stood. "Get some sleep, Captain. Tomorrow we do arithmetic with people's lives, and I want you sharp."

---

The next morning, the arithmetic began in earnest, and it was worse than advertised.

Petra ran the numbers three ways. The conservative burn got them to the corridor with four hours of margin, which everyone agreed was not margin but prayer. The aggressive burn got them there with thirty hours but stressed *Halcyon*'s old engines past their rated tolerances, and Okafor, consulted by comm, delivered his verdict in the traditional language of engineers everywhere:

"She'll do it," he said. "Once. Probably. Don't ask me to do it twice, and don't ask me what 'probably' means, because it means what it means."

"What about fuel?" Juniper asked. "Your reserves—"

"Are yours to spend," Vess said. "I know what I'm asking. Those reserves are your insurance policy. Your winter."

Juniper was quiet for a moment. On the display, the little marker that was Kestrel sat in its comfortable orbit, fat and safe and green, and the little marker that was *Halcyon* sat beside it, and beyond both of them the corridor window ticked downward in real time, a countdown nobody had voted for.

"Tomas is going to fight me on this," Juniper said finally. "My engineer. Twenty years at my side. He'll say—no, he'll be right—that emptying our tanks to chase a ghost story leaves this station defenseless for a decade. He'll say the colonists we're supposed to be serving are the ones we'd be abandoning." She rubbed her eyes. "And the worst part is I'll agree with every word, and then I'll do it anyway, because I've spent twenty years learning exactly what corporate silence costs, and I am *done* paying retail."

"There's something else," Petra said. She had gone very still at her console, in the way she went still when the news was bad and she wanted to deliver it with maximum efficiency. "While you were all being noble, I checked the corridor approach telemetry. Public channels." She turned the display toward them. "There's traffic in the approach lane. Survey-class vessel, transponder registered to TerraNova Exo-Resources, holding position just outside the corridor mouth. Loitering. Nobody loiters at a jump gate, Captain. There's nothing there to loiter *for*—unless you're waiting for a specific ship."

Vess felt the floor tilt, though the deck was steady as stone. Two months of hiding. Two months of quiet and careful words and Juniper stalling inspectors with bureaucratic charm. And all along, someone had simply done the math—had sat down with the same chart, drawn the same line, and waited at the end of it.

"They know," Devi said softly. "They've known where we have to go. They don't need to find us. They just have to stand at the only exit."

"Then we change the math," Vess heard herself say, and was surprised by how steady her own voice came out. "Petra. Is there any other way through? Any variant? A different insertion angle, a different burn profile—anything they haven't planned for?"

Petra looked at the chart for a long moment. Her glasses caught the display light, and behind them her eyes moved through the spiderweb like someone reading a letter from an old friend.

"There's one," she said. "One variant. It's ugly. It means threading the belt's inner edge on manual thrust for six hours with no computer assistance, because the automation won't survive the shear gradients. It shaves our margin to almost nothing and it will rattle fillings loose." She took off her glasses and cleaned them on her sleeve. "But nobody waits at the bottom of an ugly road, because nobody sane drives it."

"Good," Vess said. "We were never sane. It's how we got here."

Outside the viewport, Kestrel's gardens turned slowly in the station light—wheat for the unborn, green against the black—and somewhere past all of it, invisible, patient, the clock ran on.
