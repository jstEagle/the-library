## 9. Loyalty of Small Crews

Tomas Reyes had a rule about problems: if you could fix it with a wrench, it wasn't really a problem. If you couldn't fix it with a wrench, you could usually fix it by ignoring it until it became someone else's problem. And if it couldn't be fixed with a wrench, or ignored, or blamed on anyone else, then—and only then—it was a real problem.

The Halcyon was a real problem.

He'd known it for three days now, ever since Juniper had come to him in the machine shop with that look on her face—the one she got when she'd already decided something and was only pretending to ask his opinion. She'd told him about Vess, about Meridian, about the records that showed three hundred people alive on a station where they were almost certainly dead. Then she'd said, "We need to talk about what we do," in the tone of a woman who had already talked about it extensively with herself.

So here he was, in the mess hall at 0600 station time, because Juniper believed all important votes should happen before coffee ran out, which was either wisdom or cruelty depending on how much sleep you'd gotten.

There were nine of them left aboard Kestrel. Nine souls to run a station built for two hundred: Juniper, himself, Dr. Oyelaran, the twins Priya and Priti Chandrasekhar who handled comms and hydroponics respectively and finished each other's sentences so completely that Tomas suspected they shared one brain between them with a backup drive, old Ferro who maintained the outer hull walks and hadn't spoken more than six words in a row since the incident nobody mentioned, young Mei-Lin who ran life support and still called things "cool" in a way that made Tomas feel ancient, Okonkwo on security—a retired colonial marshal with shoulders like an airlock door—and Sable, the quartermaster, who was currently counting heads with the expression of a woman doing inventory she didn't like.

Plus twelve from the Halcyon, four of them hidden in the seed vaults since the survey vessel's visit, eight more pretending to be "contract cargo specialists" with paperwork so thin Mei-Lin had joked it would blow away in a stiff breeze. There was no breeze on a space station. That was the joke. It was not a good joke. Everyone laughed anyway, because that was the kind of week it was.

"All right," Juniper said, standing at the head of the long table with her hands flat on its surface, the way she did when she wanted everyone to know this was serious but not *serious* serious. "You all know why we're here. I want every voice. Including the ones that disagree with me."

Tomas kept his face neutral. He disagreed with her. He disagreed with her so thoroughly that he'd spent half the night drafting arguments in his head like a man composing love letters to a position he hated having.

"Option one," Juniper continued. "We shelter them. All twelve, plus whatever evidence they're carrying. We become complicit in whatever they're running from, and when TerraNova comes back—and they will come back—we're not bystanders anymore."

"And option two?" asked Sable.

"We hand them over. Politely. We claim we never knew their history, we cooperate fully with the survey team, we stay exactly what we've always been: a boring station growing boring vegetables in a boring orbit that nobody has cared about in twenty years." She paused. "Invisibility has kept us alive this long."

"It's kept *me* alive," Ferro said suddenly, which was practically a speech from him. The table went quiet. Ferro looked down at his hands. "I signed on here because I didn't want to be part of anything anymore. Big crews mean big politics. Big politics mean somebody decides your life is a rounding error." He shrugged, a small motion like something settling. "I'd like to keep being a rounding error."

Tomas felt something in his chest loosen painfully, because Ferro had just said out loud, in six words plus a shrug, everything Tomas had been afraid to say to Juniper's face.

* * *

Here was the thing about loving someone for twenty years: you learned their weather patterns. You learned that Juniper Eriksen's anger came in slow fronts, building for days before it broke. You learned that her humor was dry as vacuum and twice as dangerous. You learned that when she said "I want every voice," she meant it—but that hearing a voice and being changed by it were different systems entirely, and only one of them had a manual override.

He loved her. That was the whole problem, really. If he didn't love her, he could have just voted and gone back to fixing the number-three coolant pump, which had developed a rattle that sounded like a dying cat and which he'd been meaning to get to for eleven days.

But he loved her, and he remembered her at thirty-one, coming off the *Meridian Run* contract with TerraNova, sitting in this same mess hall—well, an earlier, shabbier version of it—with her resignation letter crumpled in her fist because she wouldn't falsify atmospheric readings and they'd made sure she'd never work a corporate berth again. He remembered her crying once, exactly once, in twenty years, the night her sister's message stopped coming. He remembered her planting the first seed tray in Garden One with hands that shook, saying, "Somebody has to grow things where nothing grows," and him thinking, *God help me, I'd follow this woman into a star.*

And that was why he had to vote against her. Because he knew exactly how much she'd already lost to corporations, and he could not watch them take the rest.

* * *

The debate went on for two hours. It was, in its way, magnificent.

Okonkwo laid out the tactical reality with the calm of a man describing weather to tourists: "A frigate carries forty marines. We carry nine crew, four hidden passengers, and a security locker with two stun batons and a flare gun I've been meaning to dispose of since the last administration. If they come for us, we are not a fight. We are a delay."

"A delay can matter," said Priya.

"A delay gets people killed," Okonkwo said. "I spent thirty years watching delays get people killed. The math doesn't care about your feelings."

Mei-Lin argued for sheltering them with the fierce optimism of the young: "They're *people*. They came back from the dead. You don't hand people back to the thing they died escaping."

"They didn't die," Priti pointed out gently.

"You know what I mean!"

Dr. Oyelaran spoke last among the crew, in his unhurried way, polishing his glasses while he talked. "Medically, I'll note the reversal is real and accelerating. Captain Vess has perhaps months before her body burns through whatever happened to it. But medically is not my vote. My vote is this: I have spent my career keeping people alive, and I find I cannot vote to stop doing so now, even at a distance, even by proxy. Handing them over isn't neutrality. It's a procedure. And I won't perform it."

Then it was Tomas's turn, and his mouth went dry, because Juniper was looking at him—not challenging, not pleading, just looking, the way you look at a diagnostic readout waiting for the fault code.

"I'm going to say the thing nobody wants said," he began. "So somebody should say it."

"Go ahead," Juniper said quietly.

"TerraNova already took twenty years of your life once. They took your career, your name in the guild registries, your family—they made you into a ghost, and you built a garden in the ghost's shadow, and it worked. It *worked*, Junie. Twenty years, no incidents, no audits, no interest. The colonists who come in sixty years will land on something green because we stayed small and quiet and unimportant." He spread his hands. "If we shelter them, we stop being unimportant. And TerraNova doesn't send survey vessels for unimportant stations. They send frigates. And when the frigate comes, we don't get to negotiate. We watched them erase Meridian. Three hundred people. What do you think our two hundred and one lives weigh on their ledger?"

"Two hundred and one?" Sable asked.

"Two hundred colonists who haven't been born yet," Tomas said, "plus us. That's what we're actually voting on. Not twelve refugees. Whether the future we've been growing for two decades survives contact with the people who erased a station full of scientists for a profit margin."

Silence. Mei-Lin was glaring at him. Ferro was nodding, barely, the way he agreed with things. And Juniper—Juniper was looking at him with an expression he couldn't parse, which frightened him more than any frigate.

"That's the argument," she said finally. "That's the whole argument?"

"That's the honest argument."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "That's half of it. Here's the other half: those twelve people came back younger than when they left. Something out there *gave them back years*, Tomas. Whatever happened to them, TerraNova killed three hundred people to monopolize it. If we hand them over, we're not buying safety. We're buying time—time until TerraNova decides Kestrel's orbit is useful, or our water reclamation is efficient enough to take, or some auditor notices our environmental reports have been too clean for too long. Invisibility isn't a shield. It's a schedule. We're just on it further out."

"I know," Tomas said. "Believe me, I know. I've done the math too." He looked down at his own hands, scarred and grease-stained, hands that had rebuilt this station's spine a hundred times. "I'm asking you to let me be a coward. I'm allowed. I've earned one cowardice in twenty years of service."

"Tomas—" Mei-Lin started.

"Let him finish," Juniper said. "Let everybody finish. That's the point of tonight."

* * *

They voted at 0830, by raised hands, because Juniper refused electronic ballots on principle—"a vote should have weight, and electrons are weightless"—which Tomas privately thought was nonsense but publicly respected, the way you respect most of Juniper's principles: from a safe distance, admiringly.

For sheltering the Halcyon crew: Juniper, Mei-Lin, Priya, Priti, Dr. Oyelaran. Five.

Against: Tomas, Ferro, Okonkwo, Sable. Four.

Nine voters. Five to four. A split so narrow you could drive a seed drill through it.

Sable stared at her own raised hand like it belonged to someone else. "I hate this," she announced to the table. "For the record. I hate both options. I voted to protect the station and I feel like I voted against my own grandmother."

"Whose side would your grandmother have been on?" Mei-Lin asked.

"My grandmother," said Sable, "was a strike organizer on Ceres. She'd have chained herself to the airlock." She dropped her hand. "Which is why I hate this."

Okonkwo stood, joints cracking like small ordnance. "Five to four means we shelter them. So hear my conditions. One: the Halcyon crew does not leave this station. Two: we do not transmit anything, answer anything, or acknowledge anything beyond standard traffic. Three: when—if—the frigate comes, my job is getting *you* people into shelters, and I will not spend lives defending theirs. They run, hide, or surrender on their own recognizance. Are we clear?"

"Clear," Juniper said.

"And four," Okonkwo added, pausing at the door, "whoever leaked our approach vector to that survey vessel—I will find them. Not today. But I will find them." His eyes swept the table once, without accusation, which somehow made it worse, and he left.

Tomas sat very still after the others filed out. The mess hall hummed its familiar hum—air handlers, coolant loops, the distant heartbeat of the gardens' irrigation pumps. Home sounds. Sounds he'd helped build.

Juniper lingered. Of course she lingered.

"You voted against me," she said.

"I voted against the frigate," Tomas corrected. "The vote against you was incidental."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Twenty years, and that's the best line you have."

"It's early. Coffee hasn't fully deployed." He turned his wrench over in his fingers—he'd brought it to the meeting without thinking, a talisman, a reminder that some problems yielded to simple tools. "Junie. If I'm wrong, tell me I'm wrong. Tell me there's a version of this where we come out the other side."

She was quiet for a long moment. "I can't tell you that. I can only tell you that I've spent twenty years growing food for people I'll never meet, on the theory that the future is worth serving whether or not it knows your name." Her voice softened. "Those twelve are somebody's future, Tomas. They walked in here carrying it. I can't pretend they didn't."

He nodded, because there was nothing else to do with his head. "Then I guess I'd better go fix that coolant pump. Number three sounds like it's eating a cat."

"It's been eating that cat for eleven days."

"Some problems you ignore until they resolve themselves."

"This one's resolving itself into a funeral for the pump."

"There it is," he said. "There's the optimism I married—"

"We didn't marry."

"The optimism I *entered a twenty-year codependent engineering partnership* with."

This time she did smile, brief and tired, and touched his shoulder on her way out, and the touch said everything the vote hadn't settled: *I know what it cost you. I'm spending it anyway.*

* * *

He found the rattle within the hour—a bearing worn past tolerance, easy fix, forty minutes of work that felt like the last honest problem of his life. He worked with the door open so he could hear the station breathe, and he thought about Ferro's rounding error, and Sable's grandmother, and the way Mei-Lin had looked at him like he'd personally kicked a puppy, and mostly he thought about Juniper's hand on his shoulder, light as a seed falling.

Maybe he was wrong. Maybe cowardice was just fear wearing a sensible coat. Maybe—

His console chimed. Docking alert. Unauthorized approach, single occupant, no escort.

He pulled up the feed and felt his stomach drop through the deck plating.

It was the young inspector. Rhee. Alone, in a rented skiff, running dark except for a single courtesy beacon, drifting toward Kestrel's docking collar like a man walking into a church to confess something.

Tomas stared at the screen for a long ten seconds. Then he keyed the intercom to the garden office, where he knew she'd be.

"Junie," he said. "You're going to want to see this. And Junie—whatever it is he wants—I think our vote just got a lot less hypothetical."
