## 18. Running the Relay

### Juniper

The jump gate hung in the black like a wedding ring dropped by a god—a perfect circle of engineered light, eleven kilometers across, its relay spines glittering with the traffic of a hundred colonies. And between Juniper and it, arranged with the casual confidence of people who had never once been told *no*, sat the TerraNova picket line.

Three ships. A corvette, a cutter, and the frigate *Meridian's Ghost*—which was either the corporation's idea of a joke or its idea of a trophy. Its running lights burned the cold white of a morgue.

"Okay," Juniper said, hands light on the controls of the Halcyon, which she had never flown before today and would never fly again. "Okay. Let's review, class."

In the cockpit behind her, Devi snorted. Even now. Even here. Juniper decided she loved this woman.

"The gate relay accepts injection through the maintenance uplink on the ring's lower quadrant," Juniper continued. "Which means we have to get within four hundred meters of a structure that three armed ships are guarding, plug in a cable, and hold still while the largest data package in colonial history uploads through a port designed for lubricant telemetry."

"When you put it that way," Devi said, "it sounds insane."

"It sounds insane every way I put it. I've tried."

Devi's laughter was thin and bright and Juniper held onto it like a handrail in zero-g. Behind them, in the cargo bay, Tomas was already suited up, standing at the external coupling housing with the patience of a man who had spent twenty years fixing things that other people broke. His face behind the visor was calm. Too calm. The calm of a man settling accounts.

"Kestrel," Juniper said, before she could stop herself. "Tomas. You don't have to—"

"Juniper." His voice came through warm and rough, the voice of a thousand shared meals in a cramped galley, of arguments about compost ratios, of twenty years of being the only two people who understood what it cost to keep something alive in the dark. "I spent three weeks selling you out to save that station. I held the coupling. That's not heroism, that's just the invoice coming due."

She wanted to tell him it didn't work that way. That shame didn't have a currency exchange. But the corvette was moving, its drive flare blooming against the ring's glow, and there was no time left for absolution—only for the work.

"Halcyon, this is TerraNova interdiction," the comm crackled. "You are ordered to heave to and prepare for boarding. This is your final—"

"Devi," Juniper said. "Kill the comm. Kill the transponder. Kill everything except the engines."

The Halcyon was a freighter. She had been built to haul ore, not to dance. But Juniper had spent two decades piloting a station's worth of tugs and skiffs through debris fields and she knew something the picket line's tactical computers did not: that a heavy ship, committed fully, with a pilot who has already said goodbye to everything she owns, moves in ways that make no tactical sense.

She turned the freighter straight at the corvette.

"Juniper—" Devi said.

"Trust me."

The corvette's captain, to his credit, flinched. A freighter the mass of a small town, burning hard at a ship one-tenth its size, is not a threat you calculate—it's a threat you avoid. The corvette rolled and burned to starboard, opening the gap Juniper had bet her life it would. She threaded the Halcyon through the space the corvette had just vacated, close enough that the corvette's sensor wash made the freighter's hull sing, and then they were inside the picket line, inside the killing box, inside the range where every gun that could hit them was also pointed at the gate they'd been ordered to protect.

"They won't shoot the ring," Devi breathed.

"They won't shoot the ring," Juniper agreed. "So now we're not a target. Now we're a hostage."

The cutter was coming around, slow and furious. The frigate held position, its weapons hot but its fire solutions tangled in the geometry of a gate it could not damage. Juniper flew the Halcyon down the ring's inner curve, riding the relay spine like a river, four hundred meters, three hundred, two—

"Lower quadrant," she said. "Tomas. It's your garden now."

### Vess

Imara Vess had been thirty-one years old when the Halcyon jumped into the dark past Meridian. She was fifty-one in her service record. She was, by the merciless arithmetic of whatever had found them out there, somewhere in the neighborhood of forty now and accelerating—and her body was spending its reclaimed years like a drunk with a winning ticket.

The bridge of the Halcyon had become a small, warm room at the end of a long tunnel. She sat strapped into the engineering console with Devi's neural rig crowning her skull like a crown of thorns designed by a kind engineer, and she watched the gate fill the viewport, and she thought about three hundred people who had once filled a station with noise.

*Anya,* she thought. *Petra. Old Man Sefu who cheated at cards and let you win anyway.* The names were getting harder to hold. The years she'd gotten back were eating the years she'd kept, and the memories of Meridian were the first course.

"Hold on, hold on," she murmured, to the names, to herself. "Almost time to say you out loud."

The evidence package sat in the buffer beside her: the genetic profiles of the tissue samples, the sensor logs of the phenomenon, the radiation signatures of Meridian's destruction, her own confession recorded in the voice of a woman who was now younger than her own testimony. All of it keyed to inject into the gate relay's uplink the moment Tomas closed the physical coupling. The gate would do the rest. The gate was built to talk to everyone. That was the whole point of a gate. TerraNova had spent thirty years and a hundred massacres trying to forget that.

"Vess." Devi's voice, from the cockpit, tinny with distance. "Coupling in thirty seconds. You ready?"

"I've been ready for eight years," Vess said. "I just didn't know it."

The viewport filled with the gate's lower quadrant, the maintenance spine, the uplink housing—small and ugly and utterly ordinary, a lubricant port on the throat of a miracle. And there, in the floodlights of the Halcyon's docking rig, a figure in a patched enviro-suit, arms wrapped around the coupling assembly like a man embracing an old friend.

Tomas.

Vess had known him for eleven days. She knew him better than she had known most people in thirty years. She knew he argued with his coffee. She knew he kept a dried flower from Earth in his toolkit and pretended it was a spare gasket. She knew he had betrayed them all and hated himself with a purity that was almost holy, and she knew that none of it—not the betrayal, not the shame—mattered as much as the fact that right now, under fire, he was holding the door open for the truth.

"Coupling is green," Tomas said. "Vess. Whenever you're ready."

She put her hand on the injection key.

Somewhere out there, the phenomenon waited in the deep dark—the thing that had unmade them, the thing TerraNova had murdered three hundred people to own. She had spent eleven days listening to the crew argue about what it was for. She no longer cared what it was for. She cared what it *did*: it gave back. It took and gave back. And today, right now, she was going to give back the one thing that could not be bought, weaponized, or buried.

Three hundred names.

She pressed the key.

### Tomas

The coupling housing was cold through his gloves and the fire was warm on his back, and Tomas Okonkwo, age fifty-four, chief engineer of Waystation Kestrel, betrayer of his oldest friend, holder of dried flowers, was having the strangest thought of his life:

*This is the best work I've ever done.*

The coupling was old. Of course it was old—everything on the gate's maintenance spine was old, designed by committee, installed by contractors, and maintained by nobody. The physical interface had corroded in ways that no diagnostic had predicted, and the data handshake between the Halcyon's buffer and the gate relay kept stuttering, dropping, retrying. The injection was going through in fragments. And the only reason it was going through at all was that Tomas had both hands inside the housing, physically bridging a failed relay contact with a jumper cable clamped between his glove's conductive palm-patch and the bus bar, holding the circuit closed the way you'd hold a wound closed—with your whole body and no anesthetic.

Current crawled up his arms. Not enough to kill. Enough to matter.

"Tomas, your vitals—" Devi's voice in his ear.

"I'm fine," he lied, cheerfully. "Never better. I've never felt younger."

Above and behind him, the cutter had gotten its firing solution. The first railgun round skipped off the Halcyon's dorsal plating with a sound he felt through the gate's spine rather than heard—a bell the size of a cathedral, struck once. The second round found the freighter's number three cargo bay and the hull tore open with a shriek of atmosphere and a bloom of frost and fire, and Tomas held on.

Because the upload progress, in the corner of his visor, read sixty-one percent.

*Juniper,* he thought. *You should have let me take the fall back then, twenty years ago, when I asked. You were right not to. You were right about everything, and I was a coward with a mortgage, and this—* another round, closer, the gate ringing, his teeth aching— *this is me being right for once. Let me have it.*

Seventy percent. The fire was spreading along the Halcyon's flank, licking toward the coupling rig's power feed. He could see, through the tear in the hull, the cargo bay where Devi had grown the Meridian samples in living wood, and he thought: *even the plants are testifying.*

Eighty percent.

His arms were shaking. The current had found his shoulders, his spine, a low singing pain like the hum of an overworked reactor. His vision was going grainy at the edges. He thought about the station—about the gardens he had helped kill with his cowardice, the wheat rows and the dwarf apples and the algae vats that smelled like a pond having a bad day, all of it sterilized now or soon to be, all of it gone because he had believed the corporation's promise the way you believe a lullaby.

Ninety percent.

"Come on," he whispered, to the coupling, to the gate, to the corroded, committee-designed, contractor-installed, nobody-maintained machinery of human civilization. "Come on, you beautiful ugly thing. Talk."

Ninety-seven. Ninety-eight.

The Halcyon's fire reached the coupling rig's feed line and the floodlights died, and Tomas worked the last three percent by the light of the burning ship he was standing on, and when the progress bar filled—when the gate relay's status light flicked from amber to green, when the entire colonial network's throat opened to swallow the truth—Tomas Okonkwo laughed out loud, alone in the dark, electrified, on fire, forgiven or not, and said:

"There. *There.*"

### Juniper

Juniper felt the upload complete the way you feel a held breath release—a shudder through the ship, through the deck plates, through her own ribs. The gate's relay spines, which had glittered their cold routine white for as long as anyone could remember, pulsed once, softly, gold.

Then the whole ring began to speak.

She didn't hear it—there was nothing to hear in vacuum—but every screen on the Halcyon lit up at once with the relay's confirmation cascade: transmission accepted, distribution initiated, colonial network wide. Meridian's true records. Vess's confession. The genetic proof, tagged and timestamped, undeniable. Three hundred names, spoken again across human space, on every feed, in every colony, in every company office where someone was about to have a very bad day.

"They're through," Devi shouted from behind her. "Juniper, they're through, it's everywhere, it's—"

"I see it."

She saw it. And she saw the rest too: the cutter breaking off its attack run, its fire solutions dying in real time, its captain suddenly confronted with the arithmetic of shooting witnesses on camera in front of the entire colonial network. The corvette going dark, going quiet, going *elsewhere*. And the frigate *Meridian's Ghost*—the trophy, the joke—hanging in space with its weapons still hot and its orders suddenly worthless, because you could not massacre your way out of a story that everyone was reading.

But the Halcyon was burning. The fire had found the fuel lines and the freighter was dying under her hands, and the jump window—the narrow, eleven-day window that had driven them across the dark—was closing behind the ring like a door.

"Devi," Juniper said quietly. "How much atmosphere do we have left?"

"Hours. Maybe less. The cargo bay's breached and the fire's in the frame."

"And the gate?"

Devi was quiet a moment. "Closed. Juniper, the window's closed. We're on the wrong side of it. Whatever's left of us, we're—"

"We're through," Juniper said.

She looked at the ring filling the viewport, gold light still pulsing along its spine, carrying the names of the dead to every living ear. She looked at the fire crawling along the Halcyon's hull, and thought of a garden on a station she would never see again, and found, to her enormous surprise, that she was smiling.

"Tomas," she said, keying the comm. "Tomas, come in. It's done. Come home."

Static.

Then, faint, cracked, unmistakably grinning: "Juniper? You should see the state of your ship."

And behind the gold pulse of the gate, in the burning dark, the Halcyon began to fall.
