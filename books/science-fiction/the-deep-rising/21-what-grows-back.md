## 21. What Grows Back

The Halcyon's cargo bay smelled like burnt insulation and old vacuum, and Juniper loved it anyway.

She stood in the middle of it with a salvage manifest on her wrist display and a headlamp strapped over her braid, taking inventory of the wreckage the way she'd once taken inventory of her own heart—carefully, without flinching, and with the firm understanding that most of what she found was going to need repotting.

Kestrel was gone. That was still true every morning when she woke up and remembered it, a small cold fact that arrived like a draft under a door. Twenty years of terracing and soil chemistry and patient, stubborn coaxing—gone, abandoned, its gardens either dead or thriving under corporate ownership, and she'd decided she didn't want to know which. But Kestrel's seeds had come with her. Four hundred and eleven varieties, packed in vacuum-sealed vaults that had ridden out the whole ugly business at the relay in the dark, uncomplaining, the way good seeds do.

And now they had a ship. Not a good ship. The Halcyon had taken a hull breach aft, a radiation scorch along the spine, and a general air of having been through something it would prefer not to discuss. But her bones were sound, and her cargo bay was forty meters long, and—this was the part that mattered—she had working atmospheric processors and a light array that Devi swore could be retuned for photosynthesis with "maybe a week of swearing."

"Devi," Juniper called across the bay, "how's the swearing going?"

Devi's voice drifted down from the catwalk, where she was elbow-deep in a lighting conduit. "I've completed the first three phases. I'm now in the bargaining stage, which is technically a grief response, but I find it applies."

"Take your time."

"I will not, actually, because I found something." Devi's head appeared over the railing, backlit and triumphant. "Come look at what the wreck gave us."

Juniper climbed. In the conduit housing, wedged where no one had thought to look because no one had ever had a reason to, was a secondary seed locker—Halcyon's own, from her deep-space run. Eight years in transit, unopened, forgotten by a crew that had come back younger and stranger and carrying heavier things than cargo.

Devi cracked the seal with a hiss. Inside: trays of sample vials, nutrient wafers gone stale, and seeds. Dozens of varieties Juniper didn't recognize—deep-space cultivars, experimental stock, things collected from ports she'd never heard of by a crew that had been, in a sense, collecting the future.

"They were going to plant these somewhere," Juniper said quietly. "Before Meridian."

"Before everything," Devi agreed. She turned a vial in the light. "You know what I keep thinking? We spent eight years running from a massacre, and the whole time we were carrying a garden and didn't know it. Seems like a theme."

"It's not a theme," Juniper said. "It's a job description."

* * *

They started the next shift, which was to say they started at what everyone had agreed was morning, because a ship full of people from four different time zones and one dead station needed rituals more than it needed accuracy.

Tomas came down on his own schedule, which was slower now. The radiation exposure from holding the coupling at the relay had been kinder than the medics feared and crueler than anyone said aloud—his hands trembled, his stamina was a rumor of itself, and he'd taken to wearing gloves to hide the tremor, which fooled nobody and hurt everybody. But he could still weld, and welding was mostly listening, and his ears worked fine.

"You want me to build what," he said, looking at Juniper's sketch on the bay wall.

"Terraces. Tiered. Along the port side, under the lights."

"This is a cargo bay. It's for cargo."

"It was for cargo. Now it's for the people aboard, not the unborn." She said it lightly, but she watched his face when she said it, because she'd said it to herself a dozen times in the dark and it had never once been light. "Twenty years I grew food for colonists who will maybe, possibly, in some optimistic timeline, arrive. Beautiful work. Meaningful work. Also, I never once got to see anybody eat a tomato I grew. I'd like to try the other version."

Tomas was quiet for a while. He rubbed his gloved thumb along the edge of the sketch, the way he did when he was measuring something that wasn't on the drawing.

"Kestrel's gardens were for people you'd never meet," he said finally. "This one's for people you can't get rid of." A crooked smile. "I can live with that. I'd like to, actually. I'd like very much to live, and to do it somewhere with tomatoes."

"Then weld me a terrace, engineer."

"Then weld you a terrace," he said, and got out his tools, and for three hours the bay rang with the sound of a man building a reason.

* * *

The messages started arriving on the fourth day.

Not the corporate kind—those had stopped, mostly, after the frigate broke off under the weight of three hundred million witnesses. These were different. They came in on open channels, from addresses Juniper had to look up: the Halcyon Reach Cooperative. New Caledonia Freehold. The Oort settlements. The long tail of human space, the places that had never signed TerraNova's charters, the colonies that had spent decades being small and ignored and quietly furious about it.

*To the survivors of the Halcyon and the crew of Waystation Kestrel,* the first one began, and Juniper read it three times because she couldn't quite believe the salutation applied to her. *We have seen the testimony of Captain Vess. We have read the names. We ask: will you come to us? Will you speak to us in person, so that our children know this was real?*

They wanted testimony. Public, in-person, sworn. Hearings—not corporate hearings, not the kind where the judges owned the courtroom, but the old kind, the kind that happened in assembly halls and community domes and anywhere with enough chairs and enough stubborn people. The independent colonies were organizing something none of them had ever managed alone: a reckoning.

Juniper brought the messages to the mess, where the crew had developed the habit of gathering without officially gathering, the way families do.

"They want us to testify," she said. "In person. On the record, their record, in front of everyone."

Silence, the specific silence of twelve people and one ex-terraformer all thinking at once.

"They want us to *travel*," said Okonkwo, the Halcyon's engineer, who had strong opinions about travel since the relay. "In a ship with a hole in it."

"They want *me*," said Devi, "to stand up in front of a room and talk about the tissue samples, which I can do, but I want it noted that I'm much funnier in writing."

"They want the truth," said Rhee, quietly, from the end of the table. He'd been quiet a lot since Ceres—quiet the way a man is quiet when he's still counting the cost of his sister's name being said aloud at last. "That's the part I keep getting stuck on. Nobody's ever wanted the truth from me before. They've wanted compliance, or silence, or a signature. This is new. I don't entirely trust it."

"Trust it anyway," said Devi. "Some things are new because they're finally allowed to be."

Juniper looked around the table. Vess wasn't there—Vess was resting, and everyone had silently agreed to call it resting—but her absence was a presence, the way it always was now. The captain had given her testimony to the network, and the network had given it to everyone, and now here was the strange aftermath: people wanted the *people*, not just the recording. They wanted to look a witness in the eye.

"I spent twenty years being invisible on purpose," Juniper said. "It was a strategy. It was also, if I'm honest, a wound I dressed up as a strategy. And now—" she spread her hands— "now I get to choose. Walk into the light on purpose, or stay dark on purpose. Either way it's mine."

"And which way are you leaning?" Tomas asked.

She thought about the seed vaults of Kestrel, sealed and patient. She thought about a message from Earth she still hadn't answered, sitting in her queue like an unopened jar.

"I'm leaning toward tomatoes," she said. "But first we plant."

* * *

Planting, it turned out, was the easy part. The hard part was soil.

They had none. A ship doesn't carry dirt; a ship carries the *idea* of dirt in the form of nutrient substrate and hydroponic racks, and hydroponics was fine, hydroponics was efficient, but Juniper had spent two decades learning that plants were not the point. Soil was the point. Soil was a community—a whole civilization of bacteria and fungi and decomposing things and living things, all negotiating. You couldn't grow belonging in a sterile rack.

So they made soil.

It started with the compost, which started with the galley scraps, which started with the crew actually eating together in the mess every shift instead of grabbing rations alone, because Juniper put a notice on the door that read *SCRAPS FEED THE GARDEN, AND THE GARDEN FEEDS YOU, AND YES THIS IS EMOTIONAL BLACKMAIL, EAT TOGETHER ANYWAY.* Devi contributed the Halcyon's old sample media and half a decade of lab waste. Rhee, of all people, turned out to have a talent for vermicomposting, which he explained with the flat dignity of a man who had once had security clearances and now had worms, and was, on balance, better off.

"Red wigglers," he said, showing Juniper the bin with something dangerously close to pride. "They eat everything. They have no politics. I find them restful."

"They're the only crew members who never ask me hard questions," Juniper agreed.

By the sixth week, they had soil. It wasn't good soil—it was young soil, angry soil, soil that smelled like ambition—but it was *theirs*, made from the meals they'd shared and the waste they'd transformed, and when Juniper pressed her hands into it for the first time, she had to stop for a moment and stand very still.

"What is it?" Devi asked.

"Nothing. It's just—" She laughed at herself. "I've started gardens a hundred times. This is the first one where I'll be here to eat it."

* * *

Vess came down to the bay in the seventh week, on a good day, and Juniper walked her along the terraces slowly, because the captain's body was spending its years faster than the calendar could account for, and good days were a currency to be spent carefully.

The first shoots were up—mustard greens, always the optimists, and a row of the Halcyon's mysterious deep-space beans that Devi had germinated against all reasonable odds. Vess looked at them for a long time.

"I planted a garden on Meridian," she said. "Did I ever tell you that? Small one. Outside the mess. I used to tell the junior crew that if they wanted to know the health of a station, don't read the logs—look at the garden. If people have time to grow things, they believe in a future." Her voice caught. "They had a future. It was taken."

Juniper said nothing. There was nothing to say that the mustard greens weren't already saying.

"But this one," Vess went on, touching a leaf with one aged finger, "this one's different, isn't it. Kestrel was a promise to people who hadn't come yet. What's this one?"

"This one's a promise to people who are here."

Vess nodded slowly. "Then it's the better garden. It only took me sixty years and a massacre to learn the difference." She smiled, and it was the old Vess smile, the one that had commanded a freighter through the dark. "Don't wait sixty years, Eriksen. Whatever you're not saying to whoever you're not saying it to—say it while the soil's still warm."

That night, Juniper opened the message from Earth.

It was from her brother, the one she'd left behind when she chose exile over a falsified report, twenty years and one lifetime ago. It was short. Most of it was ordinary—his knee, his daughter's graduation, the weather in a place with weather. At the end: *I saw the names on the feed. I saw your name. I always knew you hadn't done what they said. Come home someday, or don't, but write back, Jun. The garden misses you. That's a joke. We don't have a garden. Mom planted one when you left and never told you.*

Juniper read it four times. Then she sat in the cargo bay, under the grow-lights, in the smell of green things and young soil, and she wrote back.

*I'm alive. I did what I did and I'd do it again. I'm on a ship with good people and a bad hull and the first garden I've ever planted for people I love. I'll tell you everything when I see you. I'm going to see you.*

It wasn't forgiveness—that was a door that took longer to open. But it was a hand on the handle.

* * *

The replies from the colonies kept coming, and the crew kept answering, and somewhere in the answering, the Halcyon stopped being a wreck and started being a home. Okonkwo patched the hull and complained about it, which everyone understood was how he prayed. Rhee's worms multiplied. Devi started a log of the garden's progress that was, in her words, "part science, part memoir, part threat." Tomas built a bench along the port terraces, because a garden without a place to sit was just agriculture, and Juniper sat on it every evening and watched the shoots get taller and thought: *this is what I was practicing for. All those years, growing food for the unborn. I was practicing for the day I'd grow it for the living.*

On the eighty-ninth day after the broadcast, the harvest came in—small, ridiculous, mostly mustard greens and a handful of beans—and the whole crew ate a salad that tasted like victory and slightly like the nutrient substrate, and nobody cared, and Devi cried into her bowl and claimed it was the vinegar.

And that evening, the long-range array flagged a message that was different from all the others. Not a colony. Not a cooperative. Not a grief-stricken stranger or a furious assembly.

It was a formal request, routed through three independent relay chains to hide its origin, with authentication codes that made Rhee sit up very straight at his console.

"Juniper," he said, and his voice had gone strange. "You need to see this. It's from the Meridian families. The actual next of kin—the ones the corporations listed as *relocated*. They were never relocated. They've been looking for their people for eight years." He turned the screen toward her. "They're asking to meet us. In person. And Juniper—they're not asking for testimony."

Juniper read the message twice, her heart doing something complicated in her chest.

*We know what your captain carried. We know what she saw out there in the deep. We want to know if it's true. We want to know if our people are somewhere on the other side of it. And we want to know if the thing that gave your crew back their years—* the message paused there, as if its author had struggled with the words— *if it can give us ours back too.*

She looked up at the garden, at the green rows under the lights, at the people she'd chosen and who had chosen her back.

"They're asking about the phenomenon," she said quietly. "They're asking the question we swore we'd never answer."

And somewhere in the dark beyond the hull, the deep was listening.
