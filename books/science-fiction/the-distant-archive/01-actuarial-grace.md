## 1. Actuarial Grace

The Meridian came home on a Tuesday, which Elodie Thackeray would later decide was the first wrong thing about it.

Ships that went out to the Kessel Reach didn't come home on Tuesdays. They came home in pieces, or they didn't come home at all, or — if the gods of contested orbit were feeling generous — they limped back eleven months late with a hull scarred like an old man's hands and a crew manifest that matched, more or less, the one that had left. The Meridian had been gone four years. It was scheduled to be gone six. And it docked at Halcyon Freight's Ring Seven terminal at 0400 station time, quiet as a debt being paid early.

Elodie was three weeks into a fraud case involving a grain hauler whose captain had claimed a micrometeorite swarm ate his cargo hold when, as far as anyone could prove, he'd sold it twice. She was good at that work. She'd been good at it for ten years, since the day she'd signed her first field contract at twenty-six and learned that "impossible accident" was not a contradiction but a price point. Somewhere in those ten years she had become the investigator Halcyon called when a claim didn't make sense and couldn't be allowed to keep not making sense. Her name carried weight in the adjusters' wing. Not affection. Weight.

The message came through at 0612: *Meridian returned. Manifest discrepancy. Flagged priority. You're on it.*

No details. Priority flags without details meant someone upstairs already knew what the discrepancy was and wanted it priced by someone who wouldn't ask questions in elevators.

She took the shuttle across to Ring Seven with her kit bag and a thermos of coffee gone bitter from reheating, and she stood in the observation gallery above Docking Bay 12 while the Meridian's airlock cycled, and she watched the crew walk out.

That was the second wrong thing.

She had their files memorized before the shuttle finished docking — it was how she worked, always reading the claim before the claimant. Captain Ivo Renner, fifty-eight, twenty-two years in the black, a widower with a reputation for bringing ships home heavy. Chief Engineer Tomas Vey, sixty-one, bad knees, worse temper. Navigator Priya Chandra, forty-nine. Medic Ruth Osei, fifty-five. Six crew total, average age fifty-four, a ship full of people who had signed on for one last long haul because long hauls paid pensions.

The people walking down the ramp were not those people.

They moved like them. That was the thing that stopped Elodie's coffee halfway to her mouth. The captain walked with the same slight favoring of his left leg she'd seen in his medical file — an old fracture, badly set, documented twelve years ago. But the face above the limp was thirty years old. Thirty, maybe younger. Dark hair, no gray. Hands without the arthritis the file swore he had.

She checked the others as they passed under the gallery lights. Same pattern, every one of them. Vey's knees bent like a young man's while his scowl belonged to a grandfather. Chandra looked barely old enough to hold a navigator's license. Osei — the medic, who should have been the easiest to read, because medics wore their age in their eyes — had the unlined face of a resident fresh out of training and the posture of someone who had spent decades catching other people's deaths.

Elodie set down her coffee and did what she always did when the universe handed her something impossible: she started counting.

* * *

The audit room Halcyon gave her was a converted storage bay with a table, six chairs, and a recorder that predated her career. She ran the intake interviews one at a time, standard procedure, standard questions, and for four hours the answers were so clean they made her teeth ache.

Yes, they departed on schedule. Yes, the route was filed correctly. No, no anomalies until day ninety-one. Then, all six of them, independently, in nearly identical words: the dark stretch past the Verge where the relays thinned out and dead space began. A signal where no signal could live. A station where no station was charted.

"A structure," Renner said. He sat across from her with his too-young hands folded on the table, and his voice was the voice of a fifty-eight-year-old man coming out of a thirty-year-old throat, gravel over glass. "Not on any chart. Not moving. It just *was*, out there, like it had been waiting for us specifically."

"What kind of structure?"

"Big. Old. It didn't look built so much as grown, or remembered." His eyes came up and held hers, and Elodie had interviewed enough liars to know the difference between evasion and exhaustion, and this was exhaustion. "You're going to ask me what we traded. Everyone wants to skip to that part."

"I'm going to ask you about your biometrics, Captain. Your file says fifty-eight."

"My file is accurate. I'm fifty-eight." He said it without flinching. "I remember fifty-eight. I remember my wife dying at fifty-four, and I remember burying her, and I remember signing the articles for this run two years after that with knees that screamed every time the gravity cycled. All of that happened. And then we docked with that thing out there, and something was taken out of me, and what's left doesn't have the mileage on it anymore." He turned his hands over, studied the palms. "I've spent four months trying to make peace with having a body that's lying to me. You've had four hours. Take your time."

Elodie let the silence sit, then asked the question she'd been circling. "What did it take?"

"Thirty years. Give or take. Off each of us." He said it the way a man reports a theft of tools — precise, resigned, already itemized. "And in exchange—"

"In exchange?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him close his mouth. "Ask your biometrics first," he said. "Get the numbers straight before you get the story. Numbers don't grieve."

* * *

The numbers didn't grieve. The numbers were immaculate.

Halcyon's medical contractor ran the full panel that afternoon — cellular age markers, telomere assays, bone density scans, ocular lens grading, the whole battery that existed precisely to catch the fraud cases Elodie usually handled. She watched the results compile on her screen in the audit office with the particular dread of a professional watching her own expertise turn against her.

Every marker said the same thing. Captain Renner's body was twenty-eight years old. Vey's was thirty-one. Chandra, twenty-seven. The aggregate deviation from manifest age across six crew members: negative thirty-point-two years, uniform, uncorrected by any known process. Not surgery. Not regeneration therapy — she'd priced enough black-market regen cases to know its fingerprints, the asymmetries, the telltale patchwork. This was uniform. Systemic. As if the years hadn't been hidden or replaced but simply *removed*, cleanly, the way a ledger removes a line item.

Her opening report wrote itself in her head, and it read like fraud. Of course it did. Six crew members, de-aged by identical margins, returning early from a route with no resupply points, claiming contact with an uncharted object in dead space. In ten years she had seen cults, cloning rings, identity swaps, a captain who'd replaced his entire crew with biosynthetic duplicates to dodge a labor audit. Every instinct she had honed said: *fabrication, coordinated, expensive.*

But the biometrics didn't lie. That was the whole architecture of her profession — everything else could be forged, bought, performed, but cellular age was the one number the universe kept honestly. She had ended careers with that number. She had sent men to prison with it. And now it sat on her screen telling her that six people had each lost three decades somewhere between the Verge and Ring Seven, and that the loss had been, by every measurable criterion, *real*.

She pulled up the departure manifests next. Cross-checked tonnage, fuel burn, life support draw, consumables against return figures. If the ship had turned around early, the math would show it. The math showed nothing. The Meridian had flown its route, burned its fuel, drawn its air, aged its systems — the hull itself showed four years of wear, micrometeorite pitting consistent with the full transit, drive components worn exactly as specified. The *ship* had experienced four years. Only the crew had been exempted.

Elodie sat back in her chair in the emptying audit office and felt something crack open in her chest that she didn't have a name for yet. It wasn't fear, exactly. Fear she knew; fear was a line item. This was older than fear. This was the feeling she imagined her sister Mara would describe — Mara, who was thirty-four and dying by degrees in a care facility on Ceres while the treatments ate money faster than Elodie could earn it — the feeling of standing in front of something that did not fit inside any category you owned.

Because here was the thought she could not put down, the one that followed her through the station's evening shift-change crowds and onto the tram home and into the small apartment where she kept Mara's latest billing statement pinned above her desk like a saint's icon:

*If something out there can take thirty years off six people — cleanly, measurably, verifiably — then somewhere in the physics of that transaction is the reverse transaction.*

Somewhere out there was a machine that held years the way a bank holds money. And Elodie Thackeray had spent ten years learning to price everything, and there was nothing — nothing — in her entire actuarial vocabulary that could tell her what a decade of her sister's life was worth, or what it would cost, or who she would have to pay.

She opened the incident file and began typing the summary she would submit in the morning. *Manifest discrepancy confirmed. Biometric deviation: -30.2 years, uniform across crew. Cause: unknown mechanism, uncharted object, coordinates pending crew debrief.* Her cursor hovered over the last field. Coordinates pending. Renner had given her the direction of travel, roughly, and a bearing, and then gone quiet in a way that had weight to it.

She saved the draft. She poured the last of the cold coffee. And she understood, sitting alone in the hum of Ring Seven's night cycle, that this claim was different from every claim she had ever survived. The others she had denied, or settled, or buried. This one could not be denied. The numbers were too clean, the bodies too honest, the impossible too thoroughly documented.

This claim could only be survived.

Her comm chimed. Internal routing, executive tier, which she had never once received at 2300 hours. The message was four lines long, unsigned, and it changed the temperature of the room:

*Initial findings noted. Classification recommendation: equipment malfunction, sensor ghosting, crew psychological event. Do not pursue origin coordinates. Full cooperation expected.*

Elodie read it twice. Then she looked up at Mara's billing statement pinned above her desk — *balance outstanding, treatment phase three begins in nineteen days* — and she thought about a station in the dark that traded in years, and a corporation that wanted the whole thing filed as a sensor error before sunrise.

Weary competence cracked another degree toward wonder, and wonder tilted, slowly, toward dread.

She reached for her coat. There were five more crew interviews tomorrow, and a captain who hadn't told her what they got in exchange, and a company that already knew what answer it wanted.

The Meridian had come home on a Tuesday. Nothing else about it was going to be simple.
