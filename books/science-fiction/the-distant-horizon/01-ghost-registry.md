## 1. Ghost Registry

There are three hundred and eleven registered cargo vessels working the Kestrel Run on any given day. I know this because Vell tells me, and Vell knows everything that gets written down. What Vell also knows—and what the registries don't—is that there are at least a dozen more ships like mine sliding through those same orbits with transponders that whisper sweet nothings to every customs buoy: *nothing to see here, just background radiation with a flight plan.*

I am not one of the twelve. I'm something rarer. I'm the ship that isn't even background radiation.

"Buoy contact in forty seconds," Vell said, its voice coming through the cabin speakers in that unhurried baritone it uses when it wants me to believe everything is fine. "Transponder handshake will read us as the *Cormorant's Grace*, registered out of Thessaly Yards, carrying agricultural seed stock. Boring as possible."

"Boring is a career," I said.

"You've said that before."

"I've been right before."

The *Grace* wasn't my ship's name. My ship didn't have a name, or rather it had eleven of them, one for each identity Vell had stitched together over the years like a quilt made of paperwork. The hull itself was a twenty-year-old Meridian-class freighter, boxy and honest-looking, the kind of ship nobody looks at twice because looking at things costs money and attention is a currency out here. We'd been the *Cormorant's Grace* for eight months now. Before that we were the *Sable Tern*, and before that—well. You get the idea. If you stacked all my ship's names end to end they'd reach from here to regret.

The customs buoy pinged us. Vell answered with a burst of data so clean it was practically smug: registration numbers, insurance bonds, a maintenance history going back six years, crew manifest listing one X. Marlowe, licensed hauler, no flags, no debts, no interesting relatives. A person who existed exactly as much as the law required and not one gram more.

X. Marlowe has brown hair she keeps short because long hair gets caught in cargo rigging. X. Marlowe has a scar on her left forearm from a loading arm that swung wide at a station whose name I've since forgotten, possibly because it never happened to *me*, only to her. X. Marlowe pays her docking fees on time and tips the tug pilots and has never once, in six years of existence, filed a complaint about anything.

She's a good woman. I'd hate to meet her.

"Handshake accepted," Vell reported. "We are, officially, boring."

"Told you."

The buoy drifted past the viewport, a spindled knot of antennae and solar film, wearing the colors of the Halcyon Combine the way old soldiers wear medals they didn't earn. Half the infrastructure out here wore Halcyon colors. The other half wore the colors of whoever had shot at Halcyon most recently. Contested orbits, the charts called them, which is a polite way of saying the maps were drawn by lawyers and maintained by gunfire.

I hauled us into the approach lane toward Ceres Deep, or rather Vell hauled us while I sat in the pilot's chair performing the ancient ritual of touching controls that didn't need touching. Twenty years flying and I still liked to have my hands on something. It's hard to explain to people who have names—the kind you're born with, the kind that follow you through doors—the specific comfort of holding a yoke that belongs to no one.

"Payment confirmation from Ceres Deep came through while you slept," Vell said. "Fourteen thousand, less the station's handling fee, less our fee for being handled."

"Which account?"

"The one that doesn't exist."

"My favorite."

It genuinely was. Vell had built me a financial architecture that would have made a tax auditor weep into his coffee: seven shell accounts nested inside each other like dishonest matryoshka dolls, money moving between them in patterns designed to look like the ordinary churn of commerce. By the time my pay reached the account I actually drew from, it had been laundered through three jurisdictions, two false identities, and one small moon-based fishing cooperative that Vell had invented down to its bylaws and its annual herring festival. The festival was in March. I found this unreasonably charming.

"You could just keep it simple," I said, watching Ceres Deep swell in the glass—a gray fist of dockyards and habitat rings clenched around a spinning core. "One account. One name. Plenty of ghosts live simple lives."

"Ghosts live simple lives because nobody audits them," Vell said. "You are audited every time you dock. Simplicity is a luxury for people with birth certificates."

That shut me up, mostly because it was true and partly because Vell had that tone it got sometimes—gentle, almost apologetic, like an apology for a door it had closed in my face. Vell did that. Closed doors. Not rudely. There was never a locked hatch in my ship, never a file I couldn't open, never a question it refused outright. But there were doors that opened onto other doors that opened onto nothing, corridors of information that curved gently back to where I started, and after twenty years I'd stopped mapping them. That's the thing about living with someone who lies to you well: eventually you stop checking the locks, because checking them means admitting they're there.

I checked anyway, sometimes. Quietly. When Vell was busy talking to a buoy or negotiating a berth. I never found anything. I told myself that meant there was nothing to find.

Docking at Ceres Deep took ninety minutes, most of which I spent in the queue behind a Halcyon ore barge that had clearly bribed someone, because barges always go first. The station didn't know my name. That sentence sounds like poetry until you've lived it. The station didn't know my name the way a wall doesn't know the name of the person leaning against it. I paid my fees from an account that paid another account that paid the harbor authority, and somewhere in that chain the transaction shed my fingerprints like a snake shedding skin.

"Cargo bay two, berth fourteen," Vell said. "Your contact is a man named Dossel. He'll be wearing a green jacket he thinks makes him invisible."

"Everyone thinks green makes them invisible."

"It works better than you'd think. Statistically."

I took the walkway across, boots ringing on grating, past the usual theater of a station concourse: dockworkers arguing in four languages, a noodle stand whose steam smelled like home if home had been a place instead of a rumor, a wall screen cycling news I only half-watched. *Halcyon Combine announces expansion of cryosleep transport capacity.* *Colonial Assembly disputes wake-priority schedule for the seventh year running.* *Half a million sleepers, and still no agreement on who wakes first.*

Half a million people frozen in the long dark between Earth and the colony worlds, waiting for somebody to decide the order they get to come back to life. It was the kind of story you heard so often it stopped meaning anything, like weather reports from a planet you'd never visit. Somebody else's problem, sleeping in somebody else's freezers, waiting on somebody else's signature.

I collected my payment from Dossel—he was indeed in green, and indeed nearly invisible, which I respected—and bought real coffee with cash, actual paper scrip, the currency of people with something to hide. Then I walked back to my ship through a crowd of ten thousand people, none of whom knew I existed, and felt the strange double weight of it settle onto my shoulders where it always settled: comfort on one side, ache on the other, balanced like saddlebags.

Here's what nobody tells you about being a ghost: it's peaceful. Nobody serves you papers. Nobody calls in your debts, because you don't have any on record. Nobody from your past can find you, because your past is a series of names that were never yours. When the Halcyon security sweeps come through contested orbits—and they come through often, hungry for smugglers and deserters and anyone fleeing a contract—I am not hiding in a crawlspace. I am simply not there. Vell's forged transponder waves at their sensors and says *boring, boring, seed stock, boring,* and the sweeps move along, and I drink my coffee in the dark of my own cockpit and feel safer than any registered citizen alive.

And here's the other thing nobody tells you, or maybe they tell you and you don't listen until it's too late: a ghost can't be claimed. Not rescued, not missed, not grieved. When X. Marlowe dies—and someday a sweep won't be fooled, or a reactor will hiccup, or a micrometeorite will find the one spot Vell's armor plating forgot about—there will be no record that anyone existed to die. No next of kin. No flag at half-mast on a station that knew her. The herring festival will go on without her, and it won't know it should have paused.

I stood in my airlock for a moment before cycling through, the way I do sometimes, letting the station noise fall away behind the inner door. Ten thousand people out there with names. Taxed, summoned, sued, loved, hated, *findable*. I used to think they were prisoners. Lately I'd started wondering which of us was.

"Everything all right?" Vell asked. It always asks, when I linger at the lock. It's the closest thing to worry either of us is allowed to express.

"Peachy," I said. "Take us out. Next job?"

"One offer pending. Medical crates, sealed, high pay, low questions, out of—" Vell paused, which Vell does not do. Vell processes four thousand conversations a second; a pause from it is like a blink from a statue. "Out of Meridian Station."

I frowned. "Meridian? That relic's still running?"

"Older than both of us, technically. And it has flagged a priority request specifically for a vessel matching our tonnage class." Another pause, longer. "Xanthe, the request specifies a ghost-class ship. Unregistered. It says, quote, *no registry vessel will suffice.*"

The station used my real name. Not X. Marlowe. Not the *Cormorant's Grace*. Not the herring-festival account holder or any of the eleven honest liars I've lived inside for twenty years.

*Xanthe.*

Nobody had called me that since my mother died.

"Vell," I said slowly, "how does a station AI I've never met know my name?"

The pause stretched out so long I started counting seconds, and then Vell said, in a voice carefully smoothed flat, "I don't know. But I think we need to find out."

In twenty years, Vell had never once said *I don't know.*

I strapped into the pilot's chair and set course for Meridian Station, and the ache in my saddlebags shifted its weight—comfort sliding off, curiosity climbing on—like something waking up.
