## 1. Contraband of the Mind

The trick to smuggling memories, Astrid Quintana had learned over ten years and four hundred runs, was to be forgettable in every particular that mattered and memorable in exactly one.

The memorable thing, today, was grief.

He stood in the customs queue of Ring Station Halcyon with a forged grief-license in his breast pocket and the correct expression on his face, which was no expression at all — the flat, patient look of a man carrying a dead wife's final year inside a sealed data-core, a man who had already cried everything he had and now just wanted the bureaucratic ordeal to be over. He'd practiced the look in a mirror for two hours the night before, until it stopped being an expression and became a weather condition. That was the craft of it. Anyone could fake a smile. Faking the absence of one took discipline.

"Next," said the scanner gate.

Astrid stepped into the cradle of the arch. It hummed. Somewhere behind its matte housing, a sensor array older than the colony itself catalogued the contents of his coat, his boots, the lining of his hat, and the small of his back, where a licensed courier was permitted to carry exactly one sealed memory-core, class G, grief exemption, non-transferable.

He was carrying three.

Two were tucked into the hollowed heel of his left boot, which the arch had never once flagged in six years, because the arch's designers had assumed no one would be stupid enough to hide contraband in a place that got walked on. Astrid had built a career on the observation that people who design security systems rarely imagine the smuggler as a practical man. They imagined him clever. Clever men hid things in clever places. Practical men hid things where the floor was dirty.

"Courier Quintana," said the gate, in a voice like a patient aunt. "Purpose of travel."

"Family archive," Astrid said. "Downwell delivery."

The gate paused — a theatrical pause, he suspected, designed to make smugglers sweat — and then the archway light turned a forgiving green. "Grief-license verified. Have a gentle day, Courier Quintana."

"Thank you," he said, and meant it in the way you mean things to machines: politely, and without any expectation of reciprocity.

He collected his hat from the tray, settled it low, and walked through the customs hall of Halcyon Ring at the speed of a man with nowhere interesting to be. Around him, four hundred people were doing the same thing, and every one of them was lying about something — a cargo manifest, a second passport, a marriage. That was the thing nobody told you about an orbital customs ring: it wasn't a filter between honest people and criminals. It was a filter between people whose lies were boring and people whose lies were interesting. Astrid had spent a decade ensuring his lies were the boringest in the system.

Rule one: never look inside what you carry.

Rule two: never carry for anyone twice.

Rule three: never, under any circumstances, let anyone know where you sleep.

He recited them the way other men recited prayers, not because he believed they'd save him — belief was for people with futures they couldn't predict — but because the recitation itself was a kind of armor. Ten years ago he'd been a different man, a man who said yes too easily and asked too few questions, and that man had learned what the inside of a sealed core could do to you. That man had died, more or less. What was left had rules instead.

* * *

The downwell elevator took forty minutes, and Astrid spent them the way he spent most of his life: alone, and good at it.

He had a seat by the window because the window seat let him watch the colony resolve out of the dark — Meridian, turning below him, all its weather systems and wheat belts and the grey scar of the original habitat ring, decommissioned before he was born. Forty thousand people down there. He knew the number the way you know a fact about a foreign country. He could name perhaps eleven of them, and he had worked hard to keep it at eleven.

There was a woman across the aisle with a child asleep against her shoulder, and Astrid looked at them for exactly as long as it took to confirm they weren't watching him, and then he looked at the window instead, because that was the other craft of it. You didn't stare at families. Families noticed being stared at, and families talked, and talk was the one commodity Astrid had spent ten years refusing to deal in.

The child, a girl of maybe eight, opened her eyes as the elevator shuddered into its braking curve. She looked straight at him. Not at his face — at his coat, an old grey thing with a collar that had gone soft as paper from a decade of weather. She frowned at it the way children frown at things that are almost familiar and not quite, and then she said, to no one in particular, in the flat voice children use for things they believe are simply true:

"She's still singing."

The mother didn't stir. Astrid kept his eyes on the window.

Meridian turned below, patient and golden, and Astrid Quintana did what he always did with things that didn't fit into his rules: he put them down, and he walked away, and he did not look back.

The core against his ankle was warm. Cores were always warm. He'd stopped noticing it years ago, the way you stop noticing your own pulse.

* * *

His contact was waiting at the downwell terminus, in the noodle bar that smelled like scorched ginger and recycled steam, the one with the owner who had never once asked Astrid a question in nine years of near-daily custom. This was, Astrid had often thought, the closest thing he had to a friendship: a man who charged him fairly and knew nothing about him. He'd have trusted the man with his life. He'd have trusted almost anyone with his life. It was his memory he kept under lock.

"You're late," said the contact, who was called Tam, who was not called Tam, and whose real name Astrid had made a point of never learning, because rule two worked in both directions.

"Customs was slow."

"Customs is always slow. That's why you're paid." Tam slid a chit across the table, face-down, under the shadow of a noodle bowl, with the smooth boredom of a man passing salt. "Downwell client. Grief-work, class G. She wants it delivered to a shrine address in the Tarn district. You know it?"

"I know it."

"Sealed?"

"Sealed," Astrid said, and it was even true. The two cores in his boot were sealed. The one in his hatband was sealed. Everything he carried was sealed, because that was the whole religion of it: he was a courier, not a reader. Other men smuggled memories they'd already lived in, memories they'd sampled like wine, and those were the men who ended up in the harbor with their minds wiped down to a shine. Astrid carried other people's pasts the way a mule carries ore — without tasting it.

"Same terms?"

"Same terms."

Tam almost smiled, which for Tam was a major emotional event, and Astrid felt the familiar small satisfaction of a run going cleanly. Four hundred and one. He'd mark it in his ledger tonight, in the flat little book he kept in a floor safe beneath the floor of a room he rented under a name that didn't exist, in a building whose landlord he paid in cash and had never spoken to beyond *rent's due* and *paid*. The ledger had four hundred lines in it. Not one of them had a name on it besides his own.

That was the point. That was the whole shape of his life, and he had built it deliberately, joint by joint, the way you build a wall: not to keep something out, but to keep something in. He'd learned, ten years ago, what happened when you let people in. People died, or worse, they asked you to carry things, and then they died, and then you were the one holding the thing, and the thing was heavy, and there was no one to hand it to.

Better to be a mule. Mules don't grieve.

* * *

The Tarn district was all rain and old concrete, the oldest quarter of the downwell city, where the colony's founders had built in a hurry and the colony had never gotten around to building anything else on top. Astrid walked the last kilometer because the shrine had no transit stop — grief addresses never did; the licensed couriers' guild had lobbied for that decades ago, on the theory that mourners deserved privacy, and smugglers had been grateful ever since.

The shrine was a converted water cistern, candle-lit, smelling of wax and cold stone. The client was waiting inside, an old woman with a face like a folded map, and she took the core from his hatband with both hands, the way you take an urn.

"You didn't look?" she asked. They always asked. It was a ritual, not a question.

"Never do."

"And it's — " She stopped. Her thumb moved over the core's seam. "It's her voice. The whole last year. The doctors said I could have it transcribed, but a transcription isn't — " She stopped again, and Astrid stood in the candle-smell and waited, because waiting was also part of the service.

"It isn't her," he said, when the silence had gone on long enough. "But it's closer than paper."

The old woman looked at him then, really looked, with the particular X-ray attention of the recently bereaved, who can see straight through everything except their own grief. "You've done this a long time," she said. "Carrying other people's dead."

"Ten years."

"And you've never once been tempted to open one? To hear someone's last year? To know how it ends for somebody?"

Astrid considered the honest answer, which was *every day, and that is exactly why I don't*, and gave the professional one instead. "The cores aren't mine to open."

"No," she agreed softly. "I suppose they aren't." She paid him — overpaid him; the bereaved always did — and then, as he turned to go, she added something that he would think about later, at night, in his anonymous room, when the ledger was closed and the rules had been recited and the dark was doing what the dark did.

"She was in the dream again last night," the old woman said. "My daughter. Everyone on my street is saying it. The children, I mean. They all dreamed the same thing — fire, and ash falling like snow, and a woman singing. My granddaughter woke up crying and said the woman was wearing a grey coat." The old woman's eyes, mild and terrible, rested on his. "Like yours."

Astrid stood very still.

"Children dream strangely," he said. "It's weather. It passes."

"Mm," said the old woman, unconvinced, and lit a candle for her daughter, and let him go.

He walked back out into the Tarn rain with three empty pockets and one full boot, and told himself, in the flat reciting voice he used for rules, that it was nothing. Coincidence. Weather. It passes.

But the core in his boot was warm, and for the first time in ten years, Astrid Quintana noticed the warmth — and wondered, with a cold he hadn't felt since the beginning, what exactly he'd been carrying, all this time, without ever once looking inside.
