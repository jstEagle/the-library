## 1. The Children Who Dream in Unison

The decompression had killed no one, which was the first thing wrong with it.

Oscar Marchetti stood in Bay Nine of the Halcyon Drift and let the numbers argue with each other. Bulkhead 4-C had blown at 0317 station time. Not failed—*blown*, with the clean, decisive violence of a shaped charge, except there was no charge residue, no thermal scarring, no fracture pattern consistent with anything manufactured. The metallurgy report said the hull steel had simply decided, in a forty-centimeter circle, to stop being steel.

He'd filed reports like this for five years. Impossible accidents were his specialty, or rather his assignment: the Meridian Insurance Consortium didn't send investigators to accidents that made sense. They sent Oscar to the ones that would cost them if anyone looked too closely, and his job was to look just closely enough.

"Run the pressure telemetry again," he told the bay's maintenance console.

The console ran it. Same result. Atmospheric pressure in Bay Nine had dropped from standard to vacuum in 0.4 seconds—faster than physics strictly allowed through a hole that size—and then, this was the part Oscar kept replaying, it had *paused*. For 1.2 seconds the bay had held at near-vacuum, pressure flat as a held breath, before equalizing fully.

Pressure doesn't pause. Pressure is water finding its level; it doesn't stop halfway down the hill to think about it.

"Log anomaly," he said, and the console chirped agreement, filing it under the same heading he'd used two hundred times before: *unresolved, low priority, consistent with sensor error.*

It wasn't sensor error. He'd checked the sensors himself, twice, before the salvage crew came through to seal the bay. That was the other thing wrong with the decompression: he'd *wanted* it to be sensor error, and the wanting felt like an old habit wearing his hands.

Oscar Marchetti had been on Halcyon Drift for three years and eleven months. Before that, per his file, he'd worked claims out of Ceres Station. Before *that*, the file offered a polite ellipsis—a gap of indeterminate length that every background system in contested space had agreed not to notice. Gaps like that cost money to build. He'd paid it. He'd spent five years paying for it, one scrubbed record at a time, until Oscar Marchetti was a man with a history so shallow you could see the bottom.

He liked it that way. He'd built the shallowness deliberately, the way you'd dig a pond to hide a well.

His quarters reflected the philosophy: one cot, one desk, one wall screen, nothing personal except a chipped ceramic mug he'd bought new and then aged by hand, because a man with no past needed at least a plausible present. He slept four hours a night. He never dreamed—or he recorded no dreams, which amounted to the same thing in the logs he kept on himself, because yes, he kept logs on himself. A man erasing a history learns to document what remains.

At 1400 station time, the schoolteacher found him in the claims office.

Amara Voss knocked even though the door was open, which told him she'd been raised somewhere with manners, somewhere with seasons. She was maybe thirty-five, dark-skinned, with the particular tiredness of someone who supervised children all day and hadn't yet learned to leave them at the door when she left. She carried a folder the way people carry things they don't want to hold.

"Investigator Marchetti? I'm Amara Voss. I teach at the colony school, tier two." She set the folder on his desk without being invited. "I need to report something, and I don't know who else to take it to."

"Meridian handles property and casualty," Oscar said. "If it's a family matter—"

"It's a dream."

He should have ended the conversation there. Instead he heard himself say, "Whose?"

"All of them." She sat, uninvited now, as if her legs had made the decision independently. "I have twenty-six students between the ages of six and twelve. Nineteen of them have reported the same dream over the past six nights. Identical. Not similar—identical, down to details six-year-olds shouldn't retain. I've taught for nine years, Mr. Marchetti. Children's shared stories drift. They contaminate each other. This hasn't drifted. It's *fixed*, like they're all looking at the same thing."

"What thing?"

She opened the folder. Inside were drawings—crayon, marker, the waxy smears of children working fast, like the images were leaving their heads quicker than their hands could catch them. Oscar turned the top sheet around.

A corridor. Long, curved, ribbed with structural members every few meters. Emergency lighting. And at the far end, filling the frame, a light: green, deep green, almost the green of old instrument panels, rendered with a care that made the hair on his arms rise. The child had drawn sound lines coming off it. Little arcs, like ripples.

"They say it sings," Amara said. "Every one of them. A green light, singing, from dead space. They say it's calling someone home." She paused. "One of my students asked me yesterday why the man in the dream looks sad. I asked what man. She said, 'the one the song is for.'"

The office was very quiet. Somewhere below them, the Drift's ventilation cycled, a sound Oscar had stopped hearing months ago and now heard again, loudly.

"Children share nightmares," he said. "It's called collective reinforcement. There's literature—"

"I've read the literature." Her voice stayed level, but something underneath it didn't. "Mr. Marchetti, last night I dreamed it too. I've never spoken to my students outside class hours. I've never discussed their dreams with another adult. And I dreamed a green light singing from dead space, and I woke up crying, and I don't cry, and I would like very much for you to tell me this is a gas leak or a radiation fluctuation or something else with a form I can file."

Oscar looked at the drawing again. The corridor was drawn from inside it—the perspective of someone standing in the passage, walking toward the light. Children drew what they saw. His mind, trained by five years of fraud analysis, laid out the possibilities in neat rows: contagion, hoax, environmental toxin, mass hysteria. All reasonable. All survivable.

None of them explained why his chest had gone tight the moment he touched the paper.

None of them explained the word that rose in him unbidden, unattached to any memory he owned, arriving the way a name arrives when you've forgotten you knew it:

*Halcyon.*

Not the Drift. The word meant more than the hauler. He could feel the extra weight of it sitting in his mouth like a coin under his tongue.

"Mr. Marchetti?" Amara said.

"The decompression in Bay Nine," he heard himself answer. "The night it happened. Did any of your students sleep badly that night?"

She blinked. "All of them. I sent half of them to the med bay. Why?"

Because the pressure had paused, he thought. Because pressure doesn't pause unless something is listening. Because a hull section doesn't decide to stop being steel unless something on the other side of it wants in—or wants out.

"Nothing," he said. "Routine cross-reference."

It wasn't routine. Nothing about this was routine, and the professional move—the move that kept the shallows shallow—was to close the file, log the anomaly, and let the consortium's quiet machinery bury it. He'd done it two hundred times. He knew exactly how it felt: clean, weightless, forgettable.

Instead he heard himself say, "Leave the drawings. I'll need to interview the children. Individually, starting tomorrow."

Amara Voss studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod slowly, like a person confirming a suspicion she'd been carrying since the door.

"You already know something," she said. "Don't you."

"No," Oscar said, and it was almost true. What he had wasn't knowledge. It was the feeling of standing in a room of his own life and noticing, for the first time, that one wall sounded hollow.

That night he logged no dream. But at 0317 station time—the same minute, his internal clock insisted, though clocks didn't work that way—he woke with the taste of the word *Halcyon* still under his tongue and a green afterimage burned behind his eyes, singing at the edge of hearing, patient as something that had waited a very long time to be answered.

In the morning, nineteen children would tell him about the man the song was for.

He was not ready to hear them describe his own face.
