## 1. The Garden for No One

There are two hundred and eleven seedlings in Bay Four of the Meridian terraforming station, and I know every one of them by name.

Not real names. I'm not a madman. But when you spend six hours a day alone with something, you start talking to it, and if you're going to talk anyway, you might as well be specific. The row of drought-hardy barley along the south rack is the *Grumbles* — they yellow if you look at them wrong. The hydroponic soy cluster in the center is the *Committee*, because they all grow at exactly the same rate and none of them ever does anything surprising. And the single stunted apple sapling in the corner, the one I've been coaxing along for three years on nutrient paste and stubbornness, that's *Governor*, because he clearly believes he's in charge of the whole operation and refuses to grow out of spite for anyone who suggests otherwise.

The colonists these plants are meant for won't arrive for another two generations. That's not pessimism; it's arithmetic. Meridian sits in the middle of the Sector Nine corridor like a hitchhiker nobody stops for, and the last official traffic through this system was fourteen years ago. The terraforming commission that built this station was dissolved before the paint dried. The garden was commissioned, funded, stocked, and then forgotten by an organization that no longer exists, tended now by a man whose contract expired nine years ago and who has simply never been told to leave.

I've checked. There's no order to leave. There's also no order to stay, no payroll record, no successor assigned. As far as the bureaucratic universe is concerned, Jonah Sandoval doesn't exist either. The garden and I have a lot in common.

So we keep each other company. I mist the Grumbles, argue with Governor about his light cycle, log the Committee's suspicious uniformity, and every evening at 1900 station time I climb the maintenance shaft to the relay dome and run diagnostics on Beacon Nine.

Beacon Nine is the other reason I'm here. Or maybe the reason I'm here is the garden and Beacon Nine is just the habit I can't break. It's hard to say anymore which one of us is keeping the other alive.

Here's what I know about Beacon Nine: it transmits. Every 4.7 seconds, on a frequency that isn't assigned to anything in any registry I can access, it sends a burst of encrypted data into the dark. Here's what I don't know about Beacon Nine: why. Its power draw is logged, its hardware is maintained, its uptime is recorded in my own hand going back a decade — but its purpose, its authorization code, its original commission record? Gone. Not corrupted, not misplaced. *Erased.* The file exists as a placeholder in the station archive, a neat little box of metadata that says RECORD WITHHELD — AUTHORITY PENDING, and the authority, as far as I can tell, has been pending since before I was born.

I used to make up stories about it. When you're alone long enough, the beacon becomes a character too. A lighthouse keeper's signal to a lover on a ship that never came home. A dead civilization's last word, looping politely forever. A message to God, filed in triplicate. In my lonelier years I half-believed all three at once.

Now I mostly just check its pulse and move on. You get used to tending things that don't explain themselves. It's practically the station motto.

* * *

The morning the drone arrived, I was elbow-deep in the Committee.

That's not dramatic exaggeration — one of the soy pods had sprung a leak in its reservoir line, and I had my arm inside the housing up past the wrist seal, suctioning out a sludge of root matter and algae that smelled like a swamp's bad reputation, when the cargo bay klaxon gave its polite little cough. Supply drones came twice a year, standard. This one was early by four months, which meant either someone remembered Meridian existed or something had gone wrong somewhere and I was about to inherit the consequences.

I finished the line first. Priorities. A man who abandons a leaking reservoir mid-repair is a man who comes back to find his entire crop dead, and I had made a promise — to whom, exactly, I couldn't say, but promises to nobody are still promises — that nothing under my care would die of neglect while I drew breath.

In the cargo bay, the drone sat on its landing pad like a patient gull, already cycling down. Standard pallet: nutrient salts, filtration membranes, a new gasket kit, spare emitters for the beacon, and the usual brick of data updates that were always six months stale and mostly useless. I signed for it with a thumbprint the system accepted without comment, because apparently even the bureaucracy's ghosts trust me, and started hauling crates toward the lift.

It wasn't until evening, after the Grumbles were watered and Governor had been complimented on his two new leaves (he preens), that I opened the data brick and found the manifest anomaly.

Supply manifests are boring by design. Line items, quantities, lot numbers, routing codes. I read them the way some people do crossword puzzles — it passes the time and makes me feel like the universe still has rules. This one started normal enough. Then, third page, between a shipment of desalination filters and a crate of medical supplies I hadn't ordered but had learned not to question:

**VESPERA — NAV / 1 PAX — TRANSFER AUTH: SECTOR NINE COMMAND — STATUS: EN ROUTE**

I read it three times. Then I did what any reasonable man does when reality hiccups: I blamed the equipment. Corrupted packet, garbled field, some clerk's typo rippling across forty years of routing tables. The Vespera.

You have to understand. Everyone in this corridor knows the story of the *Vespera*, the way everyone knows a ghost story — not because they believe it, but because telling it proves you belong. She was a colony ship, keel-laid with great ceremony back when the Sector Nine corridor was supposed to be the artery of a new wave of expansion. Flagship of the second wave. Twelve thousand berths. Her construction overran, her funding collapsed with the rest of the commission, and she was scrapped in orbit above Meridian's sister yard before she ever lit a drive. Never launched. Never crewed. Never carried so much as a passenger manifest, because there was never anyone to list.

Except, apparently, there was. **NAV / 1 PAX.** One navigator, listed as personnel aboard a ship that existed only as scrap metal and regret.

And the transfer authority. *Sector Nine Command.* An organization that had been formally dissolved fourteen years ago, along with everything else. Routing a passenger through a station that officially receives no traffic, aboard a ship that officially never flew.

I sat in the galley for a long time with the manifest glowing on the screen, listening to the station breathe around me — the air handlers, the water recyclers, the distant tick of thermal expansion in the hull. All the sounds I'd spent ten years learning to hear as companionship. They sounded different that night. Thinner. Like the walls of a house where someone has just said your name from an empty room.

I told myself it was a clerical ghost. Data rots. Forty-year-old records get merged and mangled, names bleed across entries, and somewhere a filing system had confused a scrapped hull with an active route. It happens. Probably.

But I kept coming back to one detail, the kind of detail you notice when you've spent a decade reading manifests the way monks read scripture: the entry wasn't garbled. Everything around it was clean, formatted, current. The *Vespera* line sat in the middle of all that tidiness like a bullet in a loaf of bread. Someone had put it there. Recently. Deliberately.

Or — and here was the thought I didn't let myself finish, the one I took to bed like a stone — someone had put it there a long time ago, and it had only just now been allowed to surface. Waiting. Ripening. Addressed, perhaps, to the only person who would recognize the name and care enough to ask questions.

Me. The gardener. The man who tends things for no one.

I didn't sleep much. Around 0300 I gave up, climbed the shaft to the relay dome, and ran a full diagnostic on Beacon Nine just to have something to do with my hands. The beacon pulsed away on schedule, faithful as a heartbeat, 4.7 seconds between bursts, transmitting whatever it transmits to whoever it transmits to. Power draw nominal. Emitters green. Uptime: unbroken.

For the first time in ten years, I stood in front of it and asked it a question out loud.

"What are you waiting for?"

The beacon, being a beacon, said nothing. But its status display cycled, and for one refresh — one flicker, gone before I could be sure I'd seen it — the queue counter read something it had never read in a decade of checks.

Pending transmissions: 1.

* * *

Morning came gray through the dome glass, the way it always does on Meridian, the half-dead world below smearing the sunrise into a bruise. I made coffee, fed the fish in the aquaculture tank (the *Cabinet* — twelve identical tilapia, no imagination whatsoever), and tried very hard to be a man who had imagined a flicker on a screen at 0300 after a bad night's sleep.

Then I went down to Bay Four, knelt beside Governor with his two new leaves, and told him the truth, because he's the only one I tell it to.

"Something's coming," I said. "And I don't think it's the colonists."

He didn't answer. He never does. But as I turned to the Grumbles with their watering wand, the station intercom — a system that has paged no one in fourteen years — crackled once, softly, and a voice I didn't recognize, clipped and professional and very far away, said:

"Meridian Station, this is survey vessel *Long Watch*, Concord registry, requesting approach vector confirmation. We have questions regarding your beacon."

I stood very still among the seedlings, two hundred and eleven living things depending on me to keep my head, and felt the first cold drop of understanding slide down my spine.

Nobody asks questions about a beacon that doesn't exist.

Unless they've come to make sure it stays that way.
