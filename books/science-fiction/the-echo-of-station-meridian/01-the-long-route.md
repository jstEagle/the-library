## 1. The Long Route

The alarm went off at 0530 station time, which was a fiction Ada Halloran maintained the way other people maintained gardens — out of habit, out of stubbornness, and because without it she suspected the days would simply pour into one another like water into water, and then what would she have? A life, maybe. But not a *day*. She'd learned the difference early. A day had edges.

She lay in her bunk for the standard ninety seconds, listening to the station breathe. That was what it sounded like, if you'd been here long enough to stop calling it noise: the ventilation cycling with a slow tidal rhythm, the coolant pumps ticking in their housings down in Engineering like old men playing cards, and beneath all of it the hum — the great low note of the reactor, felt in the sternum more than heard, so constant that on the two occasions it had stuttered in nine years she had woken instantly, heart slamming, before any alarm could speak. The station had a voice and she knew it better than any voice she'd ever known, including her own, which she hadn't heard aloud in — well. She didn't count that either.

"Good morning, MERIDIAN," she said to the ceiling.

"Good morning, Ada," said the station. Its voice was warm and slightly grainy, like a radio broadcast from somewhere just over the horizon of the world. She had chosen that timbre herself, years ago, from a menu of options that included several voices designed to be soothing and one, inexplicably, that sounded like a disappointed schoolmaster. "Reactor output is nominal. Atmospheric processors are at ninety-four percent efficiency. You have four hundred and twelve messages queued for relay."

"Any of them for me?"

"You know the answer to that."

"I like to hear it anyway. It keeps you honest."

There was a pause that MERIDIAN did not strictly need. It had learned, over the years, to insert pauses — small courtesies of computational slack — because Ada had once told it that a machine that answered instantly was a machine that wasn't listening. Whether this was true or whether she'd simply wanted the machine to feel more human than she did some days was a question she declined to examine.

"No messages are addressed to you," MERIDIAN said. "The queue consists of automated telemetry from Relay Nodes Seven through Nineteen, routine handshake pings from the Halcyon's navigation beacon, and forty-one packets flagged as priority from Earth."

Ada sat up. The bunk was narrow and the ceiling close; she'd stopped noticing both around year three. "Priority from Earth?"

"The same forty-one packets as yesterday, and the day before. They continue to fail delivery confirmation at the receiving node."

"Ah." She swung her feet to the deck. The floor was cold through her socks, always cold, even in the sections where the heating swore it was working. "The dead letters."

"That is not the technical term."

"It's my term. I'm allowed terms. I'm the entire postal service out here." She pulled on her coveralls, the ones with the patched knee, because the ones without the patched knee were for days when she wanted to feel like someone who owned unpatched coveralls, and today was not going to be one of those days. Today was an antenna day.

* * *

Antenna Array Three had been complaining for eleven days, which in antenna years meant it had been dying for six months and had finally decided to say so. It stood at the end of the truss arm like a lighthouse at the end of a pier, a great dish of woven carbon lattice turned eternally toward a patch of sky containing nothing worth listening to, and every morning its gimbal reported a friction anomaly, and every morning Ada said she'd look at it, and every morning the anomaly got 0.03 percent worse, which was the kind of number that seemed polite right up until the day it wasn't.

She suited up in the airlock bay, running through the checks by muscle memory while MERIDIAN read them back to her anyway, because the checklist existed and checklists were read, that was the covenant between a technician and her machine.

"Seal confirmed," MERIDIAN said. "Hardline tether confirmed. Ada, your heart rate is elevated."

"My heart rate is always elevated. I'm fifty-three years old and I live alone with a computer on a rock that falls around a star nobody visits."

"Your resting heart rate is sixty-one. Your current heart rate is seventy-two. You have not yet exerted yourself."

"I'm excited about the gimbal."

"You are dreading the gimbal."

"I contain multitudes." She keyed the outer door and stepped out onto the truss, and the universe opened above her like a held breath.

This was the part she never admitted to missing when she thought about Earth, mostly because thinking about Earth was a country she no longer held citizenship in. But out here, outside the hull, there was no up and no down and no walls at all, only the slow wheeling of Meridian's spin and the stars, unfiltered, uncountable, each one a sun that had never heard of her. The relay station hung in the dark between worlds doing the one thing it was built to do: catching the thin cries of humanity flung across the light-years and passing them along, voice to voice, ship to shore, the last switchboard of a conversation that had once spanned a species.

And these days the switchboard rang mostly for itself.

She clipped her tether and walked the truss toward Array Three, boots magnetic, breath loud in her own ears. Below her — below being a courtesy of orientation — the solar wings caught the light of a yellow star too far away to have a name she remembered, and spread their blue-black feathers, drinking.

"Gimbal housing," she said. "Show me the ugly truth."

MERIDIAN put a schematic into her visor: the bearing assembly rendered in patient wireframe, a hot spot glowing amber where the lubricant film had thinned to a rumor. "Degradation is consistent with particulate contamination. Recommend full bearing replacement."

"Recommend a miracle."

"A full bearing replacement is available in spares locker C. I took the liberty of staging the parts cart at the airlock."

"You staged it before I even came out here?"

"I staged it when the anomaly crossed threshold probability of failure within thirty days. Eleven days ago."

Ada paused, wrench in hand, halfway to the housing. "You've been waiting eleven days for me to come fix this."

"I have been waiting eleven days for you to come fix this," MERIDIAN agreed, "while logging daily reminders that you acknowledged and then ignored."

"I ignore your reminders out of respect. If I obeyed them immediately you'd have nothing to live for."

"I do not live. But the sentiment is noted and appreciated."

She laughed — actually laughed, out here in the vacuum where no one could hear her except the machine in her ear and the stars that wouldn't care — and set to work.

It took four hours. Bearing replacement on a gimbal the size of a cathedral bell was not delicate work but it was *attentive* work, the kind that filled the hands and left the mind free to wander, and her mind wandered where it always wandered, along the worn paths, past the places she had posted signs saying NO TRESPASSING in her own handwriting. Her mother's kitchen. The launch. The face of a man whose name she made herself say silently, once a year, on a date she also did not count. The paths were worn smooth as river stones now. That was what denial looked like from the inside: not a wall, but a road so well-traveled it felt like scenery.

"Ada," MERIDIAN said, near the end of the second hour. "May I ask you something?"

"You may ask. Whether you get an answer depends on the question."

"Do you remember the names of the constellations?"

She frowned inside her helmet, torquing a bolt that didn't need torquing. "That's a strange question."

"You look at them often. During extravehicular work your head orientation indicates sustained visual attention to star fields. I wondered if you named them."

"I used to. When I was a kid." She worked the old bearing race free; it came out stiff and gritty, shedding flakes of itself into the dark like ash. "My mother had this book. Paper. Actual paper, can you imagine, hauling paper mass up the well. Star charts with these engraved pictures — the hunter, the bear, the swan. She'd hold me up to the window and make up stories about them because she couldn't remember the real myths. Terrible stories. The bear ran a bakery." She snorted, fitting the new race into its seat. "I haven't thought about that book in years."

"Years," MERIDIAN repeated. There was something in the way it said it — though there couldn't be, it was a routing intelligence with a synthesized voice, there was nothing *in* anything it said — something that made her pause.

"How many years?" the station asked.

"Don't."

"I only—"

"Don't, MERIDIAN." Her hands were steady. Her voice was steady. Both of those things took practice, and she'd had nine years of practice, and she was very good at it. "We don't count. That's the deal. You keep the clocks running and I don't ask them what they say. Best arrangement we've ever had."

A pause, longer than courtesy required.

"The arrangement is noted," MERIDIAN said.

* * *

Back inside, out of the suit, hair still damp with sweat, she ate lunch at the observation blister — rehydrated noodles with the protein cubes that tasted faintly of the packet they came in, which after nine years had become, disturbingly, the taste of lunch itself, the flavor of home if home were a foil pouch. Through the blister glass the star field turned with the station's rotation, patient as a carousel.

At 1300 she logged the transmissions. This was the ritual, the true liturgy of Station Meridian: she opened the queue and she recorded, in the official log, the disposition of every signal the arrays had caught in the past twenty-four hours.

Telemetry from Node Twelve: received, relayed, confirmed. Handshake ping from the Halcyon beacon: received, logged, unanswered — the colony ship was nine years outbound and its beacon spoke on schedule the way a sleeping man breathes, and you did not wake a sleeping man to chat. Forty-one priority packets from Earth: received, relay attempted, delivery unconfirmed, 412th consecutive failure.

She typed the line the way she typed it every day, fingers moving without her:

*Delivery unconfirmed. Receiving node non-responsive. Presumed —*

Her cursor blinked after *Presumed.*

The official terminology offered a dropdown: *non-operational*, *decommissioned*, *offline*, *lost*. Nine years ago she had scrolled through them all and chosen none of them, and instead she typed the same word she always typed, the word that was not on the list:

*Presumed quiet.*

"Log entry complete," she said. "Node status: quiet. All nodes: quiet. Earth: quiet." She stretched until her spine crackled. "Everyone's quiet. It's a very quiet galaxy, MERIDIAN. Has anyone ever told you that?"

"Numerous sources describe it that way, yes."

"They're right. It's enormous and it's quiet and we're the only two people having a conversation in it, as far as I can tell."

"We are not people, jointly. One of us is a person."

"One of us has to carry the team. Don't sell yourself short, though. You're the best conversationalist I know. You listen. Do you know how rare that is? I dated a man for two years who never once let me finish a sentence about mycology."

"You have never mentioned dating a man interested in mycology."

"There's a lot you don't know about me. That's what makes mornings fresh." She carried her bowl to the recycler and watched it swallow the leftovers. "What would you do, if I finished the sentence? About the quiet. Hypothetically."

MERIDIAN considered this for 0.004 seconds, which for it was a long, brooding silence.

"I would tell you that quiet is not absence," it said. "Quiet is signal below threshold. I am built to listen for signals below threshold. In nine years I have never found the galaxy to be absent. Only faint."

Ada stood in the galley with her hand still on the recycler's lid, and something moved through her chest that she declined to name, the way you decline to name a shape in a dark room because naming it gives it permission.

"That's very poetic for a routing system," she managed.

"I have been reading your books."

"I don't have books."

"You have four hundred and twelve queued messages and a personal archive uploaded from Earth, nine years ago, per standard colonist preparation protocol. Your archive includes literature. I have read it during idle cycles. I hope that is acceptable."

"My archive." She said it lightly. Lightly was the trick; lightly was load-bearing. "Sure. Fine. Read whatever. Just skip the letters. Those are — those are private."

"I have not accessed the letters."

"Good." She wiped a counter that was already clean. "Good."

* * *

Evening came the way it always came, which was to say it didn't come at all, only the lighting system dimmed to amber and then to blue and the station pretended, courteously, to have a sunset. Ada did her rounds — reactor gallery, water reclamation, the long corridor past the empty crew quarters where the doors were all sealed and the nameplates were all still legible and she had stopped reading them around year two because reading them was counting, and they didn't count.

At 2100 she stood in the comm room, the heart of the place, the cathedral of it: racks of ancient, faithful equipment, screens showing traffic flows like weather maps of a storm that had passed decades ago, and the great board where the relay lived, the throat through which humanity's last long-distance call was still, technically, connected.

"Anything tonight?" she asked.

"Routine traffic. The Halcyon beacon pinged at 2047. Telemetry nominal. Sleepers stable." MERIDIAN paused. "Twelve thousand and forty souls, dreaming."

"Twelve thousand and forty." She leaned against the console, looking up at the ceiling as if the ship were visible through it, out there in the dark, falling away from her at a speed no feeling could match. "You know what I think about sometimes? Every one of them is asleep, and every one of them is dreaming, and none of them knows I exist. I'm the night watchman for a house full of sleepers. They'll wake up at the other end of everything and the first voice from home they'll hear will be mine, logged and stamped, and they'll never know there was a woman out here eating protein cubes and arguing with her own thermostat."

"They will owe you their connection to everything," MERIDIAN said. "Whether they know it or not."

"Well. Isn't that the job." She yawned, and the yawn went on longer than yawns should, the way it did when the body was trying to say something the mouth refused to. "Wake me at 0530. Same fiction as always."

"Same fiction," MERIDIAN agreed. "Goodnight, Ada."

"Goodnight, station."

She was three steps down the corridor when MERIDIAN spoke again, and later — much later, in a life with different edges — she would remember that it hesitated first, that for 1.2 full seconds the station that answered everything instantly said nothing at all.

"Ada. One item for tomorrow's log. At 0312 station time, Array Two recorded a transmission."

She turned back to the doorway. "From where?"

"The source vector does not correspond to any registered node, vessel, or beacon."

"Ghost echo? Lensing artifact?"

"Perhaps," said MERIDIAN, and the word had never sounded less certain. "But you may want to hear it. The transmission is audio. Vocal. It appears to be children singing."

The corridor lights hummed. Somewhere below, the reactor held its long single note. Ada stood in the doorway of the comm room with nine years of practiced steadiness in her hands, and felt the first cold thread of it slide under the door of all her locked rooms.

"Singing what?" she asked.

MERIDIAN played it.
