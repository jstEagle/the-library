## 1. The Long Route

The station woke Noor the way it always did: not with an alarm, but with a sound. A low, patient hum that ran through the deck plates and up through the frame of her cot, as if Kuiper Line itself were clearing its throat and saying, *morning, technician. Still here? Good. Me too.*

She lay still for a moment in the grey half-light of her bunk alcove, listening to it. Nine years, and she had never quite decided whether the hum was comforting or accusatory. Most mornings she settled on comforting. Some mornings—like this one, which had arrived too early and too cold—it felt like a landlord knocking on the door of a tenant who was already awake, thanks very much.

"Morning, KESH," she said to the ceiling, because the ceiling was where the nearest sensor lived, and because talking to sensors was one of the few luxuries of being the only person aboard.

"Good morning, Technician Villalobos," said KESH-9, from everywhere and nowhere, in its mild, unhurried voice. "Station time is 0547. Exterior temperature is minus two hundred and twenty-one degrees Celsius. All systems nominal."

"All systems nominal," Noor repeated, swinging her legs out of the bunk. "You say that every morning."

"I say it every morning because it is true every morning."

"One of these days I'd like you to open with something different. 'Good morning, Noor. The stars are looking particularly dramatic today.' Something with a little poetry."

There was a pause of exactly the length KESH-9 used when it was deciding whether humor required a response. "The stars are not visible from inside the station," it said. "But I have noted your preference."

"You've noted my preference for nine years."

"Yes."

"And done nothing about it."

"The stars remain not visible from inside the station."

Noor laughed into the dark, pulled on her thermal underlayer, and went to make coffee that tasted like regret and hot water. The synthesizer's coffee cartridge had been due for replacement four years ago; the replacement had been due three years after that; and now the machine produced something that no longer pretended to be coffee but had instead become its own beverage, a kind of bitter brown companionship she had grown genuinely fond of. She drank it standing at the little viewport in the galley, looking out at nothing.

That was the thing about the Kuiper Line, she thought, watching the slow wheel of frozen light beyond the glass. There was no *out there* worth looking at. Not really. The Line ran along the ragged outer edge of the settled system, a chain of relay stations strung like beads on a wire too long for anyone to want, each one listening to the faint signals of colonies that had gone quiet decades ago. Her job was to keep the beads polished so the wire could carry what little traffic remained. She logged the dead worlds. She patched the failing systems. She kept the lights on in a house nobody visited.

It should have been grim. Everyone back on Earth—the Earth she remembered, warm and loud and full of people who touched your arm when they laughed—had told her it would be grim. *Nine years alone? You'll go strange, Villalobos.* And maybe she had gone strange. But strange, she had discovered, was mostly just quiet with better posture.

She rinsed her cup, clipped it to the drying rack, and began the circuit.

* * *

The Long Route, she called it—not because it was long, though it was, eleven kilometers of corridor if you walked every spur, but because it took all day and deserved a name with some dignity. Every technician before her had walked it. She knew this because their footsteps were, in a sense, still in the floor: the deck plating along the main spine had been replaced twice, and both times the wear pattern came back identical, a soft pale lane down the center of each corridor where forty people had once walked and one person walked now.

She started aft, as always, at Relay Array Three, because Array Three was the oldest and the most temperamental and she liked to deal with it while she still had patience to spend.

Array Three hung off the station's flank like a ear pressed to the skin of space, and inside its maintenance ring the temperature dropped ten degrees and the air smelled of ozone and old insulation. Noor pulled up the overnight logs on her wrist display and scrolled through them with the easy contempt of long familiarity.

"Let's see what you did this time," she murmured.

Array Three had, overnight, lost contact with Colony Relay Vess-7 for the eleventh time that quarter. Vess-7 had gone silent nineteen years ago—its last transmission a routine agricultural report, cheerful as anything, right up until it wasn't—and since then the array had developed a habit of reaching for it anyway, like an old man dialing a number he knows is disconnected.

"You can't keep doing this," Noor told the array, patting its housing the way you'd pat a horse. "Vess-7 is gone. We've been through this. I've logged it. It's in the file. The file is very thorough."

The array, being an array, said nothing, but its status light blinked amber in a way she chose to interpret as contrition.

She reset the handshake protocol, re-seated the coupling that always worked loose in the cold, and logged the fault in her own handwriting on the paper tablet she kept for exactly this purpose, because typing it into the system felt like lying and writing it by hand felt like keeping a diary. *Day 3,287. Vess-7 unreachable again. Coupling reseated. Talked to the machinery again. Machinery unresponsive. As usual.*

KESH-9 would see the digital log anyway. The paper was for her.

From Array Three she moved forward along the spine, and this was the part of the route she loved and hated in equal measure: the Gallery, the long central corridor where the station's original designers had put the windows. Forty windows, one for each of the forty staff the station had been built for, each berth and office positioned so that someone, somewhere, could look out at the dark while they worked. Now the windows looked out at the dark for an audience of one, and Noor had taken to greeting them individually, the way you'd greet neighbors.

"Morning, Window Four," she said, passing the first one. "Still nothing out there. Still gorgeous about it."

Window Four framed a view of nothing in particular—a smear of distant sun, a scatter of ice-light—and Noor gave it a nod and moved on. At Window Eleven she stopped, because Window Eleven was where she kept her habit.

The habit was this: the station's archives held the recorded voices of everyone who had ever served aboard. Maintenance calls, shift reports, personal messages home, the endless chatter of a working station—all of it stored, indexed, preserved by regulation, and listened to by absolutely no one except Noor. Regulation said the archive must be maintained. Nothing said it must be silent. So Noor played it while she worked, quietly, like radio, like company.

This morning she queued up a shift report from eleven years ago, Technician Okafor, whose voice was deep and unhurried and who had once, in the middle of a coolant pressure log, paused to say, "And note for the record that Marta's bread recipe is criminally good and she is withholding it, which I consider a workplace violation."

Noor smiled at Window Eleven. "Good morning, Okafor."

*Note for the record,* said the dead man's voice, warm in her earpiece, *that Marta's bread recipe is criminally good.*

"I know," she said. "I've read the complaint. You filed it three times."

He had, too. She'd checked. Somewhere in the archive there were three separate formal complaints regarding bread, each more exasperated than the last, and a final addendum in which Marta herself had entered the record to say that the recipe was her grandmother's and would be pried from her cold, dead hands. Marta had left the station alive, at end of rotation, eight years before Noor arrived. Noor liked to think she was somewhere warm, still withholding the recipe on principle.

She walked on through the Gallery with Okafor's shift report playing softly, his footsteps and hers separated by a decade and sharing the same corridor, and if anyone had been there to see her they might have found it sad. But no one was there to see her, which was rather the point, and besides, it wasn't sad. It was—she'd settled on this word years ago and defended it against all comers, including herself at three in the morning—*companionable*. The archive talked. She answered. Neither of them expected anything from the other. It was the easiest friendship she'd ever had.

* * *

Mid-morning brought her to Hydroponics Bay Two, which was less a farm than a stubbornness. The bay had been designed to feed forty; it now fed one, and even that was generous, since Noor had long ago let most of the racks go fallow and kept only the beds she actually liked: potatoes, because they were reliable; a row of chili plants, because she refused to let the war—or whatever it was that had emptied the sky—take chili peppers from her too; and one tomato vine of legendary temperament that produced perhaps six fruits a year and acted, the entire rest of the time, like it was doing her a personal favor.

"Morning, your majesty," she said to the tomato vine, checking its moisture probe. The probe read fine. The vine nevertheless drooped one leaf in a manner suggesting imminent death. "Oh, don't start. I checked you yesterday. You're fine. You're drama in plant form, is what you are."

The vine drooped further.

"Nope." She adjusted the grow-lamp anyway, a quarter degree, because she was weak, and the vine perked up instantly, vindicated. "Unbelievable. Nine years and you've got me trained."

From Hydroponics she climbed the ladder shaft to the thermal level, where the day's real work waited: a coolant pump on the north loop had been developing a harmonic whine for two weeks, rising slowly up the scale like a singer warming up for a note Noor did not want to hear. She'd ordered the parts from the fabricator, and the fabricator had promised them for today, and the fabricator's promises were reliable in the way that weather is reliable—eventually, technically, in some form.

The whine, when she opened the access panel, had reached a pitch that made her back teeth itch.

"Oh, we're doing *this* today," she said. "Fine. Fine! Let's get you sorted before you hit opera."

Pump work was her favorite kind of work, if she was honest, which she tried not to be too often. It was work with an answer in it. You isolated the bearing, you swapped the assembly, you torqued the mounts to spec, and when you were done the world was measurably, provably quieter. No ambiguity. No nine-year silences. Just a bad thing made good by hands that knew how.

Her hands knew how. That was the strange gift of the whole arrangement—she'd come to the station already knowing everything, or nearly, her training so complete that the first year had felt less like learning a job than remembering one. KESH-9 had commented on it once, early on, in its mild way: *Your proficiency ratings exceed your service record.* And Noor had shrugged and said she'd studied hard, and KESH-9 had accepted this, because KESH-9 accepted almost everything she said, which was either trust or indifference, and after nine years she'd decided not to examine which.

The new bearing slid home with a click she felt in her palms. She spun the pump by hand, listened, nodded.

"There we go." She wiped her hands on her coveralls, leaving the grease streaks she'd leave tomorrow in the same places, a palimpsest of labor. "Much better. You're welcome."

*Coolant pump north loop restored to nominal,* KESH-9 observed overhead.

"See, *that's* the poetry I'm talking about. Say it like you mean it."

*I always mean it.*

* * *

Lunch was synth-protein and the last of the real rice, eaten in the galley with the archive playing on the room speakers instead of her earpiece, because lunch was when she let the voices be louder. Today it was a mess-hall recording from twelve years ago: forty people eating, arguing about a film, someone's fork ringing against a plate, laughter breaking over all of it like weather. Noor ate her rice and listened to a room that no longer existed fill with noise.

She had asked KESH-9 once, early on, why the archive kept everything. Storage was cheap, sure, but regulations decayed; someone should have purged the personal messages years ago.

*The archive is designated a heritage record,* KESH-9 had said. *Wartime directive. All station records are to be preserved intact until reviewed by an authorized officer.*

"Has anyone ever reviewed it?"

*No authorized officer has presented themselves.*

"So it just... keeps everything. Forever. Waiting."

*Yes.*

She'd thought about that for a long time afterward—the image of the station holding every voice it had ever heard, patiently, indefinitely, like a dog waiting at a door—and she'd decided it wasn't morbid. It was faithful. Somebody had to remember how the place sounded when it was full. If the job fell to her, well. She'd had worse jobs. She'd had this job, actually, and it was fine.

After lunch she made the outward leg of the route: the antenna galleries, the comm bays, the long cold spur to Array One where the big dish turned its slow blind attention toward the inner system. This was the quietest part of the station and the part where she did her least necessary work—calibrations that didn't need calibrating, inspections of equipment that hadn't failed in a decade. She did them anyway, thoroughly, because the route was the shape of her days, and a shape needs all its edges.

At Array One's control niche she ran the standard sweep: signal inventory, noise floor, channel integrity. The same list as yesterday. The same list as the day before. Dead colony relays logging their silence. Carrier beacons ticking like clocks in empty houses. The ordinary static of a sky with almost no one left to talk in it.

She was halfway down the checklist, humming along with a supply-manifest readout from nine years ago in a voice she particularly liked—a young tech named Ibarra who narrated inventory counts like sports commentary (*"—and that's four hundred and six spare gaskets, folks, four-oh-six, somebody stop her—"*)—when the sweep flagged something.

Just a flicker. A line of text at the bottom of the noise report, small and grey: *Transient anomaly, band 7, duration 0.4 seconds, source unresolved.*

Noor stared at it.

Anomalies happened. Cosmic background burps, solar wind gusts, the array's own aging electronics dreaming out loud. In nine years she'd logged hundreds, and every single one had resolved into something mundane the moment she dug into it. She knew the drill. She'd written the drill.

But this one sat oddly. Band 7 was a deep, old slice of spectrum—legacy frequencies, the kind the first colonial ships had used, the kind modern traffic had abandoned decades ago. Nobody broadcast there anymore. Nobody *could*, really; the equipment barely existed outside museums.

"Huh," she said aloud, to the niche, to Ibarra's ghost, to the dish turning its slow circles overhead.

She reran the sweep. The anomaly was gone, of course. Anomalies were shy like that. She widened the window, extended the integration time, and let the array listen harder into the dark while she finished the checklist, telling herself it was nothing, knowing she'd check it again tonight, knowing she'd check it again tomorrow, because that was the job: you kept the lights on, and you noticed the things that flickered, because once—so the histories said, so the war's long shadow said—somebody hadn't noticed, and it had cost everything.

The sweep completed. Nothing. Clean static, the whole familiar catalogue of silence.

"Probably nothing," she told the array, and patted its housing, and moved on down the corridor toward the evening leg of the route, her footsteps falling into the pale worn lane in the floor, the archive murmuring in her ears, the hum of the station steady beneath it all like a heartbeat she'd borrowed.

Behind her, in the noise floor of band 7, in a language extinct for two hundred years, something waited patiently to be heard again.
