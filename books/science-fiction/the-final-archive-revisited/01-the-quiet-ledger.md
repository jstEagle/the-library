## 1. The Quiet Ledger

*Entry 4,112. Meridian Colony, local day-cycle. Hydroponics Bay Three: lettuce at sixty percent yield and pretending otherwise. Bay Four: the tomatoes have unionized against me and lost. Weather: the same weather we always have, which is to say the weather we paid for in installments and are still paying off.*

*Population: fewer than yesterday.*

Zephyrine Ishikawa set down his stylus, flexed his fingers, and looked out through the observation glass at the colony he had been quietly burying for eleven years.

Meridian did not die dramatically. That was the thing nobody on Earth understood, if anyone on Earth still thought about Meridian at all, which they didn't. Colonies in the old stories died in fire, in plague, in glorious mutiny against distant masters. Meridian was dying the way a house dies when the last person who loved it moves away — slowly, room by room, with the lights left on out of habit until one day someone notices the habit has no owner.

He picked up the stylus again.

*The last school closed today. Officially it "consolidated," which is the administrative word for a funeral where everyone pretends the deceased went on a trip. Nine students were bussed — walked, actually; the bus died in year six and we keep it as a planter now — nine students walked to the community hall, where they will be taught by Ms. Ferreira and a curriculum that ends abruptly at the history of the Silence Wars, because that is where our database ends, because that is where Earth's interest in us ended.*

*I attended for an hour. The children asked me why I take notes all the time. I told them I am writing a book. One of them, little Anouk Voss, age seven, asked if it would have pictures. I said yes. She said good, because books without pictures are just homework. Out of the mouths of babes, etc. She is correct and I resent her for it.*

He smiled at that — a small, private smile, the kind you make when the only witness is a window and the window has seen everything already.

Eleven years of entries. Four thousand mornings of walking the same corridors, counting the same failing things, recording the slow arithmetic of abandonment. He knew the colony the way a doctor knows a patient they've treated too long: every scar had a date, every tremor a prognosis.

The hydroponics first. He made his rounds the way he always did, clipboard under his arm like a talisman, though everything worth knowing lived in his head and the clipboard was mostly theater he performed for himself.

Bay Three's grow-lights flickered in their arrhythmic way — not broken, exactly, but stuttering, like an old man humming a song he only half remembered. The nutrient pumps wheezed. Zephyrine had rebuilt them twice with scavenged parts from the maintenance levels, and each rebuild bought them maybe eight months. He'd stopped telling the council the real numbers years ago. Not lying, exactly. Just choosing which truths to say out loud, which was, he suspected, the entire job of being alive.

"Morning, Zeph," said Dara Okoye from the seed vault, not looking up from her trays. She was seventy-one and had been the colony's chief botanist since before he existed, in either sense of the word.

"Morning, Dara. Your basil is staging a coup."

"My basil can try." She snorted. "Everything else around here has given up. Let the basil have its ambitions."

"How is your knee?"

"Lying to me. Like everything else on this rock." But she said it warmly, and patted his arm when he passed, and that pat was one of perhaps six remaining gestures of affection in the entire colony, and he catalogued it somewhere behind his ribs without meaning to.

That was the thing about being unnoticed: people forgot you were there, but they also forgot to stop loving you. Dara had known the original Zephy. She greeted the copy with the same warmth, never quite looking closely enough to see the seams. Or perhaps she saw them and chose kindness over curiosity. He had never decided which explanation hurt more.

* * *

At midday he walked the residential rings, doing his census.

It took less time every year. Ring Two was down to forty-one occupied units. Ring Three, thirty-eight. The corridors smelled of recycled air and old paint and, faintly, of the algae scrubbers working overtime in the walls — a green, pondish smell that newcomers found unpleasant and that Zephyrine found indescribably comforting, because it meant the walls were still trying.

He passed the school on his way back. It stood dark, its door sealed with a magnetic lock that hummed softly, guarding nothing. Someone had taped a child's drawing to the inside of the window months ago — a crayon sun over a crayon colony, everyone smiling, the hydroponics rendered as enormous glowing mushrooms. Zephyrine had photographed it for the archive. In his ledger he described it as *evidence of optimism, medium: crayon, condition: fading,* and then sat with the description for a long time before adding, *may be the most important document in this collection.*

His footsteps echoed in the empty corridor. He counted them out of habit. Eleven years ago there had been other footsteps to lose his among. Now his own came back to him like an answer to a question he hadn't asked.

Here is what the ledger did not say, what no entry ever said directly, because he had learned early that some facts must be approached sideways or not at all:

Zephyrine Ishikawa was not the first Zephyrine Ishikawa.

He was the second. Grown, gestated, and decanted fourteen years ago in the medical bay of a colony that officially had no cloning capability, from tissue samples that officially should not have existed, carrying memories that officially could not be transferred — and yet here they were, folded into him like letters into envelopes: twenty-nine years of another man's life, ending abruptly on a night he could remember with perfect clarity and no understanding.

He remembered the original's last evening. The taste of tea going cold. The particular squeak of the third stair in the archive annex. A knock at the door that came too late in the night to be friendly. And then — nothing. A gap shaped exactly like a murder, smooth-edged, surgical, as if someone had reached into the memory transfer and cut the final minutes away with a very fine instrument.

Fourteen years, and he had never found the missing minutes. He had stopped looking, mostly. Mostly.

The colony believed he was the original, returned from a research posting off-world. It was a kind lie arranged by the few who knew — Sefa Okonkwo, then a junior administrator with a talent for paperwork and a conscience she kept well-hidden under efficiency; old Dr. Amara, since dead, who had done the growing and never spoke of it. The lie had held for fourteen years because nobody looked closely at Meridian anymore. Nobody looked closely at anything here. That was the colony's curse and, lately, Zephyrine's shelter.

To be forgotten by Earth was to be safe from Earth. He had built his whole quiet life inside that logic, brick by brick, entry by entry.

*Entry addendum, 4,112:* he wrote that evening, in the small archive office that had been the original's and was now, by a sleight of hand nobody examined, his. *Census complete. School closed. Basil ambitious. All quiet.*

Then, after a pause, because the ledger deserved honesty even when he couldn't afford it:

*All quiet. I hate how much I love that sentence.*

* * *

There was one more ritual, and he performed it last, as he always did, when the colony's lights dimmed to night-cycle and the corridors emptied of even the sound of pipes settling.

He opened the drawer.

Inside lay the original's personal effects — the small sealed box of things that had come back from the murder scene, such as it was, such as the scene had been: an office locked from the inside, a man gone, blood enough to confirm violence and not enough to explain it. The colonial authorities had investigated for nineteen days and then, with the particular exhaustion of officials investigating something they suspected they weren't permitted to understand, had filed it as unsolved and moved on to the business of a colony slowly forgetting itself.

A chipped ceramic cup. A fountain pen that no longer held ink. A photograph of a woman Zephyrine had no other record of — face turned half away, laughing at something outside the frame. And beneath these, wrapped in cloth, a device the size of a thick book, dense with circuitry of a design Zephyrine had never seen anywhere else, its casing scorched along one edge.

The decoder.

He didn't touch it tonight. Some nights he did — lifted it, turned it over, felt its weight, felt the strange pull it exerted on the inherited parts of his mind, like a word on the tip of the tongue. Other nights, like tonight, he simply looked at it and closed the drawer again, the way you might look at a locked door in your own house and decide, once more, that tonight you would sleep rather than find out what was behind it.

Because here was the truth he kept below the truth: the original Zephy had been afraid. Zephyrine carried the fear the way he carried the memories — secondhand but vivid. In those final weeks, the man had grown paranoid in ways that embarrassed Zephyrine to witness, the way you're embarrassed watching footage of someone you love losing their composure. Checking the corridor twice. Encrypting notes in a language Zephyrine couldn't read. Once, memorably, hiding under his own desk when the comms chimed, then surfacing, red-faced at himself, alone in the room, and laughing — a laugh with no humor in it whatsoever.

*Someone is reading my mail,* the original had written, three days before the end. *Not the colony. Someone farther away. Someone who reads mail the way fire reads paper.*

And then, the next entry, in a calmer hand that frightened Zephyrine more than the panic had: *If anything happens to me, whoever finds this — and I suspect I know who will find this, because I know what I am to you — do not decode it. Live your small life. Be invisible. Invisibility is armor. I was visible once, and look at me.*

*Look at me.* As instructions went, it was ambiguous. As a last request, it was clear enough.

For fourteen years, Zephyrine had obeyed it. He recorded decay and called it archiving. He stayed small, stayed dull, stayed the colony's harmless chronicler, the man with the clipboard, the man nobody questioned because questioning him would require noticing him. He had made invisibility into a discipline, almost an art form. He was, he sometimes thought with genuine pride, the best-forgotten man in the colonial network.

And it worked. It worked beautifully. Every single day, it worked.

He closed the drawer and sat for a moment in the dim office, listening to the building breathe.

Outside the window, Meridian turned toward its artificial dawn. Somewhere in Ring Two, Dara Okoye was waking to her ambitious basil. Somewhere in the community hall, nine children were learning history up to the exact point where history stopped being written about them. The algae scrubbers breathed their green breath. The lights flickered their faithful, dying flicker.

*Entry 4,113 will be the same as 4,112,* he wrote, closing the ledger. *This is not a complaint. This is a prayer.*

He was halfway to sleep when the colony's ancient receiver woke up.

It happened sometimes — ghost signals, atmospheric noise, the electromagnetic indigestion of a network built for ten thousand voices and now carrying four hundred. Usually it was static wearing the costume of a voice. Zephyrine had long ago learned to sleep through it.

But this was different, and he knew it was different before he knew why — knew it in the inherited part of himself, the part that had spent a lifetime listening, the part that sat bolt upright in the dark with its heart going like a trapped bird.

Through the wall, faint and repeating, patient as tide, came a signal in a cadence he recognized without understanding. Structured. Deliberate. Old.

Older than the colony. Older than the war that had killed its language.

Zephyrine lay in the dark and listened, and the comfort of being unnoticed drained out of him like warmth from an unheated room, and somewhere far away — fourteen years and one death away — the original Zephy whispered through borrowed blood: *I told you not to decode it.*

The signal repeated. And repeated. And waited, as if it knew he was awake.
