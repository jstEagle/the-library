## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The colonies had stopped answering, and I was hauling their mail anyway.

That's the freighter life in one sentence: you carry the cargo of people who no longer speak to each other, and you pretend this is a job and not a metaphor. The *Long Route* was carrying four hundred tons of agricultural seed stock, two hundred crates of medical polymer, and one sealed diplomatic pallet that nobody aboard was cleared to open — which meant, in my experience, it contained either weapons or apologies, and lately the difference between those had gotten thin.

I was in the mess at 0340 ship-time, drinking coffee that tasted like someone had boiled a tire in regret, when gravity died.

Not failed. Died is too dramatic. It *stuttered* — like a voice catching on a word, like a singer losing the note for half a breath. My coffee cup rose six centimeters off the table, hung there rotating with terrible dignity, and I watched the brown liquid climb out of it in ropes, defying everything sensible, and for nine seconds the entire system forgot which way was down.

Nine seconds. I counted them. Counting is what I do instead of screaming.

* * *

You should understand something about me before we go further: I am not a normal kind of crazy. I am a very specific, very documented kind of crazy, the kind they used to give grants for.

For thirty-one years I translated dead languages. Not dead like Latin — dead like *extinct*, dead like the species that spoke them burned out or drifted or simply stopped, leaving behind transmissions that outlived their mouths. The Kessari. The Vell. The thing from the Ossuary Deep that linguists still argue about whether to call a language or a weather pattern. I sat in soundproofed rooms on three different stations and let those voices pour into my head until my own dreams stopped being mine. That's what immersion does. You don't learn a language; a language colonizes you. By the end I dreamed in structures no human throat ever made, woke up tracing shapes on the wall that meant nothing in any alphabet I could name, and once — this is in my file, Admiral Okonkwo's favorite anecdote about how far gone I am — answered a question in Vell before the person asking it had finished speaking in Standard.

They retired me with honors and a diagnosis. Then the war happened, then the truce, then the long silence, and suddenly the admiralty remembered that the woman who understood the remnant was still alive and could be pointed at things. So now I haul cargo, because pointing me at things turned out to be worse than useless. Every translation I gave them during the war years was accurate and every one of them was unwelcome, because the truth was that the alien remnant wasn't saying *attack* or even *stay away*. They were saying something much more disturbing, and the admiralty didn't have a category for it, so they filed me under *compromised* and put me on a shipping lane.

Fine. Good. Cargo is honest. Seeds don't lie about what they are.

But here's the thing about having a rewired mind: you can't turn it off. The languages don't leave. They just go quiet, like tenants who've stopped paying rent but won't vacate. And when the gravity stuttered across the whole Thessaly system — every ship, every station, every drifting hulk, all at once, all together — everyone else heard a malfunction.

I heard a sentence.

* * *

Let me walk you through the nine seconds, because I've replayed them maybe four hundred times since, and I want to get them right.

Second one: the drop. That stomach-lurching freefall, the universal human panic response, older than fire. Around me in the mess, Dara Okoye — my crewmate, my only friend within forty light-years, a loadmaster with forearms like dock cable — grabbed the table and swore in three languages, only one of them polite. Somewhere aft, the reactor tech, Pell, started shouting about inertial dampers. Normal. Correct. What a healthy mind does when the floor betrays it.

Second two: I noticed the coffee. Ropes of liquid climbing, wobbling, holding shape longer than surface tension should allow. Pretty. Wrong. I remember thinking, distantly, *that's odd,* the way you think *that's odd* about a bird flying in a straight line when birds never fly in straight lines.

Third through fifth: the flicker developed texture. This is where I lose most people when I tell the story, so bear with me. Gravity isn't smooth. Nobody feels this because human bodies are bad instruments, but gravitational fields have grain, noise, jitter — the universe's static. And in those seconds, the static organized. It pulsed. Short pulse, long pulse, short-short, a rest, then a rising triplet that ended on something I can only describe as a *falling inflection* — the way a sentence ends when the speaker already knows your answer.

I have spent three decades listening to physics that other people dismiss as noise. I know what random looks like. Random doesn't repeat. Random doesn't *resolve*. This resolved.

Sixth second: Dara said my name. I didn't answer. I was hearing — God help me, I was hearing *syllables*. Folded into the field fluctuations, encoded in the stutter amplitude, riding the same channel as the force that holds every world in the system in its lap. Someone — something — had figured out how to modulate gravity the way we modulate radio, and it was broadcasting, and the broadcast had grammar. There was a subject. There was, unmistakably, a predicate. There was an address structure at the front of it, the way every language I've ever parsed opens with *attention, this is for you.*

Seventh: the triplet again. Same form, slightly varied. Repetition with variation is not noise. Repetition with variation is *music*, and music is just grammar wearing its good clothes.

Eighth: silence inside the signal — a deliberate gap, a held breath, the pause a speaker leaves before the part that matters.

Ninth: down came back. All at once, everywhere, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. My coffee fell back into its cup, minus what I'd lost to the air. Dara was still gripping the table, staring at me, because I was standing — I don't remember standing — with both hands flat on the bulkhead and tears running down my face, and I was saying something out loud. She told me later what it was. Not Standard. Not anything she recognized. She said it sounded like I was answering someone.

* * *

"Okay," Dara said, after the alarms had cycled down and Pell had confirmed that the dampers were fine, the drive was fine, everything was fine, nothing was fine. "Explain it to me like I'm a loadmaster."

"You're a loadmaster."

"Then explain it to me like I'm smart."

I wrapped my hands around the coffee cup because they were shaking and I wanted that to be private. "Gravity flickered," I said. "System-wide. Simultaneously."

"So? Some relay blew, some test went sideways—"

"There's no mechanism for simultaneous system-wide gravitational fluctuation. None. A relay blowing gives you a ripple, delayed by distance. This was synchronized across eleven light-minutes. That's not an accident, Dara. Accidents propagate. This *coordinated*."

She looked at me the way people look at me when I start talking like this — with affection and wariness in equal parts, the look you'd give a dog that's started pointing at an empty corner. "Coordinated by what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I pulled up my wrist display and called the sensor logs. Freighters log everything; insurance companies see to that. "And here's the other question." I played her the amplitude record of the flicker, raw, converted to audio because that's how my brain ingests data best, always has. Nine seconds of a low, structured thrum.

Dara listened. Her face did the thing faces do — confusion, then discomfort, then that flicker of almost-hearing that ordinary people get and immediately talk themselves out of. "That's... there's a rhythm in there."

"There's more than rhythm." I stopped the playback. "There's morphology. There's an opening address marker and a terminal fall-off and a repeated construction in the middle that varies exactly the way a speaker varies when they're making sure you caught it the first time." I heard my own voice getting faster, the way it does, the way it always does, the way that made my old colleagues back away slowly. "It's a message, Dara. Somebody said something to this entire system using gravity as a carrier wave, and either nobody else noticed or nobody else wants to notice, because if the remnant can do this, then the truce fleet's entire strategic picture is—"

"Tamsin." Gentle. Firm. Loadmaster-firm, the tone she uses on crates that are stacked wrong. "When did you last sleep?"

I blinked. "What does that have to do with—"

"When did you sleep last?"

"...Thirty hours. Maybe forty."

"And you're telling me the floor talked to you."

"The floor *broadcast*—"

"Honey." She put her hand over mine on the cup. Her palm was warm and rough and real and I hated how much it steadied me. "I love you. You know I do. But you also told me last month that the cargo manifest whispered to you, and that turned out to be sleep deprivation and a bad bearing in the number-two hold fan."

The manifest had *rhythmed*, actually, but I had the grace not to say so. "This isn't the fan."

"It's never the fan." She squeezed my hand and let go. "Get some sleep. Log the anomaly through channels like a good little civilian, and if Command says jump, we say how high, and in nineteen days the truce either holds or it doesn't, and none of it is ours to carry. Deal?"

Nineteen days. Right. The truce expired in nineteen days, and every news feed on every colony that still had feeds was counting down like it was a holiday nobody wanted. Nineteen days until the fleet either negotiated or opened fire on the remnant, and the betting pools favored ordnance three to one.

"Deal," I said, because lying to Dara felt worse than lying to myself, and I needed to do at least one of them.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Of course I didn't sleep.

I went to my berth — a coffin-sized room I'd decorated with exactly one luxury, a sound-dampening field, because silence is the only gift I've ever wanted — and I pulled the full sensor logs, all of them, going back six months, and I ran the flicker against everything. Because here's what kept me awake, here's the splinter under the skin:

If it was a broadcast, it wasn't the first. Broadcasts repeat. Broadcasters are patient. And somewhere in six months of routine telemetry from a boring freighter on a boring lane, there might be earlier instances — fainter ones, ones nobody flagged because nobody was listening for structure in gravitational jitter, because why would you be, unless your mind had been hollowed out and refilled by dead tongues until you couldn't hear the universe any other way?

Found it at 0612. Or rather, found *them* — eleven prior micro-fluctuations over five months, each one dismissed in the logs as instrument error, each one matching the ninth-second structure of the big event. A sequence. Building. Like a speaker clearing her throat, softly, for months, before finally saying the whole sentence loud enough that the whole system heard it.

Eleven attempts. Twelve, counting the big one. Whoever they were, they'd been trying to reach us since before the truce countdown even started, and every attempt had been logged as noise by machines built by people who had decided, decades ago, that the remnant had nothing left to say worth hearing.

I sat in my coffin-berth with the dampening field humming and I did the thing I swore I'd never do again: I listened. Really listened. Headphones, eyes closed, the amplitude trace of twelve events stitched into one continuous stream, and I let the old training take me under.

And it opened. Not into words — I'm not claiming words, not yet, not sober on forty hours without sleep. But into *shape*. The address marker at the front, the one that means *attention, this is for you* — except now, stretched across twelve events, I could hear that it wasn't addressed to the system. It was narrower than that. It had a qualifier embedded in it, a restrictive clause, the grammatical equivalent of a hand extended toward one specific person in a crowded room.

A message to the whole system, yes. But addressed to someone in particular.

To whoever could still parse it.

Me. It had to be me. There were perhaps six people alive with my level of immersion in remnant linguistic structures, and I was the only one not employed by the fleet, which meant the only one whose incoming signals weren't filtered through a command structure that classified everything the remnant said as either threat or trickery.

Or I was insane. That remained on the table. Dara would be gentle about reminding me of it at breakfast.

I laughed — alone, in the dark, a small unhinged sound — and then I did the second thing I swore I'd never do again. I answered. Not aloud. Into the log, into a file, a translation draft, the first one in four years: *Event twelve, gravitational channel, provisional parse.* And beneath it, in the field reserved for translator's notes, where thirty-one years of my professional soul lived, I wrote:

*Opening formula consistent with remnant address conventions. Message contains self-identification, positional data, and what appears to be an invitation clause. Translator notes: it sounds like directions. It sounds like someone giving directions to their house.*

*Translator further notes: nobody sends directions to their house unless they want company.*

My hands were shaking again. I let them. Wonder does that, when it's been locked up long enough — it comes out trembling, like anything else that's been starved.

Outside my berth, the *Long Route* hummed onward through the dark between colonies that had stopped answering, hauling seeds and polymers and one sealed diplomatic pallet full of somebody's secrets. Nineteen days on the truce clock. Eleven light-minutes away, whatever had spoken was presumably waiting to hear whether anyone had heard.

I saved the file twice, encrypted it, and lay down on my bunk staring at the ceiling, wide awake, happier than I had any right to be, terrified in exact proportion.

Because the invitation clause had a deadline folded into it. Grammatically, temporally — the tense structure made it unambiguous. Whatever door they were offering to open, it closed when the truce did.

Nineteen days. And tomorrow I would report it through official channels, like a good little civilian, and find out whether the fleet still employed anyone who could listen.

I already knew the answer. I went to sleep anyway, and for the first time in four years, I dreamed — and the dream had grammar.
