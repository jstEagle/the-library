## 6. Intercept at Thessaly Drift

Thessaly Drift is what happens when a port gives up on being a port and settles for being a rumor. A ring of welded-together habitat modules spinning slowly around a fuel depot that hasn't passed inspection since before the war, lit by whatever ships happen to be docked at any given moment. When nobody's there, it goes dark. Some of the locals claim they prefer it that way.

We'd been holding position off the Drift for six hours when Dara stuck her head into my cabin without knocking, which is her way of knocking.

"Cargo inspection team requesting permission to come aboard," she said. "Fleet transponder. You want to take this one?"

I looked up from the sensor logs I'd been pretending to review for three days. "Why would I want to take it?"

"Because you've got that face."

"What face?"

"The face you get when you're about to do something stupid and you want witnesses who'll lie about it later." She leaned against the doorframe and crossed her arms. "Tamsin. It's an inspection. We're hauling agricultural substrate and medical isotopes. Even the fleet can't find contraband in fertilizer."

"You'd be surprised what people hide in fertilizer."

"I would not, actually, be surprised. I grew up on a farm." She studied me for a long moment, and I watched her decide — deliberately, visibly — not to ask the real question. That's Dara all over. She notices everything and interrogates nothing, which in the close quarters of a freighter is either cowardice or grace, and I've never had the heart to ask which. "I'll put them in the wardroom. Try not to make them cry. Fleet officers bruise easily; no one's ever told them no."

She left, and I sat with the logs, and I thought about the message sitting in my outbox like a loaded weapon: coordinates, decoded from nine seconds of falling, addressed to Kestrel-9. I'd sent it up the chain through official channels eleven days ago. The reply had come back in fourteen hours, which for fleet bureaucracy is roughly the speed of panic: *Acknowledged. Content classified. Cease analysis. Do not transmit further. Acknowledge.*

I had acknowledged. I had also, in flagrant violation of the spirit if not quite the letter of the order, kept a copy.

And now here came the fleet, wearing its inspection hat, which everyone knows is just its arrest hat with the brim turned down.

* * *

He introduced himself as Lieutenant Commander Idris Vane, Fleet Intelligence, and he had the particular stillness of a man who has practiced being unremarkable until he achieved it. Medium height, medium build, hair cut by regulation or by someone who loved regulation very much. His papers were immaculate. His eyes were not. They moved over my ship the way a translator's eyes move over an unfamiliar script — cataloging, parsing, refusing to assume.

"Lieutenant Commander," I said. "You're a long way from anywhere worth being."

"So are you, Ms. Sandoval." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something that might be rigged. "Routine manifest inspection. We're sweeping traffic through the Drift this quarter."

"Nobody sweeps traffic through the Drift. The Drift is where traffic comes to be forgotten."

"Then consider me sentimental." He took the chair across from my desk in the wardroom without waiting to be offered it, which was either rudeness or training, and folded his hands. "Your cargo. Substrate, isotopes, cold-frame parts. All accounted for?"

"All accounted for. Would you like to count it yourself? Fair warning, the substrate smells like it sounds."

"That won't be necessary." He didn't open his case. He didn't produce a scanner. He just sat there with his immaculate hands and his unremarkable face, and I understood that we were both waiting for the other one to say the true thing first.

Fine. I've spent forty years making first contact with things that couldn't speak. One more silent negotiator wasn't going to break me.

"Can I get you anything?" I asked. "The coffee is a war crime, but it's our war crime."

"No, thank you." A pause. Then, mildly, as if remarking on the weather: "You filed a report eleven days ago. Anomaly analysis, nine-second gravimetric event, system-wide."

Here it was, then. "I did."

"The content of that report has been classified."

"It has. I received the notification."

"And yet your vessel's sensor array ran a secondary decode pass on the raw capture data four days after the classification order." He unfolded his hands, laid them flat on the table. Not aggressive. Precise. "That's flagged in the fleet network, Ms. Sandoval. Everything is flagged in the fleet network. That's rather the point of it."

I could have lied. I considered lying, briefly, the way you consider jumping off a roof — aware that some part of you wants to, and that the wanting is diagnostic. But there was something in his flatness that made lying feel like handing him a gift. He wanted the lie. The lie would let him do his job cleanly.

So instead I said: "Do you know what a gag order does to a xenolinguist, Commander? It's like telling a bell not to ring. The sound is already in the metal."

Something crossed his face then — fast, quickly suppressed, but I've spent decades reading faces that weren't designed to be read. It wasn't suspicion. It was recognition, and it clearly annoyed him.

"You heard something," he said. "In the flicker."

"I heard structure. Whether it was something is above my clearance now, apparently."

"Describe it."

"Why? So you can classify the description too?"

"Because I've read your file." He said it simply, without apology. "All of it. Your translations were the foundation of the original truce framework. The Meridian Protocols. Before you were a cargo hauler, you were the reason there's been peace for nineteen years. And before that—" he paused, and for the first time he looked slightly uncomfortable, "—there's a psychological evaluation from thirty-one years ago. Your own words, quoted back. 'I have stopped dreaming in any language I share with another living person.'"

The wardroom hummed around us. Somewhere aft, Dara was loudly supervising a pallet move she didn't need to supervise, giving us our privacy the only way she knew how.

"Yes," I said. "That's accurate."

"How does that happen? To a mind?"

"You immerse. Decades of it. Dead languages, mostly — the remnant's older registers, the ones even they don't speak anymore. You learn a grammar deeply enough and it stops being a tool you hold and starts being a room you live in. My dreams redecorated. Eventually they spoke a dialect I don't." I shrugged. "It's the occupational hazard. Pilots lose their vestibular sense. Miners lose their hearing. I lost the ability to think entirely alone."

He was quiet for a while. Then he reached into his case — finally — and produced not a warrant, not a scanner, but a slim data slate, and set it on the table between us, screen down.

"I'm going to tell you something that will end my career if you repeat it," he said. "Which means I'm trusting you, which is a thing I don't do, and I'd like to note for the record that I resent it."

"Noted."

"The anomaly review board classified your report as a probable weapons calibration signature. Preemptive strike planning is underway, timed to truce expiry. Nineteen days." He turned the slate over but didn't activate it. "I was assigned to review supporting evidence. I found yours. And in your spectral decomposition of the flicker, there is a section you marked 'prosodic regularity' — recurring interval ratios in the gravitational oscillation, nested at three scales, self-similar."

"Yes."

"Ms. Sandoval, I am not a linguist. I am an intelligence officer, and before that I was a signals analyst, and I have spent twenty years looking at emissions and seeing weapons, because that is what I was trained to see. But I stared at your decomposition for six hours, and somewhere around hour four I stopped seeing a waveform and started hearing—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "There's a rhythm in it. Like someone counting under their breath. Like grief, keeping time."

The words went through me like a current. Because that was exactly it — exactly, precisely it — and he had gotten there alone, in six hours, with no training, using nothing but attention. Twenty years of institutional blindness and the man could still hear.

"Play it for me," he said. "The raw capture. Not the fleet's cleaned version. Yours."

"You know I can't."

"I know you already decided to." He almost smiled. It was a small, dry, apologetic movement, gone as soon as it arrived. "You kept a copy of classified data after a cease-and-desist. You requested a berth at Thessaly Drift, a port with no strategic value and no fleet presence, two days after receiving the order. You are currently stalling a routine inspection that you know is not routine. You wanted the fleet to send someone. You wanted to show them."

"That's paranoid."

"It's procedure," he said, and this time the smile stayed a moment longer. "Everything is procedure, Ms. Sandoval. That's rather the point of it."

* * *

I played it in the sensor bay, because the bay has the good speakers — salvaged audiophile junk from a decommissioned liner, Dara's pride and joy — and because I wanted him standing where I'd stood when I first understood what the flicker was saying.

Nine seconds of data. I'd listened to it perhaps four hundred times. Slowed down a thousandfold, the gravitational oscillation becomes audible: a deep pulse, then a cascade of harmonics, then silence. To the fleet's analysts it was a power surge, a test pattern, a threat. To me it was —

Well. Let me try to say what it actually is, since I'm apparently going to spend the rest of my life trying.

It's a voice. Folded into physics the way a message is folded into a paper bird — the folding isn't decoration, it *is* the message. The remnant doesn't broadcast in radio anymore; they haven't had the infrastructure since the war. What they have is mass, and gravity obeys them the way a well-trained choir obeys a conductor. Nine seconds of falling, system-wide, and every gram of matter in the volume was their instrument.

Idris stood very still while it played. I watched his hands. People's hands tell you what their ears are doing, and his hands slowly curled closed, like a man catching something small and alive.

When it ended, the silence afterward lasted a long time.

"Again," he said.

I played it again.

On the third pass he said, "There. The third phrase group. It repeats — inverted. Question and answer."

"You hear that?"

"I hear that." He turned to look at me, and his face had changed — not dramatically, nothing so useful as drama, but the way a locked door changes when you notice it was never locked. "How long has it been saying this? Is this the first time?"

"It's the first time in nineteen years. Which means it's either the last time, or the beginning of something, and the fleet has decided it's a targeting solution."

"The fleet decides quickly. It's how the fleet stays calm." He looked back at the dark screen. "My orders are to determine whether you're compromised or complicit. Those are the only two categories I was given."

"And which am I?"

"Neither, I think." He said it reluctantly, like a man admitting the map is wrong. "Compromised implies you were captured by something. Complicit implies you're working against us. But you're not working against anyone. You're working toward something, and the distinction matters enormously, and no one at Command has a form for it."

"There's no form for it," I agreed. "I checked."

He laughed — one short breath of it, surprised out of him — and then pressed his lips together like a man sealing a hatch. But something had shifted in the room. Two strangers, standing in a sensor bay full of salvage speakers, recognizing each other across the barbed wire of their respective institutions. He was the first fleet officer in nineteen years to hear what I heard. I was the first person in his entire career to tell him the truth without wanting anything from him except that he keep hearing it.

"What happens now?" I asked. "Officially?"

"Now I complete my inspection, log that your cargo is in order, and file a report stating that Sandoval shows no signs of compromise and full compliance with classification directives." He paused. "Unofficially, I request a copy of your full decode. For my files. Intelligence officers keep extensive personal files; it's practically religious."

"And if your superiors read your files?"

"They don't. Reading downward is beneath them. That's rather the point of them, too." He held out his hand, and I gave him the slate, and the transfer light blinked green between us like a small truce of its own.

At the airlock he stopped, one hand on the frame, and didn't turn all the way around.

"The third phrase group," he said. "The question and the answer. In your professional opinion — and I mean your actual opinion, not the one you'd put in a report — what is it asking?"

I had an answer ready. I'd had it for days, polished and rehearsed, the safe version, the version shaped like a hypothesis.

What came out instead was the truth: "It's asking whether anyone is still listening. And the answer it carries is goodbye."

His shoulders went still. Through the airlock viewport behind him, Thessaly Drift turned its slow dark wheel, and beyond it, past the shipping lanes, past the picket lines, past every gun the fleet was quietly loading, dead space waited with its mouth open.

"Commander," I said, "how many days to truce expiry?"

"Nineteen."

"And how long would it take you to resign?"

He looked at me then — really looked, the way you look at a text you realize you've misread your whole life — and I saw the exact moment the arithmetic started, behind his eyes, in a language neither of us had learned yet.

"I'll need to get back to you on that," he said, and stepped through the lock, and the ship felt emptier with him gone than it ever had with him aboard.

Dara appeared at my shoulder within the minute, because of course she did.

"So," she said. "Inspection go fine?"

"Fine. Cargo's all in order."

"Mm." She watched the fleet cutter detach and fall away toward the Drift's dim lights. "And the lieutenant commander?"

"He forgot something," I said. "He'll be back for it."

She raised an eyebrow. "What'd he forget?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's the problem. He remembered everything."
