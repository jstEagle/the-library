## 7. Nineteen Minus Three

The first thing you learn when you steal a ship is that stealing a ship is mostly paperwork.

Long Route was mine in every way that mattered — I'd held her license for eleven years, I'd signed for her cargo, I'd slept inside her hull longer than I'd slept in any bed. All I had to do was file a flight plan. The trick was filing one that said *Thessaly Drift to Meridian Yards, routine bulk run* while my hands set the actual course for coordinates that existed nowhere in the shipping registry except folded into the last nine seconds of a gravity flicker.

I filed the lie at 0340 station time, with Dara asleep two bunks down, and then I sat in the dark of the cockpit and listened to my own heart argue with itself.

Here's what nobody tells you about defection: it doesn't feel like a decision. It feels like a translation. You take a sentence you've been reading your whole life — *I am a loyal employee of the Concordance Shipping Authority* — and you look at it long enough, from enough angles, and one day you notice the grammar is rotten underneath. The sentence was never true. It was just the only text anyone would let you publish.

Sixteen days until the truce expired. Sixteen days until the admiralty answered a farewell with missiles. And somewhere out past the edge of the shipping lanes, a dead relay named Kestrel-9 was holding the only honest conversation humanity had been offered in forty years.

I keyed the pre-flight sequence and let Long Route wake up around me like an old dog hearing its name.

* * *

Dara found me at the airlock at 0415, which meant she'd never been asleep at all.

She stood in the corridor in her sleep shirt with her arms crossed, and she looked at me the way you look at a math problem whose answer you already dread. Behind her, the dock lights turned her shadow into something long and thin and trembling.

"Tell me I'm wrong," she said.

"You're wrong."

"Tamsin." My name in her mouth had always been a small miracle — she pronounced it soft, no irony, even after six years of sharing a bunkroom and a bathroom schedule and the particular misery of each other's cooking. "The inspection officer boarded an hour ago. He's still aboard. His cutter is still docked. And your filed course is a load of compost, because Meridian Yards doesn't take bulk ore this quarter — I checked, because I check everything, because one of us has to."

"I'm going to Kestrel-9," I said. Because lying to Dara Okafor was not among my available skills. I'd tried once, early on, over a card debt. She'd known before I finished the sentence.

Her face did three things in quick succession: fear, fury, and a grief so clean it looked almost like relief. "That's dead space. That's a burned-out star and a relay that died before either of us was licensed. That's—" She stopped. Recalculated. "That's where the flicker pointed. Isn't it."

"It's where the flicker pointed."

"And the fleet gagged you."

"The fleet gagged me very politely. There was a form."

Dara laughed, one syllable, wet and awful. She came forward and took my face in both hands, the way she did when I had a migraine, thumbs at my temples, and she studied me like she could find the rewiring in there if she looked hard enough. Forty years of dead languages had left marks on me; I'd seen them once in a neural scan, ghost-tracts where human syntax should have run. Maybe she could see them too. Maybe she'd always been able to.

"They'll call you a traitor," she said. "You understand that. Whatever you find out there, however right you are, they will put your name on a list between pirates and bomb-makers, and I will have to watch them do it."

"Yes."

"And you're going anyway."

"Yes."

She let go of my face. She stepped back. And then she did the thing that broke me open: she reached into the pocket of her sleep shirt and pressed her hand flat against Long Route's doorframe, the old dockhand's blessing, knuckles to metal, the gesture you make so the ship knows someone loves her.

"Then take the good coolant lines," she said, voice steady by main force. "I swapped the number-three line last month but the four and five are original fittings, they'll shiver above point-eight burn. Take it easy through the Thessaly shear or she'll rattle your teeth loose." A breath. "And eat something before you jump. There's rice in the warmer. You never eat before a jump and then you come out the other side gray as ash."

"Dara—"

"Don't." Her eyes were shining and furious. "Don't make it a speech. If you make it a speech I'll cry, and if I cry you'll stay, and if you stay they'll arrest you, and I refuse to be the reason xenolinguist Tamsin Sandoval went down in history as a footnote in somebody's court-martial." She wiped her face with her wrist, briskly, like swatting a fly. "Go be magnificent somewhere I can't see it. Send word if you can. Don't send word if you can't. And Tamsin —"

"Yeah?"

"When you talk in your sleep out there," she said, "whoever's listening — tell them we were sorry. For the war. Tell them some of us were always sorry."

I hugged her hard enough to hurt us both, and then I walked up the ramp, and I did not look back, because looking back is how you turn a departure into a funeral.

* * *

Idris Vane was waiting in my cargo bay, sitting on a crate of agricultural filters with his fleet-issue sidearm holstered and his hands visible, which told me he'd thought carefully about how to sit.

"Your crewmate has a colorful vocabulary," he said.

"What did she say to you?"

"She informed me that if I dragged you off this ship, she would report my cutter for seventeen separate safety violations, most of which she invented on the spot, all of which she documented." He paused. "Two of them are actually real. I checked."

"So you're here to drag me off."

He looked at his hands. Then at me. Fleet officers are trained to hold eye contact like it's ammunition, rationed and aimed, and Vane spent his like a man who'd decided the war was over before it started. "I'm here because I requested this assignment alone, and because I've spent four days reading your file instead of sleeping, and because when Command classified the flicker as a weapons test, I flagged its linguistic regularity in writing and got overruled by a man who thinks iambic pentameter is a type of drone." He stood up off the crate. "So. Am I arresting you?"

"That depends," I said. "Do you want to hear it again?"

"Hear what?"

I crossed to the sensor console, pulled the raw log — the one I'd decoded at 0300 while the gag order sat in my queue like a stone in a shoe — and played him the flicker. Nine seconds of gravity stutter, system-wide, dismissed by the admiralty as remnant weapons calibration. Nine seconds in which the fabric of local spacetime had been modulated, deliberately, precisely, in patterns I had spent thirty years learning to read in the mouths of a species that no longer spoke aloud.

Vane listened with his eyes closed. I watched his jaw tighten at second four, watched him lean toward the speaker at second seven like a man leaning into wind. When it ended he opened his eyes and they were wet and he looked personally offended about it.

"That's language," he said. "That's — it has structure. It repeats with variation. It's *addressed*." He shook his head slowly. "I don't know how I know that. I've never — I'm not —"

"You're not a linguist. Doesn't matter. It's addressed to whoever can hear it. That's how address works." I killed the playback. "Command says it's a weapons test."

"Command says a lot of things." He straightened his jacket, a reflex, armor being buckled on and then unbuckled again in the same motion. "If I report that you've fled toward the anomaly coordinates, Ashfall wing gets scrambled, and whatever's at the end of that signal dies under our guns before anyone reads it." A muscle worked in his cheek. "If I report that I found nothing aboard but routine contraband and a broken coolant sensor, then I'm a negligent officer, and you're a ghost, and nobody comes looking for sixteen days."

"Seventeen days," I corrected. "Truce expires in seventeen now. We lost one to your paperwork."

Something moved across his face that on a less disciplined man would have been a smile. "Nineteen minus three," he said. "My old commander used to count down deployments that way. Nineteen minus three means you've already spent three days you don't get back."

"So which report are you filing, Officer Vane?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Out in the dock, a loader drone trundled past the viewport, patient and blind. When he finally answered, he didn't answer me directly — he unclipped his sidearm, checked the charge, and stowed it in the wall locker beside my emergency kit, closing the locker with a click that sounded, in the quiet bay, exactly like a door.

"I'm filing that I found nothing," he said. "Now show me these coordinates. If I'm going to be complicit, I intend to be competent."

* * *

We jumped at 0530. I ate the rice first, because Dara would have known somehow, across light-years, if I hadn't.

Coming out of transit into the Thessaly shear was like surfacing in rough water — Long Route shivered down her spine, the number-four coolant line singing its thin complaint exactly as promised, and I rode the burn gentle the way Dara had ordered, half superstition and half devotion. Vane strapped into the second seat and watched the navigation plot resolve: the shipping lanes falling away behind us in their bright braided rivers, and ahead, nothing. Not empty space — space doesn't do empty, it does *unvisited*, which is worse. Dead space. The chart showed a star that had finished dying before humans learned its name, and a small cross of ink where a relay had been catalogued and abandoned sixty years ago.

Kestrel-9.

"No traffic," Vane said, scanning. "No beacons. No fleet presence within nine light-hours." He said it like a man reporting good news and bad news in the same breath, unable to decide which was which.

"That's the point. Nobody comes here. That's why it's still alive."

"It's a dead relay."

"It's a dead relay that answered a question nobody asked it yet." I settled us onto the long burn, point-six, easy on the old coolant lines, and engaged the dark-running protocols: emissions minimal, transponder cold, running lights off. Long Route went silent against the black, a held breath. "Sixteen hours to the marker at this burn. Get some sleep."

"Sleep." He tested the word like unfamiliar cargo. "You realize that by this time tomorrow, officially, I'll be a deserter and you'll be a traitor. They'll have names for us. Lists."

"They already have lists. We just weren't on them before." I pulled up the flicker fragment on the auxiliary display and let it loop, silent, its waveform crawling across the screen like a tide chart for an ocean that wasn't water. "For what it's worth, I've been on lists my whole career. *Difficult.* *Obsessive.* *Speaks in her sleep in unknown languages, recommend monitoring.* This is just the first list with teeth."

He looked at the waveform for a while. "What does it say? The fragment. You said coordinates. What else?"

I hesitated. Here was the thing I hadn't said aloud to anyone, the thing that had made the gag order irrelevant, because you cannot gag a woman from herself. "It says *come*. Not in those words — their language didn't have imperatives the way ours does, it had more of a... a leaning. A grammar of inclination. But the sense of it is *come*, and underneath that, repeated eleven times in eleven grammatical moods, is a word I translated forty years ago in my first published paper, working from war-corrupted fragments everyone else read as threat-designations." My throat tightened around it. "*Inheritance.*"

"Inheritance," Vane repeated.

"Their charts. Their maps. Everything they knew about the deep routes, the stable corridors, the places where physics behaves." I watched the waveform breathe. "They're offering us the keys to the whole galaxy, Officer Vane, and the admiralty wants to answer with ordnance because the message arrived during a power fluctuation and nobody upstairs has ears anymore."

He was silent long enough that I glanced over. He was staring at the waveform with the expression of a man watching his own reflection rearrange itself.

"My father was at First Contact," he said finally. "Signals division, before the war. He used to say the remnant sang before they shot. That we heard the singing and assumed it was targeting." He laughed, short and bitter. "I thought that was an old man's sentimentality. Grief dressed up as history."

"And now?"

"Now I'm deserting toward a dead star because a woman I arrested yesterday played me nine seconds of gravity and I can't stop hearing it." He unbuckled, stood, and paused at the cockpit door. "Get some sleep yourself, Sandoval. You'll want your ears sharp."

"That's the problem," I said, before I could stop myself. "Sleeping is how I hear them best."

He stopped in the doorway. Turned halfway. And asked, quietly, without institutional armor anywhere in his voice: "What do you dream about? Really."

"Stars leaving," I said. "A sky full of stars, all of them going out together, politely, in formation. And something enormous saying goodbye in a language made entirely of light." I shrugged, embarrassed. "I used to think it was a nightmare. Lately I think it might be an invitation."

He nodded once, slowly, and went aft to the spare bunk, and I sat alone in the dark cockpit with the waveform breathing beside me and the dead star swelling by millimeters on the plot.

Somewhere behind us, the fleet was updating its lists. Somewhere behind us, Dara was eating breakfast alone and hating me in the specific way that love hates. Somewhere ahead, a relay older than the war was waiting with its hands full of maps.

Sixteen days. Nineteen minus three.

I put my palm flat against the console, the dockhand's blessing, borrowed from her, and whispered to Long Route in the only language I had left that was fully mine: *hold together, old girl. We're going to say hello.*

Behind us, faint as a scar, the shipping lanes' light faded from the sensors — and then, at the edge of the plot, a new contact blinked awake. Fast, military, inbound on an intercept vector that no routine patrol should ever bother calculating.

Someone, it seemed, had filed a different report than the one Vane promised.
