## 8. Dead Space Radio

There is a particular kind of silence that exists beyond the shipping lanes. I'd read about it in old pilot memoirs and assumed it was poetry — the way people assume anything they haven't personally suffered is poetry. It isn't. It's an absence with weight. Out here, past Thessaly Drift, past the last buoy that anyone bothers to maintain, the background hum of civilization drops away entirely, and what's left is the sound of your own ship thinking to itself: coolant ticking through pipes, the reactor's low patient snore, Dara's absence ringing like a struck bell in every empty corridor.

Idris Vane noticed it first, actually. Day two out from the Drift, he stood on Long Route's bridge with his hands clasped behind his back — fleet posture, even as a fugitive, even in civilian coveralls I'd dug out of a storage locker for him — and said, "It's quiet."

"It's dead space," I said.

"No," he said, and there was something almost startled in his voice, "it's *quiet*. There's a difference."

I decided not to tell him he sounded like a poet. He was having enough trouble adjusting to being a traitor without also discovering he had literary inclinations.

* * *

Teaching him took longer than I expected, mostly because I had to un-teach him first.

He came to the signal the way fleet officers come to everything: as a problem with a solution. The flicker was data. Data could be parsed, cross-referenced, reduced. He sat across the mess table from me our third night out with my sensor logs projected between us and his jaw set at the angle of a man preparing to defeat something.

"Show me the pattern," he said.

"It isn't a pattern you see. It's one you hear."

"Tamsin."

"Idris." I let the silence sit for a moment, because I'd learned — from the Vessic archives, from thirty years of talking to things that were no longer alive to be offended — that silence is not the absence of communication. Sometimes it's the medium itself. "When you listen to music, do you look at the waveform?"

"That's different."

"It's exactly the same. You're asking me to show you a song as a graph. I can do that. You'll learn nothing. Or you can put the headphones on and let it do what it does."

He put the headphones on. He lasted ninety seconds before pulling them off like they'd burned him.

"What *was* that?"

"The beginning of a sentence."

"A sentence doesn't sound like — " He gestured vaguely at his own skull. "Like someone disassembling a cathedral inside your ear."

"Good," I said. "That's the most accurate description of Vessic grammar anyone's given me in twenty years, including mine."

Here is what nobody tells you about teaching: you cannot give someone your ears. Thirty years of immersion had rewired me so thoroughly that I heard the flicker the way a native hears their mother tongue — instantly, structurally, without translation happening anywhere I could point to. The syllables folded into the gravity fluctuation weren't data to me. They were *speech*. Idris had to build the whole cathedral from the foundation stones, and the foundation stones were things I hadn't consciously thought about since I was young enough to still dream in English.

So we started there. Not with the signal. With the shape of listening.

"Vessic grammar has no subjects," I told him, pacing the mess deck while he frowned at my chalk marks on the wall — real chalk, salvaged from a colony schoolhouse, because some lessons want to be written by hand. "No 'I' doing something to a 'you.' Everything is expressed as relationship. Position. Resonance. A Vessic sentence isn't 'the ship crosses the sea.' It's more like... the sea holds the ship, and the crossing is the space where holding and moving agree."

"That's not a language," he said. "That's a philosophy."

"Yes," I said. "That's what a language is. Yours included. You just inherited yours too young to notice."

He looked at me for a long moment then, and I recognized the expression because I'd worn it myself, decades ago, in a seminar room in Meridian City when Professor Anselm said something similar and rearranged the furniture of my mind without permission. Recognition. The specific vertigo of a person realizing the world is larger than the room they've been keeping it in.

"Say it again," he said. "The part about the sea."

I did. And that was the moment, though neither of us marked it at the time, when the mission stopped being about the signal and started being about the two of us sitting in the dark learning to hear together.

* * *

We fell into a rhythm, which is a dangerous thing to say about a man who arrested you and a woman who made him complicit in treason, but rhythm is rhythm and it doesn't check credentials.

Mornings we ran the ship — I taught him cargo handling, he taught me fleet-standard damage control drills, and we both pretended these exchanges were practical rather than a form of courtship conducted through emergency procedures. Afternoons we worked the signal. Evenings we argued.

The arguments were the best part. I'll admit that freely.

"You keep saying it's a farewell," he said one night, fifth day out, both of us running on tea and stubbornness. "But you haven't translated enough to know that. You're projecting."

"Of course I'm projecting. Everyone projects. The question is whether the projection survives contact with the evidence."

"And if it doesn't? If it's a targeting handshake and we're delivering ourselves to it?"

"Then I'll have been wrong in excellent company."

He didn't laugh. He almost laughed. There's a difference, and I've learned to treasure the almost — the way his mouth would start the motion and then some garrison of duty would call it back to quarters. But that night the garrison arrived late, and the laugh got halfway out before he caught it, and I thought: *there you are.*

"Tell me why farewell," he said, quieter. "Really."

So I played him the fragment again — the nine seconds, slowed, stretched, the gravity oscillation converted to audio — and I walked him through it phrase by phrase. The opening motif, three rising tones with a decay tail: greeting, but greeting-as-leave-taking, the way you might say *hello* to someone across a departure lounge. The middle section, dense, recursive, phrases folding back into themselves: recitation. Inventory. *What we had.* And the closing structure, which was the part that undid me every time, a long descending sequence that didn't resolve — it *thinned*, the way a voice thins when it's walking away down a corridor.

"It doesn't end," I said. "Listen. It attenuates. Languages don't do that in commands or threats. Threats resolve. Commands terminate. This just... gets further away while staying audible. That's a goodbye, Idris. Structurally, mathematically, that is the shape of goodbye."

He listened. All nine seconds, eyes closed, and this time he didn't flinch at the cathedral-disassembly feeling. When it finished he sat in the quiet for a while.

"My grandmother," he said finally, "used to stand at the window when I left her house. She never said goodbye. She'd just keep waving until the car turned the corner, and you could feel it — the wave getting smaller but not stopping. Like she was trying to make the goodbye last long enough to reach wherever I was going."

I didn't say anything. Some moments you don't translate. You just let them exist in the original.

"That's what this sounds like," he said. "Someone standing at a window."

And that was the night I knew we were going to be all right — not safe, not successful, possibly not even survivable, but *all right*, in the way that matters, which is that we had become the kind of people who could hand each other our griefs and have them held properly.

* * *

On the eighth day I dreamed, and he was in it, and the dream was in no language at all.

I should explain what my dreams are like now, since they're relevant. Decades of Vessic did something to my sleeping mind. Normal human dreams run on narrative — scenes, characters, the brain's soap opera. Mine had become spatial, relational, resonant. I dreamed in positions and pressures, in the feeling of one thing held against another. Waking from them used to feel like surfacing from deep water into air too thin to breathe.

That night I dreamed of a relay station orbiting a dead star — Kestrel-9, though I didn't know its name yet, only its position, the coordinates still burning in me from the decoded fragment. In the dream the station was singing the descending sequence, the goodbye-that-waves-from-the-window, and Idris was there, and in the dream he wasn't hearing it the way students hear — laboriously, through scaffolding. He was hearing it the way I did. Native. And the dream had no words in it anywhere, and when I woke I was crying, quietly, the way you cry in dreams that aren't sad but are simply too large for the container.

He was already awake — he slept badly, ex-officers, I was learning; the body keeps filing reports after the war ends — and he heard me, and he didn't ask. He made tea instead. Set it beside my bunk without commentary, and went back to the nav console, and gave me the room to surface at my own speed.

Later, over breakfast, he asked, "Do you dream in their language?"

"Not anymore. I dream underneath language. Below it. Where it comes from."

"What's down there?"

I considered the question seriously, because he'd asked it seriously. "The part of a person that existed before anyone taught them a word. Everyone has it. Most people bury it under theirs. I just... excavated mine and found someone else's city built on top of it."

"Does it hurt?"

"Some mornings." I wrapped my hands around the tea. "Less lately."

He looked at me then with an expression I couldn't file under any of my working categories — not pity, not fascination, something with warmth in it and also a kind of carefulness, like a man handling something he'd been ordered to detain and had privately reclassified as precious. I drank my tea and let the moment be what it was. We had nineteen days minus eight, a dying relay at the edge of nowhere, and a fleet that wanted us dead. There was no time for pretending we didn't know what was growing between us. There was also no time to name it. So we named it nothing, and fed it anyway, meal by meal, lesson by lesson, argument by argument.

* * *

The breakthrough came on the eleventh day, and it came through him, which surprised us both.

We'd been stuck for two days on the middle section — the recursive inventory, the *what we had* passage. My translations kept collapsing into vagueness. *Their history. Their works. Their memory.* True, and useless, the way a label reading *food* is useless when you're starving. Something specific was encoded in those nested phrases and I couldn't crack it, and I was tired, and tired xenolinguists get brittle.

Idris had taken to sketching the signal structures during his watch shifts — not translating, just drawing them, the recursion patterns, the fold-backs. He's a visualizer; I should have guessed. Fleet intelligence attracts minds that need to *see* a thing before they believe it. On the eleventh evening he spread his sketches across the mess table, forty-some sheets of paper covered in spirals and nested arcs, and said:

"These repeat. Not exactly — but the nesting depth cycles. Seven levels, then reset. Except here—" he tapped a sheet near the middle, "—and here, and here. Three places where the cycle breaks and goes deeper. Nine levels. Ten. Eleven."

I stared at the sheets. And felt the specific cold-and-hot flush of a translator about to understand something enormous.

"Nesting depth encodes age," I said slowly. "In Vessic archival grammar — the little we recovered from the war wreckage — deeper embedding meant older strata. Deeper memory. These three breakouts are..." I flipped through his sketches, hands not quite steady. "These are the oldest things they're carrying. The deepest memories. Everything else in the signal is recent by comparison."

"So what are they?"

I looked at the three breakout points, and their positions in the sequence, and the descending goodbye trailing after them, and it assembled itself in my head with an almost audible click.

"They're the things they chose to save. Out of everything — a whole species' worth of *what we had* — these three memories got the deep archive. The vault treatment." I looked up at him. "You just found their inheritance, Idris. You found what they're leaving behind."

For a moment he just breathed. Then: "Can you read them?"

"One of them. Maybe. The shallowest of the three." I pulled the audio file, isolated the segment, and worked while he watched — eleven minutes of the most concentrated translation of my life, the old rewired circuits firing like they'd been waiting years for exactly this load. And when it resolved, it resolved into something so simple and so devastating that I had to push back from the table before I could say it aloud.

It was a star chart. Not a chart of where they'd been. A chart of where they were *going* — departure vectors, destination clusters, the routes of an entire civilization leaving the galaxy. And attached to it, in the relational grammar that has no subjects and no objects, only positions and resonance, a single embedded clause that my mind rendered, inevitably, imperfectly, as:

*What we know about the roads. For whoever is still here after.*

"They're not attacking," I said, and my voice cracked on it, thirty years of vindication and grief compressed into six words. "They're not threatening. They're *leaving*, and they're leaving us the maps."

Idris stood very still. Then he gathered up his sketches, aligned their edges with a precision that I recognized as his version of emotion — order imposed on overwhelm — and said, "Then we'd better get to Kestrel-9 before Okonkwo turns the answer key into a crater."

"We have seven days to the relay. Truce expires in eight."

"Then we're ahead of schedule," he said, and almost smiled, and this time the garrison didn't call it back in time.

* * *

That night I took the second watch alone, and he appeared on the bridge an hour in with two cups of tea and no pretext whatsoever, which was its own kind of confession. We sat in the dark watching dead space do its nothing, and somewhere around the second cup he said, without looking at me:

"When this is over — if this is over — I don't think I can go back to the fleet. I keep trying to picture it. The briefings. The classifications. Calling this—" he nodded at the sensor display, where the signal's residue still glowed faintly in the log buffer, "—a weapons test. I can't picture saying it anymore. The words won't assemble."

"Welcome to my entire career."

"I'm serious, Tamsin."

"I know you are. That's why I'm not laughing." I turned my chair toward him. "For what it's worth — the words won't assemble because you've heard something true, and truth is incompatible with certain vocabularies. You can't unhear it. Believe me. I've tried for thirty years."

"And what do you do instead?"

"You find the people who can still hear it. However few. However far away." I hesitated, then finished it, because he'd earned the finish: "However much of your old life it costs."

He looked at me across the dark bridge, tea steam rising between us like something being transmitted, and whatever he was going to say next was interrupted by the comm board chirping — the long-range receiver waking for the first time in days, catching a fragment out of the static.

Not the Vessic signal. Human traffic. Fleet-band encryption, tight-beam, addressed to strike wing designations.

Ashfall.

My blood went cold in a very orderly fashion, the way it does when dread arrives with paperwork. I pulled the fragment, decoded the header — Idris watched me do it, and I watched him decide not to stop me — and read the routing tags twice to be sure.

Strike wing Ashfall. Underway. Vector: Kestrel-9. ETA: five days.

Five days. One day ahead of us.

Idris read the intercept over my shoulder, and I felt him go very still, the stillness of a man hearing his own name spoken in a language he'd sworn off.

"They're not sending a salvage team," he said quietly.

"No," I said. "They're sending the window-wavers' opposite. Someone who says goodbye with ordnance." I spun up the drive. "Hold on to something, Commander. We just became a race."
