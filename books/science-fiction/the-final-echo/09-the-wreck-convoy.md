## 9. The Wreck Convoy

There is a particular kind of silence that only exists in dead space, and I had spent three days teaching myself to stop flinching from it.

Not that dead space is actually silent — that's the first lie they tell you in navigation school. Dead space is full of noise: the creak of a hull settling into temperature differentials, the hum of a drive pushed to cruising efficiency, the small domestic sounds of two people sharing a ship built for eight. What it lacks is *traffic*. No chatter on the bands, no beacon pings, no automated customs drones demanding manifests. Just you, your co-conspirator-slash-prisoner-slash-whatever-Idris-was-this-week, and eleven days on the clock.

"Tell me about the war," Idris said.

He was in the copilot's seat, which he'd claimed on day one with the unselfconscious territoriality of a man who'd spent twenty years assigned to chairs. He'd stopped calling himself my prisoner around day two, mostly because I kept forgetting to feed him like one. Prisoners don't get coffee. Prisoners don't get asked their opinion on thrust vectors. Prisoners definitely don't get to reorganize the galley because "the ration packs were filed by date and not by type, which is frankly a war crime."

I didn't look up from the nav display. "Which part?"

"The part nobody talks about."

"That's a large part."

"I've noticed," he said dryly. "I served in it. The official version has a lot of gaps where things used to be."

I pulled up the long-range chart and let it hang between us. We were coming up on a stretch of the old shipping corridor that hadn't been maintained since before the armistice — a shortcut through the debris field left over from the Battle of Thessaly Approach, or as the fleet's history texts called it, *The Decisive Engagement of the Outer Reaches*. Or as I called it, the place where Halvard died.

"There's something ahead," I said. "You should see it before we're in it. Not because it's dangerous." I paused. "Because it isn't, anymore. That's the whole point."

* * *

The wreck convoy announced itself as a glinting.

That's how it always looks from distance — not wreckage, not death, just light. Thousands of hull fragments catching the distant star and throwing it back in slow rotation, so the whole field shimmered like a river of coins. For about four seconds it was almost beautiful.

Then Long Route closed to two hundred kilometers and the glints resolved into ships.

Not fragments. Ships. Whole hulls, most of them, torn open along seams that told you exactly where the plasma had gone in but otherwise intact — corridors still visible through ruptured plating, bridge windows still glazed with vacuum-frozen condensate, names still legible on bows that had been drifting here for thirty years. *CS Meridian*. *Halcyon Reach*. *Ardent Vigil*. A convoy of forty-one vessels that had been running supplies to the outer colonies when the remnant's first strike caught them in open transit, and that the fleet had pursued into this stretch of nothing and destroyed to the last hull, and then declared victory over, and then never come back to.

"They never recovered them," Idris said quietly. He was standing at the viewport now, both hands flat against the glass like he needed to hold himself upright. "Forty-one ships. There should have been a recovery operation. There should have been—"

"There wasn't money in it," I said. "And there wasn't glory. The war was already won by then, officially. This was mopping up. You don't build monuments to mopping up."

"You know an awful lot about an engagement that predates your service."

"My mentor was on the *Meridian*."

Idris went very still. In the week I'd known him I'd learned his tells — the way he catalogued information before responding, the half-second pause where other people would have filled the silence with comfort. Comfort was a reflex he'd apparently had trained out of him at the Academy.

"Halvard Osei," he said. "Your translation work cites him constantly. The Kestrel grammar papers."

"He was aboard as a civilian consultant. Fleet wanted someone who could parse remnant communications in real time, in case the convoy got hailed." I turned back to the display. "They got hailed. He was translating when the strike wing fired. His last transmission was a partial parse — he'd gotten maybe sixty percent of what they were saying. The fleet logged it as evidence of hostile intent."

"And was it?"

I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged at the edges. "Idris. I finished his parse twenty years later, using techniques he invented. Do you want to know what the remnant was saying to that convoy?"

He didn't answer, which was answer enough.

"*Withdraw your ships,*" I said. "*We are leaving this region. Withdraw.*" I let each word sit in the cockpit air like something with weight. "Sixty percent understood. One hundred percent ignored. And then we killed forty-one of our own ships to destroy a threat that was, at that moment, telling us it wanted to go home."

* * *

I took us through the field at minimum thrust, which was less a navigational choice than a funeral rite. There's a speed at which you pass wreckage respectfully, the way you walk past an open grave. Idris stood at the viewport the entire time and didn't speak, and I found I preferred that to anything he could have said.

It was Dara who'd once told me — back when I still talked to people about Halvard — that grief in vacuum behaves differently than grief planetside. Down a gravity well, grief falls. It lands somewhere and stays put and you can visit it. Out here it just drifts, orbit after orbit, year after year, patient as radiation. Forty-one ships full of grief, circling a star that had never been theirs, waiting for someone to come collect them.

The *Meridian* came up on the port side at 0400 ship-time, if you can call anything time out here. She was listing fifteen degrees off her original axis, her port cargo bay opened to space like a ribcage, her name still crisp because vacuum doesn't weather paint. I brought us alongside and matched her drift, and for a while we just hung there, two ships keeping company with a dead one.

"She was a good vessel," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, flattened. "Halvard used to say she had a *generous* hull. Roomy in the ways that mattered. He liked ships the way he liked languages — for their capacity."

"What was he like?" Idris asked. "The citations make him sound like a monument. Monuments aren't people."

That, unexpectedly, undid me a little. Because he was right, and because no one had asked me that question in nineteen years, and because the answer was easy and warm and I'd kept it sealed so long it had pressurized.

"He was terrible," I said. "He was wonderful. He smoked despite having lungs he needed for zero-g work, and he lied about it with tremendous flair. He fell asleep during his own lectures — twice, famously, mid-sentence, and woke up both times and continued without losing his place. He believed that every language was a way of being a person, and that learning one meant briefly becoming someone else, and that this was the closest thing to immortality our species had managed." I watched the *Meridian*'s dark windows slide past. "He also believed the remnant could be reasoned with, and everyone told him he was naive, and when the war started they told him he'd been dangerously naive, and he said — I'll never forget it — he said, 'No. I was early.'"

Idris made a sound that might have been a breath or might have been something breaking quietly.

"'I was early,'" he repeated.

"That's what I'm doing now," I said. "In case you were wondering what this is. Everyone thinks I've lost my mind, defected over a nine-second flicker. But I'm not late to something. I'm early again. Halvard taught me the difference."

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I want it on the record that I did it deliberately, knowing exactly what it would cost him, because Idris Vane had spent a week asking me to trust him with my treason and I figured it was past time he trusted me with my grief.

I played him the *Meridian*'s final transmission.

I'd carried it for nineteen years — a copy pulled from the public archive before the fleet classified the raw audio, back when I was young enough to think classification was temporary. Nineteen seconds of Halvard Osei's voice, working live, parsing a remnant hail in real time. You could hear him thinking. That was the worst part, the part I'd never been able to stop hearing: the little hum he made when a construction surprised him, the quickening cadence as the meaning assembled itself, and then — at second fourteen — the sharp intake of breath as he understood, and the words, calm as a man reading a weather report:

*"They're withdrawing. Tell the wing to hold fire, they're withdrawing, repeat, they are—"*

And then the transmission ended, because the wing did not hold fire, and whatever happened on the *Meridian*'s bridge happened faster than sound could carry it to me.

I played it twice. On the second pass I watched Idris instead of listening, because I knew the recording by heart the way you know a scar.

He listened like a translator. That's the thing I never expected from a fleet intelligence officer — he listened the way Halvard had taught me to listen, hunting structure, tracking stress patterns, and I realized somewhere around second ten that he was hearing what I heard: not a dying man, but a working one. A professional doing his job beautifully, in the last seconds available to him.

When it ended, Idris sat down very slowly in the copilot's chair, like his legs had submitted a resignation letter his body hadn't yet processed.

"The strike wing fired ninety seconds later," he said. "I've read the engagement log. The hail was flagged as 'unverified alien communication, possible deception tactic.' The wing commander cited standard protocol." He looked at his hands. "I've *cited* that protocol. In briefings. I've stood in rooms and explained why unverified alien communications couldn't be trusted, and I never once—" He stopped. Started again. "Tamsin. How many hails did they send? During the whole war. How many times did they try?"

"Fourteen confirmed attempts to establish communication before the first exchange of fire. Sixty-three during hostilities. All logged as psychological operations." I shrugged, and hated how casual I made it sound, because inside I was nineteen years old and screaming. "You can't deceive someone who won't listen. Deception requires an audience."

"The truce," he said slowly. "The one expiring in eleven days. The one I was prepared to enforce with ordnance. It was negotiated using *your* translations. Your file says so — I read it before I came to arrest you. I thought it meant you were valuable." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "It means you're the only reason there was ever peace at all. And the fleet is about to end it because of a signal it refuses to read, sent by an enemy that has spent thirty years trying to say goodbye."

"Now you understand why I ran."

"No." He shook his head. "Now I understand why you ran *fast*."

* * *

We cleared the far edge of the convoy an hour later, and I set course for Kestrel-9, and neither of us spoke for a long time, which was its own kind of conversation.

I felt the change in him before I saw it. Loyalty isn't a wall; it's a load-bearing structure, and you don't always hear the crack. But I'd spent my life listening for fractures in things that pretended to be solid, and I heard this one — in the way he checked the sensor logs without being asked, in the way he'd started saying *we* instead of *the prisoner and I*, in the way he looked at the fleet's chart annotations now, all those confident red designations — HOSTILE, THREAT, ENGAGE — like a man rereading old letters from someone who'd lied to him.

"Admiral Okonkwo was at Thessaly Approach," he said finally. "Junior staff. He signed the after-action report that buried Halvard's transmission." He was quiet a moment. "He gave me my first command."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because the fleet doesn't send anyone else to do its dirty work," I said. "Only the ones it trusts. Only the ones it's *made*." I turned from the console and looked at him straight on. "It made you, Idris. The question is whether it finished."

He held my gaze for a long moment, and I watched him decide not to answer, which was itself an answer — the honest kind, the kind that leaves the door open.

Then the proximity alarm chimed, soft and wrong, and we both turned to the display.

A single contact, far out at the edge of sensor range, moving fast and closing on an intercept vector. Fleet transponder code. Strike-wing configuration.

Eleven days, I thought. We were supposed to have eleven days.

The universe, it seemed, had learned to lie in English too.
