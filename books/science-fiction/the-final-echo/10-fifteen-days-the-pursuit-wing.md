## 10. Fifteen Days: The Pursuit Wing

There are two kinds of officers in the fleet: the ones who joined because they believed in something, and the ones who joined because they were running from something. I used to think I was the second kind. Fifteen days before the truce expires, standing on the deck of the strike carrier *Vigil* while Admiral Okonkwo's orders scroll across my display like a sentence being handed down — though to whom, exactly, is still an open question — I've stopped being sure.

My name is Commander Elena Reyes, and I am a true believer. That's not a confession. It's a job description.

"Say it back to me," Okonkwo says from the holotank, her image rendered in the fleet's standard blue, which always makes people look drowned.

"Strike wing Ashfall proceeds to Kestrel-9 at best speed," I recite. "Objective one: destruction of the relay facility designated Kestrel-9. Objective two: recovery or neutralization of the defectors Sandoval and Vane. All operations to conclude before truce expiry at zero-hundred, day nineteen."

"And if the defectors resist?"

"Then they're combatants." I say it cleanly. I've had fifteen years of practice saying things cleanly.

Okonkwo studies me a moment longer than she needs to. She has a face built for studying people longer than she needs to — broad, patient, carved by thirty years of watching junior officers lie to her. "You have questions, Commander. You always have questions. Ask them now, because after you jump you're going to be too busy executing to indulge them."

One question, then. The obvious one. "Ma'am — the anomaly. Nine seconds of system-wide gravitational flicker. My brief calls it a weapons test. But nobody can tell me what weapon produces that signature without producing anything else. No thermal bloom. No debris. No casualties. A test of what?"

"A demonstration," Okonkwo says. "They wanted us to feel it. Gravity is the one thing every human being in this system trusts absolutely. They reached into nine seconds of our lives and took it away, just to show us they could. That's not a weapon test, Commander. That's an opening move."

It's a good answer. It's the kind of answer that sounds like thinking when it's actually just fear wearing a uniform. I know this because I've given versions of it myself, in briefing rooms, to frightened crews who needed the universe to make sense in a way that pointed somewhere actionable.

But I'm a true believer, and true believers don't interrogate their own doctrine mid-mission. So I say, "Understood, ma'am," and I take the orders, and I don't ask the question underneath the question, which is: *what if it wasn't a threat? What if it was a knock?*

That question belongs to xenolinguists and other civilians. My job is simpler. My job is arithmetic.

* * *

Ashfall is four ships: the *Vigil*, two frigates named for dead admirals, and the tender *Mercy of Ceres*, which everyone calls the Meat Wagon and which exists to collect what's left of people. Two hundred and six souls under my command, give or take whatever the void decides to subtract along the way.

I walk my ship before we jump. Every commander does it differently — some pray, some drill, some hide in their cabins reviewing contingency trees until the numbers blur. I walk. Deck by deck, past gunnery stations and torpedo racks and the long grey corridors where the crew pretends not to watch me pass. They're young, most of them. Young enough that the First War is history-book material to them, a chapter with a tidy ending: humanity bloodied, humanity unbowed, humanity triumphant, armistice signed and truce renewed every five years like a subscription nobody remembers starting.

I'm not that young. I was eleven when the war started. I remember the evacuation barges over Thessaly, the ones that came down burning. I remember my mother listening to casualty lists on the kitchen radio with her hand pressed flat against her mouth, as if she could hold the names inside the speaker through sheer pressure. I remember deciding, with the terrible clarity available only to children and fanatics, that someone had to make sure it never happened again.

That someone turned out to be me. Or rather: that someone turned out to be anyone willing to do the arithmetic honestly. And the honest arithmetic has never changed. The remnant outnumbers us in hulls we can't see and outguns us in physics we don't understand. Nine seconds of stolen gravity proves it. Every year we don't fight them is a year they get stranger, deeper, more incomprehensible. Every year we wait is a year the opening move gets cheaper for them and more expensive for us.

So when the truce clock runs out, you hit them first. Not because you want to. Because wanting has nothing to do with it.

"You look like a woman doing math she doesn't like," says Lieutenant Okafor, falling into step beside me on the hangar deck. He's my exec, twenty-nine, funny in the specific way of people who use humor as a load-bearing structure. "Bad news?"

"The news is fine. It's the arithmetic."

"Ah." He nods sagely. "The arithmetic. The enemy of sleep everywhere."

We stop at the rail overlooking the launch bay, where the bombers sit racked in their cradles like sleeping animals, all that ordnance folded into shapes designed to fit through doors. Forty-eight hours ago these racks held training rounds. Now they hold the real thing, and the whole deck smells different — hotter, sharper, the way a room smells when someone has brought a loaded question into it.

"Can I ask you something off the record, Commander?" Okafor says.

"That's a dangerous genre, Lieutenant."

"I read the file on the defectors. The linguist. Sandoval." He keeps his eyes on the bombers. "Her translations are why we have a truce at all. The original one. She wrote the grammar that let both sides stop shooting. And now Command's sending us to kill her for hearing something in the dark."

"Command is sending us to destroy a relay and recover two personnel who went dark in violation of a gag order," I correct him. "Nobody's killing anybody who comes quietly."

"And if she doesn't come quietly?"

"Then she made her choice, same as we're making ours."

He's quiet for a moment. Below us, a loader crew swears cheerfully at a stubborn munitions clamp, the sound echoing up like the ship itself grumbling in its sleep. Then Okafor says, carefully, "Do you ever wonder if maybe both things can be true? That she's a traitor *and* she heard something real?"

And there it is. The question underneath the question, surfacing in my own hangar bay, wearing my own exec's face.

"No," I say. "Because 'real' isn't the standard, Okafor. Survivable is the standard. If she heard something real, it changes nothing about what we have to do. If she heard nothing, it changes even less. Either way, Ashfall flies."

"Yes, ma'am," he says, and his voice does the thing military voices do when they're agreeing without agreeing, and I let it go, because I walked this deck to steady myself, not to conduct philosophy seminars.

But walking back toward the bridge, alone, I catch myself doing it anyway. Wondering. Not whether she's right — I'm a true believer, I told you, and true believers don't wonder about that — but about the shape of her. What kind of person spends thirty years learning to hear dead languages and then throws it all over a nine-second flicker? Fanatic, obviously. The file says compromised. The file says complicit. The file is very thorough about what she is.

The file says nothing at all about what she heard.

* * *

We jump on schedule. Four ships folding themselves across eleven light-hours of dead space, the stars smearing into the long grey nothing between lanes, and for the transit I do what commanders do: I review, I drill, I sign things. I also, in the privacy of my cabin, with the door locked and the log paused, pull the intercept transcripts.

Not the sanitized ones. The raw sensor logs from the freighter *Long Route*, pulled through channels I'm not strictly supposed to access, because I find that I cannot plan an operation around a phenomenon I haven't personally examined. This is not doubt. This is due diligence. There's a difference. I've built a career on the difference.

The gravity data is beautiful, actually. I'll grant the physicists that much. Nine seconds of fluctuation so precisely structured that it looks less like a malfunction than like handwriting. The analysts flagged the periodicity, the nested harmonics, the way the oscillations repeat with variations that repeat with variations — fractal, recursive, deliberate. Their conclusion: an advanced propulsion or weapons signature, purpose unknown, intent presumed hostile per standing doctrine.

Standing doctrine. The three most expensive words in the fleet.

I put the audio on. Just the sonified version, the gravity fluctuations mapped to frequency so human ears can parse them. It sounds like rain on a roof, at first. Like rain that's learned rhythm. Like rain that's —

Like rain that's *saying something*.

I listen to it eleven times. On the eleventh time I notice that I've been holding my breath, and that my hands are flat on the desk, and that some animal part of me has gone very still, the way you go still when you realize the noise downstairs isn't the house settling.

It doesn't mean anything. Pattern-seeking is what brains do; we see faces in electrical outlets and hear voices in wind. Thirty years of intelligence work and I know exactly how unreliable the instrument between my ears is.

But I also know what hostile looks like. Hostile has a shape. Hostile is the evacuation barges coming down over Thessaly, is the burned colony ships, is nine seconds of terror delivered without warning or mercy. And this — this folded, patient, repeating rain — doesn't have that shape. If anything it has the shape of the thing I've spent my whole life refusing to imagine:

It has the shape of someone trying very hard, across an enormous distance, to be understood.

I delete the file. I wash my face. I go back to the arithmetic, which is clean, which is survivable, which does not require me to believe that the enemy spent its opening move on a lullaby.

Fifteen days. The clock doesn't care what I almost heard.

* * *

Day thirteen out from Kestrel-9. We're deep in dead space now, past the last beacon, past the wreck fields where the First War left its litter of coffins drifting in formation. Transit protocol puts the wing on silent running, comms dark except for scheduled bursts, and the silence gets into everything — into the decks, into the food, into the conversations in the mess, which have gone short and careful, the way conversations do when everyone aboard is pretending they aren't thinking about the same thing.

I find Okafor in the observation blister, which is empty at 0300 except for him and the slow wheel of stars outside the glass. I don't pretend I came here by accident. Neither does he.

"Status of my conscience, Lieutenant?" I say, leaning on the rail beside him. "Since you've clearly appointed yourself its keeper."

"Somebody has to. Yours is carrying classified cargo." He doesn't smile when he says it, which from Okafor is practically a distress flare. "Reyes. Can I tell you what's bothering me? Not the mission. The timing."

"Go on."

"Nineteen days. The truce expires in nineteen days, and Command dispatches a strike wing the day after the flicker. Not on day eighteen. Not after diplomatic channels, not after a single attempt at contact. Day one." He turns to look at me. "You don't mobilize Ashfall that fast unless the decision was already made. Unless the flicker didn't cause the strike order. It just gave them the excuse they'd been waiting for."

Outside the glass, a star that isn't really there crawls backward as the ship corrects. I watch it go and I think about my mother's hand pressed flat against the radio, holding the names in.

"You think I don't know that?" I say finally. "Okafor, I've read the strategic assessments going back eight years. Half of them recommend preemptive action at next truce expiry regardless of provocation. The flicker didn't create this mission. It scheduled it. Fine. Noted. Logged. And none of it changes a single number in the arithmetic, because the alternative to hitting them first is finding out — permanently, and all at once — how right we were to be afraid."

"And if we're wrong?"

"Then we're wrong," I say, "and we'll have killed the only people who could have told us."

The words come out before I can decide whether to say them, and once they're out, the blister goes very quiet, the kind of quiet that has mass. Okafor looks at me. I look at the stars. Somewhere behind us, eleven light-hours back, a xenolinguist and a fleet officer are running for a relay at the edge of everything, and I am the clock they're racing, and I have just said aloud the sentence that true believers are not supposed to have ready.

"Commander," Okafor says gently, "that sounded almost like doubt."

"It sounded like honesty, which is worse." I push off the rail. "Get some sleep, Lieutenant. Tomorrow we run the strike rehearsals, and I want this wing so sharp that no amount of doubt — mine or theirs — slows us down."

"Aye, ma'am."

I'm at the hatch when he adds, "For what it's worth — if she did hear something real, I hope she's fast."

I don't answer. I stand in the corridor outside the blister for a long moment, in the hum and the dim, and I let myself do the thing I've been not-doing since the audio file: I picture her. Tamsin Sandoval. An old woman in a freighter cockpit, headphones on, chasing a ghost made of gravity, betting thirty years of credibility and her actual life that the ghost is friendly. Against her: me, four ships, forty-eight bombers, and the entire institutional weight of a fleet that decided decades ago that understanding was a luxury war had priced out of reach.

Two ways to hear the same nine seconds. One of us is about to be catastrophically wrong.

The clock says twelve days. The clock doesn't care. But for the first time in fifteen years of service, standing in the dark of my own ship, I find that I care — and that caring, in a true believer, is the most dangerous weapons system Ashfall carries.

I seal the blister behind me and set course for Kestrel-9, and the stars wheel on, indifferent, counting down.
