## 11. Kestrel-9 Rising

There's a particular kind of silence you only get at the edge of dead space. It's not the absence of sound — a ship is never quiet, the Long Route hums like a hive with opinions — it's the absence of *answer*. You hail a beacon and nothing hails back. You ping a relay and your voice goes out into the dark and dies there, unmourned. For three days we'd been flying through that silence, and I'd begun to suspect that the universe had simply hung up on us.

Idris had stopped asking how much longer. He'd learned that my answers came in two varieties: a number that kept changing, and a look that made him regret the question. Instead he'd taken up residence in the co-pilot's chair with a maintenance manual he was reading the way other men read scripture — devoutly, and without comprehension.

"That's a coolant diagram," I said.

"It's a fascinating coolant diagram."

"You're reading it upside down."

"Then it's a fascinating coolant diagram for someone else."

I almost smiled. That was the thing about Idris Vane that I hadn't put in my report, mostly because there was no report anymore, mostly because I was a traitor now and traitors don't file paperwork: the man was funny in a dry, apologetic way, like humor was a regulation he'd found a loophole in. Three days in the dark with a fleet officer who'd chosen me over his commission, and I still hadn't decided whether that was the most romantic thing anyone had ever done for me or the most alarming. Possibly both. Possibly the categories had collapsed, the way everything else had.

The star came into view on the fourth day.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of my old self: it wasn't a star anymore. It was the *memory* of a star, a cinder the color of a banked coal, wrapped in a shroud of cooling dust that caught our running lights and threw them back in dull garnet. Dead space wasn't empty. Dead space was a graveyard with excellent acoustics.

"There," Idris said, and his voice did something I'd never heard it do. It went young.

The relay hung in orbit around the cinder like a bone around a throat. Kestrel-9. I'd seen the archival images — a squat, brutal cylinder, all function, the kind of engineering that assumes its audience is a forklift. What hung in front of us now was something else. The hull had grown. Or been grown. Ribs of some dark material curved away from the original human frame like a cathedral deciding to annex a warehouse, and across its surface ran lines of pale light, most of them dark, a few still burning in slow, arrhythmic pulses.

Like a heart deciding, one beat at a time, whether to bother.

"It's bigger than the records," Idris said. "The records say four hundred meters. That's — Tamsin, that's *kilometers*."

"The records were taken during the war." I brought us in on a slow, respectful arc. "Nobody was taking pictures after. Nobody was coming back."

"Comforting."

"I'm a xenolinguist, not a comfort. You knew that when you defected."

"I defected *at* you, technically. It was very sudden. I hadn't done the reading."

The Long Route is a cargo hauler, which means she handles like a cathedral with a hangover, but she handles, and I brought her alongside the relay's original docking collar — the human one, the one some long-dead engineer had welded on with the touching optimism that humans would always be welcome here. The collar was furred with the same dark growth as the hull, but the docking clamps, when I magnified them, were clean. Maintained. As though something had kept them ready.

For sixty years.

"That's not possible," Idris said, when I showed him. "Nothing maintains itself for sixty years."

"Something does," I said. "We're looking at it."

I ran the approach litany out of habit — clearance request, transponder, the whole polite bureaucracy of docking — knowing there was no one to answer. The cinder star said nothing. The relay said nothing. And then, as our hull shadow crossed one of the pale lines on its surface, the line brightened.

Not flickered. *Brightened*. Deliberately. In sequence with the shadow, a half-second behind, like a pupil dilating.

"Tamsin." Idris's voice had gone very flat, which I'd learned was his version of screaming. "The station just reacted to us."

"Stations don't react."

"That one's doing it anyway."

I cut our thrust to a drift and watched. The line of light pulsed again, and then a second line caught, and a third, a slow cascade spreading outward from where our shadow had touched, light propagating along the hull in patterns that were — I gripped the console, because my hands had decided to be honest about what my brain was refusing to admit — patterns that were *syllabic*. Long, short, long-long. Stress and release. Prosody.

It was speaking. Not to us. Not yet. It was speaking the way a sleeper mumbles before waking, running through old phrases in the dark, and our shadow had been the first thing to touch it in a lifetime.

"Play me the flicker," I said.

Idris didn't ask which flicker. There was only one flicker that mattered now, the nine-second gravity stutter we'd pulled from the Long Route's sensor logs, the signal buried in the noise that had ended my career and begun whatever this was. He routed it to the external array because he understood, without my explaining, that some things shouldn't be played in a small room.

The signal went out. The relay went silent.

For eleven seconds — I counted, because counting is my prayer — nothing. The cinder star burned its coal-red nothing. I felt the old loneliness rise in me, the one that had been cracked open by wonder and never fully closed again, and I thought: *you fool, you dragged a man across dead space to talk to a corpse.*

Then the relay answered.

Not in light. In gravity.

The Long Route shuddered — a gentle shudder, an apologetic one — and my inner ear told me we'd gained mass, a fraction of a percent, a hand closing softly around the ship. Around *me*. The flicker's own grammar, returned. It had heard nine seconds of falling and it gave us back a tenth of a second, a demonstration, a *handshake*: I know this language. I know this language because I spoke it first.

Idris had gone white. "Is it — is that an attack? Tell me if that's an attack."

"It's an introduction." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "It's shaking our hand. Very carefully. The way you'd shake the hand of something small and frightened."

"Which one of us is the small frightened thing?"

"Yes," I said.

* * *

We docked in the long shadow of the human collar, and the clamps closed over us with a sound like a held breath released. Idris insisted on armor. I insisted on the recording array, the translation deck, and my good boots. We compromised: he wore the armor, I carried the array, and we both wore the boots, because even at the end of the world I am not stepping onto an alien structure in ship socks.

The airlock cycled. The inner door opened onto the relay's original corridor — human make, human height, emergency striping gone gray with age — and then, ten meters in, the corridor *changed*. The dark material took over, and the walls curved, and the lighting came up around us in that slow syllabic pulse, and I understood that we were no longer inside a station.

We were inside a throat. A throat that had decided, for the moment, not to swallow.

"Sixty years," Idris said quietly. "The war ended sixty years ago. The fleet wrote this place off as destroyed. There are *memorials* to Kestrel-9. There's a plaque at Command Academy." He looked at me. "We built a plaque for a thing that was alive."

"The fleet's good at plaques," I said. "It's the listening we were always bad at."

The corridor opened into what had been the relay's operations deck, and I stopped, because the operations deck was gone. In its place: a chamber like the inside of a bell, ribbed and vast, and suspended in its center, a lattice of light. And in the lattice — I made Idris confirm it twice, because my eyes had begun to negotiate terms with my sanity — in the lattice hung *words*. Human words. Fragments of transmission, decades old, war-era chatter, distress calls, a weather report from a colony that no longer existed, all of it suspended in light like flies in amber.

"It archived us," Idris said. "It's been — it kept everything. Everything we ever broadcast at it."

"It kept what it could parse." I walked the lattice's edge, reading. There — a fragment of the Thessaly evacuation, the one my mentor had died relaying. There — a child's school broadcast, multiplication tables in a singsong, and the relay had kept that too, had kept the *singsong*, had preserved a dead child's cadence in a dead language's memory for sixty years. My throat did something complicated. "It's not a military archive, Idris. It's a *library*. It kept the beautiful things."

"Libraries get burned," he said. "That's usually why we're here."

"Speak for yourself. I'm here for the card catalog."

But my hands were shaking as I set up the translation deck, and he saw it, and he didn't comment, which was its own kindness. I keyed the first phrase — the opening formula from my decoded fragment, the one I'd been turning over in my head for nineteen days, the one I'd begun to think of as *hello* in a grammar that had no word for stranger.

I spoke it aloud.

I don't know what I expected. A light. A chime. Some dignified machine response. What I got was the chamber itself: every rib of it resonating at once, the lattice flaring, the whole station taking my small human voice and *answering it in kind* — the phrase returned, unfolded, expanded into a chord of meaning that hit me not through my ears but through my teeth, my sternum, the fluid in my inner ears. The station had no mouth. The station was the mouth. And it had been waiting, sixty years, half-dead, one pulse at a time, for someone to say the first word.

The chord resolved. And beneath it, in the lattice, new words assembled — not archived ones. *New* ones. Composed now, for us, in a structure I could read, because I had spent thirty years rewiring my mind with the grammar of a people everyone else had written off as dead.

Idris watched my face. "What's it saying?"

I made him wait while I translated, properly, the way my mentor had taught me — twice through, once for the words and once for the grief under the words. Then I said it out loud, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I let it.

"It says: *Query. Are you the ones who kept singing?*"

"Kept — " He frowned. "Kept singing what?"

"I don't know yet." I looked up at the lattice, at the ambered fragments of a dead colony's multiplication tables, and I thought I might know, and I wasn't ready to say it, because if I said it and was wrong it would break something in me that I'd need intact for whatever came next. "But it's been listening to us for sixty years, Idris. Through the war. Through the truce. Through all of it. It heard *something* worth asking about."

The lattice pulsed again. A new phrase, patient, unhurried, in the tone of a host who has waited a very long time and can wait a little longer. I bent over the deck to parse it, and Idris put his hand on my shoulder — not to steady me, I think, but because he needed to touch something human while the station spoke.

The phrase resolved. I read it twice. Then I read it a third time, because the third time was not for translation.

"It's asking," I said slowly, "how many of us are left. And then it's saying—" I stopped. Started again. "It's saying it has something for us. Something it's been keeping. And it wants to know if we've come *before the others*."

Idris went very still. "What others?"

"That's the thing," I said. "It didn't say *the humans*. It said *the armed ones*."

Outside, in the dark beyond the cinder star, at the edge of dead space, the fleet was already moving. I didn't know it yet. But the station did. It had felt them the way you feel weather in an old scar.

It had been waiting for us. And it was afraid.
