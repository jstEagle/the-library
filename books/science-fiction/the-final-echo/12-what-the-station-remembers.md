## 12. What the Station Remembers

The archive room of Kestrel-9 smelled like ozone and old honey.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left. Ozone from the relays cycling back to life after — by my best estimate — four hundred years of silence. And honey, or something so chemically adjacent to honey that my hindbrain refused to make the distinction, seeping from the walls themselves. The station's interior surfaces were grown, not built: some resinous biological composite that the aliens had cultivated the way we cultivate oak, and which, as it warmed under our portable heaters, exhaled a sweetness that made the whole place feel less like a ruin and more like something sleeping.

Idris stood in the doorway with his sidearm still holstered and his hand nowhere near it, which I had learned to read as his version of relaxation. "It's warm," he said. "Why is it warm?"

"Because something turned the heat on when I said *hello* in a dead language," I said. "I've decided not to think about that too hard before breakfast."

We'd been aboard for thirty-one hours. In that time the station had woken in stages, each one triggered by phrases from my old translations — the Vessic corpus, the work that had eaten my twenties and most of my sanity. A phrase opened a door. A phrase lit a corridor. A phrase, spoken into what I could only describe as an altar of smooth black stone near the archive chamber, had caused the walls to bloom with light in slow, tidal pulses, like a heart remembering its rhythm.

And now the archive itself was playing.

Not on screens. There were no screens. The broadcasts rendered themselves directly into the resin of the walls, light woven through material, images hanging in the amber like insects preserved across geological time. Idris called them murals. I corrected him twice before giving up. They weren't murals. They were memories with the volume turned down.

The first one showed a star.

Not our star — Kestrel-9's burned-out cinder hung outside the viewport, a dead coal wrapped in its own ash — but a younger one, blue-white and furious, and around it a civilization dense enough to look from orbit like mold spreading across fruit. Cities in rings. Cities in shells. Structures that climbed their own gravity wells and kept going, tethered to nothing, braided into orbital lattices so intricate the whole system looked like a mind contemplating itself.

"That's them," I said quietly. "Before."

"Before the war." Idris's voice did the thing it always did when he was trying to sound like an intelligence officer instead of a man standing in an alien cathedral at the edge of dead space. Flat. Professional. Unconvincing.

"No," I said. "Before a lot of things. Watch the star."

He watched the star.

Over what felt like minutes but was probably encoded centuries, the blue-white dimmed. Not dramatically — no supernova theatrics, no convenient apocalypse. It just... tired. The way a fire tires. The light in the mural shifted spectrum by spectrum, and with each shift, parts of the great lattice went dark. Not destroyed. *Sealed.* Whole rings of cities shuttering themselves like eyes closing, district by district, conserving.

"They were dying," I heard myself say, and my voice came out strange, stripped of the wryness I usually wore like armor. "The star was failing. Slowly. Over millennia. This isn't postwar footage, Idris. This is thousands of years before we ever pointed a telescope at this system and got nervous."

*

Here is what every officer in the fleet believed, what I had believed once too, back before the languages got into my blood: the remnant had started it. The first war, eleven years ago, three hundred thousand dead — the official history began with an alien strike on the Meridian colony relay, unprovoked, inexplicable, the act of a species that hated us for the crime of arriving.

I had spent two years of my life translating captured Vessic transmissions during the ceasefire negotiations, and even then, even elbow-deep in their grammar, I had never found hatred in it. I'd found grief so dense it resisted parsing. I'd told the negotiation committee this. They had thanked me, paid me, and filed my finding somewhere it could do no harm, because grief does not justify budgets and hatred does.

Now the walls of their dying station were showing me the receipts.

*

The second broadcast was a census.

I know how that sounds. But there was no other word for it. Numbers rendered as light — population figures cascading down the amber walls in columns of pulsing nodes, each node a life, the whole display shrinking generation over generation with the terrible patience of a graph nobody wants to read. I stood in front of it doing arithmetic in my head until Idris asked me what the numbers meant, and I told him:

"It's a hospice chart. They knew. They'd known for ten thousand years that the star was going, and they ran the projections, and they built all of this—" I gestured at the sealed cities, the lattices, the gorgeous engineered everything— "as a way to outlast it. And they almost did. Look." I touched the wall, and the light rippled around my fingers like water around a stone. "This plateau here. Four thousand years where the numbers hold. They stabilized. They were *winning*, against a dying sun, they were actually winning—"

"And then?"

I didn't answer right away, because the next segment answered for me, and answering first would have meant admitting I already understood what I was about to see.

The war.

But not our war. Theirs. An internal one — brief, vicious, rendered in the archive with a bluntness that suggested they had chosen not to curate it. Two factions of the remnant, split over the endgame. One faction wanted to spend everything remaining on a last gamble: convert the entire civilization into a departure fleet and abandon the galaxy entirely, riding some technology I couldn't yet parse toward whatever lay between galaxies. The other faction wanted to stay. To die at home, in the light of the star that had raised them, on principle, the way you might refuse to leave a parent's deathbed.

They fought over it. With weapons whose signatures I recognized from wreckage I'd helped catalog during the ceasefire — wreckage the fleet had attributed to attacks on us.

"Oh no," Idris said. He'd gone very still. "Oh, *no*. The Meridian strike. The opening attack. That wasn't—"

"It was the stay faction hitting the leaving faction's launch infrastructure," I said. "Or the reverse. I can't tell yet. Either way—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Either way, we were never in it. We were never *in it*, Idris. Three hundred thousand of our people died in the crossfire of somebody else's argument about euthanasia."

*

We sat down after that. Not because we decided to. Because our legs decided for us, more or less simultaneously, and we ended up on the floor of an alien archive with our backs against a wall full of someone else's grief, sharing a canteen of recycled water like teenagers at a funeral.

"The fleet has this whole doctrine," Idris said eventually. He was turning the canteen in his hands, not drinking. "Containment through strength. Preemptive posture. Every briefing I've ever sat in frames the remnant as a predator biding its time. Okonkwo quotes casualty figures from Meridian like scripture." He paused. "I quoted them myself. Twice. In formal assessments."

"I know. I read your file."

"You read my file?"

"I read everyone's file. It's a xenolinguist thing. We're nosy by professional necessity." I bumped my shoulder against his arm, which was as close to comfort as either of us had gotten good at. "For what it's worth, you also flagged the flicker's linguistic regularity and got overruled by a man who thinks grammar is a conspiracy."

"A man who is currently aiming a strike wing at this station," Idris said, "on a nineteen-day clock that is now a twelve-day clock, based on the premise that the flicker was a weapons test." He looked up at the walls, at the census numbers still cycling their slow diminuendo, and something in his face rearranged itself — the officer scaffolding coming down, not all at once but in pieces, like a stage set struck between acts. "Tamsin. It wasn't a weapons test."

"No."

"It was never a weapons test."

"It was a *beacon*." I stood, because sitting still had become impossible, because the shape of the truth was finally fully assembled in my head and I needed to move or scream or both. "Look at the sequence. Star fails, civilization splits, civil war, the leavers win — barely — and then what? Then this." I pulled the raw flicker recording up on my slate and held it against the wall-light, the waveform ghosting over the amber. "Nine seconds of structured signal folded into a gravity fluctuation, broadcast system-wide, in a compression scheme only someone who'd spent decades inside their language could possibly unpack. They didn't test a weapon. They rang a bell. They stood in their own burning house and rang a bell, and the message — the part I've decoded so far — isn't a threat and isn't even a plea. It's an *offer*."

"An offer."

"Coordinates. Charts. Everything they ever learned about this galaxy — every stable route, every habitable pocket, every hazard mapped across a hundred thousand years of exploration — packaged as an inheritance." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "They're leaving, Idris. Whatever's left of them is leaving the galaxy, and before they go, they're leaving the keys. To anyone. To *us*. To whoever bothered to listen instead of shooting."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. Somewhere deeper in the station, a relay hummed through its waking cycle, and the honey-smell thickened, and I thought: they built this place to outlive them on purpose. Not as a fortress. As a *will*.

Idris said, very quietly, "My entire career is built on the premise that these people are monsters."

"Your entire career is built on the premise that understanding them is impossible, so fear is the rational default." I crouched to his eye level. "It was a reasonable premise. Nobody gave you the data. The data was in a language only one burned-out xenolinguist in the whole damn fleet could read, and she was hauling cargo because the admiralty decided translators were a peacetime expense."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he'd looked at the flicker audio aboard Long Route, the way he'd looked when he chose, in the space of one breath, to become a traitor rather than arrest me. "Then we give them the data," he said. "All of it. Before the clock runs out."

*

We worked through what passed for night aboard a station orbiting a dead star — which is to say, we worked until exhaustion made the translations start lying to us, which is the occupational hazard of my particular madness. You push too deep into a non-human grammar and it starts pushing back, starts reorganizing the furniture of your thoughts, and then you find yourself translating confidence into error because the alien structure feels more true than your own skepticism.

Dara used to knock on my bunk wall when I talked in my sleep. She said I sounded like I was praying in a church with no god in it. I thought about her then, eleven systems away, heartbroken and loyal and safe, and I missed her with an ache that had edges.

The third broadcast segment found us anyway, auto-cycling while we dozed in shifts against the warm resin wall.

I woke to Idris shaking my shoulder, and his face was wet, which I had never seen and would have bet money on never seeing.

"Tamsin. Wake up. You need to see this one."

It was a message. A single transmission, archived and flagged — the flag-structure translated roughly as *final* — composed in the last years before the departure. And it was composed, I realized within the first few phrases, in *our* languages. Corrupted human transmissions from the war, harvested from the wreckage of our fleets, studied, mourned over, and learned. They had taken our dying radio chatter — the maydays, the last words, the prayers of people burning in the black — and they had taught themselves English from it. From our grief.

The message was short. I translated it aloud, haltingly, my throat closing around every second phrase:

*"We heard you dying, and we could not stop the dying, ours or yours. We are sorry. We were always sorry. When we go, we leave you the maps, so that you will not die lost as we nearly did. Forgive us the crossfire. We never aimed at you. We were aiming at ourselves."*

I got through it. Barely. Then I sat down on the floor of the archive of a species the fleet had taught me to fear, and I wept like the languages had never rewired me at all, like I was just a woman from the colonies crying over the sheer stupid waste of two civilizations that had spent eleven years pointing guns at each other across a misunderstanding the size of a galaxy.

Idris held onto me. Neither of us said anything for a long time. The walls pulsed their slow amber heartbeat, and the dead star wheeled past the viewport, and somewhere in the deep structure of the station, the farewell broadcast waited, patient as geology, for someone to carry it home.

When I finally found my voice, what came out was not grief. It was arithmetic.

"Twelve days," I said. "Okonkwo launches in twelve days unless we get this to him. And he'll call it a forgery — a defector's fabrication, enemy propaganda, whatever lets him keep his doctrine intact." I wiped my face and stood, and the wryness came back, because it always does; it's load-bearing. "So we don't send him an argument. We send him the archive. All of it. Raw, unfiltered, undeniable. Let the walls testify."

"How?" Idris asked. "This transmitter is four hundred years dead."

I looked at the altar of black stone. At the relays humming themselves awake section by section, responding to my voice like a dog responding to a name it half-remembered.

"I don't think it is," I said. "I think it's been waiting for someone to ask nicely."

I put my hand flat against the stone, took a breath, and spoke the oldest phrase in the Vessic corpus — the greeting, the one that had opened the first door, the one that had eaten my sleep for twenty years.

The station answered.

Every light in the archive surged at once, and beneath my palm the black stone went warm as skin, and somewhere far above us, in the antenna spines of Kestrel-9, something ancient began, slowly, to remember how to sing.

And then, threaded through the rising hum, faint but unmistakable, came a new signal from outside — a tight-beam transmission, military-grade encryption, source vector locked on the station.

Ashfall had found us.
