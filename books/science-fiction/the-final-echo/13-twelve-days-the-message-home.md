## 13. Twelve Days: The Message Home

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a transmission. Not the absence of sound — the relay hums constantly, a low organ note in the deck plates that I've decided to find comforting rather than haunted — but the absence of *answer*. You send twelve days of work, the proof of your life's meaning, out into the dark toward the people who taught you what language was for, and then you sit with your hands around a cup of recycled water and wait to be believed.

I have waited before. I waited three years for the linguistics board to accept my reconstruction of the Vessic tense system, and they did, eventually, with a footnote crediting my supervisor. I waited nine months for my mother to answer a letter about my father's funeral. Waiting is an old country of mine; I know its customs.

But this waiting had teeth.

* * *

The transmission itself took four hours to assemble and eleven seconds to send. Eleven seconds — the same duration as the flicker, which Idris noticed and I pretended not to, because I wasn't ready to decide whether that was poetry or omen.

We sent everything. The archive recordings from the station's memory vaults, timestamped centuries before humanity had engines worth the name. The star-charts, rendered in the aliens' own notation and in ours side by side. My full translation of the farewell broadcast, annotated, cross-referenced, keyed to every grammar I'd ever published so that any fleet linguist could verify the methodology in an afternoon. And the centerpiece, the thing I'd stayed up thirty hours polishing because I knew exactly how it would be attacked: a parallel text. The flicker signal from the Long Route's sensor logs on one line, the Kestrel-9 archive's stored copy of the same broadcast on the other, matching phrase for phrase, syllable for syllable. Two independent sources, separated by light-years and decades, saying the identical thing.

You could not forge that. I mean that literally — you could not forge it. The sensor logs were raw instrument data with chain-of-custody checksums intact, logged by a freighter's dumb, honest, un-hackable navigation brain while I was asleep in my bunk dreaming in someone else's grammar. To fake it, someone would have needed access to both ships' systems across two years of space, plus a fluency in Remnant deep-structure notation that, as far as I knew, existed in exactly one living head. Mine.

I said as much in the cover letter. Then, because despair makes me pedantic, I said it again in a technical appendix.

"Maybe lead with the emotional appeal next time," Idris suggested, reading over my shoulder. "Fleets are famously moved by technical appendices."

"Fleets are moved by nothing. That's rather the point of them."

"That's slander. I'm moved by several things. Coffee. You. The thought of Admiral Okonkwo's face when—"

"When she reads appendix B."

"Especially appendix B."

He was doing his job, I realized later. He was keeping me upright with jokes while I pressed SEND on the most important message anyone had ever entrusted to me, because if I let myself feel its actual weight I would have curled up under the console and stayed there until Ashfall arrived. He'd learned my tells faster than any student I'd ever had. When my hands went still over the keys — truly still, the way they only went still when the old languages were moving through me too fast to speak — he would put his hand flat on the desk between us, palm down, an anchor point, and I would breathe until the flood passed.

Twelve days left. We sent the truth home at 0400 station time, and then we waited.

* * *

The reply came in nineteen hours, which by fleet standards was either contemptuous speed or respectful diligence, depending on how you wanted to be hurt.

It was addressed not to me but to Idris, rank-conscious even in treachery, and it was short enough to read twice before the sting landed.

*RECEIVED AND LOGGED. MATERIAL ASSESSED AS FABRICATED. SOURCE COMPROMISED (SEE ATTACHMENT A). NO FURTHER TRANSMISSIONS WILL BE ACKNOWLEDGED. SURRENDER TERMS REMAIN AS PREVIOUSLY COMMUNICATED.*

Attachment A was a psychological profile. Mine. Assembled, judging by the citations, from my academy evaluations, my medical records, my crew psych reviews going back fifteen years, and — this was the part I read four times, the part that made something cold settle behind my sternum — Dara's debrief transcript.

Dara. Who had held my hair back through translation fevers, who had learned to make tea the way I liked it without being asked, who had stood on the Long Route's loading dock and watched us go with her jaw set like a woman refusing to cry in front of cargo. She hadn't betrayed me. Of course she hadn't. They'd sat her in a room with a stenographer and asked the right questions in the right order, and she'd answered honestly, because Dara didn't know how to do anything else, and honesty in that room sounds exactly like testimony.

*"Subject exhibits classic pattern of occupational identity dissolution,"* the profile read. *"Decades of immersion in non-human linguistic structures have produced measurable cognitive drift. Subject reports dreaming in unknown languages. Subject has described her own work as 'being spoken through.' Assessment: subject sincerely believes her translations. This sincerity is itself evidence of compromised reliability."*

"They used my own words," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "The dream reports. I gave those to a fleet physician in confidence eight years ago. For treatment."

"Everything is evidence," Idris said quietly. "That's the first thing they teach you in intelligence school. Everything you say is evidence, everything you don't say is evidence, and everything you feel is a vulnerability assessment waiting to happen." He paused. "I wrote profiles like that. Three of them. Good ones."

"I know."

"You know?"

"Idris. I've read your file. I've read it more carefully than you have, probably. There's a commendation in there for a threat assessment on the Meridian colony delegation that got two diplomats detained for a year." I turned to look at him. "They were innocent. It's in the follow-up inquiry. Buried, but it's there."

He didn't flinch, which I'd learned was his version of flinching. "Yes."

"And you're here anyway."

"And I'm here anyway." He set the tablet down with the gentleness of a man disarming something. "Tamsin. The profile isn't wrong about the mechanism. You do drift. You talk in your sleep. I've heard you. Some nights it doesn't sound human and I lie awake cataloguing which phonemes belong to which dead civilization, and I love you, and I still hear it." He exhaled. "What's wrong isn't the observation. It's the conclusion. They looked at a woman who can hear things no one else can, and instead of asking what she hears, they decided the hearing itself is the disease."

"That's because the hearing is inconvenient."

"The hearing is *inconvenient*, yes." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "And in eleven days it becomes militarily decisive. So they'll bury it. They're already burying it."

* * *

Idris's own message went out that evening, against my advice and with my blessing, which is the kind of contradiction long relationships are built on.

His old commander was a man named Halvorsen — Colonel, retired, living out his days on Thessaly Drift teaching strategy to cadets too young to remember the war. Idris had served under him for six years. More than served: the file notes I'd skimmed made it clear Halvorsen had pulled Idris out of a burning boarding action at Ceres Gate, had carried him, physically, through a decompressed corridor, and had never once mentioned it afterward. Some debts don't get discussed. They just get honored, silently, for decades.

The message was not diplomatic. Idris drafted it seven times, and I watched each draft get shorter and plainer, the officer-polish sanding off until what remained was bare wood:

*Sir. You taught me that intelligence exists to keep fleets from fighting stupid wars. I am sending you, under private seal, material the Admiralty has classified as forgery. It is not forgery. Verify it yourself — you still know people at the Ceres archives who owe you favors. If I'm wrong, court-martial me when this is over. If I'm right, you have ten days to make someone listen. You carried me once. I'm asking you to carry this.*

He sent it at 2200. Then he did the thing I was coming to recognize as his ritual for unbearable waiting: he cleaned. Stripped, field-dressed, and reassembled the station's ancient airlock seals, recalibrated the water recycler, realigned a storage rack that had been crooked since before the war. I sat in the archive vault translating the aliens' elegiac registers and listened to him move through the station like a man sweeping a floor that would not stay swept.

The reply window closed at 0600. Nothing.

At 1200, nothing.

At 1800, Idris checked the transmission log one more time and found what I had already found and hadn't told him, because I am not always a good person: the message had been received. Confirmed receipt, standard handshake, 2214 hours. Delivered into Halvorsen's inbox and sitting there, unread-flagged or read-and-unanswered, for twenty hours.

"He's deciding," Idris said.

"Yes."

"Or he's been told not to answer. Private-seal traffic from a flagged traitor — they'd flag the recipient too. One word from Halvorsen and he loses his pension, maybe worse." He braced both hands on the console. "He might be composing something right now. Something careful. Something that takes—"

"Idris."

"—something that takes time, because Halvorsen was always—"

"*Idris.*"

He stopped. In the vault's low amber light he looked suddenly very young, which was absurd, because the man was forty-one and had a face like a cliff that had seen weather. But there it was. Hope does that. Hope strips people down to whatever age they were when they first learned to need someone.

"If he answers," Idris said, "we might live. If he doesn't—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. If Halvorsen didn't answer, then the last thread between us and the institution that raised him was cut, and all that remained was Ashfall, inbound, and a clock ticking down from twelve.

The silence stretched. Somewhere above us, the relay's antenna turned slowly on its ancient bearing, tracking nothing, listening to everything.

"He'll answer," Idris said finally, and I heard the boy in it, the lieutenant at Ceres Gate, the man who had requested to go alone because he told himself it was procedure. "He has to."

I put my hand over his on the console and said nothing, because I had spent thirty years learning the difference between the silences that comfort and the silences that lie, and I could not bring myself to lie to him in either direction.

* * *

Three days later — nine days to expiry — the answer came.

Not from Halvorsen. From the fleet.

A single burst transmission, fleet-standard encryption, addressed to *the occupant(s) of Station Kestrel-9*. No names. No signature block beyond a command authentication code that Idris verified twice and then verified a third time with shaking hands, because the code belonged to Admiral Okonkwo personally.

*FINAL NOTICE. YOUR TRANSMISSIONS HAVE BEEN REVIEWED AND ASSESSED. THE MATERIAL IS A SOPHISTICATED DECEPTION, CONSISTENT WITH KNOWN REMNANT INFORMATION OPERATIONS. YOU ARE INFORMED THAT STRIKE WING ASHFALL HAS BEEN DISPATCHED TO YOUR POSITION. ITS ORDERS INCLUDE THE DESTRUCTION OF STATION KESTREL-9 AND ALL ASSETS THEREIN. THIS ORDER WILL NOT BE RESCINDED. YOU MAY TRANSMIT SURRENDER INTENTIONS ON THIS CHANNEL UNTIL 0000, DAY SEVEN. AFTER THAT TIME, NO FURTHER CONTACT WILL BE ACCEPTED OR ANSWERED.*

Below the official text, one line more, in plain unencrypted characters, the digital equivalent of a whisper:

*Halvorsen tried. — R.*

Reyes. The Ashfall commander. I didn't know her yet — wouldn't, not really, not for days — but I understood the shape of that sentence immediately. Someone inside the machine had risked a finger's width of herself to tell us the machine was coming. Halvorsen had tried, and failed, and someone named R. had watched him fail and chosen, illegally, to let us know.

"They're going to kill us for telling the truth," Idris said. He said it the way you'd report the weather. Flat. Final. The voice of a man whose last hope had just been shot down in front of him.

"No," I said.

He looked at me.

"They're going to try to kill the *message*," I said. "We're incidental. We've always been incidental, Idris — that's the whole architecture of institutions, they exist so that no individual ever has to be responsible for anything. Okonkwo isn't destroying us. She's destroying the possibility that she was wrong. That's the asset Ashfall is carrying. Not missiles. Deniability."

"That's a distinction without a difference when you're standing on the target."

"It's the only distinction that matters." I pulled up the archive index, the vast patient catalogue of everything the dying species had left behind, and felt the old flood rising in me — the one where language stops being a tool and becomes a tide. "Because if it's the message they fear, then the message is the weapon. And weapons, Commander Vane, can be aimed."

Nine days. Nine days to teach an entire fleet to hear a farewell.

I opened the translation files and began, for the second time in my life, to build a language from scratch — this time not for scholars, not for boards and appendices, but for frightened people at consoles who had eleven seconds of a flicker and a lifetime of war, and who needed, in nine days, to understand a goodbye.

Behind me, Idris wiped his eyes once, quickly, the way soldiers do, and started cleaning something that didn't need cleaning.

* * *

At 0300, unable to sleep, I checked the private channel one last time.

There was a message waiting. Unencrypted. Four words, no authentication, no signature, sent from an address that the routing headers showed had bounced through six relays before reaching us, scrubbed, deniable, the work of a man who had spent his life teaching officers how to fight stupid wars and had finally decided to practice what he preached:

*Verify the charts. Trust her.*

Halvorsen. Alive, unarrested, and — against every institutional instinct he owned — believing.

I woke Idris. I put the tablet in his hands and watched him read it, watched the cliff-face of him crack along some fault line twenty years deep, and when he looked up at me his eyes were wet and furious and lit from underneath with something I recognized from my own mirror: the terrible, unkillable, completely unreasonable conviction that if one person can be reached, the message is not dead.

"One person," he said hoarsely.

"One person," I agreed. "That's how every language I ever learned survived. One speaker at a time, refusing to stop talking."

Eight days. Somewhere beyond the shipping lanes, Ashfall was falling toward us at burn velocity, and Admiral Okonkwo's launch protocols were counting down on automated timers no human hand had touched in weeks.

I turned back to the console, and began to translate.
