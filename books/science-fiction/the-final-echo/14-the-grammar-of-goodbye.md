## 14. The Grammar of Goodbye

The thing about grief is that it doesn't announce itself. It arrives the way water arrives in a hull breach — quietly at first, a bead of condensation you wipe away with your sleeve, and then one morning the whole bulkhead is weeping and you're standing in it up to your knees wondering how long you'd been pretending not to notice.

I had been pretending for about three days.

Idris found me at 0400 station-time — Kestrel-9 kept its own hours now, synced to nothing, a clock orbiting a dead star out of sheer stubbornness — asleep at the translation console with my cheek on the interface plate and my hands still moving, typing phrases into the archive search that I didn't remember composing. He didn't wake me. Later he told me he stood there for eleven minutes watching me work while unconscious, and that it was the most frightening thing he'd seen since the war, including the war.

"You were speaking," he said over breakfast, which aboard Kestrel-9 meant reconstituted protein and coffee so old it had developed opinions. "In your sleep. But it wasn't any of their languages. It wasn't anything."

"It was all of them," I said, before my brain could stop my mouth.

He set down his cup. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning I've been dreaming in the archive." I turned my spool of caf in slow circles, watching the vortex. "The final echo isn't a message, Idris. I keep treating it like a message — parse the grammar, extract the payload, ship the coordinates home. That's what a linguist does. That's what *I* do. But I finally ran the full structural analysis last night, and the charts, the star-maps, the inheritance — they're not the content." I looked up at him. "They're the envelope."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about dead languages: they don't die because the speakers got careless. They die because the speakers died, and the languages go where the speakers go, which is nowhere, which is everywhere, which is into the mouths of people like me who spend forty years learning to pronounce syllables for an audience of zero.

The Vessari — the remnant, the enemy, whatever the admiralty's filing system called them this week — had been dying for longer than humanity had been spacefaring. We knew that now from the archives, but knowing it as data and knowing it as language are different animals. The data said: population decline, genetic attrition, voluntary withdrawal. The language said something the data couldn't hold.

Because here's what I found when I stopped trying to read the final echo and started trying to *hear* it:

It was composed. Not generated, not broadcast, not encoded by machine. Composed — the way a requiem is composed, the way a eulogy is written by someone who keeps stopping to blow their nose. And it was composed in layers. The outermost layer was pure mathematics, the universal handshake, the part any civilization with radio could decode. Beneath that, the star-charts, the inheritance, the gift. And beneath *that* —

Beneath that was every language they had ever learned.

All of them. Every species they'd ever contacted across their long dimming history, every pidgin and trade-tongue and greeting protocol, preserved like flowers pressed between pages. And threaded through them, woven in like dark thread through bright —

Human words.

Corrupted human transmissions from the war. Fleet encryption keys broken mid-battle. A distress call from the *Meridian*, the colony ship lost in the first engagement — I recognized the voice pattern, we all did, it was in every memorial broadcast for a decade. Chunks of propaganda. A child's school lesson in Standard, intercepted from some frontier station. Fragments of our own chatter, our own noise, gathered up and carried inside their farewell like shells collected on a beach by someone who knows they're leaving the ocean forever.

"They weren't studying us," I told Idris. My voice did something embarrassing on the last word and I let it. "They were *memorializing* us. We're in there. Humanity is in there, in the goodbye, filed between the Ossuary Canticles of a species that went extinct eight thousand years ago and the mating songs of something from the galactic core. They learned us. They kept us. And then they put us in the will."

Idris was quiet for a long time. He'd gotten good at quiet, these past weeks; I'd taught him the grammar of listening and he'd turned out to be fluent.

"Show me the Meridian fragment," he said finally.

* * *

I want to explain what translating a dead species' farewell feels like, and I'm going to fail, because the honest answer is that it feels like being the last speaker of yourself.

The Vessari tongues — there were nine major ones, and the truce documents used only the diplomatic register of the third, which is like describing a cathedral by its fire exit — each had a different relationship to time. Human languages mostly point forward. We say *will*, we say *going to*, we build futures out of grammar the way beavers build dams. The third Vessari tongue pointed forward too, but the first tongue — the oldest one, the one they composed the echo's heart in — pointed *backward through forward*. Its future tense was built from memory. To say *we will end* in First Vessari, you had to begin by saying everything you remembered, and the ending arrived as the sum of it.

So the farewell wasn't a statement. It was an accumulation. You couldn't translate the last line without carrying every line before it, and every line before it was a remembered world, a remembered friend, a remembered enemy, a remembered song. The structure itself was the sentiment. The grammar *was* the grief.

I sat in the archive cradle for six hours translating the opening movement, and when I came out Idris took one look at me and steered me to a chair and put the coffee in my hands and waited, because he'd learned that too.

"Read it to me," he said. "Your translation. Not the notes. The actual words."

"The actual words don't exist yet in a form you'd — fine. Fine." I closed my eyes, because looking at him while saying it was impossible. "This is rough. This is a linguist's crib, not poetry. It opens: *We were many, and the many was good. We were fewer, and the fewer was also good, because the fewer remembered the many more carefully. Now we are few, and soon we will be memory, and memory is the only country that never falls.*"

"That's the opening?"

"That's the first forty seconds of a transmission that runs eleven days."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been rationed lately. "Eleven days."

"The inheritance alone takes four. The maps have to be sung, not sent — the notation is performative, the coordinates carry harmonic checksums, if you transmit them flat they corrupt. Whoever receives this has to *sing the galaxy back to themselves* to prove they understood it. It's not a gift, it's an exam. It's — " I stopped. My throat had closed.

"Tamsin."

"It's a lullaby," I said. "The whole thing. Eleven days of farewell structured like the songs they sang to their children when the children were dying, back before the war, back when their whole species was a pediatric ward. They're singing us to sleep with the galaxy. They're tucking the inheritors in. And underneath, in every language they ever learned — " I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "Underneath, in corrupted Standard, in broken fleet cipher, in the *Meridian*'s last distress call, they included us. In the goodbye. As things remembered. As things worth remembering."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the relay's ancient bones, a coolant pump cycled, a sound like a long exhale.

"We spent thirty years calling them the enemy," Idris said. His voice had gone very level, the way voices go when they're holding something heavy at arm's length. "We built a truce on their silence and a war plan on their flicker. And the whole time they were — "

"Writing our eulogy into theirs. Yes." I lowered my hands. "And here's the part that broke me, Idris, the part I can't get past. The Meridian fragment. They didn't just record it. They *answered* it."

"What?"

"The distress call had a gap — ninety seconds of carrier silence where the crew was presumably evacuating or dying or both. The Vessari filled the gap. In the archive version, the Meridian's call continues past where it ended in our records. Someone answered those people, in Standard, badly, beautifully, in the accent of a species that had learned human speech from intercepted traffic and never once spoken it aloud to a living human. And the answer says — " I had translated it four times and it undid me every time, so I said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "*You are heard. You are heard. We cannot reach you and we are sorry. Your name is entered among the remembered. Sleep well.*"

Idris didn't move for a moment. Then he said, very quietly, "The Meridian's black box was recovered. There was no reply on it."

"No. Because the reply came after the box stopped recording. Or because whoever answered chose frequencies the dying couldn't hear, so the words would exist for the living instead." I shrugged, helplessly. "Or both. Grief does strange bookkeeping."

* * *

I worked for two more days. I barely slept, and when I slept I dreamed in the archive, walked through corridors built of other species' sentences, and woke with my pillow wet and no memory of why, which at my age you learn not to examine too closely.

On the second day I finished the movement I'd come to think of as the Roll Call — the section where the farewell names what it leaves behind, and I discovered that the naming wasn't random. It was ordered. The Vessari had arranged their inheritance in the sequence things should be grieved: first the small losses, then the large, then the irreplaceable, then — last, given the place of honor, sung in the richest register, in *our* diplomatic language, in the tongue the truce was written in, the tongue they had learned specifically to speak peace to us —

Us. Humanity. Listed last. Listed as the most irreplaceable thing.

Not because we mattered most in any cosmic ledger. I checked; I'm a professional, I checked the harmonic weights twice. We mattered most because we were the newest. Because we were the ones who arrived just in time to watch them go. Because to a dying species, the young are the ones you entrust things to, and we were the youngest thing they'd ever met.

"They left us the maps because we're the children," I said to Idris that night, in the observation blister, with the burned-out star turning slowly below us like a coal going out. "That's the whole revelation. Thirty years of war and truce and paranoia, and structurally, grammatically, in the deepest layer of the deepest register — we're the kids. The heirs. The ones you tell the stories to so the stories survive."

"And the flicker? The nine seconds?" He'd asked before; he always asked again; I think he needed to hear it the way you need to hear a diagnosis repeated.

"A doorbell," I said. "Rung in every language at once, because they didn't know which one we'd answer. Addressed to whoever still listened. And the admiralty wants to answer it with missiles."

Below us the dead star pulsed its slow infrared nothing. Above us, nineteen days had become twelve, and somewhere beyond the edge of dead space, Ashfall was coming, though we didn't know it yet — we'd learn that tomorrow, when Reyes's demand for surrender crackled in on frequencies we'd stopped monitoring, and Idris would walk an airlock line alone to buy us hours with nothing but words.

But that night we didn't know. That night there was only the translation, complete at last, all eleven days of it compressed into a data-spike no bigger than my thumb, and the question I couldn't stop asking myself:

Would anyone sing it back?

I had transmitted proof to the fleet already. Okonkwo's office had buried it — *forgery, psychological operation, compromised source* — and Idris's private appeal to his old commander had vanished into the same silence. Institutions, I was learning, are machines for converting evidence into paperwork. The truth sat in my hand like a seed, and the ground was concrete, and the clock was running.

"I need to send it again," I said. "All of it. Not the summary, not the abstract — the raw echo, the full eleven days, on every channel, wideband, unencrypted. Let the whole system hear the Meridian's answer. Let them hear their own war-chatter woven into a lullaby. You can bury a report, Idris. You can't bury a song once everyone's heard it."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched the officer in him and the man in him conduct some brief internal negotiation, and I already knew which side would win, because he'd boarded my ship to arrest me and stayed to learn grammar.

"The transmitter," he said slowly. "The main array. It has the power for a broadcast like that?"

"It has the power for exactly one," I said. "Full-power, full-spectrum, eleven-day continuous transmission. After that the array's capacitors are slag and the relay goes dark for good." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like a word in a language I was only beginning to speak. "One shot. One song. Sung once, into the dark, for whoever's listening."

"Then we'd better make sure the whole system is listening," Idris said.

Neither of us knew yet what that would cost. Neither of us knew that the price would be counted in heat and air and blood, that the boarding parties were already burning toward us out of the dark, that the choice was coming down to a single terrible arithmetic: the song, or survival, and never both.

That night, I dreamed wordlessly for the first time in decades. No language at all. Just stars, leaving — a whole sky of them, turning politely, like guests at the end of a party, bowing on their way out the door.

And in the dream, someone was singing them home.
