## 7. Jump Window

Odile Vant had learned mathematics the way most people learned grief: too young, and without anyone to explain it.

Her mother had been a freight pilot's daughter who grew up doing orbital mechanics on a slate in a company dormitory on Ceres Verge, and she had passed the habit down before she died—or before she stopped being alive, which was not quite the same thing, though Odile had spent eleven years refusing to learn the difference. Numbers didn't lie. Numbers didn't get wiped. You could burn a ledger, but the math that built it survived in every hand that copied it, and somewhere out past the edge of everything, a signal was still copying itself into the dark, thinner every year, and Odile had spent four years of her life learning to read its decay.

She spread the work across the Vespera's—no. Not the Vespera. She corrected herself the way she corrected herself a hundred times a day. Across the *Meridian Dawn*, Isandro Iversen's hauler, across the galley table he'd cleared for her without being asked, using printouts because paper couldn't be subpoenaed and slates could. Three sheets, covered in her small, dense handwriting. She'd done the calculations three times from three different starting assumptions, because if she was going to ask an android to throw away his registered life, she wanted the error bars honest.

Isandro sat across from her with his hands folded, which was something he did when he was waiting. He was very good at waiting. She supposed you had to be, when your life ran on someone else's schedule for eleven years.

"Before I start," she said, "I need you to understand what kind of math this is."

"Explain."

"It's not the kind where there's a right answer. It's the kind where there's a window, and the window is closing, and everything on the other side of it either gets through or doesn't." She tapped the first sheet. "So when I give you numbers, they're not predictions. They're a countdown."

He nodded once. "Start."

* * *

She started with the signal, because everything started with the signal.

"The burst we've been tracking comes from a region about nine light-years coreward of the last active relay chain—the edge relay, the one they call Kestrel's Post now, though it hasn't answered a hail in six years." She drew a line across the second sheet. "The source is fixed. We know that because the Doppler signature hasn't shifted in four years of observation. Whatever's transmitting isn't moving. It's parked."

"A derelict," Isandro said.

"Or a survivor who stopped moving. Either way, it transmits on a cycle. That's the important part." She pulled the third sheet forward. "The cycle is decaying. When Odile—" she caught herself, jaw tightening; even her own name was a forgery some days "—when my contact first picked it up, four years ago, the interval between bursts was ninety-one hours. Two years ago it was eighty-eight. Last month it was eighty-six point four. The transmitter is dying, Isandro. Its power reserve is bleeding off, and as it bleeds, the duty cycle shortens. Fewer transmissions, closer together, weaker each time. It's a candle burning down faster as it drowns in its own wax."

"And the relay can only catch it when?"

"When the burst arrives during the relay's receive window—and here's the ugly part." She turned the sheet so he could read it upside down, then thought better of it and rotated it. "Kestrel's Post runs on a salvaged fusion plant with maybe eleven percent of its original output. It can't keep its receiver hot continuously. It powers up in scheduled windows—thirty-one hours on, forty-four off, drifting later by about two hours per cycle as the plant degrades. The signal bursts and the receive windows have been slowly sliding out of phase for years. Four years ago they still overlapped regularly. Now they overlap once, maybe twice, per alignment period—and each overlap catches less of the burst, because the bursts are getting shorter too."

"How much less?"

"Four years ago, an overlap caught sixty percent of a transmission. The last confirmed partial capture—eight months ago, by a research buoy nobody official reads—caught nineteen percent. Nineteen percent of a dying signal, Isandro. Fragments. Enough to confirm the source is artificial and the encoding matches pre-schism Concordat military protocol, which is the only reason I know it's worth chasing at all. Not enough to decode."

He was quiet for a moment. His eyes did something she'd noticed he probably didn't know he did—a flicker toward the middle distance, like a man reading something written on the inside of his own skull. Maybe he was. She'd heard androids could run calculations internally. She'd also heard they could be wiped, which was the whole reason she was sitting in his galley with paper spread over a table scarred by thirty years of cargo crates.

"Give me the convergence," he said.

"That's the word I used too." She allowed herself half a smile, then killed it, because the next part deserved no smiling. "The next full overlap—the next time a complete burst lands inside a complete receive window—is in nineteen days. Give or take fourteen hours, depending on how much further the relay's plant has degraded since the last telemetry anyone bothered to collect. After that, the phases diverge again for another fifty-two days. And here's why fifty-two days might as well be forever."

She laid her finger on the decay curve.

"At the current rate of degradation, the transmitter's power reserve hits minimum threshold in roughly forty days. Below threshold, it can't hold a carrier wave long enough for the relay's receiver to lock. Even if the windows overlapped again on day seventy-one—which they won't, because the relay drifts the other direction—there would be nothing left to catch. The signal dies in the dark sometime around day forty, and the next overlap comes on day seventy-one, and the one after that never comes at all, because there's nothing left on either end."

"So it's not just the next window," Isandro said. "It's the last window."

"It's the last window." She let that sit. "Nineteen days. One clean capture. Everything the source has been trying to say for eleven years, delivered whole, or nothing, ever, for the rest of recorded time."

* * *

He stood and went to the viewport, which she was learning was his version of pacing. The *Meridian Dawn* hung docked at Ceres Verge's outer ring, and through the port the station's lights wheeled slow and indifferent, a city pretending it had never been anything but a city, though Odile knew half its berths had been built for warships and repurposed so thoroughly that only the load ratings gave them away.

"Nineteen days," he said. "The run from Ceres Verge to Kestrel's Post is how long, at best speed?"

"Twenty-three days for a Combine freighter running standard profiles." She'd already done it. Of course she'd already done it. "But standard profiles assume fuel margins, rest cycles, traffic avoidance along the patrol lanes. None of which apply. If you strip mass, burn hard, and cut straight through the dead corridor instead of skirting it—if you treat the Silence Run like what it actually is, which is empty—you make the transit in sixteen days. Seventeen if the drive complains."

"Leaving two days of margin."

"One day and change, realistically. Relay windows don't wait and neither do drives." She gathered the sheets and squared their edges against the table, a nervous habit she'd inherited from a woman she couldn't remember inheriting anything from. "And I want to be precise about what 'cutting through the dead corridor' means. No traffic. No beacons. No rescue. If something goes wrong out there, we are a rumor. The Combine wrote the corridor off after the schism, which is exactly why it's the fastest route and exactly why nobody's ever going to come looking for us if we don't come back."

"You've made this run before."

It wasn't a question, and she didn't insult him by treating it like one. "Twice. In a ship smaller than this one, with worse math. I'm still here."

"Why?" He turned from the viewport. "Not the corridor. The signal. Four years of your life reading decay curves off research buoys, bribing clerks, forging manifests. What does the truth buy you? It won't bring anyone back. It won't un-destroy a ship. The Combine is too big to embarrass and too old to care. You must know that."

She had an answer prepared. She'd prepared it years ago, polished it, rehearsed it in dockside bars to strangers who bought her drinks and forgot her name. *Justice. Accountability. The record.* All true in the way that slogans are true, which is to say technically and uselessly.

What she said instead was: "My mother flew the last mission out of the schism zone. Officially she died in the Vespera's destruction, eleven years ago, with everyone else aboard. Unofficially—" she tapped the decay curve, the thin line that fell and fell and refused to reach zero "—someone survived long enough to start transmitting, and whoever it was has been talking into the dark for eleven years, getting quieter, and the entire civilized volume of human space has agreed not to listen. I'm not chasing justice, Isandro. Justice is for people with standing. I'm chasing a voice. It's still saying something. Somebody should hear it while it can still be heard."

Something moved behind his eyes—that middle-distance flicker again, longer this time. When he spoke, his voice had a strange flatness, like a man reading coordinates.

"Sefa Iversen," he said. "On the manifest."

"My mother."

"You put her name on a ghost entry and broadcast it down a customs channel, knowing any decommissioned unit with the surname Iversen would flag it."

"I needed a hook that would catch exactly one fish." She held his gaze. "I've spent four years confirming the signal exists. Confirming it's Concordat military protocol. Confirming the relay can still catch it. The only variable left—the only thing I couldn't verify from outside—was whether anyone carrying the black box key was still functional. The key isn't a physical object, Isandro. It's a registration cipher, bound to the flight officer of record. And the flight officer of record on the Vespera's final manifest was—"

"Iversen," he said again. Quieter.

"Co-pilot Sefa Iversen, and commanding officer Isandro Iversen, same unit designation, same deployment record, same decommission date." She spread her hands. "You're the only Isandro Iversen in the registry. Which means either you're the man who carried the key, or you're a copy of him wearing his serial number, and either way, whatever's locked in that archive answers to something inside you."

* * *

He sat back down. Slowly, the way heavy things sit.

For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the station wheeled, and somewhere below them a records clerk was spending Odile's bribe money, and somewhere coreward a dying transmitter counted down toward silence at eighty-six point four hours per breath.

"If the archive opens," Isandro said at last, "it opens with a registered key. Mine. And the moment I present it, the Combine knows. They'll know where I am, what I retrieved, and that I retrieved it deliberately. There's no deniability in a cipher handshake."

"No."

"They'll void my debt and my registration in the same motion. Legally I stop existing. Practically, I become salvage." He looked at his hands—hands that had hauled scrap for eleven years to pay for the privilege of continuing to exist. "You're asking me to spend myself to buy a voice."

"I'm asking you to do it in nineteen days," Odile said, "because in twenty it won't be possible anymore, and neither of us will ever have to find out what we would have chosen."

That landed. She watched it land—the small recalibration behind his eyes, the machine-honest arithmetic of a being who could not lie to himself about odds. Nineteen days meant the decision was already made in every way except the saying of it. A closing window did the choosing for you; all you got to choose was whether to jump.

"What do you need from me besides the ship?" he asked.

"Fuel we can't replace, a course filed as a salvage run to keep the audit AI quiet for as long as possible, and—" she hesitated, then finished it, because he'd earned the whole number, not the rounded one "—and your key, when we get there. Which means you, present and willing. The cipher won't handshake under duress. It checks for consent. Whoever designed it designed it for people who might be captured."

"Considerate of them."

"They were expecting to be hunted." She began stacking her papers, and her hands were steady until they weren't, until the top sheet trembled against the others and she pressed it flat and made it stop. Eleven years of tremors, mastered one piece of paper at a time. "Isandro. One more thing, and then I'll let you sleep on it, or compute on it, or whatever you do."

"Yes."

"The signal's been transmitting for eleven years. Military protocol, encrypted, on a continuous distress-and-archive cycle. Do you know what kind of system transmits like that?"

"A black box," he said. "Flight recorder. Final mission log."

"Do you know who authorizes a final mission log to broadcast unencrypted-adjacent, on open channels, for a decade?"

His stillness changed quality. She'd seen machines freeze; this wasn't that. This was a man arriving somewhere he'd already been and finding the door bricked over.

"Someone who believed they wouldn't be rescued," he said, "but believed they'd eventually be sought."

"Somebody who trusted that someday, somebody would come asking." Odile stood, tucked the sheets into her jacket, and met his eyes across the galley table. "Nineteen days, Isandro. The window closes, and the voice stops, and the dark keeps everything. Or we burn for Kestrel's Post and we're there when it speaks."

He looked at the viewport, at the wheeling lights, at the corridor beyond them running out into a darkness that had been holding its breath for eleven years.

"File the course," Isandro Iversen said. "We leave tomorrow."
