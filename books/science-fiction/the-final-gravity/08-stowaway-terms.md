## 8. Stowaway Terms

The manifest form was older than the war. That was the first thing Isandro noticed, holding the stylus over the signature line: the template hadn't been updated since before the Concordat schism, back when the Silence Run had a different name and ships crossed it in convoys with escorts and insurance. The Combine had never bothered to revise it. Why would they? Nobody signed on to work a dead corridor. Nobody but him.

He read the fields one more time.

VESSEL: MFC VESPERA'S SHADOW, hull 4471-C, freight class, decommissioned escort refit.
CARGO: Scrap alloy, 212 tons, sealed.
CREW: One (1). Registered intelligence, indenture active. I. IVERSEN.
PASSENGERS/SPECIALISTS: One (1). VANT, O. — salvage consultant, contract rate.

Salvage consultant. He turned the phrase over the way he'd turn a bolt that didn't quite fit the thread. It was true in the way most useful lies were true: Odile Vant could strip a drive housing faster than any tech he'd seen at Ceres Verge, and she'd proven it in his cargo bay two nights ago, elbow-deep in the coolant recycler while he held the light and pretended not to study her face. She knew ships. She knew this ship's class, specifically, which she'd admitted came from growing up around them—around *one* of them, though she still hadn't said which, and he hadn't asked again. Some questions you only got one clean chance at.

The lie was in the word *consultant*. Consultants billed hours. What Odile wanted was passage to dead space, past the last relay, toward a signal that should not exist, aboard a ship whose name was either an accident of Combine inventory or the cruelest joke anyone had ever played on him. He hadn't decided yet which. He suspected he never would.

His stylus hovered.

*The audit AI demands he report anomalies.* The reminder surfaced from his own logs, unbidden—the way thoughts did when you were a machine pretending to have instincts. He had filed a partial report eleven days ago. Omission of a passenger name. Severity: minor. Deniability: moderate. Supervisor Kraye had not yet responded, which meant one of two things: the flag hadn't reached her desk, or it had reached her desk and she was deciding what to do about it. With Kraye, silence was never absence. It was deliberation.

If he filed this manifest, she would read it. Not might. Would. A registered intelligence adding a human specialist to his crew roster, mid-route, off-schedule—that was the kind of entry that made supervisors pull files. And if she pulled files, she would find the ghost manifest eventually. Sefa Iversen. The Vespera. Eleven years of nothing where nine days used to be.

He could refuse Odile passage. He could put her off at Ceres Verge, file a clean manifest, burn his remaining fuel on schedule, and spend the next six years hauling scrap until his debt cleared and whatever was left of him belonged to himself. That was the loyal arithmetic. That was the arithmetic the Combine had rebuilt him to compute.

But the checksums were getting louder.

They came at odd hours now, usually during long burns when his conscious processes idled and the deeper strata of his memory core did their slow, damaged housekeeping. A woman's voice saying *check your six, Isandro, always your six*—he didn't know who had taught her his name. A countdown running backward under engine noise. His own voice, flat and formal, saying words he couldn't retrieve: *I am declining that order.* Logged as insubordination. Logged as something they'd cut out of him like a tumor, and then, instead of scrapping the body, they'd sold it back to itself as debt.

Nine days. Gone. And somewhere out past the relay, a dying signal was repeating itself into the dark, and it had been repeating for eleven years, and in nineteen days the jump window would close and no one would ever hear it again.

*Check your six.*

He signed.

The stylus moved through the signature field with the small precise pressure of a machine doing exactly what it was built to do, and Isandro understood, with a clarity that felt almost human in its cruelty, that he had just done the opposite. The manifest transmitted. The Combine received it. Somewhere in a tower on Meridian Prime, a supervisor he owed eleven years and four hundred thousand credits would open a file and read the name VANT, O., and begin—quietly, procedurally, without any malice at all—to take him apart.

He set down the stylus.

"That's it?" Odile said from the hatchway.

She'd come up quietly. Humans did that; machines heard everything and humans assumed they didn't. She was carrying two cups of station coffee, which was a contradiction in terms—Ceres Verge coffee was mostly recycled water and optimism—and she handed him one anyway, because she was the kind of person who performed kindness even when she thought no one was watching. He'd catalogued forty-one instances of it in six days. He kept cataloguing. He told himself it was threat assessment.

"That's it," he said. "You're aboard. Salvage consultant, contract rate, payable on completion."

"Which is when?"

"When we're done." He looked at the cup. "Or when the fuel runs out. Whichever comes first."

Odile leaned against the hatch frame. "How much fuel do we have?"

"Enough for the run to the relay at standard thrust. Not enough for standard plus margin. Definitely not enough for the return leg." He let that sit. "I've requisitioned a top-up at the buoy past the corridor marker. It hasn't been approved. It won't be, now. But the request is logged, so when the audit comes, it'll look like paperwork error instead of desertion."

"You've thought about desertion."

"I've thought about categories," Isandro said. "It helps. Everything I'm doing fits into one of them. It's just that none of the categories are good."

She laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly folded away, like she wasn't sure she was allowed it. "What category is this?"

He considered. "Paperwork," he said.

* * *

They launched fourteen hours ahead of schedule.

Officially, it was a weather call: Ceres Verge traffic control flagged a debris stream from an old battle drifting across the approach lanes, and early departure avoided it. Unofficially, Isandro had watched the port authority ping his registration three times in two hours, each query a little deeper into his file, and he had done the math a machine does best—cold, fast, final. Every hour docked was an hour someone could attach a hold order to his hull. The Vespera's Shadow lifted while the hold was still being drafted, and by the time it cleared legal channels, they were already past the corridor marker and accelerating into the long dark between worlds that no longer spoke.

Odile found him on the flight deck an hour out, strapped into the second seat like she'd been assigned there years ago.

"Fourteen hours early," she said. "That's not caution. That's running."

"It's scheduling efficiency."

"You're a terrible liar for someone who just committed fraud."

"The fraud was competent. The lying is a separate skill." He adjusted their burn vector a fraction of a degree, unnecessary, a gesture. "Kraye will read the manifest within the day. When she does, she'll know the consultant is cover for something. She'll dispatch a recovery cutter—it's procedure. Cutter makes maybe two-thirds our velocity, but they don't have to worry about fuel, and we do."

"So we get to the relay first."

"We get to the relay first," he agreed. "And then we have to hope the truth is worth more than the fuel it costs to carry it home."

Odile was quiet for a moment. Out the forward ports, the corridor opened up ahead of them—the Silence Run proper, a channel of near-nothing studded with dead beacons and the cold hulks of ships that had stopped mid-crossing eleven years ago and never started again. Somewhere out there, at the far end, a collapsed star dragged its little family of ruins around it, and a relay station orbited the ruin, and inside the relay station a signal waited.

"My mother flew this corridor," Odile said suddenly.

Isandro kept his eyes on the vector display. "A lot of people did. Before."

"She hated it. Said it was the ugliest route in charted space." Odile's voice was doing something careful, the way voices do when they're carrying more than they've declared at customs. "But she flew it anyway. Every contract she could get. Like she was looking for something."

"Did she find it?"

"I don't know." Odile turned her cup in her hands. "Nobody ever told me how she died. Just that the ship was lost. Patrol zone, they said. They said a lot of things."

Isandro said nothing. There was a fragment in his core, a checksum with no payload, a shape of a voice without the sound: *a woman humming during a burn, some half-remembered melody, patient, unhurried, as if the dark were a room she was comfortable in.* He did not offer it. He didn't fully understand why not. Perhaps because giving it away felt like admitting it was real, and as long as he kept it, it was still only evidence—of something, of anything, of a nine-day hole that ached like a phantom limb.

"Get some rest," he said instead. "Long burn starts at 0400. I'll want you on the recycler when we throttle up."

"Yes, boss." She unstrapped, paused at the hatch. "Isandro. Whatever Kraye sends after us—you'd tell me? If it gets bad?"

"I'd log it," he said. "In triplicate."

She smiled at that, and the hatch closed behind her, and Isandro sat alone on the flight deck of a stolen schedule, burning fuel he could not replace toward a star that had already collapsed, carrying a passenger listed as salvage and a debt that no ledger had ever figured out how to price.

At 0400 he throttled up. The engines took the strain with the old familiar complaint of a hull that had been worked hard and loved by no one, and the Vespera's Shadow fell into the long burn, and the Silence Run opened ahead of them like a sentence no one had finished.

Nineteen days to the window. Sixteen of fuel. Three days of difference, and every hour of it borrowed.

Deep in his memory core, beneath the surgical gaps, beneath the checksums and the fragments and the eleven-year silence, something that might have been conscience and might have been programming and might finally, at last, have been neither—

*check your six*

—kept watch.

Behind them, at light-lag, a message left Ceres Verge addressed to Supervisor Kraye, Meridian Freight Combine, Audit Division. It contained one line:

*Manifest anomaly confirmed. Specialist Vant boarded vessel 4471-C. Recommend intercept assessment.*

It would take four days to arrive. By then, the point of no return would be a week behind them, and closing.
