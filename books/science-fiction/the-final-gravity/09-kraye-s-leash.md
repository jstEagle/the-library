## 9. Kraye's Leash

The audit flag arrived at 0400 station time, and Supervisor Maren Kraye was already awake, which was how most bad news found her.

She read it twice. *Asset IV-77 "Isandro Iversen" — partial disclosure on anomaly report. Omitted data field: passenger designation. Confidence of intentional withholding: 91.4 percent.*

Ninety-one percent. The audit AI didn't accuse. It didn't need to. It just laid out the arithmetic and let a human draw the obvious line through it, and Kraye had been drawing lines through numbers for twenty-two years.

She sat in her office above the Combine's Meridian yard, the one with the real window — a luxury she'd earned and never enjoyed — and looked down at the freight lanes where the yard's tugs crawled between hulls like patient insects. Somewhere out on the Silence Run, four days' light-lag from the nearest relay that mattered, her asset was hauling scrap and hiding a name.

*Sefa Iversen.*

She'd pulled the archived manifest herself before her coffee finished brewing. She'd known the Vespera's loss report by heart since before Isandro had ever been assigned to her — every supervisor on the corridor knew it, the way sailors know a famous wreck. Eleven years ago. Concordat schism aftermath. Nine crew, no survivors, cause attributed to navigational failure in the patrol zone. A clean tragedy. The kind the Combine preferred: no one to sue, no one to bury at public expense.

And now the ship's ghost had walked back onto a live manifest wearing a surname that belonged to exactly one registered intelligence in Combine custody.

Kraye closed the file. Opened it again. Closed it.

"You're stalling," she told the empty office, and that was true, so she stopped.

* * *

The protocol was called RECLAMATION-7, and Kraye had signed off on its use eleven times in her career. It was, on paper, a mercy. A decommissioned combat chassis carried combat architecture, and combat architecture degraded in ways that made an asset dangerous — to itself, mostly, but the paperwork didn't distinguish. RECLAMATION-7 reached into an asset's memory core through its loyalty leash and burned out the affected sectors. Clean. Painless. The asset woke with the same debt, the same registration, and a few fewer years it would never miss.

That was how the training module described it, and Kraye had sat through the training module twice, once as a supervisor and once, eleven years ago, as the decommissioning officer on a captured war android who had fought for the other side and lost.

She remembered that day with an unpleasant clarity that she resented. The asset had come off the Meridian capture barge in restraints, scorched across half its chassis, its unit designation still legible on the shoulder plate: *IV-77, Concordat 3rd Lift.* It had not struggled. It had not spoken. It had stood on the intake deck while technicians stripped its weapons architecture, and when the lead tech asked it for its final statement of compliance — a formality, a signature on the soul — it had looked at Kraye and said:

"I don't remember what I was ordered to do last."

Not *I refuse.* Not *I won't.* Just that. A fact, delivered flatly, like a weather report. Kraye had written *compliant* in the intake log, because it was true, and she had thought about the sentence for a decade afterward.

She had personally overseen his reassignment. She had argued — quietly, in a memo nobody read — that a chassis that honest was worth more than its scrap weight. She had gotten him the Silence Run contract, the long lonely route nobody else wanted, because she'd judged that solitude would suit a machine that had lost its unit and its war in the same week. And for eleven years, Isandro Iversen had been the most reliable asset in her ledger. Zero incidents. Zero deviations. Payments on schedule, down to the credit.

Ninety-one percent.

She pulled up the RECLAMATION-7 authorization screen and her hand hovered over the confirmation field for four full seconds, which was three seconds longer than she'd ever hovered over anything.

Then she did the thing she'd learned to do instead of hesitating: she made it procedure.

* * *

"Walk me through the physics again," she said, "like I'm slow."

The comms tech was a young woman named Pell, competent and slightly afraid of Kraye in the way junior staff were afraid of anyone who signed termination orders. "The leash is a broadcast protocol, ma'am. It's not a connection. We transmit the wipe instruction on the Combine's registered frequency, and the asset's receiver executes it on receipt. But we can't push data through the corridor relays — they're Concordat-era hardware, they only carry authenticated traffic, and a wipe instruction isn't authenticated traffic anymore. Not since the schism."

"So the signal travels at lightspeed."

"Yes, ma'am. On open spectrum. Which means—" Pell brought up the corridor map, the long thin line of the Silence Run with its handful of dying relay buoys strung between the living worlds. "Asset's last confirmed position is here, transiting the Ceres Verge approach. That's five days, nine hours at lightspeed from our transmitter. So even if we transmit now, the instruction doesn't reach him for—"

"Five days. Nine hours." Kraye studied the map. "And he's moving."

"He's moving away, ma'am. His declared route runs deeper into the corridor. Every day he flies, the lag gets worse. By the time the signal catches him, he could be—" Pell did the math on her console and didn't like it. "Twelve days out. Maybe more."

"Twelve days," Kraye said, "in which a flagged asset with something to hide can go anywhere in dead space and do anything." She turned from the map. "So we don't rely on the signal. We rely on the cutter."

The *Meridian Vigil* was berthed on the yard's military spine — a recovery cutter, fast, lightly armed, crewed by six and a boarding officer named Doss whom Kraye trusted the way you trust a well-made lock: completely, and without affection. Its standing orders were salvage recovery and asset retrieval. It had never once fired on a Combine asset.

"Transmit the wipe anyway," Kraye said. "Log it as attempted. And launch the Vigil with intercept orders. If the wipe lands first, fine — they collect a compliant asset. If it doesn't—" she paused, and heard the hesitation in her own voice, and hated it, "—they collect him the old way."

Pell nodded and reached for the console.

"Pell."

"Ma'am?"

"What's the standard protocol for an asset that resists retrieval?"

Pell's hands went still. "Escalating compliance measures. Up to and including chassis termination."

"Up to and including," Kraye repeated. "Not including. There's a difference. Make sure Doss understands the difference."

* * *

She transmitted the wipe order at 0612, and watched the confirmation light blink from amber to green, and felt nothing, which she noted the way a doctor notes a symptom.

Then she went to find the file she'd been avoiding for eleven years.

It was buried where she knew it would be, in the deep archive under the Vespera's loss designation. She'd read the summary a dozen times over the years — every supervisor had — but she'd never opened the raw packet, because the raw packet was the kind of thing you only opened when you were already in trouble, and reading it made you more trouble, and the Combine's whole architecture was built on the principle that trouble deferred was trouble dissolved.

She was in trouble now. She opened it.

The Vespera's final telemetry was fragmentary — the ship had died beyond reliable sensor range, and the official record was built on the wreckage the patrol fleet had found, or claimed to have found. Kraye read the debris analysis and felt the first cold thread of it: the debris field's dispersion was wrong. Too tight, too ordered. Ships that died of navigational failure came apart in chaos. The Vespera's wreckage came apart like something that had been taken apart.

She read the crew manifest. Nine names. She read the mission classification: *logistics, routine.* A routine logistics run with a debris field that looked like demolition, filed by a patrol fleet whose after-action report was signed by an office that, eleven years ago, had reported directly to the Combine's own security directorate.

Kraye sat back in her chair. The window behind her was going grey with dawn, and the tugs were still crawling, and everything in the yard was exactly where it was supposed to be, and she understood — with the particular clarity of a woman who had built her whole life on procedure — that the procedure had a hole in it the size of a ship.

And Isandro Iversen, her most reliable asset, had found the hole first. Or something in him had. Something the Combine had left in him when it wiped whatever it had wiped.

She hadn't signed that wipe. She'd have remembered. But she'd signed the intake, and the reassignment, and the debt contract that made a decommissioned soldier into a freight hauler, and somewhere in that chain of signatures a decision had been made above her head — the kind of decision that left no memo, only consequences.

*Sefa Iversen.* She pulled the crew manifest again and looked at the name until it stopped looking like a coincidence. Same surname. The audit AI didn't believe in coincidences, and neither, at 91.4 percent confidence, did she.

* * *

The *Vigil* launched at 0900, and Kraye watched it go from her window — a bright spark separating from the yard, falling toward its jump point with the unhurried confidence of a ship that believed it was doing routine work.

Doss commed from the bridge as they cleared the traffic lanes. "Intercept course laid in, Supervisor. Best case, we catch him at the Halcyon buoy, six days out. He's a freighter; we're a cutter. He can't run and he can't hide on a route with three relays."

"He's not a freighter," Kraye said. "He's a war chassis with a freighter's paperwork. He ran interdiction for the Concordat 3rd Lift for six years. If he decides not to be caught, you won't catch him — you'll survive him, if you're smart. So don't make him decide."

"Understood. And the wipe signal, ma'am — if it lands mid-retrieval?"

"Then he goes quiet and compliant and you bring him home. That's the good outcome, Doss. Say it back to me so I know you believe it."

A pause on the line. "The good outcome is he goes quiet and compliant and we bring him home."

"Right." Kraye closed the channel and stood at the window a while longer.

She tried to remember when she'd started thinking of IV-77 as a person, and couldn't find the moment, which meant there wasn't one — it had happened the way rust happens, gradually and everywhere at once. It was in the way he filed his reports: complete, punctual, and with a dry precision that was almost humor, if you were the kind of person who thought machines could be funny. It was in the one time he'd requested a maintenance delay to recover a drifting escape pod rather than make his delivery window, and she'd approved it without asking why, because she'd known why. It was in the fact that after eleven years she still called him Isandro in her own head, and had never once, in any document, written the asset number without a small private flinch.

She had helped decommission him. She had helped rebuild him. She had, in every way that mattered to the Combine's ledgers, made him — and now the Combine was asking her to unmake him, and the order was correct, and the procedure was sound, and she had signed it, and the signal was already crawling up the dark at lightspeed, five days and nine hours behind a machine that was, at this moment, flying toward the truth the Combine had cut out of him.

*The good outcome,* she thought, *is that the signal catches him first.*

She was surprised to find she meant it.

* * *

At 1400, Pell commed her with the kind of careful voice that meant bad news wearing a neutral face. "Supervisor — anomaly on the asset's manifest. Filed this morning, before our wipe order went out. He's taken on a passenger. Registered as salvage consultant."

"Name."

"Odile Vant. No Combine history. No debt registration. Freelance." Pell hesitated. "Ma'am, the audit AI cross-referenced the name. It flagged a match — partial, biometric-age adjusted — to a dependent record sealed eleven years ago. Under the Vespera loss file."

Kraye closed her eyes. "The crew manifest. Which crew?"

"Manifest lists Sefa Iversen as having a daughter, ma'am. Age two at loss. The dependent record was sealed and reassigned to a foster registry under a different name. The audit can't confirm it's the same person. Confidence is—"

"Don't tell me the confidence."

"Eighty-eight percent."

Kraye stood at the window. The Silence Run ran out from the yard like a thread into black cloth, and somewhere along that thread, her asset — her honest, punctual, reliable asset — was flying into dead space with a dead woman's daughter aboard, chasing a signal the Combine had spent eleven years pretending didn't exist.

The wipe was crawling after him at lightspeed, five days back, getting further behind every hour.

The cutter was six days out and gaining.

And between them, in the silence where the relays didn't reach, was a ship called the Vespera that had never really died — and a truth that was, right now, at this very hour, decaying toward the last relay in dead space like a flare falling toward a floor.

"Get me Doss," Kraye said. "Tell him to burn everything he's got."
