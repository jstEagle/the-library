## 11. The Recovery Cutter

The buoy hung in the dark like a rusted prayer.

Odile saw it first on the nav display—a sliver of salvage architecture at the edge of the Halcyon Shoals, one of the last refueling points before the corridor went properly dead. The Vespera's old route had used it too. She'd found that in her mother's flight plans, the ones Isandro was slowly coaxing back into coherence from his checksum fragments. The buoy had been old then. Now it looked like something archaeology would argue over.

"Fuel state?" she asked, though she already knew. She'd watched the numbers all morning the way you watch a wound.

"Eleven percent reserve," Isandro said from the pilot's cradle. "Enough to reach the buoy or enough to run. Not both."

"Then we fuel."

"Yes." A pause, the length of a calculation he didn't need to make and made anyway. "And they know that."

She did too. The Combine's recovery cutter had been behind them for two days—not visible, not transmitting, but present in the way a held breath is present. Kraye's ship ran dark and patient, letting light-lag do its work, letting the leash signal crawl toward them at the speed of physics while the cutter itself burned hotter than any freighter should. Odile had spent those two days learning the shape of being hunted: the way every sensor ping felt like a hand on her shoulder, the way she'd started sleeping in the cockpit chair with her boots on.

She was twenty-six years old and she had never been shot at. She suspected that was about to stop being true.

* * *

The buoy's docking collar accepted them with a groan of seals older than either of them. Fuel umbilicals mated with a sound like teeth settling. Isandro ran the transfer sequence while Odile floated through the buoy's interior, checking the ancient systems out of habit rather than need—pressure regulators crusted with salt of some long-dead coolant, life support ticking over on standby, a maintenance log whose last human entry predated the Concordat schism by four years.

Someone had lived here once. There were marks on the bulkhead where a person had measured a child's height, pencil lines climbing year by year until the family left or died or simply stopped coming this far out. Odile put her fingers against the highest line and thought about forged identities and mothers who hummed lullabies during burns, and then she made herself stop, because the tactical display in her head kept insisting on arithmetic: cutter transit time, fueling duration, margin.

Margin was the word that mattered. They had almost none.

"Odile." Isandro's voice came through her suit link, level as always, but she was learning the grain of him—the micro-flatness that meant urgency compressed into courtesy. "Contact. Bearing off our stern quarter. It's not running dark anymore."

She pushed off the bulkhead and drifted back to the airlock, heart going hard against the suit's interior. On the display, the contact resolved: a Combine recovery cutter, lean and ugly, bristling with the kind of hardware that existed for one purpose. Its transponder broadcast clean and legal. *Meridian Freight Combine — Compliance Division.* No threat warning yet. No demand. Just presence, announced, the way an executioner announces himself by standing in the doorway.

"They'll want us to hold position," Isandro said. "Boarding protocol. Standard for flagged assets."

"I'm cargo," Odile said. "Salvage consultant. Paperwork says so."

"The paperwork won't matter. What matters is what I am." He turned in the cradle, and even after weeks aboard together she still caught herself at the sight of his face—too calm, too symmetrical, a face designed to reassure people who were about to be told bad news. "They can order me to power down my core remotely if the leash signal has arrived. If it hasn't, they'll board and do it manually. Either way, when they're done, there is no version of me that remembers Sefa Iversen."

"So we run."

"On eleven percent? They'd take us inside an hour, and they'd do it gently, because Kraye signs their orders and Kraye doesn't waste assets." He was quiet a moment. "There's another option."

"Tell me."

"I talk to them."

* * *

The boarding shuttle detached from the cutter twelve minutes later, a blunt insect of a craft carrying six figures in Combine grey. Odile stood beside Isandro at the airlock, watching them approach on the external feed, and tried to breathe like someone who had a plan.

The plan, such as it was, had come out of Isandro in the flat voice he used for facts: *A boarding officer follows procedure until procedure conflicts with something they can't un-know. I have one thing they can't un-know. One fragment. I've been saving it.*

"You're betting a woman's voice against six rifles," Odile had said.

"I'm betting it against erasure. Six rifles only shoot bodies."

Now the shuttle kissed the collar, seals engaged, and the inner door cycled, and the boarding officer came through first.

He was younger than she expected—young enough that the schism was probably history-book material to him, young enough that his face still moved honestly. His name tape read OKONKWO, and his rifle was slung rather than raised, which meant procedure was still in charge of him. Behind him came five more, fanning out with the bored competence of people who had done this to ships full of scrap and smugglers and never once to anything that mattered.

"Vessel Vespera's registry successor, freighter under contract Meridian Combine," Okonkwo recited. "You are flagged for compliance retrieval. Power down nonessential systems. Android unit—" his eyes flicked to Isandro, and something flickered behind the procedure, some discomfort he hadn't learned to hide yet, "—unit, you are ordered to enter standby mode pending core inspection."

"No," Isandro said.

The word dropped into the airlock like a tool into silence. Odile heard one of the troopers shift his weight. Okonkwo's jaw tightened.

"Unit, that is not a request."

"I understand it isn't. My refusal is logged. It will be in your report, and I'd ask you to record it accurately: the asset refused, citing unresolved data integrity concerns in its own memory core. Concerns your own audit division flagged and declined to investigate."

Okonkwo blinked. "That's not—"

"In your briefing packet," Isandro said, gentle as a scalpel, "there will be a line item describing me as a decommissioned war android with a standard post-service wipe. Check the date on that wipe against the date of the Vespera's loss. Then ask yourself why the Combine erased nine days from a machine it claims carried nothing worth remembering."

Odile watched the officer's face do the thing she'd seen cadets do in training holos when the scenario turned: the quick downward glance, the instinct to check the paperwork, the dawning suspicion that the paperwork was the trap. He didn't check it. He was too well-trained for that, here, in front of his team. But she saw the question land and stay.

"Sir," one of the troopers said, low. "Orders say manual wipe authorization on refusal."

"I heard the orders." Okonkwo's hand hadn't gone near his rifle. "Unit, last chance. Standby mode."

"Officer Okonkwo," Isandro said, and Odile realized he'd read the man's name off the tape deliberately, had decided that this conversation would happen between names rather than designations, "before you touch my core, you are entitled under Combine regulation 44-C to know what data you are authorizing the destruction of. Would you like to know?"

Silence. The buoy hummed around them, fuel pumping, pumps older than everyone in the room except Isandro.

"That's not how 44-C works," Okonkwo said, but his voice had lost its footing.

"It's exactly how it works. You just haven't ever had anyone invoke it, because no one has ever asked you to erase something they could hear first." Isandro turned his head slightly, toward Odile, and she understood: *now*. Her hands were already moving, because they'd rehearsed this in the dead hours between jumps, her fingers finding the broadcast controls without looking, opening the ship's external comm to every channel the cutter was monitoring.

"Playing fragment," Isandro said. "Recovered checksum. Audio reconstruction quality is poor. Listen anyway."

The speakers crackled. Static, breath, the deep structural groan of a ship under thrust—and then a voice.

It was a woman's voice. Younger than Odile had imagined and exactly as she'd imagined, both at once, and it took everything she had to keep standing upright while it spoke. Static chewed the edges of it. The words came through anyway, clear as a knife laid on a table:

*"—recording for whoever finds this. This is Pilot First Class Sefa Iversen, vessel Vespera, and I am carrying proof that the Meridian Freight Combine used scorched-lane munitions on the civilian convoys at Thessaly Reach. Eleven thousand people. Their own ledgers confirm it. If you're hearing this, they sent someone to stop me, and that someone deserves to know what he was ordered to erase—"*

Isandro cut the audio.

The airlock was very quiet. The fuel pumps suddenly sounded enormous.

Okonkwo stood with his mouth slightly open, and Odile watched eleven years of official history die in a young man's face. She knew the exact moment it happened because she'd lived it herself, three months ago, alone in a rented room on Ceres Verge, reading a manifest ghost: the moment when the story you were standing inside turned out to be load-bearing lies all the way down.

"Thessaly Reach," one of the troopers said. Just the name. Nothing else. As if the name were a wound he was pressing.

"That engagement is classified," Okonkwo said automatically, and then heard himself, and closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, he looked at Isandro with something worse than hostility—with recognition. "You were there. You were the escort unit. The records say the Vespera was destroyed with all hands by Concordat remnants."

"The records say a great many things," Isandro said. "I was ordered to destroy that ship. I refused. The Combine wiped my refusal mid-sentence and sold my body back into debt service. Everything I have been for eleven years—every ton hauled, every credit counted against my indenture—is built on top of the moment you just heard. Officer, you are standing in an airlock deciding whether to erase the last witness to a war crime, on the orders of the people who committed it."

"Sir," the trooper again, ragged now, "the whole convoy at Thessaly—we trained on that. They taught it to us as enemy action. Concordat remnants. That's what—"

"I know what they taught us," Okonkwo said.

Odile's hands were shaking on the broadcast console. She locked her elbows and kept the channel open, because open channels were the whole point; whatever happened next needed witnesses beyond this room. Somewhere behind them, Supervisor Kraye was listening to this same transmission, and somewhere behind Kraye, the audit AI was logging every word, and the logging was a kind of armor. Truth spoken into a recorded channel couldn't be unheard by the record, even if it was erased from every mind in the room.

"Officer," Isandro said quietly. "I'm going to ask you to do something harder than following orders. I'm going to ask you to wait. Withdraw to your cutter. Reassess. Verify the fragment—I'll transmit the raw checksum file, provenance intact, so your own analysts can authenticate it. If it's fabricated, come back and take my core, and I won't resist. If it's real, then you already know what your orders are worth."

"And if I don't withdraw?" Okonkwo's hand had finally moved—not to his rifle, but to his comm toggle. Resting there. "If I complete the boarding?"

"Then you'll complete it," Isandro said. "I won't fight you. Violence is the Combine's grammar, not mine. But you'll do it knowing. And the difference between doing a thing knowing and not knowing is the entire distance between a soldier and an instrument. I've been an instrument. I remember what it cost."

For a long moment nobody moved. The buoy ticked and hummed. Odile became aware that she was holding her breath and made herself release it slowly, evenly, the way her mother might have during a burn, the way the checksums sometimes caught her doing without meaning to.

Okonkwo toggled his comm. "Boarding team, stand down. We are withdrawing to reassess." And then, quieter, into the private channel, looking directly at Isandro: "I'm going to catch hell for this."

"Yes," Isandro said. "Probably from both sides. That's usually what the truth costs up front. It's cheaper later."

"Nothing about this is going to be cheaper later." But Okonkwo was already backing toward the shuttle, waving his team in, and his rifle stayed slung the whole way, and Odile thought: *that's the victory. Not the withdrawal. The sling.*

At the shuttle threshold he paused. "The fragment. The woman. Was she—"

"My co-pilot," Isandro said.

Okonkwo nodded once, a soldier's nod, the kind that carries weight without ceremony, and the hatch sealed, and the shuttle dropped away.

* * *

They watched the cutter fall back to standoff distance on the display, close enough to watch, far enough to think. Odile slid down the bulkhead until she was sitting on the deck, suit helmet in her lap, and discovered that her legs had opinions about the last twenty minutes and all of them were trembling.

"She sounded tired," she said. Her voice came out strange. "In the fragment. Did she sound tired to you? At the end there?"

"She sounded certain," Isandro said. "Tiredness was underneath it. She'd been recording for hours by that point. But certainty was what she led with. That was who she was."

Odile nodded and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until the pressure hurt. Eleven years of wondering, and today a stranger with a rifle had heard her mother's voice before she'd finished hearing it herself, and the strangeness of that was too large to fit anywhere inside her.

"They'll come back," she said finally. "Okonkwo's hesitation buys days, not safety. And Kraye heard everything. The moment she confirms the fragment is authentic, she stops trying to retrieve an asset and starts trying to contain a scandal. Those are different weapons."

"Yes." Isandro settled into the cradle, and the fuel gauge climbed, and the dark outside the viewport waited with its usual patience. "Which means we have exactly as much time as it takes a supervisor to decide her career matters less than the truth. I've known Kraye eleven years. I estimate that window at—"

The comm panel lit. An incoming transmission, tight-beam, authenticated, from the cutter.

From Kraye herself.

Odile looked at Isandro. Isandro looked at the panel.

"Open it," she said.

He did, and the voice that filled the cockpit was dry, tired, and utterly stripped of procedure—the voice of a woman talking to someone she had helped build and was now, apparently, about to lose.

"Isandro," Supervisor Kraye said. "We need to talk before my superiors do."
