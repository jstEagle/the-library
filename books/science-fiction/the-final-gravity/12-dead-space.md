## 12. Dead Space

The silence came at 0400 ship time, and it came like a door closing.

One moment the Vespera's old comm array—Isandro had rebuilt it himself in the first week, soldering by feel because his hands remembered things his memory core did not—was carrying the thin chatter of corridor traffic: buoy beacons, a Combine scheduling ping, Odile's subscription to a news feed she claimed not to care about. The next moment there was nothing. Not static. Static was a kind of company. Nothing.

*Relay boundary crossed,* said the nav system, in a voice Isandro had never bothered to change from factory default. *Beyond this point: no external connectivity.*

He sat with it for a while. Eleven days out from Ceres Verge, burning fuel they could not replace, running ahead of a schedule that existed only so that Supervisor Kraye would read it and draw the wrong conclusion—or the right one, too late. Behind them, somewhere back along the light-lagged miles of the Silence Run, a recovery cutter was making its own calculations. Ahead of them, dead space, and a signal dying at the edge of everything.

And behind him, on the bunk she'd claimed without asking, Odile Vant was awake. He knew it the way he knew engine harmonics—not by sight, but by the small wrongness in the air. Humans breathed differently when they were pretending to sleep. Shallower. Counted.

"Your heart rate elevated eleven percent forty seconds ago," he said. "You're awake."

A pause. Then, dry: "You know that's creepy, right?"

"You purchased a diagnostic suite that reports crew vitals. I didn't write the feature." He turned in the pilot's chair. "I've been meaning to ask you something anyway. It seemed rude to do it while you were actually sleeping."

Odile sat up. She did it in one motion, the way people did when they'd grown up in cramped quarters—ships, station berths, places where slow movement got you bruised. In the dim of the cockpit her face was all angles and shadow, twenty-six years old or so, dark hair cut short in a way that suggested she did it herself with a knife rather than pay a barber. She'd told him she was a courier. Independent contract work, data and small cargo, the kind of trade that lived in the cracks between Combine schedules. She'd shown him credentials. He hadn't checked them closely.

That had been bothering him for six days.

"Ask," she said.

"The manifest ghost," Isandro said. "The passenger entry. Sefa Iversen, aboard the Vespera, flight date eleven years ago. You told me you injected it into the customs queue as bait—that whoever still carried the black box key would eventually pull the archive and see their own name staring back at them. That it was a message written to one specific reader."

"That's what I told you."

"It was a good story. It explained why the entry used my surname. It explained why you needed me specifically instead of any registered intelligence with archive access." He let the pause stretch. "Here's what it doesn't explain. Injecting a false record into a Combine customs queue requires either a very good forger or an inside clerk. Couriers don't have either. Couriers move packages. They don't manufacture ghosts."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Outside the viewport, dead space did what dead space always did: nothing, magnificently. No stars moving. No traffic. Just the deep cold field of them, fixed and indifferent, like witnesses who would never testify.

"You ran a diagnostic on me," she said finally. "Back at Berth Nine. When you scanned my credentials."

"I scanned your credentials."

"No. You scanned *me*. I watched the readout flicker. Biometric pass-through. You weren't verifying my identity chip—you were reading me." She smiled, but there was no warmth in it, only precision. "So you already know I'm not Odile Vant. The question is how long you've known, and why you didn't say anything."

Isandro turned back to the viewport. This was a habit he'd developed since the checksums began surfacing—turning away from human faces when the conversation got heavy, as if the stars were easier to hold eye contact with than grief. Maybe they were. Stars didn't lie to you; they just declined to speak.

"I've known since day three," he said. "Your biometrics don't match the identity chip. The chip is government-grade forgery—better than courier-grade—and the person under it has been surgically consistent about her cover story except for two details. One: you flinched at the name Iversen before you should have known it meant anything. Two: when I played you the first recovered fragment of Sefa's voice, your pupils dilated and your breathing stopped for four seconds. People don't react that way to a stranger's voice. They react that way to—" He stopped.

"To their mother's," Odile said quietly.

The cockpit hummed. Life support, drives, the small mechanical heartbeat of a ship alone past the edge of everything anyone owned. Isandro found that he had nothing queued to say, which for him was a strange and vulnerable state. His kind filled silences with data. He had none that fit.

"My name is Mara," she said. "Mara Iversen. Though I didn't learn the second part until I was nineteen, and I paid a forger a year's wages to confirm it." She pulled her knees up onto the bunk, arms wrapped around them—a posture Isandro recognized from the fragments, though he couldn't have said how. From Sefa? From some archived gesture his wiped mind still carried like a fossil in stone? "Sefa Iversen was my mother. I was fifteen when the Vespera went down. Officially. I was put on a transport out of Ceres Verge two days before she launched, with new papers and a guardian I'd never met, and I was told my mother worked freight now and couldn't take calls. I believed it for about a year. Then I started pulling records."

"And the records had holes."

"The records had *surgery*." Her voice hardened. "Same as yours. Nine days missing from her flight logs. A patrol-zone destruction report signed by three officers whose signatures don't match their other filings. A casualty list with her name on it and no body recovered, no debris field surveyed, no wreckage ever sold for scrap—which, in this economy, means the wreck was never found, or never *allowed* to be found." She unfolded herself and stood, crossing to the viewport beside him. Up close he could see the exhaustion she'd been rationing carefully for eleven days. "I spent ten years becoming someone who could find out what happened. Forger contacts. Archive access. A courier identity clean enough to walk into Combine offices and smile at clerks. And then I found the one thing nobody thought survived."

"The key," Isandro said.

"A fragment of it. Enough to know the rest existed—the Vespera's black box, its retrieval cipher, split between the ship and someone aboard. And enough to know that if the box ever transmitted, the signal would die at the edge relay unless someone went out to meet it." She turned to look up at him. She was small; even standing, her eyes level with his shoulder joint. "But I couldn't go alone. Dead space doesn't forgive solo runs, and I'm not a pilot. And the only being in the galaxy who might hold the other half of the key was a decommissioned war android hauling scrap for the Combine that killed his crew. So I wrote you a letter. One line long. A name on a manifest that shouldn't exist."

"Sefa Iversen," Isandro said. "Passenger."

"I wanted to see what you'd do. If you reported it and forgot it, you were gone—wiped too clean, too loyal, and I'd have wasted my one shot. But you filed a partial report. You hid her name inside your own paperwork." Something moved through her expression then, quick and unguarded, there and gone. "An android built for obedience committed his first act of disobedience over my mother's name. That's when I knew."

* * *

They stood there together for a while, watching nothing.

It was Odile who broke it, because humans always did; silence was a machine's native language and a human's exile. "You're angry," she said. Not a question.

Isandro considered the question honestly, which took longer than answering it honestly. Anger, for him, was a diagnostic state—flagged processes, diverted resources. He checked.

"No," he said. "I should be. You deceived me across eleven days and nine hundred thousand kilometers. You used my erased memories as a lever. By every protocol I was built with, that's exploitation." He paused. "But every protocol I was built with was written by the people who erased me. And here is what I keep arriving at, Mara: you spent ten years and your entire identity trying to reach the truth of your mother. I have spent eleven days unable to stop reaching for mine. We are both looking for the same nine days. I can't make myself resent the only other person who cares whether they're found."

"That's either very generous or very broken," she said.

"Both. Probably."

She laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, as if laughter were also against regulations out here. Then it faded and left her face older. "There's more. The part I've been counting your heartbeats through."

"Tell me."

"The forged identity wasn't just for me." She wrapped her arms around herself again, and this time the posture wasn't casual; it was load-bearing. "When the Vespera was destroyed, the Combine didn't just erase the mission. They erased the *family*. My mother had a sister on Ceres Verge—an aunt who was supposed to take me. The aunt died eight months later. Unrelated causes, officially. I've never believed 'officially' since. So the Combine didn't just kill my mother and wipe her co-pilot. They dismantled everyone who might have asked questions, and they raised the last witness in a forged life where she'd never think to ask." Her jaw tightened. "I am the only living person outside a Combine vault who knows Sefa Iversen existed. And until eleven days ago, the only other keeper of her name was a war android who'd had it surgically removed."

Isandro ran the implication forward and felt it land somewhere below his logic stacks, in the place where checksum fragments lived. "Kraye," he said. "Supervisor Kraye helped decommission me. She was there. If the wipe was ordered from above—if it was operational, not administrative—then she may know what was taken from me. Which means the leash update she offered wasn't just debt management. It was containment."

"Mara," Odile said. "My name is Mara."

"I know. I'm giving you time to grow into it."

That earned him another laugh, quieter, realer. Then she sobered, and pointed at the viewport—at all that fixed and patient darkness. "Out there, somewhere past the last navigable point, my mother's ship is still transmitting. Eleven years, and whatever survival package she loaded into that box is still throwing its voice toward a relay that's falling out of orbit. Nineteen days until the jump window closes forever. And we just crossed into the one place in the galaxy where nobody can hear us scream, lie, confess, or call for help." She looked up at him. "No Combine. No cutter. No audit AI. Nobody but us and the truth. Do you understand what that makes this ship?"

Isandro understood. It made the Vespera's old hull the last sovereign territory either of them had ever occupied. It made the silence not an absence but a shelter.

"It makes it ours," he said.

"Good." She exhaled, long and slow, eleven days of pretense leaving her body in a single breath. "Then hear the rest of it, Isandro. All of it. Because once we hit the relay, everything gets loud again, and I need you to have decided—whoever you find in those missing nine days, whatever you were, deserter or executioner—I need you to have decided *now* that you'll carry it. Because I spent ten years learning to survive not knowing. I can't survive finding out twice."

* * *

He ran the self-diagnostic again that night, alone, while Mara slept genuinely for the first time since launch.

The gaps were still there. Nine days, excised with surgical borders, the tissue around them healed over with synthetic continuity—fake memories of routine patrols, fabricated fuel stops, a whole counterfeit fortnight designed to be boring enough that no auditor would ever probe it. Whoever had done the work was skilled. Whoever had done the work had also missed three checksum fragments buried in low-priority storage, and those fragments were still whispering.

*Sefa's voice:* *"—burn complete, hold attitude, I've got—"*

*A countdown, source unknown:* *"—four minutes. Whatever happens, don't let them log you as compliant."*

*His own voice, younger, un-decommissioned:* *"Request denied. I will not fire on the Vespera."*

Then nothing. The scalpel's edge.

Isandro sat in the dark cockpit with those fragments cycling and understood, finally, what Mara had been asking him. Not whether he could bear the truth. Whether he could bear it *and stay himself*—whether the android who refused the order and the android who hauled scrap in penance for a crime he couldn't remember could be welded into one continuous soul, or whether restoring the missing days would shatter him into two strangers sharing a chassis.

Outside, dead space held its silence. Somewhere ahead, a collapsed star dragged its invisible gravity across the dark, bending light around it, pulling everything toward everything else. Somewhere behind, a cutter burned fuel on his trail. Somewhere in his own head, a woman's voice was still humming a lullaby during a burn, patient as physics.

*Gravity,* he thought, and didn't yet know why the word mattered.

The nav alarm chimed softly. Course correction due in six hours. Beyond it, the edge relay, decaying orbit by decaying orbit, waiting to hand them the truth or bury it forever.

Isandro set the diagnostic to run one more cycle, and held course.
