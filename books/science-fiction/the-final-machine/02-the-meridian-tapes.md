## 2. The Meridian Tapes

The Sparrowhawk ran dark and quiet through the long middle of nowhere, four days out from Kestrel with nothing ahead of her but empty lanes and the slow arithmetic of a burn that would take eleven more days to finish. Deep transit. The part of the run Theo loved and hated in equal measure, because there was nothing left to do but be alone with himself, and he had never been very good company.

He made tea the way his mother had taught him—leaves first, water after, never stirred—and carried it up to the cockpit in a chipped ceramic mug that had survived three owners before him and would probably survive three more. Outside the canopy, the stars did what stars do: nothing, beautifully, forever.

"You're not company," he told them, and drank his tea.

Then he went down to the hold.

* * *

The archive lived in a reinforced case bolted beneath the deck plating, under a false panel that read COOLANT RESERVE — DO NOT OPEN unless you already knew it was lying. Inside, nested in foam cut by hand, sat forty-one data-cores. Thirty-two were cargo. Nine were not.

The nine were Meridian.

He hadn't watched them in four months. He kept a schedule about it, the way some men keep sobriety chips—not because watching hurt exactly, though it did, but because *not* watching hurt worse, and he'd learned that about himself the hard way during a year in the Belt when he'd left the case sealed and had started hearing his mother's voice in the engine harmonics. Routine was the trick. You treated grief like a maintenance task. You greased it, you inspected it, you logged it, and it kept running quiet instead of seizing up on you at customs inspection or in the middle of a docking approach.

So: routine. He pulled the first core, slotted it into the deck reader, and killed the cabin lights.

The screen bloomed white, then resolved into morning.

* * *

Meridian Colony, Year One of the recordings. He knew the date without checking—spring thaw on the southern plateau, the season the whole settlement smelled like wet iron and thawing lichen. The camera was cheap, one of the survey units Halcyon Dynamics had issued for geological documentation and that everyone immediately repurposed for everything else, because a corporation's equipment is only ever loyal to its paperwork, never its people.

The image wobbled as whoever held it walked. A woman's laugh off-frame. Then his mother's voice, young, unguarded in a way he'd forgotten voices could be:

*"Theo, hold still, I'm trying to get the ridge—"*

And a boy's voice answering, seven years old and full of grievance: *"You said we were going to the greenhouse."*

*"We are going to the greenhouse. The greenhouse is that way. The ridge is just—look, look at the ridge."*

The camera swung up. The ridge stood against a sky the color of old pewter, and along its spine ran the windbreak pines they'd gene-tweaked from Terran stock, black-green and stubborn, planted in rows so straight you could set a chronometer by them. Behind the trees, half-built, the colony's second dome caught the flat light like a soap bubble.

Theo sat in the dark of his own ship and watched the sky of a world that no longer had anyone looking up at it.

That was the thing nobody warned you about recordings. They didn't preserve the past. They preserved the *angle*. Every tape was somebody standing somewhere, choosing to point a lens at something, deciding this moment over all other moments deserved keeping. His mother had filmed the ridge because she thought the pines were beautiful. She'd filmed the greenhouse because she was proud of it—her design, her irrigation schematics, her argument with the corporate liaison that she'd won by refusing to lose. She had filmed Theo constantly, relentlessly, with the shameless devotion of a woman who understood, maybe better than anyone on Meridian, that memory was a thing you had to build on purpose or it didn't get built at all.

She'd been an engineer. She'd known about load-bearing structures.

* * *

Year Two. The core clicked home and the screen filled with rain.

Not real rain—Meridian got maybe forty centimeters a year—but the colony's condenser harvest, sheeting off the dome in silver curtains while the whole settlement turned out to stand in it, faces upturned, laughing, because recycled water falling from the sky felt like a miracle even when you knew the plumbing. Theo found himself on screen at eight years old, soaked to the skin, holding a smaller girl's hand. Sefa. The Okonkwo boy's little sister. He'd forgotten her name for almost two years once, and the forgetting had felt like a small murder.

Ada Okonkwo appeared at the frame's edge, younger than Theo now remembered her being, her gray hair pinned up with what looked like a welding rod. She was shouting something joyful at the camera that the cheap mic shredded into static, and his mother laughed behind the lens, and the recording ended there, mid-laugh, because the battery died the way batteries always die at the exact wrong moment, and somehow that was the part that got him—the cut. Not the content. The cut. Nine years of tapes and every single one of them ended too soon, life interrupted at its own discretion, and he had spent so many hours of his adulthood sitting in dark cockpits bracing for each cut like a man flinching from a blow he'd already counted coming.

He paused the playback. Sat with the frozen rain.

On the bulkhead across from him, his reflection hung ghosted against the screen: a lean man in his thirties with his mother's jaw and his father's habit of carrying tension in his shoulders like ballast. The smuggler's face. Competent, watchful, faintly amused—the face he wore at customs desks and salvage auctions, the face that said *nothing to declare, nothing to know, move along.*

Behind it, visible only to himself, the other face. The one that came out only down here, in the dark, with the dead world playing on the screen.

He let the tape run again.

* * *

Year Four was the school year. Somebody—Teacher Vashti, he thought, though the voice was all he had; the camera never showed her—had started a rotating documentary project where the children took turns filming a day in their life, and the result was nine hours of footage that no archivist would ever call important and that Theo would have traded every credit in his account to protect.

He watched himself, ten years old, giving a solemn tour of the hydroponics bay, mispronouncing "nutrient solution" twice. He watched Sefa Okonkwo explain the compost cycle with terrifying authority. He watched a boy named Denny Ferro film forty straight seconds of a spider-thing molting in the soil lab while whispering *"whoa"* in a continuous reverent loop.

And he watched the day the supply ship came.

It came twice a year, a fat Halcyon hauler descending on pillars of fire, and the whole colony turned out on the landing field to watch because it was the closest thing Meridian had to a holiday. On the tape, the ship came down huge and slow, and the crowd cheered, and someone's father lifted someone's child onto his shoulders, and Theo—ten-year-old Theo, on screen, holding the camera that week—panned across the faces of two hundred people who did not yet know they were already dead.

That was the sentence he'd never managed to make peace with. *Already dead.* Because the write-off hadn't happened for another five years, but it had happened, in a boardroom, in a spreadsheet, in a quarterly projection, before any of these people knew. Somewhere in Halcyon Dynamics' accounting, Meridian Colony had stopped existing while its people were still planting pines and cheering supply ships and teaching their children the names of lichens. The colony died the way corporations kill things: quietly, in advance, on paper. Everything afterward was just the paperwork catching up.

His mother was in that crowd. Frame 44:12, if he wanted to be precise, which he did not, because he'd been precise about it once and it had cost him a full day of not functioning. She was wearing the yellow coat. She was waving at the ship, at the future, at everything.

Theo paused the tape there anyway. He couldn't help it. He never could.

* * *

He made more tea. This was also routine. The kettle, the leaves, the walk back down through the ship's spine with the mug steaming, past the coolant lines where thirty-two cores of other people's contraband memories slept in their glycol bath—a dying singer's farewell tour, a wedding on a moon that had since been strip-mined, the last sermon of a preacher whose congregation had scattered to four different systems. Cargo. Someone else's griefs, priced per terabyte, insured against loss.

His own grief rode underneath the floor plates, unpriced, uninsured, worth nothing to anyone alive except him.

Which was, he understood, the entire reason he kept it. Not sentiment. Arithmetic. Everything else in his hold had a buyer waiting somewhere. The Meridian tapes had no market, no client, no price. They were the only thing he owned that existed purely because he refused to let it stop existing. In a life built on moving other people's precious things from place to place for money, the tapes were the one shipment he was both courier and consignee of, origin and destination the same broken heart.

He sat back down. Year Six.

* * *

Year Six was when the corporation started pulling out, though the tapes didn't know it yet, and that was what made Year Six unbearable in a way the later years weren't. The later years at least had the decency to look like what they were. Year Six was still smiling.

The dome expansion finished. There was a festival—Founding Day, the ninth one—and the tape held two hours of it: string lights strung between the pines, someone's homemade cider going around in dented cups, Ada Okonkwo dancing with a precision that suggested engineering rather than joy and somehow conveyed both. Old man Ferro played a guitar with two strings missing and nobody minded. Children ran through frame after frame, growing visibly between cuts, the way children do in archives—time-lapse ghosts, here at six, gone at twelve, replaced by taller versions wearing the same faces.

And in the background of the Founding Day footage, if you knew where to look, the early signs. The medical bay's new equipment arriving with Halcyon logos and no service contracts. The liaison's visits getting longer and his answers getting shorter. The survey drones flying patterns over the southern plateau that had nothing to do with geology.

Theo had watched those background details a hundred times, hunting them, cataloguing them, building the case file in his head that no court would ever hear. He knew the exact frame where the story turned. Everyone always thinks the turn will be dramatic. It wasn't. It was a Tuesday. His mother filmed the greenhouse irrigation system running its evening cycle, water ticking through the lines, and in the far background a Halcyon shuttle lifted off carrying three crates marked ADMINISTRATIVE ARCHIVE — RECALL, and his mother never even glanced at it, and that was the moment, as much as any, when Meridian began to be erased. Not with fire. With a crate. With paperwork leaving quietly in the background of a shot about water.

He'd been fourteen that year. He remembered the actual day of the write-off announcement—Year Eight, the word *consolidation*, the liaison's apologetic shrug, the phrase *economically nonviable* spoken aloud to two hundred people who had built a world with their hands. He remembered his mother not crying. He remembered her going to the equipment locker and checking out a survey camera, and he remembered, with the terrible clarity that only matters in retrospect, her filming everything after that with a new kind of hunger. Not beauty anymore. Testimony.

The last two years of tapes were hers in a way the earlier ones weren't. Less laughter. More lens. She filmed the water reclamation plant and the school records and the seed vault and the cemetery with its forty-one graves, and she filmed Theo at sixteen, sullen and confused, asking her why she cared so much about the stupid camera, and her answer was on the tape, and he had heard it perhaps two hundred times and it had not once gotten easier:

*"Because someday, sweetheart, someone is going to say we were never here. And I want to be able to hand them the receipt."*

* * *

The last core was short. Eleven minutes. He saved it for last, always, the way you save the last of anything.

Final departure. The evacuation barges lifting from the landing field in Year Nine, one after another, the pine windbreak black against the exhaust glare. His mother had set the camera on a tripod on the ridge—the ridge, the one from the very first tape, come full circle—and walked away from it, and the machine recorded what machines record: the ships rising, the dome lights going dark section by section as the power grid wound down, the plateau settling into silence.

At minute nine, a figure walked back into frame. Small, at that distance. Yellow coat.

She stood on the ridge looking at the camera for a long moment—as close to looking at him as physics allowed—and then she raised one hand. Not waving, exactly. Something between a wave and a salute and a blessing. Then she turned and walked out of the frame toward the last barge, and the camera kept recording for ninety more seconds of empty landscape and dying lights, and then the battery died, the way batteries do, at the exact wrong moment, which was also the exact right one.

The screen went to standby blue. The cabin was very dark and very quiet.

Theo sat with it. He didn't wipe his eyes, because there was no one to see and no point pretending he needed to, but he didn't pretend he wasn't doing it either. Routine. Maintenance. Grief logged, inspected, returned to service.

"Receipt received," he said to the empty cabin, and his voice came out rougher than he intended, and he cleared it, and that was that. Another transit, another visitation. He ejected the core, slid it back into its foam slot, closed the false panel, and rested his palm flat on the deck plating above it for a moment—the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal, or a grave.

Nine years of a dead world, riding under his feet through the dark. As long as the Sparrowhawk flew, Meridian flew with her.

He was climbing back toward the cockpit, mug in hand, already composing tomorrow's checklist—coolant flush at 0600, nav recalibration, the Kestrel approach window—when the ship spoke to him.

Not the comm. Not the proximity alarm. The nav computer itself, chiming a tone he'd never heard it make, a low harmonic note like a struck bell heard through water. He froze halfway up the ladder.

On the main display, unbidden, a fragmentary transmission had written itself across the chart: coordinates in dead space, beyond the shipping lanes, beyond anything, wrapped around a line of text that repeated and degraded and repeated—

—and outside the canopy, for one impossible instant, the coffee in his mug floated free, a trembling brown sphere rising weightless into the air, and the stars themselves seemed to shiver.

Theo Lockridge stood alone in the dark of his ship, nine years of the dead humming beneath his boots, and watched the universe open its mouth.
