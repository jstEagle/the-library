## 3. Children of the Dream

Sefa Voss had a theory about mornings, which was that they were a rumor started by people who slept well. She was not one of them. She kept the colony's medical logs the way other people kept houseplants — faithfully, without much expectation of drama — and she had come to accept that her days would be a gentle procession of scraped knees, vitamin audits, and old men complaining about their joints as though the joints had personally betrayed them.

So when the alert chimed at 05:12 — *seven pediatric sleep interruptions, clustered, unexplained* — she assumed it was a glitch. The colony's sensor web was older than she was and had opinions about everything, most of them wrong.

She was halfway through braiding her hair when the second alert chimed. Then the third. Then the fourth, which was less an alert than a small polite cough from the system, as if to say: *I know you saw the others. I'm going to keep going.*

Sefa looked at herself in the mirror — one braid done, one hanging loose like a dropped stitch — and said, to no one in particular, "You have got to be joking."

* * *

The infirmary's pediatric wing smelled of antiseptic and the particular sweetness of children who had been awake since before the lights came on. Seven of them sat in a row on the observation cots, which was itself remarkable, because children on cots normally behaved like water finding every possible exit from a container. These seven were sitting very still. That was the first thing that made the hair on Sefa's arms stand up. Children who are quiet have either been bribed or *changed*.

"Right," she said, pulling up her stool and clicking her stylus. "Who wants to tell me what happened?"

Six of them looked at the seventh.

The seventh was Iri Kessler, age nine, who had the specific gravity of a child who had decided, sometime in the last hour, that she was now the adult in the room. She sat with her hands folded in her lap and her chin up, and when Sefa met her eyes, Sefa had the distinct and deeply unwelcome sensation of being *interviewed*.

"We all had the same dream," Iri said.

"Okay." Sefa wrote that down. *Same dream.* "Nightmares happen. Sometimes they spread — like yawns, or rumors about the cafeteria."

"It wasn't a nightmare."

"No?"

"It was beautiful," Iri said, and the terrible thing was that she said it the way adults said it — with the particular reverence of someone describing a thing they were afraid of. "There was a tower. Made of light. And it was singing."

Sefa kept her face pleasant and her stylus moving. *Tower of light. Singing.* Classic dream material. She'd read the literature. Shared dreams in closed communities were well documented — the colony had a hundred and forty people breathing the same recycled air, eating the same protein rotations, hearing the same three songs in the common room. Minds in close quarters synchronized. It was a known thing. A *boring* thing.

"Can you tell me about the song?" she asked.

Iri frowned, and for a moment she was just a nine-year-old trying to remember something, her nose scrunching. "It wasn't a song like music. It was a song like... names. It was singing names. But not name-names. Bigger ones. Like the names of things that don't have names yet."

* * *

Sefa spent the morning doing what she did best: being methodical at a problem until it got tired and gave up. She interviewed each child separately. She drew blood, took vitals, ran the standard panels for shared toxins, mold in the dorm vents, the infamous week-old protein batch that had once given half the colony vivid hallucinations of a talking goat.

The goat incident, at least, had come with gastrointestinal symptoms. These children were pristine. Perfectly healthy. Annoyingly healthy.

And the dreams matched.

Not loosely, the way dreams usually matched — *there was water, there was falling, my teeth came out* — but precisely. Sefa sat in her office with seven interview transcripts spread across her desk like a hand of cards, and the same details kept surfacing in every hand:

A tower. Tall, thin, made of light that wasn't warm.

Darkness around it that wasn't empty — that was *watching*.

And the song. The song made of names.

Tam, age six, had said: "It was singing but it was also counting. Like it knew how many of us there were."

Sefa had laughed at that, alone in her office, and then stopped laughing, because Tam had then said, very seriously: "It said seven. It said seven like it was happy about it."

She logged it all under the heading she had been dreading and had known was coming since the third interview: *suspected mass psychogenic event*. Mass hysteria. The clinical term had a way of sounding like a diagnosis when it was really just a shrug wearing a lab coat. Shared stress, shared environment, shared suggestion. One child dreamed it, mentioned it at breakfast, and the others' sleeping minds, hungry for company, had furnished the same rooms.

It was tidy. It was plausible. It was, she wrote in the log, *the most probable explanation*.

She underlined *most probable* twice, which was a thing she did when she was trying to convince herself, and then she sat back and looked at the ceiling and admitted to the ceiling that she didn't believe a word of it.

Because of the drawing.

* * *

The drawing had come from Pip Okonkwo, age seven, who had produced it during his interview with the serene confidence of a child presenting a macaroni portrait. Sefa had almost smiled at it — children drew the tower the way children drew everything, all enthusiasm and no perspective, a bright scribble on black paper.

Then she had looked at it properly, and stopped smiling.

The tower wasn't a child's scribble. It was a *diagram*. Pip had drawn a vertical structure with a flared crown, a segmented shaft, and at the base — small, precise, repeated three times — a cluster of angular shapes that could have been decoration or could have been docking arrays. There were proportion lines. There were *annotation marks*, tiny hatchings a seven-year-old had no business making, as if he were copying something rather than imagining it.

Sefa had held the paper up to the light as though the truth might be printed on the back. *It's a relay,* said a voice in her head that sounded like her old instructor at medical school. *That's a long-range relay mast. You've seen them in the archives.*

She had seen them in the archives. Everyone had. The relay network was the colony's whole reason for existing — the thread of light connecting them to the other worlds, or it had been, before the silence, before the other colonies stopped speaking and the whole route went quiet as a held breath. The masts in the archive images were old models, but the geometry was the geometry.

And Pip, age seven, who had never left the colony and whose strongest technical interest was a beetle he kept in a jar, had drawn one from a dream. With *proportion lines*.

She had asked him, casually, kneeling down to his level: "Pip, where did you see this?"

"In the dream," he said.

"But before? In a book? On a screen?"

He'd considered the question with the honesty of the very young. "Maybe it's in a book I haven't read yet," he'd said, and gone back to describing his beetle.

* * *

By late afternoon Sefa had run out of clinical explanations and was down to uncomfortable ones, so she did what she always did when the medical answers ran dry: she went to bother the archives.

The colony's records were kept in a basement level that always felt faintly embarrassed about itself, all humming racks and bad lighting. She pulled the historical flight logs — the ones from before the silence, when ships still ran the long route regularly — and started matching.

She wasn't looking for anything in particular. That's what she told herself. She was just *checking*, the way you check a door is locked, the way you check the stove. Due diligence. The ritual of the anxious.

She found the final flight plan of Orsen Quintana at 18:40.

She knew the name, of course. Everyone did. The dead pilot whose clone had inherited his memories and his face and, apparently, his exact way of holding a coffee cup — the whole colony had a soft, unsettled fascination with the second Quintana. Sefa had met him twice at the mail dock. He'd been polite. He'd been *precise*. He'd thanked her for a routine vaccination with the same words, in the same order, that the original had used in a recorded interview eleven years earlier, and she'd gone home that night feeling like she'd shaken hands with a photograph.

The flight plan was routine. A supply run, last logged departure before the silence. Standard route, standard cargo. But attached to it — flagged, annotated, buried in the technical appendices nobody read — was the relay schematic for the Final Terminal. The far relay at the edge of dead space. The one that had gone silent first, that the old pilots had called the end of the line.

Sefa looked at the schematic.

Then she looked at the photograph of Pip's drawing that she'd taken on her slate.

Then she looked at the schematic again, and the infirmary seemed to get very quiet, the way a room does when it's listening.

The proportions matched. The flared crown. The segmented shaft. The triple docking arrays at the base, drawn three times, small and precise, because a seven-year-old's hand had copied what a seven-year-old's sleeping mind had been *shown*.

She sat down on the archive floor, because her knees had opinions of their own, and she laughed — one short, unsteady breath of a laugh with no humor in it at all.

"Okay," she said to the humming racks. "Okay. So it's not hysteria."

Because here was the thing she couldn't file, couldn't shrug, couldn't underline twice: the Final Terminal's full schematic wasn't in the children's section of the library. It wasn't in the general archives at all. She'd just spent twenty minutes confirming that. The only place it existed, as far as she could find, was in the technical appendices of one dead pilot's final flight plan — a document she had opened for the first time in her life forty minutes ago.

Seven children had dreamed a place that existed in exactly one file, in a basement, under a light that buzzed.

Sefa gathered her slate, stood up on legs that felt borrowed, and started the long walk back up through the colony's warm and ordinary levels, past the common room where someone was laughing, past the kitchen where dinner smelled like comfort, through all the gentle machinery of a life that had, at 05:12 this morning, quietly cracked.

She needed to talk to Orsen Quintana.

She just didn't yet know how to say: *Your dead original's last flight plan is being sung to our children in their sleep, and the children say the tower was happy there were seven of them, and I think something out there is counting.*

She decided she'd start with the coffee. People took bad news better with coffee. And she had the strange, certain feeling that Quintana — the second one, the echo, the man made of a dead man's memories — would already understand, before she said a word.

He'd know exactly how it felt to be handed something you inherited without asking for it.
