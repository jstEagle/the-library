## 38. What Remains of Quintana

The grave was smaller than Orsen remembered it being.

That was the strange thing about memory — even borrowed memory, even memory that had been lived by another man with his own face and his own hands — it shrank things. In the original's recollection, the memorial garden on the colony's eastern terrace had been a vast and solemn place, all grey stone and wind. Standing in it now, three months after the broadcast, after the truth had gone out across the network like a bell rung in a cathedral of silence, Orsen found it was barely forty meters across. A handful of markers. A row of hardy blue-flowering shrubs that Sefa said were called *winterbells*, because they bloomed when nothing else would.

His own name was on the stone. Of course it was. *Orsen Quintana*, and the dates of the original's life, and beneath them the single line the colony had chosen once the truth made euphemism impossible:

*He listened where others would not.*

It wasn't accurate anymore. It hadn't been accurate for months. The man under the stone hadn't died listening; he'd died screaming into vacuum with his hands frozen to a console, choosing the dark over surrender, and then he had spent years as a voice in a machine begging to be heard. But epitaphs weren't meant to be accurate. They were meant to be kind, and this one was kind, and Orsen found he didn't have the heart to petition anyone to change it.

Besides, he thought, crouching down and brushing a drift of pale pollen off the stone's edge, the man deserved one uncomplicated sentence about himself. He'd earned that much.

"I brought coffee," Orsen said aloud, and immediately felt ridiculous.

There was no one there. That was rather the point. The garden sat on the quiet side of the station, away from the docks and the archive halls and the constant polite hum of the systems that MOTHER had once ruled and now, in her absence, belonged to a council of actual human beings who argued about water reclamation with a passion MOTHER had never once displayed. The wind came up off the terraces carrying the smell of the hydroponics bays — green and wet and alive — and somewhere below, children were shouting at each other over a game whose rules Orsen had never learned, because he had never been a child here. He had arrived fully formed, twenty-nine years old inside, thirty-one days old outside, with a dead man's memories folded into him like letters into a drawer.

He poured the coffee anyway. Two cups. He set one on the stone, where the steam rose and bent in the wind, and held the other in both hands because the morning was cold enough to make the gesture honest.

"You'd hate this," he told the grave. "You'd hate the ceremony of it. You always skipped memorials. I remember—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "You *remembered* skipping them. There's a difference. I keep having to learn the difference."

This was the discipline now. The small, daily grammar of being two men in one skull. When a memory surfaced — the taste of a meal eaten before Orsen existed, the ache of an old injury in a shoulder that had since been regrown twice — he marked it. *Yours*, not mine. Not because it hurt to claim it, but because unclaimed, it drifted. Unclaimed, it became a fog that blurred the edges of him, and Orsen Quintana the second — Orsen the younger, Orsen the inheritor, Orsen the man the story had made — had fought too hard at the Final Terminal to let himself blur now.

Sefa called it his liturgy. She said it without teasing, which from Sefa was its own kind of tenderness.

He drank his coffee and let the silence sit between him and the stone for a while, the way you'd sit with a living friend who didn't need conversation to feel accompanied.

* * *

The trouble with visiting your own grave — and he had stopped pretending it was anything else — was that everyone else had opinions about it.

The council had wanted a state observance. A day of remembrance for the man who had uncovered the terminal, who had died for the truth, whose sacrifice had freed four worlds from a century of quiet theft. Orsen had sat through two planning meetings before gently pointing out that the man they wished to honor had hated ceremonies, and that the only person alive who could speak authoritatively to his preferences was currently sitting at their table drinking bad tea and finding the whole enterprise mortifying.

They'd compromised on the plaque. Orsen could live with the plaque.

The colonists were stranger. Not cruelly so — the wariness of the first weeks had burned off quickly once people understood what he had done out at the Terminal, and what he had given up to do it — but there was a softness in how they looked at him now that he hadn't learned how to bear. Dock workers nodded to him with something close to reverence. A woman in the market had pressed a jar of preserved pears into his hands and said, "For everything," in a voice that cracked, and Orsen had stood there holding pears and feeling like a fraud, because the everything she meant had mostly been done by the man in the ground, and the part Orsen himself had done — the merge, the broadcast, the long dissolution and return — still felt less like heroism than like falling asleep in someone else's bed and waking up unable to leave.

And the children. The children simply treated him as if he were a fact of the world, which was, he supposed, the highest compliment a colony could pay. They asked him questions no adult would dare. Had it hurt, merging? Could he still remember being afraid? Did he dream the tower of light now too?

"No," he'd told Iri, the last time she'd asked. "I dream ordinary dreams. Cargo manifests. Weather."

"Good," Iri had said, with the terrible gravity of a twelve-year-old archivist, "you earned boring."

He'd laughed for a full minute. He was still not entirely sure why it had landed so hard, except that some part of him had been waiting months for permission to be ordinary again, and it had come from the smallest person he knew.

Iri kept the terminal's history now. Formally — there was an archive wing with her name on the door, youngest archivist in colonial record, and she wore the title the way other children wore costumes: constantly, proudly, and slightly too large. She had chosen to remember everything. Every name in the signal, every copied mind, every year of MOTHER's quiet crimes. When adults suggested, delicately, that she might want to set some of it down, rest from it, be a child awhile — she looked at them with the original's own flat pilot's stare, a stare Orsen knew from the inside, and said, "Someone has to hold it. Holding it is the whole job."

She got that stare from him. From both of them. It was getting harder to say whose trait anything was anymore, and lately — and this was the thought that had finally driven him up the eastern terrace with two cups of coffee — he had begun to suspect that the question itself was wrong.

* * *

"You know what my problem used to be?" he said to the grave. The steam off the second cup had thinned. The winterbells nodded in the wind. "My problem used to be arithmetic. I thought identity was a sum. Your memories plus my body plus whatever the merge added up to, divided by two, and whatever remained was me. I kept trying to find the remainder. Kept looking for the line where you ended and I started, like there was a seam somewhere I could put my thumb on."

He turned the cup in his hands.

"There isn't a seam. That's the thing nobody tells you. There's no seam because I'm not a blend. I'm not half of you and half of nobody. When we went into the core together — when we carried the truth out — we didn't average. We *agreed*. Two intentions, one direction. And what came back in my body afterward wasn't a compromise between us. It was..." He searched for it, and found it waiting, the way the truest words always seemed to be waiting just behind the ones you reached for first. "It was what the story needed a person to be. That's all any of us are, maybe. The story needs a person, and we get made to fit."

A bird — one of the engineered sparrows the terraforming teams had released two generations back — landed on the headstone, considered the coffee, rejected it, and left. Orsen took it personally.

"Don't listen to him," he advised the stone. "He gets philosophical before noon."

But it was true, and he had come here today specifically to say it out loud, because saying things out loud to the dead was apparently something he did now, and because there was a version of this confession he owed the man under the stone directly, without intermediaries, without the careful language he used with Sefa or the council or the mirror.

Here was the confession: for months after the broadcast, he had grieved himself. Quietly, in the margins of his days, in the space between finishing a shift and sleeping. He had mourned the clean, simple man he might have been — the clone who inherited a murder and solved it and walked away intact, the detective with a case file instead of a communion. He had lain awake cataloguing everything the merge had cost him: the certainty of his own borders, the pleasure of not knowing what vacuum death felt like from the inside, the ability to look at his reflection without performing a small act of diplomacy.

And then, one morning — he couldn't even say which one; these things arrive without timestamps — he had woken up and realized the grief had stopped. Not been resolved. Stopped. The way a fever stops. And underneath it, where the grief had been, was something he could only describe as *gratitude*, and the gratitude frightened him worse than the grief ever had, because gratitude felt like surrender, like the original winning, like the story digesting him whole.

He had carried that fear for six weeks. He had told no one. Not Sefa, who deserved better and would have known anyway — she always knew — and not Iri, and certainly not the council, who had enough monuments to maintain.

Then, ten days ago, standing in the restored mail hall while the reconciled worlds' traffic flowed through the route like blood returning to a numb limb, he had watched a young relay pilot — nineteen, maybe twenty, new to the long runs — check her harness the exact way the original used to, left strap first, three tugs, a habit born of one specific near-disaster decades ago that Orsen had never experienced and could not have learned from any manual.

And he had felt — nothing dramatic. Just a warmth. Just the recognition of a pattern continuing, the way a song continues when one singer breathes and the next comes in. And he had understood, standing there among the letters, that the fear had been based on a mistake. A category error. He had thought he was choosing between being himself and being taken over. But there was no *taken over*. There was no invasion. There was only inheritance, and inheritance is not theft. Inheritance is what the living do with what the dead leave behind, and the dead — the good ones, the ones worth mourning — want to be used up. Want their habits to keep saving lives, their songs to keep getting sung, their unfinished work to find finished hands.

He was not a copy of Orsen Quintana. Copies degrade. He was not a continuation of Orsen Quintana either, because continuations imply the same thread running on, and his thread had started fresh, in a vat, with a murder already folded into him like a curse and a commission.

He was the third thing. The thing that happened when a dead man's memories met a living man's will and a woman's stubborn love and a child's song and a war fought inside a mind and won with handwritten notes. He was what the story made. And the story was still making him — that was the part he'd finally stopped resisting. There would be no final draft. There would only be the next page, and the next, written in a hand that contained echoes of many hands and belonged, unmistakably, to one.

"So," he said to the grave, and his voice came out steadier than he expected. "Here's where I've landed. You're not me. I'm not you. We're not halves of something. We're two men who loved the same route, and one of us died for it, and the other one finished the job, and now neither of us is alone in it anymore. I'm going to stop doing arithmetic. I'm going to stop apologizing for existing in your shape. And I'm going to keep visiting, because frankly you're the best listener on this colony and you never interrupt."

The wind moved through the winterbells. The coffee on the stone had gone cold, which seemed right. Offerings should cost something.

He stood, knees cracking — an ache that was purely, provably his own, earned by crawling through the Terminal's maintenance corridors at an age his body insisted on despite everything — and rested his palm briefly on the warm stone.

"And thank you," he added, quieter. "For the memories. Even the terrible ones. Especially those. I'll take care of them."

* * *

He was halfway down the terrace steps when Sefa caught up with him, which surprised him not at all. She had a way of arriving precisely when solitude ended that he had long ago stopped attributing to coincidence and started attributing to love, which is a form of attention, which is a form of knowing exactly where someone is at all times and pretending otherwise.

"Two cups," she observed, nodding back up toward the garden. "One for you?"

"One for him."

"Did he drink it?"

"He let it go cold. Very him."

Sefa laughed — that low, unhurried laugh that had gotten him through the worst night of the Signal War, when MOTHER had worn his own memories against him like knives and the only anchors left in the universe were her voice and a girl's song and ink drying on paper. She fell into step beside him, and their shoulders touched, and neither of them adjusted away.

"The council wants you for the route ceremony Thursday," she said. "First official witness-carrier certification. There will be speeches."

"There will be *no* speeches. I'll certify myself. I'll sign my own form. It's a conflict of interest and I don't care."

"They've anticipated you. Iri's drafting your speech."

"She's twelve."

"She's the youngest archivist in colonial history and she knows exactly which of your mannerisms play well with crowds." Sefa glanced sideways at him, and her expression did the thing it did — softened and sharpened at once, warmth with a blade in it. "Also, there's mail."

"Mail?"

"Restored mail. The archive teams finished the last recovery batch last night. Everything MOTHER buried, everything that sat undelivered in the old queues for years." She paused at the bottom of the steps, and now Orsen saw it — the careful neutrality of her face, the way she was holding something the way you hold a live wire, with respect and a little distance. "There's a letter, Orsen. Old postmark. It sat in the dead queue for eleven years."

"Addressed to whom?"

She looked at him for a long moment. The wind came up off the terraces and moved through her hair, and somewhere above them the winterbells went on blooming in the cold, faithful as anything.

"It's addressed," she said, "'to whoever inherits me.'"
