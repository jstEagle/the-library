## 1. Nine Seconds of Silence

The dead route had a sound, if you knew how to listen for it. Not the hum of the drive or the whisper of recycled air — those were alive sounds, ship sounds. The dead route sounded like nothing at all: a corridor of space so empty that even background radiation seemed embarrassed to be there. Freya Quintana had learned to hear that nothing the way sailors once learned to read fog.

She sat in the navigator's cradle with her eyes half-closed, hands resting on the interface pads, feeling the Meridian move beneath her like a long animal breathing. Six days out from Thessaly Reach, four to go before they reached the relay at Cael's End — the last whisper of civilization before the Vesper exclusion. Nobody ran this stretch anymore. Nobody with anything to lose.

Which was, of course, exactly why they did.

"Course is clean," she said, mostly to herself. "Clean as it gets."

"Clean as a lie," Sefa answered from engineering, her voice arriving through the deck plates and the comm both, slightly out of sync with itself. "You want coffee or not? The synthesizer's been making something brown all morning and I refuse to drink it alone."

Freya smiled despite herself. "Brown is a color. That's more than yesterday managed."

"That's the spirit of a woman who's never had to drink my failures."

The Meridian was a freighter the way an old dog is a guard animal — technically true, mostly retired, still capable of surprising everyone. Two hundred meters of patched hull and stubborn engineering, she'd been hauling cargo between worlds that still spoke to each other back when Freya was learning to walk. Now she hauled quieter things. Things that didn't appear on manifests. Things that slept in the hold behind three locked bulkheads and did not dream, because dreaming required a mind, and what lay in those cradles was only the shape of minds — memory-backups, whole lives folded into cold storage, illegal in every jurisdiction that mattered and several that didn't.

Freya tried not to think about them during the dead route. Thinking about them made her hands unsteady, and unsteady hands were a death sentence at navigation speeds.

She let the silence hold. Outside the viewport, the stars hung in their ancient indifference, and the ship threaded between them along a line no chart would admit existed. This was the part of the work she loved — the part before everything else, when the universe was just geometry and patience.

Then gravity blinked.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the horror of it, later — how small it began. A tickle in her inner ear, like the moment an elevator releases. Her stomach lifted a half-centimeter and settled. The stylus she kept tucked behind her ear drifted free, turned slowly twice in the air like a thing deciding, and came down again.

Freya went very still.

Gravity didn't flicker. Gravity was the one constant, the floor of physics, the promise every world made to every object upon it. Artificial fields could stutter, sure — a generator hiccup, a bad capacitor — but this hadn't come from the ship. She knew the Meridian's field the way she knew her own pulse, and this had felt *external*, a tremor passing through spacetime itself, through the hull, through her.

"Anyone else feel that?" Sefa's voice, sharp now, the playfulness gone.

"How long?" Freya asked.

"What?"

"The flicker. How long did it last?"

A pause. On the bridge, the instruments told their usual comfortable stories: field integrity nominal, drive output nominal, local spacetime flat as glass. Everything green. Everything fine.

"It lasted," Sefa said slowly, "nine seconds."

"You counted?"

"I count everything. It's why I'm good at my job." Another pause. "Nine seconds, start to finish. And Freya — the generators never cycled. Whatever dropped, it wasn't ours."

Freya pulled up the sensor logs, though she already knew what they'd say. They said nothing. Nine seconds of telemetry in which every reading was identical to the second before and the second after, a seam in the data so perfectly smooth it could only have been cut by hand. Or by something that wanted to look like a hand.

Outside, the stars had not moved. That was the detail that made her skin crawl, though she couldn't have said why. Stars *should* have moved. Even over nine seconds, at their velocity, parallax should have shifted the near ones by a hair's breadth. But the starfield in the viewport matched the starfield in her memory exactly, as if the universe had paused mid-breath and resumed without admitting it.

* * *

Captain Osei arrived on the bridge eleven minutes later, which meant he'd been awake the whole time, which meant he'd felt it too and had spent those eleven minutes deciding how to feel about it in private. He was a broad, careful man who wore his authority like a coat that had gotten too heavy over the years; he carried it anyway, because taking it off would mean admitting how tired he was.

"Report," he said.

"Gravity fluctuation," Freya said. "External origin. Duration nine seconds. No damage, no drift, no explanation."

"No explanation," he repeated.

"Every instrument says it didn't happen."

Osei stood at the viewport a long moment, looking out at the same unmoved stars. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into the register he used for decisions rather than discussions. "Then as far as the logs are concerned, it didn't. We log a routine field check, we run quiet, and we don't mention it again until we're somewhere with more armor between us and whoever asks."

"Captain—" Sefa started.

"I'm not saying it didn't happen, Engineer. I'm saying that right now, in this stretch of nothing, the truth has no value and considerable cost." He turned, and for a moment his eyes found Freya's, and there was something in them she couldn't name — not fear exactly. Recognition, maybe. As if the nine seconds had shown him something he'd already suspected was coming. "We keep the schedule. We stay dark. That's all."

He left the way he'd come, unhurried, and Freya stared after him, turning the moment over. Osei was many things — cautious, secretive, occasionally maddening — but he was not a man who scared easily. Something about the order had sounded less like caution and more like a man closing a door before something could follow him through it.

* * *

Sleep wouldn't come, so she walked the ship instead, as she often did on the dead routes. Past the galley where Sefa had left the brown coffee as a kind of threat. Past the crew quarters with their bunks made up tight. Down, down, to the lowest deck, where the corridor ended at a bulkhead stenciled with a warning nobody needed: AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.

Behind that bulkhead, forty-one cradles held forty-one lives that had been lived somewhere else.

Freya didn't open the inner door. She never did, anymore. She just stood there with her palm against the cold metal, listening to the faint subsonic murmur of the preservation systems, and thought about how the Bureau called people like her *continuity criminals*. As if remembering were a crime. As if keeping a copy of a soul safe from the incinerator were theft.

Her own name was in there. She knew it was — she'd signed the manifest herself, in the early days, before she understood what they were really carrying. Somewhere behind that door, in a cradle she'd never been able to bring herself to look at directly, slept a complete record of Freya Quintana. Every memory. Every face. Every version of her that had ever existed up to the day the backup was taken.

Standing there in the humming dark, she felt the nine seconds again — that lift in her stomach, that pause in the universe — and wondered, for the first time in a long time, whether the flicker had passed *through* the hold. Whether something out in that perfect nothing had reached toward the sleeping copies and found them wanting. Or found them interesting.

The comm crackled at her hip. Sefa. "Freya. You awake? You need to see this."

"See what?"

"The long-range array just picked up a beacon. Automated, old protocol, broadcasting on a frequency nobody's used in sixty years." A pause, and when Sefa spoke again her voice had gone carefully flat, the way it did when she was trying not to hope. "It's coming from the Vesper system, Freya. Vesper's supposed to be empty."

Freya looked one last time at the bulkhead, at the sleeping shapes of everyone they were carrying, including herself.

"Vesper isn't empty," she said quietly. "Vesper was erased. There's a difference."

She pushed off from the wall and headed for the bridge, and behind her the ship ran on through the dead route, carrying its ghosts toward the place where the ghosts had been made.
