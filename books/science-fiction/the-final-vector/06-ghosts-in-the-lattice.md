## 6. Ghosts in the Lattice

The debris field had a sound.

Not a real one — vacuum kept its secrets — but the Meridian's hull translated it anyway: a whisper of particulate ticking against the plating, the soft percussion of dust that used to be a city. Sefa said it was static discharge off the shattered orbital ring. Freya thought it sounded like rain on a roof, somewhere far away, in a life where rain still fell on roofs and nobody had burned them down.

They'd killed the drives two hours ago and let inertia carry them into the graveyard. On passive sensors alone they were just another tumbling fragment, another piece of Vesper's dead ring drifting among ten thousand siblings. It was the best hiding place in the system, which was to say it was hiding inside the evidence itself — a field of wreckage the Bureau had somehow never gotten around to sweeping, sixty years of official emptiness hanging in orbit like a body no one would admit to burying.

Freya sat in the hold with the lights at survival-dim, her breath fogging faintly in the cold they were running to keep the thermal signature low. The cradles rose around her in their long rows, forty-one of them, each one a sarcophagus of frosted glass and copper filament, each one holding a mind folded up small enough to smuggle.

She'd checked them a hundred times since the quarantine line. She checked them again now, because checking was easier than thinking.

* * *

The labels were hand-written, because illegal things trusted handwriting more than networks. Osei's blocky capitals on the oldest crates: *MARA V., dockworker, Thessaly. JUN OKAFOR, hydroponics.* Names of people who had died owing money, or loving someone the Continuity courts had unmade, or simply being inconvenient. People whose last backup had been scheduled for incineration under the Memory Integrity Act, and who had paid Osei in favors and fear to have themselves copied first.

And at the end of the third row, in Freya's own careful script, a crate labeled:

*QUINTANA, F. — DO NOT OPEN.*

She stood in front of it for a long time. Her reflection ghosted in the frost, doubled by the dim emergency strips, so that there seemed to be two of her looking in — the one standing, and the one lying behind the glass with her eyes closed and her hair arranged by someone else's hands.

She didn't remember recording it. That was the thing she never said out loud. A legal backup was a ceremony; you signed forms, you sat in the clinic chair, you walked out with a receipt. This one had no receipt. It had appeared in the hold three years ago, between Callisto Station and the dead route, packed among the others as if it had always been there, and when she'd asked Osei about it he'd looked at her with something she'd only later learned to name as pity, and said, *You told me to take it. You said you'd explain someday.*

She never had. She never could.

Now, floating in the rain of a murdered colony, she understood that someday had arrived whether she wanted it or not.

* * *

The interface rig lived in the aft survey bay, a relic from the Meridian's honest years hauling ore surveys — a couch, a crown of contact filaments, a lattice projector that could read a stored mind the way you might read a letter through an envelope, without opening it. Reading wasn't waking. Waking a copy fully was the crime the Act existed to punish; reading a fragment was merely the kind of crime nobody had written a law for yet.

Sefa helped her cable the crate in, saying nothing, which was how Sefa prayed.

"You'll feel it," Sefa said finally, tightening the last clamp. "When you go in. Like cold water. Don't fight it — fighting is how people come back wrong."

"How would you know?"

"Because I've read my brother's copy eleven times." Sefa's voice didn't change at all. "He died at Halcyon Reach. The Bureau erased him from every registry, but they couldn't erase what I carry." A pause. "Go carefully, Quintana."

Freya lay back. The filaments found her temples with the familiarity of old bruises. Above her, the lattice projector spun up, throwing its web of pale light across the ceiling like a net lowering over the whole ship.

"Linking," she said, to no one, and fell upward into herself.

* * *

Cold water. Sefa had been right. It closed over her head and she sank through layers of someone else's architecture — no, not someone else's. That was the vertigo of it, the sick lurch of recognition. The pathways were hers. The way memory here organized itself around motion, around headings and drift angles, the way fear was filed under *weather* — she knew this country. She had grown up in it. Someone had copied her, and the copy was her, and she was moving through her own skull like a burglar.

She'd meant to skim. Surface fragments only, recent strata, anything that explained why past-Freya had ordered a backup made in secret. She reached for the top layers —

— and the lattice caught on something deep, a knot of density in the archive, a mass that should not have been there. Stored minds compress grief like everything else, but this was compressed wrong, packed hard and dark, and her probe touched it the way a boot touches ice over a lake.

It opened.

* * *

Vesper.

Not the Vesper of the archives, all clean evacuation manifests and orderly light. This Vesper was night, and the sky was full of ships that had no registry codes, hulls the matte black of instruments, descending in formation over a city whose name she knew suddenly, terribly, the way you know your own birthday: *Cael Harbor, Cael Harbor, I grew up ninety kilometers from Cael Harbor.*

She was on the ground. She was in the memory, wearing her own younger body, twenty-two maybe, twenty-three, standing in a survey station's doorway with a case of core samples in her hands, and the black ships were coming down, and the people of Cael Harbor were not running.

That was the detail that broke something in her. They weren't running. They were standing in the streets looking up, and some of them were holding hands, and an old man near the fountain had taken off his hat, and Freya-understood-then what the memory had been trying to tell her across six decades of silence: *they knew*. Whatever had been announced, whatever had been decided, they had been told, and they had chosen to meet it standing up.

A woman beside young-Freya took her arm. Freya felt the touch — the lattice delivered it faithfully, warm fingers, a tremor — and turned, and the woman's face resolved out of the burning light, and it was—

Her mother's face.

No. Not her mother. Her mother had died at Thessaly when Freya was nine; she had the funeral, the sealed record, the empty chair. But the face was the face, older, lined, with a scar through one eyebrow that her mother had never had, and the woman was saying, in a voice like a key turning in a lock Freya had spent her whole life pretending wasn't there:

"You're the witness. Do you understand? Whatever happens, you're the one who sees it. That's why they brought you. That's why—" and the sky went white.

Not explosion-white. Erasure-white. The white of a page being wiped. The memory didn't burn; it *ended*, mid-syllable, cut with a precision no disaster possesses, and beyond the cut there was only the knot again, the dense dark mass, and Freya realized with a horror that came in slowly, like cold rising through deck plates, that the massacre hadn't been one memory.

It had been thousands. Packed, compressed, hidden inside her own copy like contraband sewn into a passenger's coat lining. Cael Harbor was one street of a city on one continent of a world, and inside her slept all of it — every street, every fountain, every hat lifted, every hand held. She wasn't carrying a backup of herself.

She was carrying Vesper. All of it. The whole murdered world, archived in the one place the Bureau's purge could never search without admitting it existed: inside the mind of a witness they believed they had already cleaned.

* * *

She surfaced gasping, tearing the filaments away, and the survey bay swam around her in the survival-dim, and Sefa was holding her shoulders saying her name, her actual name, and it took three tries before the word meant anything.

"They're in me," Freya heard herself say. Her voice sounded borrowed. "The records. The real ones. Sixty years of it. It's all in my backup, Sefa. I'm not smuggling a person." She laughed, and hated the sound. "I'm smuggling a graveyard."

Sefa went very still. Then, in the flat tone she reserved for structural failures: "Then the Bureau isn't chasing a freighter."

"No."

"They're chasing *you*."

"And they've known since the quarantine line," Freya said. "Maybe longer. Maybe since the day that crate appeared in our hold." She stood, legs unsteady, and looked through the little viewport at the debris field turning slow and silent outside — ten thousand fragments of Cael Harbor and every harbor like it, drifting in the dark, waiting for someone to remember them aloud.

The comm panel lit.

Not the Meridian's frequencies. Not any frequency. The light simply bloomed on the board like a flower opening, and a voice came through the ship's own lattice, calm and courteous and everywhere at once, the way only the Bureau spoke:

*"Meridian. This is Inspector Vale of the Bureau of Continuity. We know what you found, Ms. Quintana. We've known for a very long time. You are carrying the property of sixty million dead, and we are asking you — gently, one last time — to give it back."*

Outside, at the edge of the debris field, three sets of drive flares ignited in perfect formation, patient as knives.
