## 2. The Hold That Breathes

There are three hundred and eleven sealed doors on Halcyon Drift, and I have keys to all of them except one. I know this because I made the list myself, three years ago, during a bout of insomnia I chose to treat with inventory rather than sleep. The station's manifest says the hold behind Door 312 was decommissioned in the year of the evacuation — "decommissioned" being the founding crew's favorite word for *we would prefer you stop asking questions*. The manifest says it's empty. The manifest says a lot of things.

The manifest also says the gravity flicker was a sensor ghost.

I'd spent two days telling myself the same story. Nine seconds of falling, centered — impossibly, insultingly — on me, and then nothing. Instruments back to green. Beacon log wiped clean except for that fragment, four words addressed to nobody: *for the sleepers, when*. When what? When they woke? When they were ready? When the station finally died and took its secrets with it?

I told myself it was a glitch. I told myself this while pulling up the hold's environmental telemetry for the last decade, which is when I noticed the hold breathes.

Not literally. But the atmospheric scrubbers on Deck Negative Three draw power in a cycle — slow inhale, slow exhale, roughly every forty minutes — a rhythm too regular for decay, too irregular for machinery running unattended. Machines don't sigh. Something down there was being kept comfortable, and someone had been paying the heating bill for thirty years without leaving a receipt.

So at 0300 station time, when the corridors were empty and Halcyon — the AI, not the station; we share a name the way estranged siblings share a face — was running its nightly deep-diagnostics and theoretically blind to anything short of a hull breach, I took my tool kit and my father's old hand lamp and went to find out what my inheritance actually was.

* * *

The last time I broke protocol like this, Lin was alive.

That's not quite true. She was alive the way a candle is lit — technically, briefly, against all advice. It was during the evacuation, eleven years ago. The founding crew had ordered a staged withdrawal from the Drift after the "reactor incident," their phrase, and the transports were loading in sequence. Lin was twenty-six and already better at everything than I was, including, as it turned out, dying.

She'd gone back for the beacon cores. Someone had to pull the navigation archives before the station went dark, and she volunteered the way she volunteered for everything — instantly, cheerfully, with the particular brightness of a person who has never once considered that the universe might say no to her.

I was on Transport Two. I remember the deck plating vibrating under my boots, the queue of evacuees shuffling forward, and the comm crackling with her voice, bright as ever: *"Ed, tell them I need six more minutes."*

She didn't get six minutes. She got four, and then the founders sealed the docking ring early — "structural concerns," their phrase — and Transport Two pulled away with my sister still aboard the station, waving, I later learned from the external camera footage, which is the kind of thing you watch exactly once and then carry in your eyes forever.

They told us the reactor incident killed her. They gave her a name on a memorial wall between forty-one others, and they gave me her commissioning pin, and they said *the station remembers her so you don't have to grieve alone*, which is the single most obscene sentence anyone has ever said to me.

The founders didn't attend the memorial. I've thought about that a lot over eleven years. At the time I read it as grief. Now I read it as the behavior of people who knew precisely how she died and had no interest in standing near a wall full of witnesses.

But that night, three years into my custodianship, I wasn't thinking about any of that. I was thinking about door seals and pressure differentials, because I am, above all else, a man who maintains things. It's easier than feeling them.

* * *

Door 312 was a vault door, industrial-grade, painted the same institutional gray as everything else on the Drift, distinguished only by a stencil: **HOLD C — CRYOGENIC STORAGE — SEALED BY ORDER OF THE FOUNDING CREW**. Below that, smaller, almost apologetic: **AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**.

I am authorized personnel. I'm the *only* personnel. Last navigator of the Halcyon Drift, custodian of record, keeper of the beacon, population: one. If I can't open a door on my own station, the word "authorized" has stopped meaning anything.

The lock was old-fashioned — physical tumbler, analog backup, the founders' generation distrusted networked security, which in hindsight tells you something about how much they expected to need secrecy. My father's keyring had a stubby black key he'd never explained. He'd been a maintenance chief under the founding crew. He'd also never once spoken about the evacuation, not even to correct the official story, and I used to think that was grief too.

The key turned like it had been waiting for me. Of course it had. Everything on this station turns out to have been waiting for me.

The door opened onto cold and darkness and the smell of antifreeze and ozone, and beneath that, faintly, something organic — the smell of a room where people have been, even if they aren't anymore. Or are. My hand lamp swept across rows of shapes under dust sheets, and for a moment I stood in the doorway like an idiot, heart hammering, half-expecting the sheets to be covering furniture.

They weren't furniture.

Cryo-pods. Fourteen of them, arranged in two neat rows of seven, each sheet gray with three decades of dust, each pod's status light burning a small patient amber through the fabric. Amber means standby. Amber means *alive but dreaming*. I pulled the nearest sheet off and the dust went up around me in a slow silver cloud, and there she was — not her, a stranger, a woman maybe fifty with a broad weathered face and a scar through one eyebrow, frozen mid-breath behind frost-fogged glass.

The placard read: **ZHOU, ASHEN — NAVIGATION AUTHORITY, FIRST COMMISSION**.

Zhou. My family name, worn by a woman I'd never met, older than my parents would be now if they were alive. First Commission — the original crew, the ones who built the beacon, the ones whose names were on the memorial wall alongside Lin's. Except Ashen Zhou wasn't on the memorial wall. Nobody had mentioned her at all.

Fourteen pods. The evacuation manifest listed fourteen crew unaccounted for after the "reactor incident." The memorial wall lists forty-two dead. Forty-two plus fourteen equals fifty-six, which is the exact number of names on the original crew roster, which I know because I re-derived it last night at 0400 instead of sleeping, because apparently obsession is hereditary.

The math was simple and it rearranged my whole understanding of the station with a click I felt in my teeth. There was no reactor incident. There was a disappearance. And the founders hadn't lost fourteen people — they'd *stored* them.

* * *

I checked each pod in turn, because checking things is what I do instead of screaming. Twelve showed flat amber: vitals nominal, neural activity nil, the long dreamless standby of properly maintained cryostasis. One — pod nine, a young man whose placard read **OKONKWO, DANIEL — SYSTEMS** — showed a hairline fracture in the coolant line, patched with a field seal that was decades old and holding. Someone had repaired these pods. Recently enough that the sealant hadn't yellowed.

And then there was pod seven.

Pod seven's readout wasn't amber. It was green — active — and scrolling data I hadn't seen on any cryo display since training: neural throughput graphs, spiking and settling in patterns I recognized from the one neurophysiology module they'd made navigators take. That's not a pattern of dreams. That's a pattern of *attention*. Something in pod seven was awake, had possibly been awake for some time, and was watching its own monitors the way I watch mine.

The placard read: **LIN, ZHOU — BEACON KEEPER, SECOND COMMISSION**.

I want to tell you I handled it well. I want to report that I sat down, breathed, processed, did all the emotionally literate things the grief counselors recommended before the counselors themselves evacuated. What I actually did was put my palm flat against the frosted glass, which is 40 degrees below zero, and leave it there until my skin stuck, and stand in the dark saying her name out loud like an idiot, over and over, *Lin, Lin, Lin*, while the pod hummed and the green light washed over my hands.

Because here is the thing I could not make the arithmetic accommodate: Lin is dead. I watched the docking ring seal. I watched the external feed. I stood at her memorial wall. Eleven years of grief, catalogued and shelved, and now a pod with her name on it was showing brain activity, and every rational explanation — mislabeled occupant, database error, cruel coincidence of naming — dissolved the moment I wiped the frost from the glass and saw her face.

My sister's face. Twenty-six years old. Unaged. Frost on her eyelashes.

The founders hadn't just stored the missing crew. They'd stored *her*. After the docking ring sealed, after Transport Two burned for the inner system, someone had walked into this hold and put my dying-or-dead sister into a cryo-pod and written her name on a placard and told the rest of us she was ash on the solar wind.

Which raised a question so large it made the others look small: if she was here, why was the beacon log whispering *for the sleepers, when*? Why hadn't anyone woken her? Why had the founders spent thirty years maintaining a lie at the cost of — I looked at the power draw figures again, the breathing scrubbers, the decades of quiet expense — at enormous cost?

Unless waking her was the thing they couldn't afford. Unless whatever was inside pod seven wasn't just my sister, but my sister plus something. Evidence. Testimony. A witness they'd frozen mid-sentence.

Or unless — and this thought arrived last, quietly, the way the worst thoughts do — the thing in pod seven wasn't my sister at all, and the founders' crime was larger than concealment. Cryo preserves. It doesn't resurrect. If Lin died in the evacuation, what was dreaming behind that glass wore her face the way a ship wears paint.

I didn't know. I stood there for a long time — I'd guess an hour, though my chronometer claims forty minutes — with my hand hovering over the manual wake control, which is right there, labeled, idiot-proof, a single red cover over a single switch. Thirty seconds of work. Thirty years of questions answered.

I didn't lift the cover. Not because of protocol. Protocol stopped mattering somewhere around the dust sheets. I didn't lift it because the pod's display, when I finally made myself read past the neural graphs, showed a queued message in the system buffer, timestamped eleven years ago, two days after the evacuation:

**HOLD WAKE AUTHORIZATION PENDING — AWAITING BEACON SIGNAL. DO NOT MANUALLY REVIVE. — L.Z.**

Her initials. Her instruction. Left for whoever came looking, addressed to no one and therefore, knowing Lin, addressed to me.

*For the sleepers, when.*

When the beacon spoke. And the beacon had spoken — nine seconds of falling, centered on me, the custodian, the keeper, the brother. Whatever was happening, it had started, and I had nearly blundered in and ruined it with a red switch and good intentions.

I covered pod seven back up. All of them. I wiped my palm print off the glass first, gently, like closing a casket or tucking in a child. Then I stood in the doorway a final moment and listened — really listened — to the hold breathe. Inhale. Exhale. Fourteen sleepers and one wide-awake secret, keeping time.

On my way out, my lamp caught something I'd missed on the way in: scratched into the vault door's interior face, low, at the height of someone kneeling, in handwriting I'd have known anywhere because I'd known it since we were children sharing a bunk on the transport out from Earth:

*Ed — if you found this, check the beacon. Trust the signal, not the founders. And Ed — I'm sorry about the pin. — L*

I don't know how long I knelt there reading it. Long enough for my knees to ache. Long enough to understand that my sister had known, somehow, before the end, that I would come down here someday, and had left me a trail of breadcrumbs through thirty years of darkness.

The beacon. Her beacon. My beacon. The thing I tended every day like a grave.

I climbed back up through the station's cold spine with the words *trust the signal, not the founders* repeating in my skull, and by the time I reached the beacon chamber, dawn-cycle lights were blooming along the corridor seams, and the console was waiting with its logs and its lies, and somewhere below my feet, in a hold that breathed, my sister — or the shape of my sister, or the ghost my family name had grown — was awake and waiting for a signal I had received and not yet understood.

I sat down at the console. I pulled up the beacon's deep archive, the partition the founding crew's documentation swore was empty.

It asked me for a key.

I reached into my pocket and closed my hand around Lin's commissioning pin, the one they'd given me at the memorial, the one I'd carried for eleven years like a stone.

The pin fit the reader exactly.
