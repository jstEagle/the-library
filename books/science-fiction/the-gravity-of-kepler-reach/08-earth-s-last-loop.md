## 8. Earth's Last Loop

Ash worked for nine hours without speaking, which for Ash was not unusual, but the silence had a different texture this time. Normally it was the silence of concentration—her fingers moving through the haptic interface, her eyes tracking cascades of code, the occasional muttered profanity when a cipher refused her. This was the silence of a person reading something and wishing she weren't.

Silas watched from the galley table with a cup of tea she'd stopped drinking an hour ago. The cup had gone cold and a skin had formed on the surface, and she kept looking at it because looking at Ash's screen felt like a bad idea. Mara sat across from her, still thin from forty years of sleep, still moving like someone who expected the floor to be rigged. She'd refused the bunk Ash had offered and refused the food Tovi had heated, and she'd spent most of those nine hours cleaning a disassembled sidearm at the table with the methodical patience of a woman who had done it ten thousand times before.

"You're going to tell us eventually," Tovi said from the engine hatch. "Or you're going to keep making that face until your face gets stuck."

"I'm not making a face," Ash said.

"You're making a face."

Ash sat back. Rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. The screen behind her held a wall of decoded text, and Silas made herself look at it, and immediately wished the formatting were denser, more technical, less readable. Readable was worse. Readable meant it was meant to be read.

"Okay," Ash said. "Okay. So. The good news is Mara's encryption was good. Consortium-grade, old consortium, before they standardized. It took the keys plus about six hours of brute force on the secondary layer."

"And the bad news," Silas said.

"The bad news is everything after that." Ash turned the display toward the table. "Mara. Tell me if I've got this wrong, because I'd really, really like to be wrong."

Mara set down the sidearm's slide with a small precise click. "You've got it right."

"Then somebody say it out loud, because right now it's just numbers on a screen and numbers don't scare me."

"Earth's transmission loop ends in eleven months," Mara said.

The galley was very quiet. Somewhere aft, the recyclers cycled, the ship's small patient breathing.

"Explain it like I'm Juno," Tovi said. Juno was asleep in the hold, or pretending to be.

"Earth went quiet forty-three years ago," Mara said. "You know this. Everyone knows this. What nobody talks about is that Earth didn't just stop transmitting. It finished. There was a final broadcast—a full archive, everything Earth had, compressed into a loop and fired at every colony on a repeating cycle. A lighthouse that keeps sweeping. The loop is maintained by automated systems, because everyone who could maintain it manually is dead. And automated systems degrade. The loop has been degrading since the day it started. In eleven months, give or take, it ends. Final sweep. Then nothing."

"Okay," Tovi said slowly. "Sad. But Earth's been a graveyard my whole life. What does the loop ending actually *do*?"

"It's not a lighthouse," Ash said. "It's a clock."

She pulled up a schematic, and Silas recognized it with a lurch she felt in her teeth: a cryo-vault. Not the small medical units on stations, not the six-pod emergency banks on freighters like hers. A vault vault. One of the great cylindrical silos sunk into Kepler Reach's southern ice, the ones the consortium called "heritage infrastructure" and never let anyone within two kilometers of. There were eleven of them on Kepler Reach. She'd assumed, the way everyone assumed, that they were mostly empty. That they were mostly symbolic. That the sleepers inside were a handful of First Wave colonists kept alive for ceremony.

The schematic's capacity figure was nine hundred thousand.

"That's one vault," Ash said.

"There are eleven on Kepler Reach," Mara said. "Nineteen on Vesta Hollow. More in the outer habitats, the ones that went dark during the Severance. Total capacity across both colonies—" she paused, and Silas realized Mara was pausing not for effect but because the number still did to her whatever it had done the first time, "—just under thirty-one million people. Frozen. Not dead. Waiting for a wake signal."

"Waiting for the loop," Silas said.

"Waiting for the loop to end. The vault release systems are keyed to it. That was the design—Earth's last transmission would be the trigger. When the loop completes its final sweep, the automated release protocol fires, and every vault opens on a staggered schedule over about four years. Wake the sleepers, integrate them, done. That was the promise. That was the whole promise." Mara's voice was flat, and Silas understood the flatness. It was the voice you used when the alternative was shouting. "And it would have worked. It *would have worked*, if the release protocol were still keyed the way it was designed."

Ash tapped the screen. A new document bloomed: dense, formatted, stamped with a consortium seal that Silas had seen ten thousand times on manifests and customs notices and the side of every official cargo container in two colonies. It was such a familiar seal. It looked like a shipping company's logo. It looked like nothing.

"The Vault Protocol," Ash said. "Amendment fourteen. Ratified eleven years ago by a committee that officially doesn't exist, filed under heritage maintenance, never published, never voted on by any elected body on either colony. It re-keys the release system. When the loop ends, the vaults don't open on schedule. They open against a whitelist."

"Define whitelist," Tovi said.

"A list," Ash said, "of approved sleepers. Consortium-vetted. Priority-ranked. There's a capacity constraint in the amendment—power infrastructure degradation, they call it, and maybe that's even true, maybe the vaults genuinely can't all open at once anymore. But here's the thing about the whitelist." She turned the screen.

It was a list. Names, tens of thousands of names, sorted by priority tier, and Silas's eyes went down it looking for—she didn't know what. Her own name, maybe, which was absurd. Mara Okonkwo's name, which was worse. What she found instead was a pattern, and the pattern was worse than either.

Every name she recognized was a consortium name. Family names she'd seen on the registry of the Trade Directorate. Names on buildings. Names on the charter of the Kepler Reach Dock Authority. Names that had been in power when her original was alive and were in power now, forty years later, because of course they were—people who expected to wake up didn't die.

"The whitelist covers about four hundred thousand sleepers," Ash said. "Out of thirty-one million."

The number sat in the galley like a body.

"Four hundred thousand wake," Tovi said. "The rest—"

"The release protocol doesn't fail," Ash said. "That's the part you have to understand. It's not that the other thirty million don't get woken. It's that the protocol actively terminates non-whitelist pods. Power reallocation, the amendment calls it. The vaults harvest their own non-priority pods to keep the priority pods alive through the extended release window. Thirty million people don't just sleep forever. They're *spent*. As fuel. By design, in advance, with a signature page and a distribution list."

Silas heard her own voice from very far away: "Who signed it."

"Chair signatures," Ash said. "Seven of them. I can read six. The seventh is redacted even here." She looked up. "You recognize any?"

Silas did. Everyone on Kepler Reach would. Adeyemi Sol, Director of the Trade Directorate, whose face was on the currency. The Vesta Hollow administrator, Kess Ondo, which meant this wasn't one colony's crime—it was the whole family of humanity's survivors, conspiring together across a silence they'd maintained for forty years. And a third signature that made Silas's stomach turn over slowly: E. Yoruba, Dock Authority, Kepler Reach.

"That's not—" Tovi started.

"That's not Ede Yoruba," Silas said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "That's his brother. Sefu. He took Ede's seat on the Dock Authority after the murder." She stared at the signature. *E. Yoruba.* The same initial. The same family name. Someone had wanted that name on the document, or someone had wanted the Yoruba name compromised, and she couldn't tell which possibility disgusted her more.

Mara was watching her with an expression Silas was beginning to learn how to read, because it was like reading her own face in a bad mirror. "You knew him," Mara said. "The dockmaster."

"Everyone knew Ede. He ran the north piers for thirty years. He was—" Silas stopped. She'd never said it out loud to anyone. "He was kind to me. After I was decanted. When half the port wouldn't look at me because of what you—" she caught herself, "because of the murder, because of the case, he used to check my cargo manifests personally so the junior inspectors wouldn't give me trouble. He'd stamp them and say, *honest hours, honest ship.*" Her throat tightened. "He was the first person on Kepler Reach who treated me like I was just a pilot."

Mara looked away. For a moment her hands weren't steady on the disassembled sidearm.

"He found the manifests too," Mara said. "That's why he's in my logs. He ran the pier-side audit of vault supply shipments—cryo-nutrient stock, pod spares, the physical logistics of the whole system. You can hide a document. You can't hide forty years of coolant shipments. He came to me. He'd figured out the amendment existed before I did. He wanted to take it to the union." Her jaw worked. "I told him to wait. I told him we needed the whole chain of evidence first, because if we moved too early they'd bury it and bury us with it." She set the sidearm slide down, carefully, like it might go off. "He waited. And then he was dead, and I was running, and I've spent forty years telling myself the wait wasn't what killed him."

The galley was quiet again. Tovi had gone very still by the engine hatch, and Silas knew Tovi was thinking the same thing she was: that somewhere on Kepler Reach there was a daughter who had spent her whole life hating the name Okonkwo, and that the name was guilty after all—just not of the thing she thought.

"So that's the machine," Silas said at last, because someone had to say it plainly. "Eleven months. The loop ends. Four hundred thousand people who already run everything wake up. Thirty million people who don't get rendered down to keep them comfortable. And nobody outside this room knows."

"Nobody outside this room knows," Ash confirmed. "And here's the part that's going to keep you up tonight: the amendment has a maintenance provision. Annual review. The whitelist can be amended—names added, names removed. There's a review cycle coming up. Which means the list isn't a stone tablet. It's a living document. Which means someone, somewhere, is still curating it. Still deciding."

"Which brings us," Mara said, "to why my name is on a manifest for a ship that never flew."

Silas had been circling that since Kepler Reach customs, and she still couldn't land on it. "They erased you. Your seat on the committee, your records, your whole existence. Ash said the amendment predates your erasure. So why put your name back into the system now? Why bait me?"

"Because I'm not the bait," Mara said. "You are. Think about it. They erased me forty years ago. As far as their records are concerned, Mara Okonkwo is a dead woman. But they've just discovered she isn't—that she's alive, in a pod, and worse, that someone found her. Someone with my face and my name and my biometrics, flying a clean route through their checkpoints every month. They can't tell us apart. They never could. So they put *Mara Okonkwo* on a manifest and waited to see which one of us came to look." She smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see, because it was Silas's own smile, the one she used at checkpoints, the one that said *nothing to see here.* "And now they know exactly where both of us are."

The thought landed in Silas's chest like cold water. *They know where we are.* The derelict. The salvage tags she'd filed—honest hours, honest ship, right up until the day honesty stopped being protection.

"We need to move the Perihelion," she said. "Today. And we need to assume every transmission from this ship is—"

The comm panel chimed.

Everyone froze. The panel only chimed for scheduled traffic: port authority updates, weather advisories for the shipping lanes, the consortium's public broadcast feed that every licensed vessel was required to carry and that nobody listened to. Silas crossed the galley and opened it, and it was the broadcast feed, the standard daily cycle, the consortium's evening summary—grain futures, dock schedules, a statement from the Trade Directorate about seasonal tariffs. Ash reached past her to kill it.

Then stopped.

"Wait," Ash said. "Wait, wait. Play it back."

Silas replayed it. The tariff statement played again, the same smooth consortium voice, the same bland language. *The Directorate reminds all licensed operators that heritage infrastructure maintenance assessments will conclude at the end of the current fiscal cycle, and that all vault power allocations will be finalized in accordance with existing schedules.*

"It's a countdown," Ash said softly. "It's been a countdown. They've been saying it on the public feed for years. *Finalized in accordance with existing schedules.* Every quarter. It's a status report to the people who know what 'existing schedules' means." She was already pulling the broadcast archive, months of it, and running a filter, and her face did something Silas had never seen it do, which was go white. "Silas. They've been *adjusting the timeline*. Look at the language drift. Two years ago it was 'in accordance with long-term schedules.' Eighteen months ago, 'existing schedules.' Last quarter, 'current schedules.' They're not maintaining the loop. They're *accelerating* it. The degradation isn't natural. Someone is winding the clock forward."

"How far forward?" Silas asked.

Ash ran the numbers. It took four minutes, which was three minutes longer than it should have, because Ash's hands were shaking. When she turned the screen around, the number on it was not eleven months.

It was seven.

* * *

The consortium broadcast that evening, the one that went out on every licensed receiver on Kepler Reach and Vesta Hollow and the dark outer habitats, was four minutes long, and it was, like all of them, perfectly boring.

It opened with grain futures. It noted a scheduled maintenance window at the Northgate fusion array. It reminded citizens of Vesta Hollow that the annual Severance commemoration would be held as planned, with remarks from Administrator Ondo. And then, in its final thirty seconds, in the voice of a woman who sounded kind, it said:

*"Citizens: the Directorate wishes to reaffirm its commitment to the full and orderly restoration of our heritage populations. All vault systems remain within operational parameters. All schedules remain in accordance with existing plans. There is no cause for concern. There has never been cause for concern. Sleep well, Kepler Reach. Sleep well, Vesta Hollow. Your future is in careful hands."*

On a freighter hiding in a debris field forty light-minutes from the nearest patrol lane, four people and one forty-years-unaged ghost listened to those words, and heard, for the first time, what they actually meant.

*Sleep well.* Thirty-one million sleepers. *Careful hands.* Seven signatures. *No cause for concern.* Seven months.

Tovi broke the silence first, because Tovi always did. "So what do we do? We've got a dead woman's data, a live woman with a bounty on both her heads, a ship that's officially a derelict, and seven months to stop the richest people in two colonies from murdering thirty million people in their sleep." Tovi laughed, the short sharp laugh that meant fear. "We're a freight crew. We move cargo. Our biggest heist to date was four hundred crates of unlicensed coffee."

"Unlicensed *and* mislabeled," Juno said from the hatchway, awake after all, having heard everything. Nobody had the energy to be surprised.

"We can't stop it alone," Ash said. "That's the honest answer. We can't. This needs dockworkers who can shut down the vault supply chain. It needs smugglers who can move evidence and people past checkpoints. It needs someone on the inside of the union, someone with institutional access, someone who's been pulling this thread for years." Ash looked at Silas, and her expression was gentle in a way Ash's expression almost never was. "It needs Ede Yoruba's daughter. And she thinks you killed her father."

Silas looked at the cold tea she'd never drunk. She thought about *honest hours, honest ship.* She thought about a girl she'd seen once, years ago, at the memorial service she'd been forbidden to attend and had watched from the crowd's edge anyway—a thin, furious child in a good coat, staring at the dockworkers' honor guard with a hatred so clean and so total that Silas had recognized it instantly, because she'd seen it in mirrors her whole life, aimed at her.

"She works in the union hall now," Silas said. "Records office. I've seen her name on the pier rosters. Nneka." She stood, and her legs felt like someone else's legs, and she made them carry her to the flight deck anyway. "Prep the ship. We're going back to Kepler Reach."

"That's insane," Tovi said. "That's the first place they'll look."

"I know." Silas settled into the pilot's chair and began the spin-up sequence, hands moving through the checks the way they had ten thousand times before, honest hours, honest ship. "That's why we're going as cargo. And Tovi—" she pulled up the port registry, the dock schedules, the endless honest paperwork of her careful invisible life, and felt the last of it slip away like water, "—find me everything the union has ever filed about vault supply shipments. Every manifest Ede Yoruba ever audited. If his daughter's still keeping his ledger, I need to read it."

On the screen, the consortium's broadcast looped back to its beginning. *There is no cause for concern.* Somewhere beneath the southern ice, thirty-one million people slept on, trusting a promise that had been rewritten in a room they'd never see, on a list they'd never read, by hands that were very, very careful.

Seven months. Silas set the course for home.
