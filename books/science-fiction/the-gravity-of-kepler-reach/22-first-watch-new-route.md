## 22. First Watch, New Route

The manifest said grain. The hold smelled like grain. For the first time in Silas Okonkwo's flying life, that was all it was.

She ran the pre-departure check with her hands more than her eyes—throttle housings, coolant lines, the new relay Tovi had soldered in twice because he didn't trust his own first work. Outside the canopy, Vesta Hollow's yard lights strung themselves across the dark like a town deciding to stay lit. Six months ago those same lights had been blackout-scheduled, rationed by a consortium that no longer existed to ration them.

"Boarding in four minutes," Juno's voice said over the intercom, bright as ever. "We've got a passenger who wants to see the bridge."

"We've got a passenger?"

"Born eight days ago. She doesn't have a say in anything yet, so I'm exercising it on her behalf."

Silas smiled into her checklist. That was how things were now: passengers arrived without manifests, and nobody checked them against a whitelist, because there wasn't one anymore. There was just a list, and everyone was on it.

* * *

They came up the ramp together, the way they always did—Tovi first, talking with his hands about torque values nobody had asked about; Ash behind him carrying a medkit she no longer needed to carry but carried anyway, the way some people kept a weapon after a war ended; and Nneka Yoruba last, datapad under her arm, union liaison credentials freshly printed and already creased from use.

And between them, wrapped against Ash's shoulder, a baby with her father's stubborn chin and her mother's unimpressed expression.

"That's Sefu," Nneka said, as if Silas couldn't see him. "He's riding to Kepler Reach because his grandmother says the Reach docks smell like home, and I've learned not to argue with grandmothers."

"He's eight days old."

"He's a union member. We enrolled him at the hospital." She said it deadpan, but her eyes were doing something softer than her mouth would allow. "His grandfather would have hated the paperwork and loved everything else."

Ede Yoruba would have loved this, Silas thought. A dock where the manifests told the truth. A ship named for a dead woman's second chance, loading honest cargo in daylight. He'd died refusing to falsify a list of sleeping people, and now his grandson was crossing the same gangway he had, and no one was checking the boy's name against anything.

"Bridge is yours," Silas said. "All of you. It always was, apparently. You never wait for me to ask anymore."

"You stopped asking," Tovi said, dropping into the engineer's seat like gravity owed him money. "About six months ago. So we stopped waiting."

Ash settled Sefu into the spare crash couch, wedged with blankets until he looked like cargo that had been declared properly. "Vitals good," she reported anyway, out of habit. "Passenger stable. Heart rate appropriate for someone with no idea what he's been born into."

"And what's that?" Juno asked, swinging up last, taking the nav station without looking at the board because the board was already hers.

Nneka looked out the canopy at the yard, at the cranes moving again, at the long queue of ships that used to be smugglers and now mostly weren't, though a few kept the old call signs out of sentiment.

"Lucky," she said. "He's been born into lucky. Somebody burned a ship to make sure of it."

Silas felt that one land where old burns land—in the scar tissue along her left side, in the phantom ache of a core breach she'd walked away from by eleven seconds. She let it land. Some things you were supposed to keep feeling.

"Pre-flight," she said. "Let's go home the long way."

* * *

The route between Vesta Hollow and Kepler Reach took nine days now instead of fourteen, because the consortium checkpoints were gone and ships could fly straight lines instead of smuggling ones. Silas still flew the old curve sometimes, out of habit—the wide arc through the debris field where they'd found the Perihelion derelict, where she'd stood in a cold corridor and looked at her own face asleep behind frosted glass.

Today she flew the straight line. Progress was supposed to feel like that, she'd decided. Like giving up the scenic route through your worst memories.

Day two, Juno found her in the galley at 0300, which was when Silas did her best thinking and her worst sleeping.

"You're doing the thing," Juno said, taking the opposite bench.

"What thing."

"The staring-at-the-wall thing. The 'I survived something enormous and now I'm folding laundry' thing." Juno stole a protein square off Silas's tray. "You've been licensed-pending for five months. They're never going to hold that hearing, you know. Nobody wants to be the committee that revoked the license of the woman who opened the vaults."

"I know."

"So why do you still log every hour like an auditor's watching?"

Silas turned her tea mug slowly on the table. "Because for thirty years, being watched was the danger. And then I learned the opposite—that being *unseen* was the danger. That invisibility was permission they granted me, not safety I earned." She looked up. "I log honest hours because I can. Because nobody's going to use them against me. It turns out that was the whole prize. Not heroism. Just... being allowed to be legible."

Jono chewed thoughtfully. "You're very bad at enjoying winning."

"I'm working on it."

"Work faster. We arrive tomorrow and there's a dedication ceremony at Ede Yoruba's old berth and Nneka will personally injure us if we're late."

* * *

Kepler Reach came up gray-green and busy, the yards swarming with the kind of chaotic, unregulated, gloriously inefficient activity that meant freedom. Dockworkers moved cargo by hand where the autoloaders jammed, arguing, laughing, swearing in three languages. The consortium towers still stood downtown, but their windows were dark except where the new cooperative council had moved in, and someone had hung a banner down the face of the tallest one that read, in letters visible from orbit: **EVERY NAME ON THE LIST**.

They berthed at Berth 14. Ede Yoruba's berth.

The crowd was bigger than Silas expected. Dockworkers mostly, in their patched coveralls, but also families, kids running between crates, old men who remembered the Severance and young adults who'd been born after it and had never known anything but silence between the colonies—until eleven months ago, when the vaults opened and half a million sleepers woke to find their war had been decided for them, wrongly, and then rightly, by strangers.

Nneka stood at the podium with Sefu asleep against her shoulder, and she didn't give a speech so much as read names. Her father's. The dockworkers who'd refused falsified manifests and paid for it. The sleepers who hadn't woken. Then, last, quietly:

"And Mara Okonkwo, who found the list first and paid for it second. And Silas Okonkwo, who—" she paused, and looked directly at Silas, and something passed between them that had taken years and a weapon and a ledger to build— "who paid for it too, and kept flying anyway."

Afterward, while the crowd thinned and the crew argued cheerfully about whose turn it was to load the return grain, a girl detached herself from the knot of children near the gangway. Maybe seven years old. Post-Severance born, Silas guessed—she had the particular fearless posture of a child who'd never known a checkpoint.

"You're the captain," the girl said. Statement, not question.

"I'm the captain."

"Why is your ship called Perihelion? Juno said it used to be a ghost ship with a dead lady inside it. That's creepy."

"Juno tells that story wrong on purpose," Silas said. "It was a ghost ship with a *sleeping* lady inside it. Big difference."

The girl considered this. "Why Perihelion, though?"

Silas crouched to her level. Behind her, she could hear Tovi telling Ash that the number-three injector sounded fine and Ash telling Tovi that he'd said that about the number-one injector right before it tried to leave the ship, and she let the noise wash over her, because this was what safety sounded like: her family, bickering, within earshot.

"Perihelion," Silas said, "is the point in an orbit where you're closest to the sun. Closest to the light. Every orbit has one—it's built into the math. No matter how far out you swing, no matter how dark and cold the far end gets, the path always comes back around to the closest point. To the light."

The girl frowned at the hull. "So the ship comes back to the light?"

"The ship does. People do too. Even when the orbit takes you somewhere terrible—even when somebody else draws the orbit for you—you come back around. That's the promise. That's the whole name."

The girl nodded with the enormous seriousness of the young, then ruined it by asking if the dead lady had really punched anyone, because Juno had said that too, and Silas said yes, actually, the dead lady had excellent form, and the girl ran off satisfied toward the grain loaders.

Silas stayed crouched a moment longer, looking at the hull. Someone had painted a small mark near the airlock—a stylized sun, half-risen over a curve. She hadn't done it. None of the crew had admitted to it. It had simply appeared sometime during the months of hearings and repairs and quiet flights, the way gratitude does: without attribution, without needing any.

* * *

That night, dockside, the crew ate together at a table too small for five adults and a baby, which was the correct size for them. Ash fed Sefu something mashed and narrated it like mission telemetry. Tovi explained, unprompted, that he'd rebuilt the Perihelion's original drive core pattern from memory and that the new ship's engine sang the same note, and that this mattered, and nobody disagreed because it did matter. Juno dealt cards from a deck missing three eights and a king, and cheated anyway.

Mara was gone. She'd left four months ago, on a slow hauler bound for Earth's silent ruins, taking the decryption keys' originals and the question of the original with her. Her last message had come in last week, delayed and degraded, sent from somewhere past the heliopause relay: *Found the old archives. Found her grave, or a grave. Doesn't matter whose. What matters is I got to choose who I am out here, and I chose someone who knows you. Wake up the light for me sometimes.*

So Silas did. Every morning, she opened the bridge shutters before anyone asked, and let the star fall across the consoles, and logged it in the honest hours: *one sunrise, witnessed, no charge.*

Nneka caught her eye across the table. "Route assignments post next week," she said. "The council wants regular service both directions. Passenger certification, school shipments, mail." She paused. "Real mail. Between real colonies. Your ship's first in the queue, if you want it."

Silas looked down the table—Tovi mid-argument, Ash laughing, Juno cheating, Sefu banging a spoon with the rhythm of someone who had never once doubted the world would feed him—and found that the answer had been sitting in her chest since before the question.

"Yeah," she said. "I want it."

"Didn't think you'd say otherwise." Nneka raised her cup. "To the long route."

"To the short one," Tovi corrected. "Nine days, straight line, no checkpoints."

"To the crew," Ash said.

"To the names," Juno said, "all of them, every single one, on the list, forever."

Sefu threw his spoon. It landed in the gravy. Everyone cheered like it was a launch.

Later, alone on the bridge for her first watch, Silas ran the departure checklist for morning, logged the hours honestly, and opened the shutters early. Kepler Reach's sun broke over the yard cranes and laid itself across the console, across the little painted sun on the hull outside, across her hands on the controls of a ship named for the point where you stop falling away and start coming home.

On the board, a priority ping blinked. New route assignment, effective immediately: Vesta Hollow, Kepler Reach, and—one hop further, newly reopened, its beacon silent for forty years and burning again as of yesterday—a third colony. One of the ones that had gone dark during the Severance. One full of people waking up who had never heard of her, or the vote, or the ship, or any of it.

A whole world coming back around the far end of its orbit, swinging toward the light.

Silas Okonkwo smiled, accepted the route, and began to plot the burn.
