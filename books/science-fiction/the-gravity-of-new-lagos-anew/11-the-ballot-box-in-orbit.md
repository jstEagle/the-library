## 11. The Ballot Box in Orbit

The Undertow smelled like fried plantain and solder smoke, which Noor decided was as good a definition of home as any.

He'd been aboard the unregistered decks for six days now, sleeping in a hammock strung between coolant pipes in Iya Risi's back workshop, eating whatever Olu the courier brought up from the market, and watching three of the most disreputable technicians in orbital space take turns failing to crack his beacon core.

"Your machine," said Femi, who was built like a shipping container and had hands so delicate he could thread a needle in freefall, "is insulting me."

"It insults everyone," Noor said. "It's how it says hello."

Femi glared at him. The core sat on the workbench under a cage of diagnostic arms, looking — as it always had — like nothing much. A navigation beacon. Thirty years old, give or take. Black casing gone grey with radiation tan, a serial number that matched no registry, commissioning papers that matched no office. For most of his adult life, Noor had kept its light burning and thought about it exactly as often as he thought about his own heartbeat: constantly, professionally, never with curiosity.

Now it was the only thing he owned that anyone was willing to burn down a station for.

"You're sure you want to keep going?" Iya Risi asked from her chair by the door, where she presided over everything the way gravity presides over falling. She was small, silver-haired, wrapped in a robe the color of an eclipse, and she had agreed to shelter him for reasons she had not yet stated and he had not yet dared to ask about. "Every hour we spend on this thing is an hour Vane's people spend looking for us."

"That's why we should finish," Noor said. "Whatever it is, it's worth more to them than my whole life was. I'd like to know what that feels like."

Iya Risi made a sound that might have been a laugh in a younger throat. "Crack it, then."

* * *

The breakthrough came, as breakthroughs do, at four in the morning, from someone nobody had been listening to.

Her name was Chidinma, sixteen years old, Iya Risi's grand-niece, technically forbidden from touching the core and therefore the only one who had. While Femi slept and the other techs argued about encryption keys, she had done something no professional would have done: she'd stopped trying to break into the beacon and started listening to it.

"It's not encrypted," she announced, walking into the workshop with a datapad held out like a live grenade. "I mean, it is, but not against *us*. It's encrypted against deletion. There's no lock on the front door. There's just — no door at all. Everything that goes in stays in."

Noor took the pad. "Everything that goes in?"

"The memory traffic." She was vibrating slightly, the way young people vibrate when they've found something enormous and haven't yet been told to be frightened of it. "Every suppressed chip that's ever passed through customs above New Lagos Anew — every childhood somebody sold, every grief somebody surrendered, every name somebody paid to have forgotten — it all routes through relay traffic on its way to the Trust vaults. Standard architecture. And your beacon sits on the relay."

"So it repeats the signal," Noor said slowly. "That's what beacons do."

"It doesn't repeat it." Chidinma pulled up a schematic, and there it was: thirty years of signal flow, drawn in lines of light. "It *keeps* it. Every packet. Full fidelity. There's a second antenna array buried in the housing — passive, shielded, invisible to every scan because nobody scans for something that isn't transmitting. It's been copying the suppressed-memory stream since before I was born."

The workshop went very quiet. Even the coolant pipes seemed to hush.

"That's impossible," said Femi, awake now, hair flattened on one side. "The storage alone—"

"Quantum lattice," Chidinma said, with the breezy cruelty of the young toward their elders' assumptions. "Dense enough to hold a city's worth of dreams in a box the size of a bread loaf. Whoever built this thing wasn't building a beacon."

Noor stared at the black casing. His beacon. His quiet, faithful, boring beacon, the one fixed point in a life spent moving other people's secrets around. He had tended it through eleven station commanders, two fires, one quarantine, and the slow erosion of everyone he'd ever known. He had checked its power draw the way other men checked their pulse.

"What was it building?" he asked.

Chidinma looked at Iya Risi. Iya Risi looked at Noor.

"There's one way to find out," she said. "Open it."

* * *

They opened it at dawn, ship's dawn, which in the Undertow meant someone turned the lights down low and amber and everyone pretended to feel rested.

Inside the housing, beneath the transmitter array Noor had serviced ten thousand times, they found the second machine. It was beautiful, in the way that things are beautiful when they were made by someone who cared more about the work than about being caught. A quantum lattice the size of a fist, cradled in shock foam, wired directly into the relay bus. And etched into the casing, small enough to miss unless you were looking, a single line of text:

**FOR THE ONES WHO WERE SUBTRACTED.**

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

"Subtracted," Femi repeated finally. "From what?"

"From everything," Noor said. His voice came out strange, flat, like a recording of himself. He was thinking of the manifest. *Meridian Dawn.* Tomas Adeyemi, dead twenty years, listed on a crew roster of a ship that never flew. He was thinking of the memory Adeyemi had bought the week he died — the Trust's original selection criteria — and of Silas Vane saying *surrender the raw logs* in a voice like honey poured over a knife.

Vane hadn't wanted the beacon because it was evidence of smuggling. Vane had wanted it because it was a witness.

"Play it," Noor said. "The earliest file."

Chidinma's fingers hovered. "You're sure? Once we open the stream—"

"I'm sure."

She touched the pad.

The first memory bloomed into the workshop's display: a woman's voice, a kitchen, rain on a window in some district Noor didn't recognize. A birthday. A child laughing off-screen. Ordinary. Heartbreaking in its ordinariness. And stamped across its metadata, in the Trust's clean administrative typeface:

*SUPPRESSED — VOLUNTARY SURRENDER — COMPENSATION PAID — RECORD SEALED.*

Then another. A man remembering his mother's funeral, sold to pay for his brother's berth. Then another. A whole district festival, sold collectively, forty families, one payment, one forgetting. Then another, and another, and another, scrolling now, hundreds, thousands, thirty years of them, every erased memory of every person who had ever traded their past for a seat on a ship that hadn't left yet.

"They think they sold them," Iya Risi said softly. "The people who sold their memories. They think the memories are gone, or locked in a Trust vault where nobody will ever grieve them again." Her eyes were bright and terrible. "But this box remembers them all. Every single one."

"It's not a beacon," Noor said.

"No," Iya Risi agreed.

"It's a ballot box. Every memory is a vote. Every name in here—" He stopped, because the arithmetic was suddenly too large to say out loud. Everyone the Trust had struck from the manifest. Everyone reclassified as ballast. Everyone who had signed away a childhood and been told the transaction was clean, final, private. All of them, recorded. All of them, waiting.

"Thirty years," Femi murmured, running the count. "At current suppression rates... Noor, there are names in here. Actual names, attached to actual faces, attached to the exact clauses they signed. This isn't an archive. This is a *census*. Of everyone the Trust decided didn't exist."

"And proof," Chidinma added quietly. "Consent forms can be forged. Suppression records can be scrubbed. But you can't fake thirty years of continuous capture. The lattice has checksums going back to day one. Any auditor — any honest one — could verify the whole chain."

"Any honest auditor," Iya Risi said dryly, "and the Trust employs none. Which is why this box has stayed hidden in plain sight for thirty years, sitting on a relay, blinking its little light, being maintained by—" she looked at Noor with something new in her face, something between pity and respect, "—by a man whose own commissioning record is blank."

The words landed harder than she could have known.

"My record's blank," Noor said. "So is the beacon's. You don't find that interesting?"

"I find that everything about you is interesting," Iya Risi said, "which is why I'm still deciding what you're worth."

* * *

Later, when the others had fallen asleep at their stations and the workshop held only the hum of the lattice and the amber dawn-light, Noor sat alone with the core and did the thing he had avoided doing all night.

He searched the archive for himself.

He told himself it was procedure. Verify the capture chain. Test retrieval on a known subject. But his hands weren't steady, and he knew the difference between procedure and prayer.

He entered his own name — the real one, the one he barely remembered, the one Vane had said like a man laying down a winning card — and waited.

The lattice returned a single result.

*RECORD EXISTS. ACCESS RESTRICTED.*

Not absent. Not blank. **Restricted.** Somewhere in thirty years of stolen childhoods and sold funerals, there was a memory with his name on it, sealed behind a restriction flag that no ordinary suppression carried. Someone had put it there. Someone had made sure it existed and made sure no one — perhaps including him — could simply open it.

Noor sat back in the dark and felt the shape of his own life rearrange itself around that single line of text. The blank commissioning record. The beacon nobody ordered. The ghost ship using his beacon as its navigation source. The dead man who had bought the selection criteria and died for it. None of it had ever been about a smuggler in the wrong place. He had been placed. Maintained. Kept — like the beacon itself — as part of someone's design.

And somewhere in that design was a memory with his name on it, locked behind a door he didn't yet have the key to.

Above him, far above, through the Undertow's patched hull, the jump window was opening in its slow astronomical way, and the generation ships hung in high orbit like promises the city couldn't afford to keep.

Noor closed the search, laid his palm flat against the warm black casing of the ballot box, and made it a promise instead of a question.

"We're going to open every door," he told it. "Starting with mine."

Behind him, unnoticed until now, Chidinma stood in the doorway, datapad in hand, having heard every word.

"Noor," she said. "There's something else. The restriction flag on your file — it wasn't set by the Trust's standard system. I traced the signature." She swallowed. "It matches the commissioning signature on the beacon itself. Whoever built this thing locked your memory personally. And Noor — the builder's ID hash? It's registered to T. Adeyemi."
