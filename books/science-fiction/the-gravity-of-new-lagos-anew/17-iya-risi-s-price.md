## 17. Iya Risi's Price

The Undertow smelled of fried plantain, solder smoke, and the particular ozone tang of too many machines breathing in too little air. Noor had lived aboard Kestrel-9 for eleven years and had forgotten, in that time, what it meant to share a space with other people's cooking. Now, three days after his home had burned to slag above him, he found the smell almost unbearable — not because it was unpleasant, but because it was *alive*, and he was still learning how to be.

Iya Risi's parlor sat at the heart of the unregistered decks, a room that shouldn't have existed on any schematic and therefore existed on all of them. She received him at a low table set with tea things that had survived four administrations of customs crackdowns, two fires, and one brief occupation by a religious sect that had worshipped vacuum as sacrament. The teapot was chipped. The cups were mismatched. The woman pouring was the most dangerous person Noor had ever been glad to know.

"You look terrible," she said, handing him a cup painted with faded sunflowers. "Sit before you fall down. That's an order from your landlady."

"I don't pay rent here."

"You will." She settled across from him, folding her hands over her stomach. She was small and round and moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once needed to hurry, because everyone else always came to her. Silver threads shot through her braids, and her eyes — dark, patient, amused — took him apart layer by layer while he drank tea he didn't taste. "Now. Tell me again about this thing you're carrying, because my techs won't shut up about it, and I don't like when my people get excited. Excitement draws attention."

"It's a beacon core," Noor said. "From Kestrel-9."

"I know what it is. I asked what it *is*."

He looked down into his cup. The sunflowers swam. Somewhere beyond the bulkhead, the Undertow hummed its endless off-key song of generators and arguments and somebody's baby crying two decks up.

"Thirty years," he said. "It's been recording thirty years. Every suppressed memory sold through the Trust's own channels. Every childhood somebody sold to buy their mother a berth. Every dissident the Trust reclassified as ballast. It's not navigation equipment, Iya Risi. It never was. It's a ballot box. A record of every single person they erased from the manifest, written in their own hand."

Silence. Even the baby seemed to hold its breath.

Then Iya Risi laughed — a short, sharp bark, nothing warm in it. "Thirty years. And the Trust built it themselves."

"They didn't build it to keep records. They built it to make sure the records stayed buried. The beacon broadcasts noise on official channels — static, telemetry, nothing. But underneath—"

"Underneath is everybody." She set down her cup with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a lock engaging. "And now the Trust has burned your station to try to bury it, which means they've confirmed it's real, which means every hour you sit in my parlor drinking my tea, you are a lit fuse sitting on my furniture."

Noor put the cup down. "I can leave."

"Don't be stupid. Sit." He sat. She studied him for a long moment, and something in her expression shifted — the amusement draining out, leaving behind something older and wearier. "You know what I do here, Noor Yarrow. I move things. Stolen gene-therapy kits, forged work permits, memories people regret selling and want back. I move them quietly, through channels the Trust pretends not to see because pretending is cheaper than fighting. In thirty years I have never once moved anything that could get my whole deck vaporized. Do you understand the service I am describing? Thirty years of *boring*."

"And now?"

"Now a burned man walks into my parlor carrying the confession of an entire government, and asks me to smuggle it." She poured herself more tea, though he noticed her hand wasn't quite steady. Small victories. "So let's talk about price."

* * *

He'd expected money. That was the first thing the fire had taught him: he no longer thought like a man who had anything worth taking, so he kept underestimating what things were worth to other people.

"Money's no good," Iya Risi said, reading him. "If we win, your credits burn with the Trust. If we lose, credits won't matter anyway. No." She leaned forward. "I want the beacon."

"The—" He blinked. "The core?"

"The core, the codes, the commissioning rights, everything. Ownership. Registered, documented, transferred to me and mine in perpetuity." She ticked the terms off on ringed fingers. "My techs crack it open properly — not just enough to move it, but enough to *own* it. We build the distribution network. My channels, my couriers, my cut of every copy that moves through the Undertow forever. You get your truth broadcast. I get the most valuable piece of hardware in orbit."

"That's not smuggling," Noor said slowly. "That's a monopoly."

"That's *business*." Her smile showed teeth. "You want idealists, there's a poetry collective two decks down. They'll take your archive and die beautifully and accomplish nothing. I'm offering you something better: me. Nobody moves contraband through New Lagos Anew without my say-so. Not Vane, not the Trust, not God. If the truth travels, it travels in my ships."

"And if you decide the truth is worth more buried than free?"

The smile didn't waver, but her eyes did — just slightly, a flicker of something honest underneath. "Then you should have thought of that before you came to a fence to save the world."

* * *

He walked the Undertow after she dismissed him — "Think until the second bell; my offer expires when my patience does, and both are shorter than you think" — and tried to weigh it.

The corridors were narrow and crowded and utterly indifferent to him, which was its own strange comfort. On Kestrel-9 he had been invisible by design, a ghost maintaining a ghost beacon, knowing everyone's secrets and owning none. Here invisibility was simply the water he swam in. A woman repaired a recycler in the open corridor, cursing it in three languages. Two teenagers ran a shell game with memory-chips instead of shells, and were very bad at it. An old man had hung a curtain of photographs across his doorway — faces Noor didn't recognize, from a district he did. Old Lagos Quarter. Struck from the first draft of the list. He knew that now. He'd seen the strike-throughs himself, in Adeyemi's stolen memory, whole neighborhoods deleted like typos.

The old man caught him looking.

"My grandmother," the old man said, tapping one photograph. "She sold her wedding day. Got half a berth credit for it. Died before the drawing." He shrugged, the shrug of a man who had made peace with arithmetic long ago. "You're the beacon man, yes? The one Risi's techs keep whispering about."

Word traveled fast down here. Faster than light, practically.

"What do you want?" Noor asked.

The old man considered him. "Nothing. Just wanted to see you. My granddaughter says you're going to give everybody back their names." He looked at the photographs again. "Careful whose hands you put them in."

He drew the curtain closed.

* * *

By the second bell, Noor had made a list, because lists were the only prayer he'd ever learned.

Argument for giving Iya Risi the beacon: she was the only way the archive moved. Nineteen days until the window closed — no, eighteen now, the countdown ate days while he drank tea — and after that the ships jumped and the truth became history, and history was exactly what the Trust was best at managing. He couldn't broadcast it himself. He'd tried official channels once already and gotten Olu killed; the memory of that failure sat in his chest like swallowed glass. He had no ship, no station, no name anyone trusted. He had a box of testimony and a dead man's daughter waiting somewhere in the dark, and nothing else.

Argument against: the moment he signed, the truth belonged to a woman whose profession was ownership. Iya Risi would never sell the archive to the Trust — he believed that, mostly, the way you believe in gravity — but she might *lease* it. Might ration it. Might discover that an archive of grievances was more profitable as leverage than as liberation, one whispered secret at a time, forever. Truth in her hands wouldn't die. It would just... become hers. And the erased — the old man's grandmother, the struck-out districts, whoever Noor himself had been before his parents paid to hollow him out — they deserved better than a new landlord.

But then, he thought, staring at the ceiling pipes sweating condensation onto his forehead, what were the alternatives? Keep the core himself and wait for Vane's next fire? Hand it to Adaeze, whose loyalty to the law had already cost her everything except the law itself? Broadcast blind and watch another courier bleed out in a cargo bay?

There was no clean hand to put the truth in. There was only the least dirty one, and a bargain, and hope that people were better than their professions.

His parents, he thought suddenly, had faced a version of this choice. Someone had offered them a clean child with no dangerous past, and they had paid, and he had grown up empty and safe and invisible. They had decided what he was worth in other people's hands. He was still living inside their answer.

Maybe that was the real inheritance. Not the berth they'd bought him. The mistake.

"No," he said aloud, to the pipes, to nobody. "Not this time."

* * *

Iya Risi was waiting when he came back, as if she'd never doubted he would. Perhaps she hadn't. Perhaps that was the most frightening thing about her.

"Well?" she said.

"One condition. Mine, on top of yours."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction of a millimeter, which from her was the equivalent of open-mouthed astonishment. "Go on."

"The archive goes out whole or not at all. No rationing. No leverage. No selling pieces back to the people it convicts. You own the beacon — fine, own it, rebuild it, charge admission if you want. But the broadcast happens complete, in the open, before the window closes. That clause isn't negotiable, and if you break it, I'll spend whatever's left of my life making sure the Undertow knows exactly what kind of matriarch runs it."

A long silence. Somewhere above them, faintly, a hull plate pinged as the station adjusted its spin.

Then Iya Risi threw back her head and laughed — really laughed this time, warm and huge, filling the little parlor. "Oh, you *are* fun. Eleven years alone on a relay station and you come down here negotiating like a dockworker's widow." She wiped her eyes. "Agreed. Whole or nothing. Witnessed and sealed." She stuck out a small, soft hand. "Congratulations, beacon man. You're now the poorest rich man in orbit."

He shook it. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"One more thing," she said, as he turned to go. "Your enforcer. Okonkwo. She's gone dark — dropped off every grid two days ago. My people say she's running, and my people also say she's hunting, and my people are rarely wrong twice." Her eyes glittered. "When she finds you — and she will, she's better at finding than I am at hiding, which pains me to admit — remember whose roof you slept under. Loyalty flows downhill in the Undertow. Everything does."

"I'll remember."

"Do that." She picked up her tea and waved him away with the other hand, already bored, already turning to the next crisis, the next bargain, the next life rearranged across her table. "And Noor — eat something. You can't save a city on an empty stomach. Believe me, I've tried."

* * *

He found Amara in the bunk room Iya Risi had lent them, hunched over her father's files with the lamp turned low, and told her the terms. She listened without interrupting, which was a family trait, apparently — the dead man had planned further than anyone guessed, and apparently planning ran in the blood.

"The beacon belongs to her now," Noor finished. "The truth belongs to her. I signed it away."

Amara was quiet for a moment. Then: "My father bought my identity with his last purchase and gave it to strangers to hold. He never met them. Never knew if they'd honor it." She looked up, and in the lamplight her face was so much his face, the dead man's face, that Noor's chest hurt. "Trusting someone with the truth isn't losing it. It's the only way it ever gets anywhere."

"When did you get wise?"

"About the same time I got orphaned. They arrive together." She closed the file. "Besides — Risi's techs found something tonight. In the core. A partition they couldn't open. Deep encryption, older than everything around it, keyed to a biometric signature they can't match." She slid a printout across the table — actual paper, the Undertow's idea of security. "They think it's Adeyemi's. His final purchase, the original, the master key. But biometrics degrade. After twenty years..."

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Twenty-year-old biometrics meant one thing and one thing only: a blood relative. A daughter.

Noor stared at the printout, at the dead man's encrypted heart waiting for his daughter's hand, and felt the bargain settle over him like a coat that almost fit.

Outside, in the corridor, the second bell rang — and then, seconds later, a third bell nobody ever rang, high and urgent, the sound the Undertow used for exactly one thing.

Visitors. Uninvited ones.

Down the corridor, Iya Risi's voice rose, smooth and pleasant and absolutely lethal: "Well. This is a surprise. Enforcer Okonkwo. Come in, come in — and do put the weapon away, dear. You're among friends."

Mostly, Noor thought, moving toward the door, among friends.
