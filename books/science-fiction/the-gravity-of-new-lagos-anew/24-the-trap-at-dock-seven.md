## 24. The Trap at Dock Seven

Adaeze Okonkwo had spent eleven years learning to read rooms, and Dock Seven in the Undertow was the worst room she had ever tried to read.

It wasn't that it was hostile. Hostile she understood—hostile was a drunk dockworker with a cargo hook and a grudge about a citation, or a Trust auditor pretending to be your friend while counting your teeth. Hostile had rules. Dock Seven was something else: a cathedral built out of shipping containers and bad decisions, lit by strings of festival lights someone had stolen from a district that no longer existed, humming with the particular silence of two hundred people who all knew something was about to happen and had agreed not to say so out loud.

"They know," Noor said beside her. He said it quietly, the way he said everything, as though the whole universe were a customs inspection he was trying to slip past.

"Of course they know," Adaeze said. "Iya Risi knows. That's different."

"Is it?"

"It's the only difference that matters tonight."

They stood at the mouth of the dock, waiting for Iya Risi's people to bring the core up from the vault decks. The beacon core sat in its cradle like a sleeping child—a dented cylinder of old orbital alloy wrapped in shock-webbing, worth more than every container in the Undertow and heavier than any of them could carry alone. Thirty years of testimony. Every name the Trust had struck from the manifest. Every memory some desperate citizen had sold to buy their neighbor's berth, filed away in a machine everyone had assumed was just keeping the ships pointed at the jump point.

Adaeze still caught herself flinching at the thought of it. She had walked past that beacon's data streams for years. She had signed off on relay audits that touched it. Somewhere in her own case files there might be names from that archive—people she'd arrested for possession of memories they'd never actually owned, because the original owners had sold them down the well and the paper trail had been scrubbed by machines that were supposed to be counting stars.

"You're doing the thing," Noor said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at the middle distance and your jaw does—" He waggled his fingers vaguely at her face. "That. It's very enforcer. Very intimidating. Also very obvious."

"My jaw is not *obvious*."

"Your jaw has a filing system. I've seen it. Indignant, calculating, and 'I miss my old job,' which is the one you're on now."

Adaeze almost smiled. Almost. "I don't miss my old job."

"You miss having a job," Noor said. "There's a difference, but it's smaller than you think."

He wasn't wrong, which was the most irritating thing about him. Eleven days since she'd gone dark. Eleven days since Vane had slid that offer across the table—*give me the smuggler, keep your badge*—and she had watched herself say no with the strange calm of a woman watching someone else's life fall apart. Her suspension notice had come through official channels within the hour. Her access codes had died within two. Her face was now on three separate watchlists, which she took as a kind of professional compliment; she'd spent most of her career putting other people on those lists, and the view from this side was at least educational.

What she missed, absurdly, was the coffee in the enforcement break room. Machine-made, terrible, faintly flavored of recycled coolant. She would have traded the archive itself for one cup.

No, she wouldn't. But she thought about it, and thinking about it felt like a small honest sin, and she was collecting those where she could find them.

* * *

Iya Risi came up from the vault decks like weather.

She was a small woman, matriarch of the Undertow's unregistered decks, sixty-some years old and built like a question nobody wanted answered. She wore a wrapper of deep indigo and a headwrap tied with the aggressive precision of a woman who had once, allegedly, talked a Trust boarding party into paying *her* a docking fee. Four of her people flanked her, carrying the core's cradle between them on a gravity sled that hummed a half-tone flat, because everything in the Undertow was slightly off-key and proud of it.

"Nne," Iya Risi said when she reached them. She looked at Adaeze the way she always did—like an appraiser confirming a previous valuation. "You are still alive. Good. Dead allies are cheap but inconvenient."

"Iya." Adaeze inclined her head. In the Undertow you didn't shake hands unless offered, and Iya Risi's hands were offered approximately never.

The old woman turned to Noor. "And you. Smuggler. Beacon-keeper. Man with no name worth writing down." Her eyes crinkled. "You look terrible."

"Thank you, Iya. I've been working on it."

"Working on what? Looking terrible? Because you have succeeded. You should be proud." She patted his cheek with one small hand, and Noor bore it with the resignation of a man being blessed by a storm. Then her expression settled, and the humor went out of the dock like a tide withdrawing. "The core is ready. My people will take it as far as the freight lift. After that—" she spread her hands, "—it is yours, and your problem, and may whatever gods smugglers pray to have mercy on your routing."

"We appreciate it," Adaeze said. "All of it. The shelter, the techs, the—"

"Do not thank me yet." Iya Risi's voice dropped. "Thank me when you are off my dock and breathing. There is a smell on Dock Seven tonight that I do not like. Too many new faces. Too many faces standing too still. People who belong here fidget. People who do not belong here—" her chin lifted toward the far end of the dock, toward the shadowed row of containers nearest the pressure doors, "—stand like furniture."

Adaeze followed the gesture. And there it was—the thing her eleven years of reading rooms had been trying to tell her since she walked in. Three figures by the container line. Still. Patient. Wearing Undertow drab over shoulders that had never hauled anything in their lives.

"Furniture," she murmured.

"How many?" Noor asked, not moving his lips much.

"At least three visible. Which means six real, plus whoever holds the doors." Adaeze's mind was already laying out the geometry: the freight lift behind them, the pressure doors ahead, the catwalks above with their festival lights and their convenient shadows. "Vane didn't buy information. He bought patience. He's let us come all this way so we'd carry the core to him."

"He wants us to walk it right into his hands," Noor said. "Because if he takes it in a fight, it gets damaged. If we hand it over—"

"—it arrives intact, and he arrives clean." Adaeze exhaled slowly. "He's not trying to kill us. Not first. He's trying to make us *cooperate*."

"That's worse," Noor said.

"It's always worse with Vane."

Iya Risi had listened to all of this with the expression of a woman hearing children describe the ocean. Now she snorted. "So. The Trust has bought some furniture and placed it on my dock without paying rent." She cracked her knuckles, one hand and then the other, a sound like green wood splitting. "In my house."

"Iya," Adaeze said carefully, "if this goes wrong, the Trust comes down on the Undertow. All of it. You've seen what they did to Kestrel-9."

"I saw what they did to Kestrel-9 from here, girl. I watched the fire for six hours." Something moved behind the old woman's eyes—not fear, and not quite grief. Ledger-math. The arithmetic of a woman who had spent forty years keeping unregistered people alive under a city that had sold them. "Do you know how many of my people are on that list? On the list inside that machine?"

Neither of them answered.

"Two hundred and eleven," Iya Risi said. "I have kept the count myself, in my head, for thirty years, because paper burns and heads are harder. Two hundred and eleven people who sold a memory or a name or a child's birthright to pay the Trust's toll, and were struck from the manifest anyway. Struck twice. Once by poverty and once by arithmetic." She looked at the cradle, at the dented cylinder sleeping inside it. "That machine is the first honest thing this city has owned since the ships went up. You will not hand it to Silas Vane on my dock. I forbid it. This is my house, and in my house, we do not serve thieves to thieves."

"Iya—" Noor started.

"Hold." She raised one finger, and both of them—both of them, an enforcer and a smuggler, armed and desperate and out of options—shut their mouths like schoolchildren. "Here is what happens. My people take the sled to the freight lift, loud and slow, the way we planned. Furniture follows furniture. You two go the other way—up, along the catwalk, through the old coolant gallery, down to Lift Two. Boring route. Ugly route. Nobody guards ugly routes because nobody loves them." She smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "I will stay here and be magnificent. Distraction is also a service, and unlike some services in this city, mine is free."

"No," Adaeze said.

"Excuse me?"

"You're not staying here to be shot at so we can sneak away. That's not—"

"*Not what?*" Iya Risi's voice didn't rise, which somehow made it worse. "Not honorable? Child, I am seventy-one years old. I have buried two husbands, one war, and four city administrations. Honor is for people with more future than me. What I have is a house full of grandchildren-not-by-blood, a dock full of debts, and exactly one good death left in me—and I have been saving it, the way you save the good cloth, for an occasion worthy of it." She reached out and gripped Adaeze's wrist, small hand, iron grip. "This is the occasion. Do not insult me by making it small."

For a moment, nobody spoke. Somewhere above them the festival lights flickered, and the flat-humming gravity sled waited, patient as everything else in the Undertow.

"She'll do it whether we agree or not," Noor said quietly. "You know that."

"I know that," Adaeze said, and hated it.

"Then the only choice left is whether we waste her distraction arguing about it." He met her eyes. "Catwalk. Coolant gallery. Lift Two. Go."

* * *

The catwalks of Dock Seven were a lattice of borrowed scaffolding and older sins, and Adaeze climbed them with the muscle-memory of a woman who had once chased a memory-thief across the roof of the Customs House in the rain and caught him, mostly because he stopped to gloat.

Below, the plan unfolded exactly as Iya Risi had drawn it. Her people moved the sled toward the freight lift, slow and ceremonial, wrappers bright under the festival lights. And the furniture moved—three shapes detaching from the container line, smooth and coordinated, closing on the sled with the unhurried confidence of men who believed the trap had already sprung.

Which it had. Just not for them.

Adaeze and Noor made the coolant gallery—a crawl of corroded ducts that smelled of thirty-year-old antifreeze and older arguments—and dropped down into the service corridor that fed Lift Two. The core wasn't with them. That was the flaw in the beauty of it: Iya Risi's feint used the sled, and the sled carried the cradle, and the cradle—

"—is empty," Noor breathed, patting the harness under his jacket, where a cylinder-shaped bulge rode against his ribs. "Has been since the vault. She insisted. Said the sled was 'set dressing for idiots.'"

"She let us believe we were walking into an ambush carrying the real core?"

"She said we'd walk more honestly if we thought we were carrying it." His mouth twisted. "Iya Risi's psychology is a weapon with no safety catch."

The corridor lights died.

Not flickered—died, all at once, front to back, a wave of darkness rolling toward them with the crisp discipline of a switch thrown by professionals. Adaeze's hand found Noor's arm and shoved him into an alcove a half-second before the corridor filled with light of a different kind: white, hard, tactical, mounted on helmets she knew the model number of.

Trust marines. Six of them, moving in pairs, weapons low and ready. Behind them, stepping with the leisurely grace of a man arriving at his own party, came Silas Vane.

He looked good. He always looked good. Charcoal coat, diplomatic seal glinting at his collar, the expression of a man who had rehearsed disappointment in a mirror and gotten it nearly perfect.

"Noor Yarrow," Vane called into the dark, voice carrying down the corridor like oil on water. "And Enforcer Okonkwo—or should I say former Enforcer? Or perhaps *future* Enforcer, depending on the next five minutes. I'm told my offers improve under pressure."

Adaeze pressed back into the alcove, Noor's shoulder against hers, both of them barely breathing. The marines swept past their position—past, she realized, because Vane's people were watching the freight lift, watching the sled, watching the beautiful empty decoy. They hadn't swept the ugly route. Nobody loved the ugly route.

On the open dock, through a gap in the gallery plating, Adaeze could see it happening. Iya Risi standing before the sled, arms folded, indigo wrapper catching the festival light, holding court before six armed men as though they were late for an appointment with her. Even at this distance Adaeze could see her talking—could see the marines hesitating, because you did not simply shoot a grandmother on a dock full of two hundred witnesses, and Vane, whatever else he was, preferred his crimes with paperwork.

Then Vane reached the sled. Cut the webbing. Looked inside.

Even across the distance, even through steel and shadow, Adaeze heard the silence that followed. The particular silence of a powerful man discovering he has been played by a seventy-one-year-old woman in her own house.

Vane's head came up. He looked around the dock—slowly, thoroughly—and Adaeze watched his gaze climb the scaffolding, trace the catwalks, settle on the coolant gallery entrance with the precision of a man who had finally remembered that ugly routes existed.

"Find them," he said, and the marines moved.

What happened next, Adaeze would replay for the rest of her life, in the long nights after, in the years of the rebuilt city, whenever the price of things came due.

Iya Risi stepped between Vane and the gallery entrance.

She did not run. She did not scream. She unfolded to her full height—which was not much—and planted herself in the doorway like a doorstop forged by God, and she began, of all things, to *talk*. Loudly. About rent. About the unpaid docking fees of the Trust security fleet going back nine years, itemized, with interest, at a volume that scattered the marines' formation because human beings are simply not built to shoot a small furious woman reciting compound arithmetic at them.

It bought them ninety seconds. Maybe less.

"Go," Noor whispered, and they ran—down the service corridor, through the coolant gallery's rusted throat, toward Lift Two, the empty cradle abandoned on the dock below, the real core riding against Noor's heart.

Behind them, a single shot rang out.

Just one. Precise. The sound cut Iya Risi's voice off mid-figure—mid-compound-interest, mid-sentence, mid-life—and the silence afterward was worse than any alarm.

Adaeze didn't stop. Every enforcer instinct she had ever owned screamed at her to turn, to go back, to do *something*, and she ran anyway, because stopping meant the core was taken, and the core taken meant two hundred and eleven names in Iya Risi's head died with her for nothing. Grief could wait. Grief was patient. Grief had already learned her address.

They reached Lift Two. Noor slammed the call stud with the heel of his hand, once, twice, and the cage rose toward them through the dark with agonizing, clanking slowness.

In the gap of the gallery plating, far below, Adaeze saw Vane standing over a small figure in indigo. Saw him crouch. Saw him say something she couldn't hear—something brief, almost courteous, the monster minding its manners.

Saw Iya Risi's hand, lying in the festival light, close once around empty air, and go still.

The lift arrived. They got in. The cage began to rise, and Adaeze put her forehead against the cold mesh wall and let herself shake, just for the length of one breath, just once.

"She knew," Noor said hoarsely beside her. "She knew the price when she named it. She told us—she told us she was saving it."

"I know what she told us." Adaeze's voice came out flat and strange. "Knowing doesn't make it cheaper. Nothing makes it cheaper. That's the whole point of a price."

The lift climbed. Below them, Dock Seven erupted—two hundred people who had agreed not to say anything out loud saying it all at once, a roar rolling up through the Undertow's bones, grief and fury braided together into something that sounded, to Adaeze's trained ear, remarkably like the beginning of an army.

Noor pulled the core from his jacket and held it in both hands, dented and humble and heavier than the world.

"Nineteen days," he said. "Eighteen now. We get this to Amara, we finish what he started, and we make sure—" His voice caught, steadied. "We make sure she didn't spend it for nothing."

Adaeze looked at the old woman's dock shrinking beneath them, at the festival lights still burning over an empty cradle, and made the promise the way she'd once made arrests: completely, and in writing, on the only record that mattered.

"Eighteen days," she said. "Then everyone learns her name."

Above them, the way to the Meridian Dawn opened like a throat.
