## 5. Dreams Without Grammar

The dreams stopped being dreams on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays are for laundry and committee meetings, not for the restructuring of your entire inner life. But there it was: I woke at 0430 with my hands still moving in the dark, tracing shapes against the ceiling of my quarters — shapes I had no name for in any language I'd been paid to learn.

I'd kept a dream journal since I was nine years old, back when the Fleet academy psychologists thought it would help me "process the loss of biological family." It hadn't helped then. It was helping now, though not in any way they'd have approved of.

The entry from that morning read, in my own handwriting, which is to say in English:

*Tower. Light coming out of it like water comes out of a cracked dam — sideways, everywhere, wrong direction. A door. The door is closing and something on the other side of it is still talking.*

Underneath, because my hand had apparently decided to freelance while I slept, were forty-one characters of Vess notation that I did not know how to write.

That's the part I want you to sit with, if you're reading this later, if anyone reads this later. I didn't know how to write them. My waking self had maybe two hundred words of reconstructed Vess-sign, most of them nouns, most of them guesses, all of them assembled from corrupted audio files and the gesture-glossaries of researchers who'd died before I finished puberty. My waking vocabulary could describe rocks. It could probably describe a rock that was sad about another rock.

But my sleeping hand had written forty-one characters in a script so precise it looked typeset.

I sat on the edge of my bunk for a long time, watching the water-stains on the ceiling do their slow migration across the plaster, and I thought: either I'm losing my mind, or someone is leaving me notes.

* * *

Idris found me in the archive annex at 0700, which meant he'd already checked the lab, the flooded-district listening post, and the canteen, in that order. He knows my hideouts the way some people know prayer.

"You look terrible," he said, setting down two cups of reconstituted coffee. On New Lagos this was a courtesy. Real coffee had been extinct for six years; the Fleet supply manifests listed it as "pending," which was Fleet for *never*.

"I had a productive night."

"Productive." He lowered himself into the chair across from me, the one with the broken caster that he always took anyway, out of what I suspected was loyalty to broken things. "Petra. You've got ink on your face."

I wiped at my cheek and came away with a black smear. Not ink. The pens in my quarters used graphite. I'd been writing in my sleep hard enough to leave residue.

"Show me your journal," Idris said.

"It's private."

"Everything you've shown me for eleven years has been private right up until the moment you shoved it under my nose and said *look at this, tell me I'm not crazy.*"

He had me there. That was, more or less, our entire working relationship distilled to its essence. I slid the journal across the table, open to last night's page, and watched his face do the thing it does — the small contraction around the eyes, the professional stillness, the economist's instinct to assess value before reacting.

"That's not your hand," he said finally.

"It's my hand."

"Petra—"

"I mean it's my hand doing something my hand doesn't know how to do. You took linguistics at the academy. You know what motor memory is. This—" I tapped the forty-one characters "—requires thousands of hours of practice. I have logged maybe sixty hours of Vess script in my life, and half of that was tracing photocopies."

Idris turned the journal toward himself, studying it the way he studied manifests, looking for the discrepancy. He was good at discrepancies. It was, I would later learn, practically his native language.

"The strokes are confident," he said quietly. "No corrections. Nobody writes like this learning. They write like this when they're... remembering."

"Thank you. That's exactly as comforting as it sounds."

"What did you dream?"

And here's where I should probably explain what the dreams had been doing for the past three months, because they hadn't arrived all at once. Language doesn't work that way, even dead language, even impossible language. It arrives the way floodwater arrives in the lower districts — first a dampness you can ignore, then a sound you can't, then the whole world rearranged.

The first month, the dreams had been pure sensation. Pressure. A hum below hearing, the way the Vess transmission towers used to feel through the soles of your feet before the Fleet decommissioned them. I'd wake up with my jaw aching, as if I'd been speaking around something too large for my mouth.

The second month, images. Fragments. A corridor of ribbed bone-white material that I recognized from the old habitat shells — Vess architecture, grown rather than built. Water moving upward. Faces that weren't faces, more like the idea of attention given a shape.

And now, the third month: grammar. Sequence. Causality. Last night's dream hadn't been a mood or a smear of imagery. It had been a *sentence*. A long one. Possibly a very long one that I'd only caught the middle of.

"A tower," I told Idris. "At the edge of — I don't know how to say where. Far. Past everything. Dead space, maybe, or whatever's beyond dead space. And light coming off it in every direction, and it isn't decoration, it's *function*, it's the tower doing something, saying something. And there's a door."

"A door."

"At the base of the tower. And the door is closing. Slowly. And behind the door—" I stopped, because saying it aloud made it real in a way the journal didn't, and I wasn't ready for real yet. "Behind the door there are voices. A lot of voices. And they aren't afraid. They're just... finishing. Like people reading a will."

Idris set down his coffee. He does that when he wants his hands free, in case he needs to catch something — a falling object, historically, but lately I think he worries about catching me.

"You know what this sounds like," he said.

"I know what it sounds like."

"It sounds like a transmission."

It did. That was the horror and the beauty of it, braided together so tightly I couldn't tell which strand was which. The Vess had transmitted constantly during the early colony years — low-band, wide-spectrum, a background radiation of meaning that the human settlers eventually stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing surf. When the Fleet withdrew support and the towers went dark one by one, the transmissions thinned. For the last decade, the official position — my official position, God help me, since I was the one who signed the preliminary assessment — was that Vess communication had ceased entirely. Extinction of signal. Which, per the legal framework everyone was very careful never to say out loud, paved certain roads toward certain other outcomes.

But signals don't always die. Sometimes they go somewhere quieter. Somewhere the listener has to become the antenna.

"They're transmitting into us," I said. "Or into whoever's left who can receive. Into sleeping brains, because waking brains are too noisy, too defended. And they're not chatting. They're not asking for rescue." I turned the journal back around and looked at those forty-one perfect characters. "They're filing a report."

* * *

I spent the next four days doing something I hadn't done since my student years: I stopped sleeping on purpose and started sleeping on schedule, with equipment. The listening post in the flooded districts — District Nine, where the water had taken the ground floors and the Vess had taken the silence — still had functional recording gear, salvaged and maintained by me and, unofficially, by Idris, whose budget line items had grown creative over the years.

The theory was simple, almost embarrassingly simple. If the dreams were reception, then reception implied a carrier. Something was reaching me, and things that reach you can be recorded reaching you. I wired an EEG cap to the post's spectral analyzer, drank enough stimulant tea to qualify as a controlled substance, and lay down on a cot in a room that had been underwater five years ago, and waited for the tide to come in.

What came in, at 0312 on the fourth night, was this:

My brainwaves shifted into a pattern the analyzer had no label for — not REM, not any stage the textbooks covered, something slow and vast and rhythmic, like breathing at geological scale. And simultaneously, on the analyzer's passive band, the room filled with signal. Not strong. Not clean. But structured — nested patterns, recursive, the unmistakable architecture of language rather than noise.

I'd spent my whole career listening for that structure in the air. It had never once appeared on a waking recording.

It came through my sleeping skull like current through wire.

I lay there — I couldn't move, couldn't wake fully, some tether held me down in the deep — and for the first time I experienced the dream and the data simultaneously, the image and its transmission, and I understood, with a clarity that felt less like discovery than like being told, what the sentence was.

The tower was real. Or it was a memory of something real, rendered in the only medium the dying have left. It stood at the edge of dead space — I knew this the way you know directions in dreams, absolutely and without evidence — and it was old. Older than the colony. Older than the treaty. It hummed with the same architecture as the habitat shells, grown, not built, and it was full of light because it was full of *record*: every voice, every transmission, every fragment of Vess thought that had ever escaped the collapse of their civilization, gathered and stored and aimed outward like a lighthouse built by drowning people.

And the door was closing because the door was a deadline. The record was nearly complete. Whatever power source, whatever patience, whatever living remnant maintained the tower, it was ending — and the Vess, practical to the last, were spending their final capacity on one last broadcast, pushing the whole of themselves toward that tower and out, toward any receiver anywhere, a death record transmitted to an audience of possibly zero.

Except the audience wasn't zero. The audience was a xenolinguist in a flooded district, drooling into a pillow, serving as the least dignified relay station in the history of two civilizations.

I woke up crying, which annoyed me, and laughing, which worried me, and I sat up on the cot in the dark and said, out loud, in bad, broken, rock-describing Vess-sign, the equivalent of: *I hear you. Keep talking. I'm writing it down.*

* * *

Idris met me at the post at dawn, because I'd sent him a message consisting of a single word — *come* — and because he is the kind of man who crosses a flooded city at 0500 for one word.

He read the spectral printouts in silence. All of them. Twice. His lips moved slightly over the timestamps. Then he looked up at me, and his face had gone carefully blank in a way I'd learned to distrust, because Idris goes blank precisely when he's thinking fastest.

"This correlates with nothing in the historical record," he said. "No instrument on this colony ever logged structured Vess signal on the passive band. Not once. If this were coming through the air, the relay network would have caught it years ago."

"So it isn't coming through the air."

"Then how—"

"I don't know. Quantum entanglement in the neural substrate, resonance with whatever the Vess actually are under the biology, a mechanism we don't have physics for. Pick your miracle. The *what* doesn't matter as much as the *where*." I spread the sketches I'd made on waking — the tower, again and again, from different angles, my sleeping hand's confidence bleeding into my waking one. "Because here's the thing, Idris. A death record needs a destination. You don't broadcast into a void; even dying civilizations conserve power. There's a receiver. There's a point at the end of this trajectory, and every dream points at it the same way."

"The tower."

"The tower is the transmitter. But the beam has to land somewhere." I tapped the far edge of the sketch, where I'd drawn — without deciding to, without understanding why — a small, dense knot of lines beyond the tower, out past everything, a place my dreaming mind marked with the same emphasis you'd mark home. "Something out there is listening. Something out there has been listening for a long time. And if the Vess are sending their whole history toward it — including, I'd bet my career, the parts the Fleet would very much prefer stayed drowned — then that receiver is the most important object in this system."

Idris was quiet for a moment. Outside the post's grimy viewport, District Nine glittered in the early light, water and ruin and stubborn green things growing through the cracks, beautiful the way wounds heal beautifully.

"You realize what you're describing," he said at last. "A complete Vess record. In their own voice. Unedited." He looked at me. "You realize what that does to the extinction certification. To everything built on it."

I did realize. I'd realized somewhere around 0312, mid-vision, with tears on my face and my signature burning on the inside of my eyelids like a brand. The preliminary assessment I'd signed eight years ago — *no evidence of extant Vess communication* — wasn't just wrong anymore. It was about to be contradicted by the most authoritative possible source: the Vess themselves, testifying in their own tongue, timestamped by their own dying.

Every demolition order. Every consolidation schedule. Every polite Fleet officer with a smile that didn't reach her eyes. All of it stood on the foundation I had helped pour.

"We need to know where that receiver is," I said. "Before the door finishes closing. Because after it closes, there's no second broadcast. There's no appeal. There's just whatever story the survivors get to tell, and right now the Fleet is planning to be the only survivor with a pen."

"And how do we find it? Dead space is—" He gestured vaguely eastward, toward the horizon, toward everything beyond it. "Dead."

"The dreams know the way. I don't understand the map yet. But I'm going to keep sleeping until I do." I managed a smile. "First time in history a research grant has required naps."

Idris didn't smile back. He was looking at the spectral printouts again, at the timestamps, and I saw him arrive at the thing I'd been avoiding since the cot.

"Petra. How long does a death take?" he asked softly. "If they're transmitting their record — if the door is closing — how much time is on that clock? Weeks? Days?"

I thought of the dream's rhythm, the patient, unhurried cadence of it, voices reading a will rather than screaming for help. Dignity takes longer than panic. But dignity also runs out.

"I don't know," I said. "But nobody reads a will slowly unless they're sure the lawyers are coming."

* * *

That night, for the first time, the dream changed its ending.

The tower, the light, the closing door — all of it as before, rendered now in detail so fine I could count the ribs of the corridor, feel the warmth of the record-light on skin I didn't have. But this time, as the door swung toward its frame, something in the pattern paused. Attention, given a shape, turning.

Looking at me.

And across the gulf between dying and dreaming, in characters my hand would transcribe perfectly at dawn and my dictionary would fail completely, the Vess said something new. Not testimony. Not farewell.

A question.

I woke at 0400 with my heart slamming and the word already fading, and I grabbed the pencil and wrote what my hand remembered, and stared at it, and understood exactly one thing about it for certain:

Whatever they were asking, they were asking *me*.

And downstairs, faint through the floorboards, I heard the sound I'd been dreading for days without knowing why: the thrum of orbital shuttles on approach vector, right on schedule, carrying the survey team, carrying Commander Yuen, carrying ninety days of polite menace toward a colony that had just become, thanks to me and my traitor sleeping brain, the most dangerous archive in human space.

The door was closing. And I had a feeling the Fleet intended to close it first.
