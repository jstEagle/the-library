## 7. The Deep Colony

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in abandoned places, and I have decided it is not the absence of sound at all. It is the presence of everything that used to make noise, holding its breath.

The lift down into the old mining levels hadn't run in eleven years. Petra found the manual override in under four minutes, which told me she'd been planning this descent longer than she'd admitted to me, which told me, in turn, that my sister's name had been sitting in her pocket like a stone for days while we drank tea and discussed survey schedules like two civilized people.

"You could have just said," I told her as the cage groaned past the last functioning light of the upper colony.

"Said what? 'Idris, I found your dead sister on a manifest of a ship that never flew, shall we go see if she's actually alive?'"

"She might be dead."

"Then we'll know," Petra said, "instead of wondering."

I didn't have an answer for that. I never do, with her. That's the trouble with xenolinguists — they treat every conversation as a translation problem, and they always find the word you were trying not to use.

* * *

The cage dropped us into Level Nine, and Level Nine was a throat.

That's the only way I can describe it. The shaft opened out into a gallery cut through basalt by machines that had been decommissioned before I finished school, and the dark down there wasn't empty dark — it was textured, full of the memory of work. Ore carts sat on their rails like patient animals. A pressure suit hung from a hook by the tunnel mouth, arms out, as if its owner had stepped out of it mid-shift and meant to come back.

"Eleven years," Petra murmured. Her headlamp swept across the wall and caught something that made her stop walking.

Writing. Not Fleet-standard stencil, not safety signage. Handwriting, in paint or something like paint, running along the rock in a line taller than I am: numbers, dates, tallies. And beneath them, repeated over and over in careful rows, symbols I recognized from her office — the Vess glyphs she kept pinned above her desk like pressed flowers.

"That's Vess-sign," she said quietly. "Written large. Deliberately legible." She turned in a slow circle, reading the walls. "Idris. This isn't graffiti. This is a classroom."

"A classroom," I repeated, because my voice needed something to do while the rest of me absorbed the fact that my sister had spent years down here teaching — or being taught — a language the Fleet had classified as non-communicative biological signaling.

"The Fleet report called the deep levels uninhabitable after the flood," Petra said. "Structural failure, atmospheric contamination, no survivors recovered. Standard language." She touched one of the painted glyphs with two fingers, gently, the way you touch a face. "Standard language is how you bury people without digging."

We walked deeper.

* * *

I want to say something about Amara before we go further down, because if this record survives — and God knows why it should, but Petra insists on narrating everything now, says testimony is the only currency that doesn't devalue — then whoever reads it should know who she was before she became a question mark.

Amara Okafor was born fourteen months after me and was better than me at everything by fifteen. Faster, funnier, crueler when cruelty was deserved and kind when kindness cost something. When the Fleet posted our parents to the Meridian corridor, she stayed. She said New Lagos was the only place where the air tasted like anything. She worked hydrology in the lower districts, the ones that flooded first when the Vess tidal systems started failing, and she was the one who taught me that the Vess weren't animals. She was nineteen. She'd watched one repair a breach in the eastern weir for six hours without stopping, coordinating with three others through those light-patterns along their flanks, and she came home and said, "Idris, they have shifts. They take turns. Animals don't take turns."

Four years later she was gone, and the manifest of the Halcyon Step said she'd died in a launch accident aboard a ship that never left the ground, and I signed the condolence form because the alternative was asking questions the Fleet had already answered for me.

I helped falsify that manifest. I've told Petra that. What I haven't told anyone, maybe not even myself until this moment, standing in the dark with ore dust in my teeth, is how easy it was. That's the thing about complicity nobody warns you about. It doesn't feel like betrayal. It feels like paperwork.

* * *

The deserters' settlement announced itself before we saw it: warmth.

Not much. Half a degree, maybe less. But after hours of cold stone, the air ahead of us carried that faint organic sweetness of living things — soil, rot, growth — and Petra and I looked at each other and neither of us said anything, because saying it would make it real and we both wanted another hundred meters of not knowing.

Then the tunnel opened into a cavern the size of a chapel, and my knees nearly went.

They'd built a garden. Down here, under a kilometer of rock, in the runoff channels of a dead mine, someone had engineered a forest. Fungal racks climbed the walls in tiered shelves, pale caps glowing faintly where some bioluminescent strain had been coaxed into doing double duty as lighting. Water dripped through a system of salvaged pipe and cloth filters into beds of dark, living soil. Vines — engineered, grafted, tended — crawled up guide wires toward heat lamps powered by a geothermal tap that must have taken months to rig.

And everywhere, on every surface that would hold it, writing. Chalk, paint, scratched lines, charcoal. Vess-sign in columns and spirals. Human script in margins. Diagrams. Tables. A whole civilization's marginalia, pressed into stone.

"They lived here," Petra whispered. "Plural. Look — sleeping alcoves. At least six, seven—"

She stopped. Her lamp had found a shelf near the central water filter, and on the shelf were journals. Actual paper — pulped fungal fiber, bound with wire, dozens of them, spines numbered in a hand I knew.

I knew that hand. You know a hand like you know a voice. My sister wrote her sevens with a little crossbar, always had, since she was six years old and copying my homework wrong.

"Idris?" Petra said. "Idris, sit down."

"I'm fine."

"You're grey."

"I'm fine," I said, and picked up Journal One, and sat down anyway, because my legs had made an executive decision without consulting me.

* * *

Here is what the journals contain. I'm going to set it down in order, because order is the only dignity I can offer her now.

Journal One begins the night of the Halcyon Step. Forty-one names listed inside the front cover in her handwriting — the same forty-one on the falsified manifest, plus three more, forty-four souls total, which means even the lie we told was too small. They hadn't died in a launch accident. They'd walked off the colony roster during the second evacuation scare, when Fleet command announced the lower districts would be "decommissioned" — sealed, flooded, written off — and gave residents seventy-two hours to relocate to housing that didn't exist.

Amara writes: *"Commander Osei told us the Vess in the lower weirs would be handled. Asked him what handled meant. He said pest management protocols. I asked him if he understood they were sentient. He looked at me the way you look at a child who's asked where meat comes from. We left that night. Sefu Bello flew us down through the storm shutters on a maintenance hop he logged as equipment transfer. If you're reading this, Sefu, you're a criminal and a hero and I hope both keep you warm."*

Sefu Bello. I filed the name away. It would matter later; I didn't know that yet.

Journal Two is survival — food yields, water chemistry, the slow engineering of the fungal strains, the geothermal tap. But threaded through the practical entries, growing entry by entry, is the other project. The Vess.

*"Day 40. The weirs below us aren't abandoned. There are nine adults and what I think are two juveniles. They know we're here. Today one of them — the big one, I've started calling her Anchor because of how she plants herself — brought a fish to the edge of our channel and put it down and waited. Not hunting behavior. Gift behavior. I don't have a protocol for gift behavior. Nobody does. That's the whole problem, isn't it."*

*"Day 61. They have grammar. I'm sure of it now. The light patterns aren't fixed signals — they compose. Same elements, different arrangements, different outcomes. I've been keeping a log of sequences and responses. Thackeray would lose her mind. If I ever get out of here I'm going to find that woman and show her everything."*

Petra made a sound beside me when I read that aloud. I didn't look at her. If I looked at her I'd have to acknowledge that my sister, alone in the dark for years, had been dreaming of the colony's last xenolinguist the way people dream of rescue.

* * *

It was Journal Seven where the tone changed, and it changed the way weather changes — gradually, then all at once.

*"Day 214. Got hold of a Fleet internal bulletin through the old relay scrap up on Twelve. Dated eight months ago. Colonial Biology Review Board, closed session. Subject: classification review, Vess populations, New Lagos."*

My hands were shaking so badly Petra took the journal from me and read it herself, aloud, her voice flat in the way she gets when feeling would otherwise drown the words.

*"Findings summary: neurological studies confirm distributed cognition, symbolic communication capacity, tool modification, intergenerational knowledge transfer. Classification recommendation: SENTIENT, CATEGORY ONE."*

Sentient. Category One. The highest protection class in colonial law. The class that makes withdrawal of support illegal, that mandates contact protocols, that turns "pest management" into a war crime with paperwork.

Petra kept reading.

*"The Board voted 6–3 to forward the finding to Fleet Command with a recommendation for formal recognition. Fleet Command returned the finding unopened. Marked: NOT ACTIONABLE AT CURRENT STRATEGIC POSTURE. Reclassified internally as INCONCLUSIVE PENDING FURTHER STUDY. All copies ordered destroyed. This copy survived because a clerk named Odili printed it for toilet paper and couldn't bring himself to use it."*

"Inconclusive," Petra said. "They had the study. Six scientists voted to recognize them. And Command stamped it inconclusive because the timing was inconvenient."

"Not inconvenient," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Inconvenient implies accident. They were already committed to withdrawal. Recognition would have blocked the drawdown. So the finding had to die. That's not negligence, Petra. That's policy."

She looked at me. In the fungal light her face was very still. "You knew the Fleet was capable of this."

"I knew the Fleet was capable of losing things. Misfiling things. I thought — I don't know what I thought. That evil required effort." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Turns out it mostly requires a stamp."

* * *

We read until our lamp batteries forced us to stop. Journals Eight through Eleven document the plague.

I'll spare the record the worst of it, or rather I'll spare myself, but the shape is this: the flooding the Fleet called a natural consequence of tidal system decay was, per Amara's water samples and her increasingly furious annotations, seeded. Engineered runoff from the upper district treatment plants — a culling agent designed for the Vess reproductive cycle, deployed quietly, denied officially, effective horrifically. The nine adults in the lower weirs became five. Then three.

*"Anchor is dying. She knows. Yesterday she spent four hours at the channel edge making the sequence I've come to translate as remember-us — I can't prove that's what it means, I have no methodology, I have nothing but three years of watching, and I would stake my life on it, and the joke is my life is exactly what it's worth down here. If anyone finds these journals: they said remember us. Someone has to."*

Petra closed the journal with both hands, carefully, the way you close a coffin.

"The dreams," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she started again. "The transmissions I've been recording. The dying signal. Idris, I thought I was listening to a species' last broadcast. I think — some of it — I think I've been listening to *her*. Three years of sign-language exchange, logged in these journals, transmitted back through the water, through the rock, into whatever channel the Vess used. The signal isn't just theirs anymore. It's contaminated with us. With her."

"Is that possible?"

"I don't know. I need the journals. All of them. Every sequence she logged, cross-referenced against my recordings—" She stopped. Looked around the garden, the alcoves, the walls of writing. "Where is she, Idris?"

I had been avoiding the arithmetic. Eleven journals. The last one, Number Eleven, ended forty days ago by its own dating. Forty days of silence in a settlement built for a dozen people, maintained with obsessive care, water still flowing, lamps still lit.

Forty days is a long time to tend a garden alone.

Or not to tend it.

* * *

We searched the settlement properly then, and I will compress what it did to me into a single detail: in the last alcove, the one with the thickest blanket and the best position near the heat exchanger, we found a bed made up with military precision, and beside it, on a stone shelf, arranged in a row — a Fleet Academy ring too large for her finger, my mother's letters, and a small carved figure of a Vess, whittled from mine timber, its flank scored with tiny marks representing light-patterns.

A keepsake shelf. People who intend to die tidy their keepsakes. People who intend to live leave them scattered.

"She planned to come back," I said. "To this room. Whatever she's doing, she planned to come back."

"Then she's alive," Petra said. "People who plan to die don't leave a bed made."

"You're guessing."

"I'm a linguist. Guessing with methodology." She was already moving toward the far tunnel, the one leading down toward the old weir galleries, her lamp beam cutting a hole in the dark. "The water's still flowing to the lower channels. Someone's maintaining the flow. Come on."

"Petra — wait. If it's not her down there. If it's what's left of—" I couldn't finish it.

She turned back, and in the half-light her face held something I'd seen once before, years ago, when I'd confessed the manifest to her: not pity. Recognition. Two people standing in the wreckage of the same lie.

"Idris," she said. "You spent fifteen years believing your sister was dead because believing it was easier than checking. I spent fifteen years believing the Vess were gone because believing it was easier than listening. We're done with easier. Move."

* * *

The tunnel to the weirs sloped down through wet stone, and the sound reached us before the light did — a low, rhythmic lapping, water against channel walls, and underneath it, faint as a pulse through a hospital door, a pattern of clicks and soft resonances that Petra identified before I could even name it as sound.

"Vess-sign," she breathed. "Live transmission. It's weak, degraded, but it's structured — Idris, it's *structured*—"

And then the tunnel turned, and her lamp caught the water, and there at the edge of the channel, knee-deep, silhouetted against the faint blue-green shimmer of flank-lights answering each other across the black water, stood a figure in patched environmental gear with a headscarf gone grey with mineral dust — thin, upright, unmistakable, raising one hand toward the water in a sequence of gestures I had seen painted on a thousand meters of cave wall.

The lights in the water stilled. The figure turned.

Even across forty meters, even starved and feral and wrapped in eleven years of dark, there was no mistaking the set of those shoulders, the tilt of that chin, the sevens-crossbar certainty of her.

"Amara," I said. Or tried to. What came out was barely a syllable.

She looked at us — at Petra's borrowed field jacket with its Fleet-issue lining, at my boots, my lamp, all of it stamped with the colors of the institution that had erased her — and her face did something complicated and final, and she spoke, and her voice was rust and riverbed:

"If you've come from the Fleet," she said, "you can walk back up the way you came, and tell them the vermin down here remember everything."
