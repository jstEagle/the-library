## 8. Amara

The first thing you learn in the deep colony is that light is a currency. You spend it carefully or you die stupid.

I was spending mine on the fungus beds when I heard them coming — two sets of boots on the old maintenance stairs, walking like people who hadn't learned yet that sound travels down here the way water does. One walked heavy and apologetic. The other walked like someone counting her own footsteps, which meant she was either a soldier or a scientist, and after fifteen years I'd stopped believing there was much difference.

I killed my lamp and stood still among the growing racks, letting the bioluminescence do what it does, which is make everything look like a dream somebody had about a forest. Blue-green glow off the caps of the *lumen-cap* strain, the pale ribs of the scaffolding, my own hands. Down here you get used to being a ghost in your own house.

The heavy walker spoke first. His voice hit me in the sternum before his words did.

"Amara?"

Idris.

I want to tell you I felt joy. What I actually felt was a very old anger waking up, stretching, and asking if it could please, finally, come out of its room.

* * *

Let me back up, because Idris's face when he said my name deserves context, and so does mine when I didn't answer.

Fifteen years ago I was nineteen years old and I had a brother who worked in Fleet Records and a mother who worked herself into an early grave polishing Fleet floors, and I had a posting on the survey corvette *Halcyon Step*, which was going to be my whole life: jump routes, mineral assays, a bunk with my name stenciled on it. A. Okafor, stenciled in Fleet gray. That's how permanent I thought things were.

Then the withdrawal vote happened. You won't find it in any archive now — I know because Idris told me later he checked, twice, and both times the file had grown teeth and bitten him — but those of us on the survey crews saw the minutes before they were scrubbed. The Vess were sentient. The Fleet knew. The Fleet voted anyway to pull support, quarantine the deep districts, and let the record show the colony had failed for "economic reasons." Economic reasons. As if the Vess songs we'd been recording for a decade were a line item.

Six of us deserted rather than fly the last survey that would've helped draw the quarantine boundaries. Six names needed to disappear. My brother, who could forge a manifest better than most people can write their own name, made us into ghosts. Forty-one crew listed aboard a ship that never launched. He put me on a ship that never flew and sent me down a hole in the world instead, and then he went back to his desk and stayed there for fifteen years, and I'm supposed to feel grateful, and mostly I do, and the rest of me has been living down here in the dark eating engineered mushrooms and talking to nobody.

Well. Not exactly nobody.

* * *

Here's the thing about the Vess that the Fleet never understood, because understanding would have been inconvenient: they don't die the way we think of dying. They thin. Their transmissions don't stop; they slow, like a heart deciding how long it wants to keep beating. And in the deep colony, where the last unquarantined Vess nodes still hummed through the rock, I found out something else — that they will teach you their language if you're the only one listening, because a language with no second speaker isn't a language anymore. It's just noise with grief in it.

So I listened. For fifteen years. My hands learned the sign-forms the way other people's hands learn prayer. The Vess-sign isn't like human signing — it's spatial, temporal, layered. You speak in positions and durations. You can hold two meanings at once by holding two shapes. It took me four years to be able to say *I am alone* without it also meaning *you are alone*, and another three to decide that this ambiguity wasn't a flaw. It might have been the most honest thing about the language.

And in exchange for my attention, they gave me theirs. Which is why, when the two sets of boots came down my stairs, the nearest surviving node — the one threaded through the east gallery wall like frozen lightning — flickered once, hard, a warning pulse I felt in my teeth before I heard anything.

They're coming, the node said. Two of them. One carries old guilt. One carries new questions.

I killed my lamp and waited to see which kind of trouble guilt and curiosity make when they travel together.

* * *

"Amara?" Idris said again, closer now, his voice doing that thing it always did when he was afraid of the answer — going gentle, going careful, like the world was a sleeping animal. "It's me. It's Idris."

I stepped out of the growing racks.

I'll give him this: he didn't scream. Most people scream, the first time they see what fifteen years of dark and fungus-work and Vess-sign does to a person. I keep my hair short with a sharpened spade handle. I'm thinner than the word *thin* comfortably covers. My eyes have adjusted so far toward the dim that in full light I look like something startled out of a wall. And I was holding a harvesting blade, because I'm not an idiot.

He screamed a little. Inside. I could see it happen behind his face, all the brotherly machinery seizing up at once — *that's not her, that's her, what did we do to her* — and then he did the worst possible thing, which was reach for me.

I put the blade between us. Not at his throat. Just present. A fact on the table.

"Don't," I signed, and then caught myself, because he doesn't read Vess-sign, and the habit of speaking to people who understand me had gotten so deep I'd forgotten bodies need words. So I said it out loud instead, and my voice came out rusted, underused, strange even to me. "Don't touch me."

"Okay," he said. "Okay. I won't. Amara, I—"

"You look old," I said, because if I let him start apologizing I was going to have to feel things, and feeling things in front of strangers is how you die down here. Or up there. Everywhere, really.

"I am old," he said, and laughed, wet and awful. "You look—"

"Feral," I supplied. "Everyone thinks it. Say it and we can move on."

The other one — the scientist, the counter-of-footsteps — moved slightly into the glow, and I got my first real look at Petra Thackeray. I knew the name. Everyone in the deep colony knew the name, back when there were enough of us left to gossip; the xenolinguist, the last one, the woman whose recordings of the Vess songs were the only ones the Fleet couldn't fully disappear because she'd distributed them too widely too early. I'd listened to her field recordings more than once, alone in the galleries, furious at how beautiful they were. She had a gift for hearing the structure inside the grief. I hated her a little for it, the way you hate anyone who got to your private cathedral first and described it accurately.

She wasn't wearing Fleet colors. That mattered. But she was wearing the *absence* of Fleet colors in a way that looked deliberate, and deliberate people are either allies or problems, and down here I'd learned to treat both identically until proven otherwise.

"You're the linguist," I said.

"I'm trying to be," she said. "I'm not sure I qualify anymore."

"Nobody qualifies anymore," I said. "That's sort of the whole situation."

* * *

They wanted things, of course. Everyone who comes down here wants things. Idris wanted forgiveness, though he was too smart to ask for it outright, so instead he asked if I was safe, if I was fed, if I needed anything, each question a small white flag. Petra wanted the node. She stood near the east gallery wall with her head tilted like a bird's, and I watched her watch the Vess lattice pulse, and I watched her hands start moving — clumsy, half-remembered forms, academic sign, the textbook version, every gesture correct and every gesture dead, like watching someone recite a love letter from memory without knowing who wrote it.

"That's wrong," I said.

Her hands stopped. "The grammar?"

"The grammar's fine. The meaning's wrong. You're signing *hello* like it's a doorbell." I set down the blade — not far, within reach, because principles — and lifted my own hands. *Hello* in true Vess-sign isn't a wave. It's an offering of duration: you shape the space between you and the other speaker and you hold it open, and how long you hold it tells them how long you intend to stay. The Fleet version lasted half a second. Mine, I held for a slow ten-count, and I watched her understand it, really understand it, her whole face rearranging itself around the concept.

"It's a commitment," she breathed.

"It's a promise about time," I said. "Everything in their language is a promise about time. That's why it's dying so badly. Nobody's keeping any."

The node pulsed when I finished the sign — a soft ripple through the wall-lattice, the Vess equivalent of a throat clearing, or maybe just surprise that a third human voice had entered a conversation it thought was over. Petra made a sound I recognized from my own mirror, years ago: the small involuntary noise of a person meeting the living after mourning the dead.

"They answered you," she said.

"They answer anyone who signs honestly," I said. "Which is a shorter list than you'd think."

* * *

Here is what I did not tell them, standing there in the blue-green light with my blade in reach and my brother's eyes swimming: I had dreamed about this visit. Not literally — I don't dream the way Petra apparently dreams, in transmitted grammar, though the node had shown me images sometimes, fragments, the tower of light at the edge of dead space that features in every Vess death-record. But I had imagined it. Fifteen years of imagining. In most versions Idsris arrived alone, older, hollowed out, and knelt down in the fungal mud and said *I'm sorry* until the words wore smooth as river stones, and in some versions I forgave him, and in the honest versions I made him live down here a year first, eat what I ate, learn what I learned, so that sorry would mean something with duration attached. A promise about time.

Instead he'd brought a stranger and a story about a ship called the *Long Answer* and a relay at the edge of dead space and a truce expiring in however many days, and a plan that required me — me, the ghost on the manifest of a ship that never flew — to walk back up into the light and trust people again.

"We need you to come with us," Petra said, eventually, when the node had gone quiet and the silence had gotten heavy enough to need breaking. "The treaty verification runs through the relay. If no verified Vess transmission is logged before the truce expires, the Fleet can reclassify them. Legally. As vermin."

"Vermin," I repeated.

"Pest control," Idris said, and couldn't look at me while he said it.

I thought about the node in the east wall. About the smaller ones, deeper in, the ones that had gone silent over the years one by one, each silence a little death I'd sat with, because someone should sit with them. About the way the last strong node had been thinning lately, its pulses arriving slower, wider apart, a heart making decisions. They weren't asking to be saved. I'm not sure the concept translates. What they were doing — what they'd been doing through me, through their death-records, through whatever channel had reached Petra in her sleep — was insisting on being *remembered accurately*. There's a sign-form for that. It takes both hands and most of your chest, and it means roughly: *what happened to us happened, and it happened to persons.*

"What exactly," I said, "is in it for them? The Fleet. If they win. If the truce lapses and nobody broadcasts. What do they get besides a clean ledger?"

Petra and Idris exchanged a look that answered me before either of them opened their mouths. It was the look of two people who had recently discovered the same corpse and were still arguing about whose fault it was.

"A clean ledger," Petra admitted.

"And you?" I asked her. "What's your clean ledger look like? Because nobody hikes down to the deep colony out of pure scholarship. Not anymore. Scholarship died with the funding."

Something crossed her face then — quick, swallowed, but I've spent fifteen years reading beings who communicate in layers, and I caught the layer underneath. Guilt. Real guilt, structural guilt, load-bearing guilt. The kind you build a personality on top of.

"I signed something," she said. "Years ago. A preliminary certification. I believed they were gone." Her jaw tightened. "I was wrong, and I was convenient, and I'd like to stop being both."

Huh. I looked at her for a long moment. Feral hope, Idris would probably call it later, if he ever wrote any of this down — hope that's been living wild so long it bites first and asks questions never. That's what I felt, looking at this guilty, brilliant, tired woman standing in my garden offering me a reason to go back up into the world that had thrown me away.

"No Fleet colors," I said.

"What?"

"Your pilot, your crew, whoever else is on this suicide run. If I see one person in Fleet gray, I'm gone. I don't care if we're mid-broadcast. I will walk into dead space and take my chances with the nothing." I picked the blade back up, mostly for emphasis. "I spent fifteen years down here because of people in Fleet gray. I'm not spending the rest of them next to any."

"Agreed," Petra said, fast and flat, no negotiation in it.

"Sefu Bello owns exactly one uniform and uses it as a rag," Idris offered. "You'll like him. He hates everyone equally."

"Everyone equally," I said, "is the best offer I've had in fifteen years."

* * *

I told them I needed one hour. They assumed it was to pack. It takes ten minutes to pack a life like mine — blade, seed kit, water skin, the bundle of node-shards I'd harvested from the dead galleries over the years, each one a voice that had stopped, kept the way you'd keep ashes.

The hour was for the node.

I stood in front of the east wall and signed to it properly, the long form, the promise-about-time form, and I held it open for as long as my arms could bear, which these days is not as long as it used to be, and I told it — told *them*, whoever was still listening through the thinning lattice — where I was going and why and that I did not know if I'd come back. And the node did something it had never done in fifteen years.

It answered in the old pattern. The full-death-record cadence, the one from the deepest archives, the one that means: *we were here, we were persons, carry it.* And then, appended to the end, in a fragment so small I almost missed it, something new. Something none of the archived patterns contained.

A question.

I don't have the vocabulary yet to translate it cleanly — that's the truth, and I've had fifteen years, so you can imagine what it costs me to admit that. The closest I can get is: *if you carry us, what will you become?*

I turned around and Petra was watching me sign, and her eyes were doing arithmetic, and I could see she'd caught the shape of it even if she hadn't caught the meaning, and I understood suddenly why the node had warned me they were coming. Not danger. *Necessity.* This woman was going to need every fluent hand in the colony, and there was exactly one, and it was covered in mushroom compost and holding a knife.

"Ready?" Idris asked.

"One condition," I said, shouldering my pack. "On the way out, we stop at the west gallery. There's a node there that's almost gone. I'm not leaving without saying goodbye, and I'm not rushing it, and if your truce clock can't survive twenty minutes of courtesy, then the Fleet can classify *me* vermin too and we can all save some time."

Petra looked at me the way you look at a locked door you suddenly realize you need.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "Maybe thirty."

"Forty-five," I said, "and I'll teach you the goodbye form on the way. You'll want it. Trust me. Before this is over, everyone in this crew is going to need to know how to tell something they loved that it's dying — and mean it."

We started up the stairs, toward the light, toward the ship that never flew and the war that officially wasn't happening, and behind us the east node pulsed once more — slow, patient, a heart keeping its own time.

The question followed me up into the dark like a second shadow.

*If you carry us, what will you become?*

I didn't have an answer. But for the first time in fifteen years, I was somewhere warm enough to wonder if I'd live long enough to find one.
