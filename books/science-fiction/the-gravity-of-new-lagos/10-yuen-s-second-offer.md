## 10. Yuen's Second Offer

The summons came on good paper.

That was the first thing I noticed. In a colony where the Fleet had been quietly downgrading our supply priority for years — where requisition forms came through on flimsy recycled stock that smelled faintly of fish protein and regret — Commander Yuen's invitation arrived on heavy cream stock with an embossed Fleet crest and my name spelled correctly. Petra Thackeray. Not Thackery, not Thatcher, not whatever the payroll system had mangled me into last quarter. Someone had checked. Someone cared enough to check.

When the bureaucracy starts spelling your name right, it wants something.

*Commander Yuen requests the pleasure of your company at dinner, 1900 hours, Administration House.* The word *pleasure* was doing more work than any word I'd ever seen in a Fleet document, and I say that as someone who once read a cargo manifest describing four hundred tons of leaking coolant as "miscellaneous liquids."

Idris found me staring at it in the archive office, my tea going cold beside the Halcyon Step drive I'd taken to keeping under a false folder labeled SEPTIC MAINTENANCE LOGS 2141–2144. Nobody in the history of colonial administration has ever voluntarily opened septic maintenance logs. It was the digital equivalent of hiding a body under a tarp marked *definitely not a body.*

"Dinner," he said, reading over my shoulder. "With china?"

"With china."

"Last time Fleet brass ate off china at Admin House was the founding-day ceremony. Governor Okonkwe used the occasion to announce the water rationing." He tapped the card. "She's going to ask you for something."

"She's going to *offer* me something," I said. "That's worse."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he'd been looking at me since the night he told me about Amara, since the confession cracked open between us like a floor tile. He'd been different since the deep colony. Softer in some places, harder in others, like metal that's been heated and quenched. "Petra. Whatever she offers. You can say no."

"I know I can say no."

"That's not what I mean." He lowered his voice, though we were alone, though the archive's dead sensors hadn't worked in two years. "I mean you can say no and still be okay. You don't have to buy your way into her good graces to protect this." His hand hovered near the septic folder without touching it. "We've come this far."

"We've come this far because so far nobody's looked directly at us," I said. "Yuen is starting to look. And when someone like Yuen looks, you want to be the one holding the lantern, not the one standing in the dark."

"That sounds like a plan. Plans made by people about to do something stupid always sound like that."

"Wise of you to notice."

"I'm a records clerk. Noticing is my entire personality." But he didn't argue further, which was its own kind of argument, and he left me alone with the cream stock and the embossed crest and the slow, cold understanding that I had already decided to go — had decided it somewhere below language, in the part of me that had been dreaming in Vess for months now, the part that understood that survival is a grammar too, and sometimes the only correct sentence costs you something you can't spell.

* * *

Administration House had been built by the first wave, back when New Lagos was a promise instead of a liability. It had bones of real stone hauled up from the seabed quarries, wide halls, ceilings high enough to make you feel small in the flattering way architecture used to intend. Now it housed maybe forty administrators in a building designed for four hundred, and the emptiness had gone strange — rooms sealed against damp, corridors lit at half-power so the shadows pooled like standing water.

Yuen had claimed the old governor's dining room. A table meant for thirty held settings for two. Candles — actual wax candles, because the power grid couldn't be trusted but ceremony apparently could — threw light across a meal that must have cost the commissary a month of someone's protein allocation. Roof-fish, glazed tubers, a bottle of wine from the hydroponics bay that I recognized as the vintage they saved for visiting auditors.

"Dr. Thackeray." Yuen rose and shook my hand, and her smile did its usual performance: warm at the edges, vacant at the center, a window with the lights off behind the curtains. "Thank you for coming. Please — sit. I hope you eat roof-fish. The kitchen gets anxious."

"The kitchen has nothing to worry about. I'd eat roof-fish at my own funeral."

"Spoken like a true colonist." She poured the wine herself, no steward, which I noted. No witnesses either. This wasn't a dinner; it was a transaction with tableware. "How are the archives?"

"Damp," I said. "But orderly. Orderly-ish. Orderly in the way a teenager's bedroom is orderly, if you know where to look."

"And do you know where to look?"

There it was. First course, second minute, and already the knife under the napkin. I smiled and ate a tuber. "Commander, I know where everything in that building is. That's the job. It's also the curse. Ask me about the 2139 fuel levy dispute sometime. Actually, don't. There's a man who still won't return my calls."

She laughed — a real laugh, brief and unguarded, and for a moment I saw the person underneath the liaison, someone tired, someone who'd been on this rock long enough to know what the word *consolidation* tasted like at three in the morning. Then the curtain came back down and the officer returned. "You've been difficult to schedule lately," she said, lightly, not lightly at all. "Your assistant mentioned you've been making site visits. Down-level."

"Language dies in offices," I said. "If I want to record what's left of Vess vocalization patterns, I have to go where the resonance structures are. The flooded districts carry sound differently. The mining levels even more so."

"The mining levels." She turned her glass by its stem. "Interesting choice. Nothing down there but ghosts and groundwater."

"Ghosts are my specialty."

"Is that what they are?" Her eyes came up and held mine, and there it was again — that flicker of assessment, the auditor's scan, measuring me for something. "Dr. Thackeray, let me tell you why I asked you here. And let me tell you honestly, because I suspect you're a woman who can smell dishonesty the way some people smell rain coming."

*You have no idea,* I thought, and drank my wine.

"When I arrived, I reviewed every personnel file on this colony. Standard practice. Most of them were exactly what you'd expect — competent, tired, counting days. Yours was different." She set down the glass. "Oxford-trained before the diaspora. Last practicing xenolinguist in the fleet registry. Published work on non-human communication theory that people in my academy still cite, though they pretend not to. And a service record with exactly one black mark: a formal protest, eight years ago, against the decommissioning of the deep-structure listening posts. You wrote — I memorized it, I hope you don't mind — 'A civilization's languages outlive its treaties, and we will need both.' Do you remember writing that?"

"I remember being younger."

"It was a good letter. It got filed and ignored, which is how this institution says *you're right but not right now.*" She leaned forward slightly. "Here is what I want to offer you. Not evacuation priority — I know what you'll say to that, everyone on this rock treats the word *evacuation* like a diagnosis. Something better. Something earned."

She reached into her jacket and produced a tablet, slid it across the linen. On the screen: a commission order, drafted, awaiting signature. *Cultural Attaché to the Consolidation Command, New Lagos Theater.*

"Cultural attaché," I repeated.

"Rank equivalent of major. Quarters in Admin House. Direct access to Fleet channels, transport priority, and — this is the part I suspect matters to you — authority over all linguistic and cultural materials relating to the Vess during the transition period." She said *transition period* the way doctors say *comfortable.* "Every recording, every document, every artifact. Curated by you. Preserved by you. Your life's work, Dr. Thackeray, given institutional protection instead of rotting in a damp basement."

It was, I will admit this freely because honesty is the one luxury I can't seem to stop buying, a beautiful offer. It was beautiful the way a trap baited with your own heart is beautiful. Authority over the archives. Legal custody of every scrap of Vess language on the planet — including, if I signed, the things I'd been hiding inside it. The Halcyon Step logs would pass into my official care. My own evidence, handed to me with a bow.

And that was the genius of it, or the cruelty, depending on how generous I felt toward her that evening. She wasn't offering to take anything away. She was offering to give me everything, on the condition that everything became hers to define. Cultural attaché to the Consolidation Command. Attached to the erasure. My signature wouldn't certify extinction — she'd learned that lesson from my stalling, or she'd never intended to ask twice. Instead my signature would make me the official custodian of the story they were going to tell. The archivist of the ending. When the ships left and the lights went out section by section, the record would say: *preserved under the supervision of P. Thackeray, Cultural Attaché.* My name on the spine of their lie, in gold leaf.

"You've gone quiet," Yuen said.

"I'm weighing it."

"Take your time. Have more wine." She refilled my glass, and I watched her hands — steady, precise, a woman who had done nothing wrong tonight except set a perfect snare and call it hospitality. "Can I speak candidly? Off the record, as it were."

"Those are my favorite kinds of speaking."

"I've read your file, and I've watched you these past weeks, and I think you believe this colony deserves better than what's coming. Maybe you're right. Probably you're right — I'm not the villain of this piece, Dr. Thackeray, whatever you've decided I am. I'm a middle manager with a mandate." For just a moment the smile dropped entirely, and underneath it was exhaustion, genuine and human. "Ninety days becomes sixty. Sixty becomes thirty. The consolidation happens whether you sign or not. The only variable is what survives it. Sign, and you decide what's in the box. Refuse, and someone else decides, and I promise you they'll pack less carefully than you would."

"And if I sign and then disagree with how the consolidation proceeds?"

"Then you'll have rank, access, and standing to disagree loudly, in channels that matter." She sat back. "Or you can stay in your basement, Dr. Thackeray, guarding your damp and your ghosts, and watch it all get packed by strangers. I know which future I'd choose. But I'm told I'm ambitious."

I looked at the tablet. The cursor blinked in the signature field with the patience of all bureaucracy, which is to say: forever, and also right now.

Here is the thing I understood, sitting in candlelight that cost more per hour than Idris's monthly wage. Here is the thing I want written plainly, because later — after the relay, after the broadcast, after tribunal benches and amnesty offers — people will ask why I didn't see it sooner, and the honest answer is that I did see it, and I signed anyway. Corruption doesn't arrive wearing a mask. It arrives wearing your face. It arrives saying *this is the practical choice, the protective choice, the choice any reasonable person would make.* It hands you a gilded leash and lets you tell yourself it's a lifeline.

I wanted the access. God help me, I wanted it. With attaché credentials I could move through the Fleet's channels like a ghost through walls. I could reach the relay schedules, the shipping manifests, the truce documentation itself. I could find out what happened to the Halcyon Step's forty-one names, could trace Amara Okafor's ghost through official space. Everything Idris and I had been doing by flashlight, I could do with the lights on.

All it would cost was my name on their paperwork. All it would cost was becoming, on the record, a functionary of the thing I meant to fight.

Sefu Bello would have laughed at me. *They offered you a title, girl, and you're counting the silverware.* Amara would have simply looked at me — that flat, feral, unfooled look — and walked back down into the dark where the honest people lived. Idris would have said *you can say no,* and meant it, and been right, and I would have signed anyway, because Idris has never yet understood that some of us can't protect a thing by keeping our hands clean. Clean hands hold nothing.

"Commander," I said, and picked up the stylus. "One condition."

"Name it."

"The archive stays physically intact. Every item catalogued under my authority transfers nowhere without my countersignature. Including items currently in process of recataloguing." I kept my voice bored, clerical, a woman negotiating shelf space. "You'd be amazed what turns up misfiled in a place like ours. Septic logs. Fuel levies. I'd hate for anything important to get lost in the shuffle."

Something moved behind her eyes — satisfaction, mostly, and beneath it a thin wire of suspicion, quickly filed away. She thought she'd bought me. She thought she'd identified the lever — vanity, legacy, the scholar's ancient weakness for a title — and pulled it, and watched the door swing open. Let her think it. The most useful lie I ever told was the truth shaped wrong.

"Done," she said.

I signed. Petra Thackeray, Cultural Attaché. The stylus scratched against the screen, and the sound was very small, and I thought: *this is how it starts. Not with betrayals. With signatures. With a small yes in a nice room.*

Yuen raised her glass. "To the preservation of culture," she said.

"To preservation," I said, and drank, and smiled with the whole width of my face, and hated myself with the bottom half of my heart.

* * *

I didn't go straight home. I walked — attaché's privilege, nobody questions a uniformed officer walking at night, and the new insignia on my collar changed the way the few people on the streets looked at me, or rather stopped looking, the way people stop looking at anything with rank attached. The flooded districts glowed their bioluminescent glow, the Vess architecture pulsing its slow dying pulse, and I stood on the Kessler Street bridge for a long time watching the light move under the water like something breathing.

My dreams had been getting clearer. The tower of light at the edge of dead space. The door. Lately, a third image had begun recurring: a hand, vast and patient, writing in the dark — and the writing was testimony, I was sure of it now, a record being composed by a species with weeks to live and something to say. They weren't calling for help. I'd listened to enough dying signals to know the difference between a plea and a testament. They were dictating.

And I had just sold my name to the people planning to silence the dictation.

No. That wasn't fair, and unfairness to myself was a luxury I'd have to ration along with everything else. I'd bought a key. Keys open doors; it isn't the key's fault which side of the door you end up on. By Friday I'd have credentials that opened Fleet channel seven. By Monday I could pull the truce verification protocols and confirm what Amara had told us — that the relay was the only point where a verified Vess transmission could legally register before the clock ran out. Ninety days minus nine. Eighty-one days to get a dying language's testimony to a machine at the edge of dead space, aboard a gutted freighter crewed by a disgraced pilot, a deserter's brother, and a woman who spoke to ghosts in sign.

Perfectly reasonable. Any reasonable person would sign.

I took out my pocket recorder — the old one, pre-Fleet, unregistered, the one I trusted — and spoke into it while the drowned city glowed beneath me.

"Field note, Petra Thackeray. Today I accepted a promotion. Note for the record, whoever keeps the record after me: I knew exactly what I was buying and exactly what it cost. If this ends badly, write down that the corruption began here, tonight, with a small yes. And if it ends well—" I paused. The water pulsed. Somewhere under the surface, a Vess structure sang its frequency into the flood, patient as geology. "If it ends well, write down that I still knew."

I clipped the recorder away and headed for the archive, walking fast now, because tomorrow I'd report for duty in a uniform I hadn't earned and start stealing secrets from inside the machine that raised me.

I was halfway down the archive steps when I saw the light under the door — and heard, low and urgent, Idris's voice saying to someone: "—no, she's not here, and even if she were, you'd need more than a requisition form."

Someone answered. The voice was calm, official, and wearing Fleet cadence like Yuen wore her smile.

I stood in the dark on the stairs, newly minted cultural attaché, and realized my leash had already begun to pull.
