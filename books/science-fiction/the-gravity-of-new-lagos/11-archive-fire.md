## 11. Archive Fire

The records hall smelled the way all official memory smells: recycled air, ozone from ancient servers, and the faint institutional despair of paper that nobody had been allowed to throw away and nobody had been allowed to read. I'd spent eleven years of my life in that building. I knew which floorboards creaked, which terminals lied about their uptime, and which corner of the third-floor annex held the decommissioned manifests — the ones Idris and I had been quietly, illegally auditing for six weeks under the cover of a language-preservation survey that didn't exist.

I also knew that the fire alarm in the records hall hadn't worked since before the Consolidation announcement. I'd reported it twice. Both reports had been acknowledged with the Fleet's signature bureaucratic tenderness: *logged for review*.

So when I came around the corner on the morning of the fire and saw smoke threading up through the third-floor windows like the building was exhaling something it had held too long, my first thought was not *how terrible*. My first thought was: *of course*.

* * *

Let me back up, because the morning itself had been ordinary in the way that mornings before catastrophes always are — aggressively, insultingly ordinary. I'd eaten protein ration number four, the one that tastes like a compromise. Idris had been late, which was unlike him, and when he arrived he had the particular set of his shoulders that meant he'd been questioned about something and hadn't liked the questions.

"They pulled my access logs," he said, dropping into the chair across from my desk without sitting so much as collapsing into it. "Records hall. Someone in Fleet security wanted to know why I'd accessed the decommissioned manifests forty times in five weeks."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I was cross-referencing linguistic drift in colonial documentation." He almost smiled. "Which is even true, in a way. The way a lie is true when it's wearing a true coat."

"Did they buy it?"

"They bought it the way you buy a used engine. You take it home and you listen to it very carefully." He rubbed his face. "Petra, the officer asking the questions — he wasn't from Yuen's detail. He was from the *Meridian*. That's a command-level audit vessel. They don't send command auditors to ask about manifests unless the manifests matter to somebody very high up."

I thought about A. Okafor, the name that matched no registry. I thought about forty-one crew listed aboard a corvette that had never, according to any official record, left its cradle. I thought about the ninety-day clock Yuen had set ticking with her white gloves and her champagne reception and her smile calibrated to the millimeter.

"Then we should assume," I said, "that everything we touch from now on is being watched."

"Or burned," Idris said.

He said it lightly. A joke. The kind of joke you make when your unconscious mind has done the math and your conscious mind isn't ready to read the answer.

* * *

The fire started at 14:07 local, which I know because I was standing in the plaza across from the records hall when the first window blew out, and my wrist terminal stamped the moment into my personal log with the cheerful indifference of a machine that does not understand what it is recording.

I had gone back to the hall that afternoon to pull the Halcyon Step's maintenance sub-records — the manifests were only the skeleton; the maintenance logs were the muscle, and if I was going to prove that ship had flown, I needed the muscle. Idris was supposed to meet me there. He was twenty minutes late again, and when my comm pinged him, he answered with static and urgency.

"Don't go in," he said. "Petra, don't — there's a fuel tender parked in the service alley that has no delivery scheduled. I checked. There is no delivery scheduled for that alley today or any day this quarter."

"Idris—"

"I'm calling it in as a safety concern. Give me ten minutes. If I'm right, we let the alarm system that doesn't work fail to work, and we—"

The window blew out.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the whole point of everything that follows, and I have learned — expensively, permanently — that the version of events where you behave well under pressure is almost always a revision. Here is the unrevised version: when the third-floor windows of the colonial records hall blew out in a bloom of black glass and orange light, I did not think of the historical record. I did not think of the Vess, or the treaty, or the forty-one names. I thought, with a clarity that still shames me: *the annex is on the third floor, and I am across the plaza, and I could be inside in ninety seconds if I run.*

So I ran.

* * *

Smoke behaves differently in low-grav-adjacent colonies like New Lagos — our spin gravity is nine-tenths standard, just enough to make smoke rise with a kind of tired reluctance, like a swimmer who's given up on the race but hasn't admitted it yet. The stairwell was a brown throat. I went up it anyway, sleeve over my mouth, because the human animal in a crisis is not a rational creature, it is a creature with one fixed idea, and mine was: *third floor, annex, second aisle, decommissioned manifests, Halcyon Step, Halcyon Step, Halcyon Step.*

The annex was already burning at the far end. The fire had a professional quality to it — none of the ragged hunger of an accident, but the neat, patient appetite of accelerant applied by someone who understood airflow. It was eating the paper records first, the old colonial censuses, the founding correspondence, the original treaty drafts with the Vess signatory marks pressed into the fiber like fossils. I watched a shelf of them go up in a single soft whoosh, two hundred years of co-existence documentation becoming light and heat, and I felt something in my chest that wasn't grief exactly. Grief is for things you loved. This was closer to witnessing an execution.

The data archive was a hardened vault at the annex's center — fire-rated, theoretically. *Theoretically* was doing a lot of work in that sentence. The vault door's seal had been failing for years; I'd filed the ticket myself. Through the narrowing gap I could see the server racks, and I could see, on the rack nearest the door, the physical drives — because the records hall, in its infinite institutional wisdom, kept offline backups of the most incriminating category of document: anything marked *decommissioned*.

The heat was a hand pressing me back. My lungs were filing complaints. Somewhere below, voices — Idris's, and others, evacuation crews maybe, or the people who had set the fire coming back to admire their work.

I got the drive.

I want that sentence to sound clean, but it wasn't. I burned my left hand doing it — the drive's housing had already gone hot enough to cook on — and I made a sound I will not describe, and I ran back down the brown throat of the stairwell with the drive clutched against my chest like a stolen infant, and I did not stop to check whether the vault door, hanging open behind me, would feed the fire into the rest of the racks. I did not think about the other drives. The census records. The plague-era medical logs. The Vess transmission archive, the only complete physical copy in the colony.

I got *my* drive, and I ran, and the rest of it burned.

* * *

They found me in the plaza, twenty minutes later, sitting on the rim of a dry fountain with a scorched data drive in my lap and my left hand wrapped in a stranger's jacket. Idris arrived at a dead sprint, saw my face, and stopped like a man hitting glass.

"The transmission archive," I said. My voice came out as ash. "The Vess transmissions. The original treaty. The plague logs. All of it, Idris. Everything except—"

I held up the drive.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he sat down next to me on the fountain rim, very carefully, the way you sit next to someone when you're not sure if they're going to shatter or detonate.

"The Halcyon Step flight logs," he said.

"True flight logs. Maintenance sub-records. If the manifests were the skeleton, this is—" I stopped. Laughed, which came out wrong, a dry horrible sound. "I keep making that joke. Muscle and skeleton. Like the fire left me a body."

"Petra." He took the drive from my lap with his unbandaged hand, turned it over, checked the casing. "This is the only copy."

"Of the flight logs, yes."

"No. I mean—" He exhaled. "The transmission archive. The treaty drafts. I made a copy. Eight months ago, when you first asked me to pull the decommissioned manifests and I decided to trust you. I copied the whole annex to a cold drive and buried it in my sister's old storage unit in the undercity. Because I'm a coward, and cowards keep backups."

I stared at him. The fire was still burning behind us; the plaza was full of ash falling like grey snow, settling on our shoulders, our hair, the dead fountain. Somewhere a Fleet fire-response team was doing the professional equivalent of arriving after the verdict had been reached.

"You let me run into a burning building," I said, "for a drive you already had."

"You ran in before I could stop you." A beat. "And for what it's worth, I didn't know it was going to burn. I thought — I was worried they'd quietly shred things. Legal erasure. Not—" He gestured at the smoke. "Not this."

"Who?" I asked, and my voice came out flat and certain, and I noticed with a kind of detached anthropological interest that I was no longer asking the question. I was answering it. "Idris. Who do you think did this?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. We both watched the records hall burn, and we both thought the same name, and the name wore white gloves and had a smile that never once reached her eyes.

* * *

Here is what I understood, sitting in the falling ash with a burned hand and a stolen drive: I had spent my whole life believing that archives were the colony's memory, and that memory was a thing you could trust to simply *persist* — that the past was heavy, and heaviness was a kind of safety. The Gravity of New Lagos, my mother used to call it, half-joking: the weight of everything that happened here, holding us down, holding us together.

But the past isn't heavy. The past is *flammable*. It burns exactly as easily as the present does, and the only thing that makes it feel permanent is that somebody, somewhere, keeps choosing to carry it.

Yuen had made her choice. Ninety days to consolidation, and the paper trail was already ash. The manifests I'd found — the impossible document, the ship that never flew — would be explained away as clerical error, if anyone asked at all. The plague logs, the treaty drafts, the Vess voices: erased, and then the erasure itself erased, and then New Lagos itself, and then the word *New Lagos*.

Except.

Except a coward keeps backups. Except a burned xenolinguist with one scorched drive and a hand that would never fully close again. Except somewhere in the deep colony, a woman named Okafor was possibly, impossibly alive, and a disgraced pilot was nursing a fifteen-year grudge in a dockside bar, and the Vess were still transmitting their testimony into my sleep, patient as geology, certain that someone would eventually learn to listen.

I stood up. Ash slid off my shoulders.

"Idris," I said. "The drive in your sister's storage unit. And the one in my hand. And whatever's left of my dreams. That's the archive now. That's the whole record. We're the records hall."

He looked up at me, and for the first time since the fire started, he smiled — a real one, tired and scared and entirely without the Fleet's polish.

"Then we should probably stop storing ourselves somewhere flammable," he said.

I turned my scorched drive over in my bandaged hand, feeling the heat still trapped in its casing, and I thought about Commander Yuen, wherever she was, watching the smoke rise over the plaza with satisfaction or relief or maybe — this was the thought that frightened me most — with nothing at all. No feeling. Just a line item closed.

She thought she'd burned the past.

She hadn't counted on the past learning to run.

"Get the drive from the storage unit tonight," I said. "Before anyone thinks to look there. And Idris — after that, we're going to need a pilot."
