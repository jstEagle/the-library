## 16. The Bribe

There is a particular kind of silence in a records hall at three in the morning, and I have come to know it the way sailors know weather. It isn't empty silence. It's the silence of ten thousand files pretending to be asleep, of dust motes conducting their slow parliament in the light of my hand-lamp, of a building that was built to remember things and has been quietly ordered to forget.

I had learned to move through it like a burglar in my own house.

The drive sat in my coat pocket — the Halcyon Step's true flight logs, salvaged from the fire that should have eaten them along with everything else. But a drive with one ship's logs on it proves one ship existed. What I needed now was context. The Step didn't fly alone. Somewhere in the Fleet's own meticulous bureaucracy there had to be fuel requisitions, navigation clearances, a maintenance crew paid overtime for work they were told never happened. Bureaucracies are wonderful that way. They can be ordered to erase a colony, but they cannot stop being themselves, and themselves leaves fingerprints on everything.

So for eleven nights I had been coming back to the records hall after Yuen's security rotation changed shift, feeding falsified research queries into the archive terminals — *comparative phoneme drift in pre-colonial supply manifests*, which sounded academic enough that even if someone read the query log they'd assume the xenolinguist had finally gone entirely around the bend — and copying anything that smelled of smoke.

Because that was the thing about the fire. It hadn't smelled like an accident.

Accidental fires start where the fuel is. This one had started in the east annex, where the colonial-era originals were kept, and spread with a selectivity that would have embarrassed a professional arsonist. Water suppression had failed in exactly the rooms that mattered and functioned flawlessly everywhere else. I had stood in the wet ash of the reading room two days after, holding the one drive I'd managed to pull from a salvage bin, and thought: *someone wanted this clean.* And then I'd thought, with the particular cold clarity that has been my companion since the dreams started: *good. That means there's something left to want clean.*

Idris thought I was going to get us both killed. He said this every night, in increasingly creative ways. Tonight he'd said it by not saying it at all — just standing at the door of my office as I pulled on my coat, holding out a thermos of kaff, his face doing the thing it does when he's decided arguing is a waste of breath but disapproval is free.

"You could sleep," he'd said.

"I'll sleep when the Vess do."

"That's the problem," he'd said. "They don't."

* *

The records hall occupied the ground floor of the old Administration Spire, back when New Lagos still believed in administration. Its ceiling was high enough that my lamp beam got lost before it found anything, and the climate system had died years ago, so the air had the texture of old paper and older regret. I worked by feel mostly, fingers on the terminal keys, screen dimmed to almost nothing, the way you learn when you've spent your life listening for signals other people don't want heard.

Terminal nine was in the far corner, behind the shelving that held the agricultural subsidy records from the colony's optimistic second decade. Nobody had touched those shelves since before the flood districts went under. Dust lay on them in undisturbed geological strata. My own footprints from last night were the newest event in that corner's history, and even those had half-faded.

I plugged in, ran my query string, and watched the index crawl.

Here is what nobody tells you about theft: it's boring. Holodramas have you sweating under a laser grid while a countdown chirps. In reality you sit in a cold room for forty minutes watching a progress bar, jumping at sounds that turn out to be the building settling, and doing arithmetic about how much trouble you're in versus how much trouble everyone else will be in if you stop. I did that arithmetic every night. Every night it came out the same. Forty-one people on a manifest for a ship that never flew, a plague the Fleet denied, deserters living in the dark eating engineered fungus — against my discomfort, my career, my neck. The math wasn't close. It had never been close. That was the part that frightened me, actually — how easily the math came out. I kept expecting it to get harder. It only got easier, and ease, in my experience, is how good people slide into the places bad ones live.

The progress bar hit sixty percent. Then seventy. Then—

The lights came on.

Not all of them. Just the aisle I was sitting in, one bank of fixtures humming awake above me with the smugness of a trap that has been patient. And footsteps. Unhurried. Military heels on old tile, each step placed like a signature.

I had maybe four seconds. Long enough to yank the drive. Not long enough to hide it, or myself, or the fact that terminal nine was warm and my query log was going to read like a confession written by someone with nothing left to lose.

So I did what any reasonable person does. I sat very still and prepared to be catastrophically honest, which is the last refuge of people who've run out of better options.

Commander Yuen rounded the shelving unit with two of her officers flanking her, and she was smiling.

I want to be fair to Yuen here, because fairness is going to matter later in this story, though I didn't know it yet. She is not a stupid woman and she is not, I think, a cruel one. She is a woman who has spent fifteen years carrying a secret the size of a colony and has decided, somewhere along the way, that the only way to survive the weight is to keep setting it down on other people. Her smile was a tool the way her rank was a tool, and tonight she'd brought it out polished.

"Dr. Thackeray." She glanced at the terminal, at the progress bar frozen at seventy-four percent, at my face. "Working late."

"Insomnia," I said. "Occupational hazard of dreaming in dead languages."

"Mm." She nodded at my coat pocket, where the outline of the drive sat against my hip like a tumor. "And the insomnia brought you to terminal nine. Behind the agricultural subsidies. At three in the morning."

"The acoustics are better here," I said. "For thinking."

One of the officers made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Yuen didn't look at him. She looked at me the way you look at a lock you're deciding whether to pick or simply buy the building.

"Dr. Thackeray," she said, "let's take a walk."

* *

She dismissed the officers, which I noted, and which she probably knew I'd note. We walked the long gallery of the records hall, past the reading room still scarred black from the fire, past the memorial wall where the colony's founding families were listed in brass — Thackeray among them, third row, because my mother had signed the original charter and my name had opened doors my whole life without my ever once asking who else's names had closed.

"You've been distracted lately," Yuen said. "At the briefings. Absent from the compound. Commander Adeyemi logged you leaving the residential block on eleven separate nights this month."

"Eleven," I said. "She counts well."

"She counts everything. It's why I keep her." Yuen stopped walking. Above us, the surviving ceiling panels threw down flat institutional light, and in it her face was smooth and tired and, for just a moment, honest in a way that was worse than any performance. "Petra. Sit with me a moment."

There was a bench by the memorial wall, because of course there was; the Fleet loves its symbolism the way the Vess loved resonance, except the Fleet's version always rang slightly false, like a bell cast by someone who'd only ever had bells described to them. We sat. The brass names gleamed over our heads. My mother's name. Yuen's eyes flicked up to it, then back to me, and I understood she'd chosen this bench deliberately too.

"Do you know why you're here?" she asked. "On New Lagos. Why we kept you?"

"Sentiment," I said. "I'm a mascot. The last xenolinguist, the charter family orphan, the human face on a decision already made."

"You're an asset," she said, without heat. "A valuable one. And assets get protected. That's what I'm here to offer you, Petra. Protection."

"From what?"

"From yourself." She turned to face me fully. "You've found something. I don't know what yet — the fire took care of most of it, and I'm told the rest is scattered. But you've been looking, and looking is the dangerous part. Whatever you think you're going to find, it won't change anything. It will only change *you*. Do you understand the difference? The record doesn't care. The tribunal doesn't exist. There is no court coming. There is only the ninety days, and then consolidation, and then New Lagos is a line item marked *resolved*, and everyone who lives through it gets to decide what story they were part of."

"And I get to choose my story."

"You get to choose a *good* one." She leaned forward, voice dropping into the register she probably used on frightened junior officers, warm and level and absolutely certain. "Cultural attaché was the opening offer. I can do better. Full evacuation priority for you and anyone you name. A posting at Central Command — linguistics division, rebuilding their first-contact protocols. Your work matters again, Petra. Real work, funded work, work that isn't recording the death rattle of a species in flooded districts while the water rises another centimeter a year." She paused, precisely. "And Idris Okafor goes free."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped weight.

"There's a discrepancy in an old manifest," Yuen continued, watching me with terrible gentleness. "Fifteen years old. A corvette that never launched, forty-one names, one of them unregistered. Security flagged it during the consolidation audit. Normally that's a tribunal matter. Falsifying Fleet records — that's serious, Petra. That's prison, possibly worse, depending on what the investigation turns up about *why* someone falsified it." She let the silence stretch. "But investigations are discretionary. And discretion, in my experience, responds well to cooperation."

"You'd bury it."

"I'd *lose* it. Files go missing all the time. Ask anyone." Almost a smile. "You sign off on the language certification — a formality, we've discussed it, the Vess transmissions are noise, you said so yourself in your preliminary assessment years ago — and Dr. Okafor's little clerical sin evaporates. He walks out of this with a clean record and a transfer anywhere he likes. You walk out with a career. New Lagos walks out with dignity, which is more than most dying things get." She stood, smoothing her uniform. "You have until the survey team's final briefing. Six days. Think about who you owe, Petra. The dead don't collect debts. The living do."

She walked away down the gallery, heels marking time, and left me sitting under my mother's name with the drive burning against my hip like a coal.

* *

I didn't go straight home. I walked the flood-district seawall instead, because some conversations you need to have with water.

Here is the thing I have never admitted in any official document, and am admitting now only because this account is going into the record whether I like it or not: for about twenty minutes, standing on that seawall with the black water breathing below me, I considered saying yes.

Not cynically. That's the shameful part. I considered it *sincerely*. Idris in chains is a specific image, and it has weight — he's not an abstraction to me, he's a young man who brings me kaff and covers for my absences and carries guilt like a stone in his shoe about a sister he thinks he abandoned. Yuen had done her homework. She'd found the one lever that moves me, and she'd pulled it gently, the way you handle something you don't want to break before you need it broken.

And underneath that: the offer was real. The career was real. The work was real. I have spent six years cataloguing a dying language in a dying colony, and some cold practical animal in my brain whispered, *you could teach again. You could build the protocols so the next contact doesn't end like this one. Isn't that worth one signature?* The animal is not wrong, exactly. That's what makes it dangerous. Corruption doesn't come to you wearing the enemy's colors. It comes wearing your own, speaking in your own voice, making excellent points.

I thought about Amara Okafor, alive in the dark, tending her garden of pale fungi, refusing to trust anyone in Fleet colors. I thought about her learning Vess-sign from the last living speakers, keeping a language alive with her hands when the whole apparatus of the Fleet had declared it dead on paper. Paper. Yuen's entire world was paper. Certifications, manifests, tribunals that don't exist. Amara's world was hands and light and a treaty clause that said, in effect: *prove we were ever real.*

If I signed, the proof died. Not the Vess — they were dying anyway, had been dying for years, were perhaps already gone, sending their testimony out toward a relay at the edge of dead space like a message folded into a bottle. What would die was the *record*. And I had begun to understand, in the way you understand things slowly and then all at once, that the record was the only immortality anyone in this story was going to get.

The water breathed below me. Somewhere out past the shelf, the dreams said, there was a tower of light and a door closing, and the closing was scheduled, and the schedule was the truce clock, and the clock was running.

I went home. Idris was awake — he was always awake now, in the way of the guilty and the loyal, which in his case were the same thing. He took one look at my face and put the kettle on.

"She offered you the trade," he said. Not a question.

"How—"

"Because she offered me the same one yesterday. Except hers was inverted. She offered to let *you* keep your freedom if I confessed to the manifest and named accomplices." He poured the kaff, hands steady, eyes not. "I told her I'd falsify it again. All forty-one names. Twice on Sundays."

"Idris—"

"Don't." He set the cup in front of me. "Whatever you're about to say — that you're sorry, that you're thinking of taking the deal, that you're worried about me — don't say it yet. First tell me what you're going to tell *her*. Because she's going to ask again, Petra, and next time she won't be gentle, and whatever you answer is going to be the person you are for the rest of your life. I've thought about this a lot these past few days. Being lied about teaches you what lies cost."

I held the cup and watched the steam and understood that I was standing on the deck of my own self, looking at a course change, and that the helmsman was me.

Six days later, in Yuen's office, with the certification padd laid on the desk between us and her stylus beside it like a loaded weapon, she asked me the question directly. "Well, Dr. Thackeray? Have you decided whose side of history you'd like to stand on?"

And I lied to her face.

It was astonishingly easy, which is the detail I keep returning to, the one that wakes me up at night. No sweat. No stammer. I smiled — I *smiled*, with her own borrowed warmth — and I said, "I've reviewed the final transmission logs. There's nothing there, Commander. Resonance artifacts, atmospheric interference, wishful thinking dressed up as signal. The Vess language is extinct. I'll certify it." And then, because the truly great lies are generous, I added: "I'd like Idris's file handled quietly, as discussed. He's a good archivist. It would be a shame to lose him over paperwork."

Yuen studied me for a long moment, and I watched her decide to believe me, and I understood that I had just done the thing I had judged her for. I had set my weight down on someone else. The difference — the difference I clung to on the walk back, the difference I will cling to in front of any tribunal that ever convenes — is that I lied *toward* the truth. She lied to bury the dead. I lied to carry them. Whether that distinction survives contact with judgment, I genuinely don't know. I only know that when I walked out of her office with the certification unsigned and her stylus untouched, something in me had permanently changed shape, and it wasn't a shape I recognized, and I suspected I'd be meeting it again at the relay, in the tower of light, when authentication demanded my real name and my real signature and every real thing I'd ever done.

Idris was waiting in the corridor. He read my face the way he reads indexes — fast, thorough, missing nothing.

"Well?" he said.

"It's done. She believes me. She'll release your file within the week." I kept walking, fast, toward the docks, toward the gutted freighter at Berth Nine and the disgraced pilot who was waiting there with a grudge fifteen years deep and a departure window thirty days wide. "Which means we have exactly as much time as her belief lasts. Come on. Sefu wants to show us the fuel lines, and he's promised to explain, in detail, all the ways this ship can kill us."

"That's reassuring."

"He calls it a safety briefing. He's lying too. Everyone's lying now." I pushed through the doors into the grey morning light of New Lagos, the city that was going to survive by being remembered, and found that the words tasted like metal. "Welcome to the honest part of the dishonesty, Idris. Try to keep up."
