## 17. Launch

There are two kinds of pilots in this universe: the kind who check the weather and the kind who become it. I've been both, and I can tell you the second one dies younger but sleeps better.

Tonight I intended to be the first kind. The debris storm off New Lagos's trailing hemisphere was a rotating garbage cyclone the size of a small moon — hull plates from the old orbital yards, spent boosters, a decommissioned station's spine still tumbling in pieces like the vertebrae of something that died mid-swim. Every sane captain waited for it to disperse. The Fleet knew that. Which is exactly why we were going through it.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Amara said from the jump seat behind me. She'd claimed it on day one of refit and defended it like territory. She sat with her knees drawn up, watching my hands on the board the way you watch a snake charmer — respectful, and ready to run.

"Because Yuen's cutter is parked at the clean approach vector like a spider at the front door," I said. "And because debris storms are nature's way of saying *no trespassing* to people who own uniforms."

"And if we get hit?"

"We won't."

"You're lying."

"I'm being reassuring. It's different. Lying has an agenda. Reassurance is a public service." I ran the pre-flight sequence and watched forty years of muscle memory do its work while my brain did something less useful, which was remember every bad decision I'd ever made at this same console. The Long Answer had been a grain hauler before she was a gutted freighter before she was whatever she was now — a rumor with an engine. Her hull was scar tissue over scar tissue. She flew ugly. She flew honest. I trusted her more than I trusted most people, which, given the people currently aboard, was saying something.

Petra came forward from the archive bay carrying the drive against her chest like a newborn. Idris followed with the last of the water bladders. Neither of them spoke. There's a particular silence that settles over a crew thirty minutes before a point of no return, and I've heard it on every ship I've ever flown. It's not fear exactly. It's the sound of everyone privately rehearsing their regrets.

"Cutter status," Petra said.

I flicked the passive scope. "Still sitting fat and pretty at vector seven. They think we'll come to them. Nobody launches through that storm unless they're stupid or desperate."

"Which are we?"

"Yes."

That got the first laugh out of anyone in six hours, so I counted it a win.

* * *

The thing about dead space — real dead space, the stretch between New Lagos and the relay buoy network where no rescue comes and no signal carries and the Fleet's writ runs out somewhere around the boundary markers like paint fading at a property line — is that everyone talks about it like it's empty. It isn't empty. Empty implies nothing there. Dead space is full of everything except help.

I'd crossed it once before, fifteen years ago, in the Halcyon Step, on the only hop that ship ever made that wasn't in anybody's logbook. Forty-one names on a manifest for a ship that never officially flew, and me at the stick with my heart going like a drumline, running a test profile some lieutenant wanted buried. The hop took eleven hours. The cover-up took fifteen years and cost me my certification, my pension, and most of my faith in institutions. What it left me was a grudge the size of a gas giant and, as it turned out, the exact skill set these lunatics needed.

Fate has a sense of humor. It's just not a *good* one.

"Engine burn in ninety seconds," I announced. "Once we commit, there's no abort window until we clear the storm's far edge. After that we're in the black for nine days minimum, and if anything breaks, we fix it or we float. Questions?"

"Yeah," Sefu said.

Everyone looked at me. I looked at me. Right — I was asking myself questions now. Fifteen years alone will do that; you develop a full crew in your head and then have to fire them all when actual humans show up.

"What happens after?" I asked myself, out loud, because apparently we were doing this. "After we deliver the truth and the Fleet either collapses or doesn't. What does a disgraced pilot do with the rest of his life?"

"You learn to fly again," Petra said quietly. "For something instead of against something."

"That's disgustingly optimistic. I approve." I keyed the ignition interlock. "Long Answer, all hands, committing in three, two—"

The engines lit with a sound I feel in my teeth rather than hear, and the storm swallowed us whole.

* * *

Flying through a debris storm is often described as threading a needle. This is propaganda put out by people who thread needles. It's closer to swimming through a rockslide while blindfolded, using your elbows. The Long Answer's scopes painted maybe a tenth of what was actually out there; the rest you found by the way your hull rang when you clipped it, which is a detection method I don't recommend but have used professionally.

I drove her hard. Left hand on the translation thrusters, right hand on the main gimbal, feet working the rotation like a drummer keeping time with death. A refrigerator-sized chunk of station plating tumbled past close enough to read the serial number. A booster casing spun end over end across our bow and I rolled under it, and the ship groaned the way old ships groan — not complaint, just commentary.

Behind me, nobody screamed, which I took as a compliment. Idris had gone the color of old candle wax and was holding a cargo strap like it owed him money. Amara watched out the viewport with an expression I couldn't parse — not fear, not thrill. Recognition, maybe. A woman who'd spent years underground in the dark colony levels, looking at a storm of broken things and seeing weather she understood.

Petra stood at my shoulder with the drive strapped across her back, and when a fist-sized bolt punched off our dorsal plate with a bang like God knocking, she didn't even flinch. She just said, "How long?"

"Eleven minutes to the far edge. Assuming the far edge stays where physics put it."

"Assuming."

"I fly on assumptions. It's cheaper than certainties."

We took three more hits in the next four minutes. One opened a slow leak in the aft hold that Idris sealed with foam and profanity, in that order. One glanced off the drive housing and scared us both without doing damage, which is the worst kind of hit because afterward you keep imagining the version where it didn't glance. The third took out our long-range antenna, which meant that from here to the relay we were deaf beyond the reach of the short-band, and there was no fixing that until someone went outside with tools and courage, and I only had one of those to spare.

Then the storm spat us out.

I want to describe it properly, because people who haven't flown it think the transition is gradual. It isn't. One second you're inside a hurricane of wreckage, instruments shrieking, hull ringing like a bell choir. The next, the black opens up around you — clean, absolute, enormous — and the stars come out all at once like an audience that's been waiting in the dark. The silence after the impacts is so complete you can hear your own blood.

Nine days of that ahead of us. Nine days of nothing between us and the tower at the edge of everything.

And behind us — I checked the scope, because I always check the scope, because paranoia is just experience wearing a trench coat — the Fleet cutter had moved.

* * *

They were fast. I'll give them that. Yuen's cutter came around the storm's leading edge on a burn that told me her pilot was good and her captain was angry, and both of those facts arrived in my stomach at the same time.

"They're coming," Idris said.

"They're chasing," I corrected. "Coming implies an invitation."

"How long can they keep pace?"

"Until the boundary." I tapped the nav display, where the dead-space demarcation hung like a line drawn on water. "Past that marker, they're in violation of about six regulations, and more importantly, they're outside Fleet support too. Commanders don't chase ghosts into the dark. Not official ones."

"But Yuen's already running unofficial," Petra said. "She burned the records hall. She's not worried about regulations anymore."

"She's worried about witnesses." I eased the throttle up and felt the Long Answer answer, that old girl giving me everything her rebuilt drives had, which was more than the manufacturer ever promised and less than I wanted. "Watch what she does at the line. That'll tell us everything."

So we ran. Four ships' worth of history in one gutted freighter, burning hard into the black with a warship's drive flare growing in our wake like a second sunrise, and I'll be honest with you, because I'm honest with almost no one: part of me loved it. Fifteen years I'd been grounded — legally, spiritually, every way a man can be grounded. And here I was at the stick again, running from the institution that broke me, carrying the evidence that would break it back, with a crew of liars and deserters and dreamers who'd somehow become the best company I'd kept since the Halcyon Step.

My hands were steady. My mouth was dry. Both of those things were true and neither canceled the other.

The cutter closed to eleven kilometers. Close enough that I could see her running lights strobing — the Fleet pattern, the one that means *heave to and be boarded*, broadcast on open channel like a sentence being read aloud.

Idris answered them. I let him. He had a beautiful voice for insubordination, calm and reasonable, the voice of a man who'd spent his career making paperwork lie persuasively. He informed the cutter that the Long Answer was engaged in authorized salvage operations, that our transponder had suffered damage in the storm, and that we would be happy to comply with inspection upon our return to port, estimated arrival: never.

The cutter's response was a warning shot across our bow — a railgun slug that passed close enough to ionize the dust off our prow. A message in the only language the Fleet speaks fluently.

"They're not stopping," Amara said.

"No," I agreed. "Yuen's past stopping. Which means she's also past thinking clearly, and that's the only advantage we've got." I checked the range to the boundary marker. Two hundred kilometers. The cutter gaining maybe a kilometer every twenty seconds. The math was simple and I hated it: they'd catch us just before the line, or they'd break off just after it, and either way the next ten minutes were going to be the most expensive of somebody's career.

"Options," Petra said.

"One: we surrender, hand over the drive, spend the rest of our lives in a tribunal's footnote. Two: we let them catch us and hope their boarding party is worse at violence than we are, which is a gamble, since none of us are any good at violence. Three—" I pulled up the drift chart, the one I'd plotted during refit and never told anyone about, because a pilot should always hold one card face-down. "Three, we thread the Kessler field."

Idris leaned forward. "The what?"

"The graveyard cluster. Old satellite kill zone from the Resource War, before your time, before everybody's time. Sixty years of untracked wreckage in a band eight hundred klicks wide. No Fleet pilot will follow us in — their insurance doesn't cover heroics, and neither does their doctrine. But I flew supply runs through its outer margin back when I was young enough to think I was immortal."

"You weren't?"

"I was briefly mistaken. The mistake was corrected." I rolled the Long Answer onto the new heading and felt the cutter's lights swing to track us. "It adds six days to the crossing. We'll be tight on water. Tighter on patience. But it puts a wall of shrapnel between us and anything with a Fleet crest on it."

"And if you're wrong about your flying?" Amara asked.

"Then we become part of the field, and honestly? Fitting end. The whole place is made of people who bet wrong." I thumbed the burn. "Any objections, speak now or orbit forever."

Nobody objected. Petra put her hand flat on the back of my seat — not gripping, just present, the way you touch a thing you're trusting. Idris closed his eyes and prayed, or napped, or composed a falsified log entry; with Idris it could be all three. Amara watched the graveyard grow on the scope, a cloud of glittering ruin spreading across the display like frost on glass, and she smiled. Actually smiled. I decided not to ask why. Some kinds of comfort you don't interrogate.

The cutter fired twice more as we closed the distance, both shots wide, both shots furious. Then the boundary marker slid beneath us — a blinking anachronism, a lighthouse for an ocean no one admitted sailing — and I watched the scope, and I watched Yuen's cutter do exactly what I knew she'd do.

She broke off.

Braked hard, flipped, burned back toward the light of New Lagos, her drive flare shrinking from sunrise to matchhead to nothing. Leaving us alone at the border of the dark, with sixty years of dead satellites ahead and nine days of silence and a drive full of truth strapped to a xenolinguist's back.

"She stopped," Idris said, disbelief plain in his voice.

"At the line. Like I said. Commanders don't chase ghosts into the dark." I killed the pursuit burn and settled us onto the long coast toward the Kessler field, and the quiet that came down over the cabin was the deep kind, the kind you only get past the point where turning back costs more than going on. "But mark it, all of you — she didn't stop because she gave up. She stopped because she knows where we're going, and she's going to be waiting on the other side. Or she's calling ahead. Or both. Whatever waits at that relay, it won't just be Vess architecture and old light."

Petra's hand was still on my seat. "You always this cheerful?"

"I'm a delight. Ask anyone who's stopped speaking to me."

Ahead, the graveyard turned slowly in the starlight, millions of fragments catching the sun like a held breath of glitter, and I brought the Long Answer in toward the first gap with both hands loose on the controls and my heart doing something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite joy and was, I suppose, the exact weight of being irrevocably committed.

"Six days to the other side," I told them. "Try to miss me."

Nobody laughed. But nobody objected either, and out here, that's the same thing.
