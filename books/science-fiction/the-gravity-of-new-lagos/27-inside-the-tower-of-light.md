## 27. Inside the Tower of Light

My mother taught me that doors are a human invention. The Vess, she said — and by *she* I mean the journals I found in the deep colony, because my actual mother died when I was six and left me nothing but her name and her hands — the Vess never built walls to keep things out. They built them to hold things in. Gently. The way you cup water.

I thought about that for a long time standing in front of the door at the heart of the relay, because it was the first Vess door I'd ever seen whole, unbroken, still willing to open. Everything else they'd built on New Lagos had rotted or flooded or been torn down by people who called it salvage. This was older than the colony itself. Older than the Fleet's claim to this system, which meant older than every document that said the Vess had never really been here, never really mattered, never really counted as anyone you'd have to apologize to.

The door was tall — three meters, maybe more — and it wasn't stone or metal but something like grown coral, pale and faintly luminous, veined with channels that pulsed slow as sleeping breath. There was no handle. No seam you could find with your fingers unless you already knew where to look. Which, I suppose, was the point.

"You're sure about this," Sefu said behind me. He always phrased his fear as a question directed at someone else, so it sounded like professional skepticism instead of what it was.

"No," I said. "But I'm the only one who can be wrong in a useful way."

Petra stood beside me with her hands folded like a woman in church, which I suppose she technically was. She'd been staring at the door since we came through the outer shell, and there was something in her face I recognized from mirrors in bad years: the look of someone meeting a person they've only known through letters. She'd dreamed her way here. I'd crawled my way here, through fifteen years of fungus gardens and silence and eating things that were technically food. We arrived at the same door by opposite roads, and I will admit — privately, once, never out loud — that watching her see it made me feel less alone than I had since Amara Okafor stopped being a name on a falsified manifest.

"Show me again," I said. "The opening sequence."

She signed it slowly. Not fluent — Petra's Vess-sign was a scholar's Vess-sign, precise and slightly stiff, like a foreigner reciting poetry she loved but hadn't lived inside. But correct. The root gesture first: both hands open, palms forward, fingers spread wide, held at the height of the sternum. That one meant *I come without armor*. Then the spiral inward, right hand circling left, tightening toward the center of the chest. *What I carry is small but true.* Then the final sign, the one that had taken me two years of the journals to understand, because it looked like nothing — just both hands lowering, palms down, coming to rest against the thighs.

*You may look inside me.*

"Doors are questions," I said, mostly to myself. "That's what she wrote. A locked door is a question about who's asking."

"Your mother," Petra said quietly, "or the journals?"

"Same thing, near enough." My mother had been the one who copied them out, in the last years, when her eyes were going. Pages and pages of Vess-sign diagrams rendered in ink made from crushed lichen, annotated in a hand that got shakier and more beautiful as it went. I learned to read those pages before I learned to read Fleet Standard properly, and I have never once regretted the order.

I stepped up to the door.

Up close, the coral surface was warm. Not body-warm — hearth-warm, the temperature of a room where someone is expecting you. The veins pulsed a little faster as I approached, and I felt that in my own chest, an answering quickening, and I had to stand very still for a moment and breathe, because grief does this thing where it waits until you're finally about to receive something and then tries to convince you that you don't deserve it.

Fifteen years in the deep colony teaches you a lot of things, and most of them are useless up here. How to judge the ripeness of engineered fungi by smell alone. How to sleep in shifts so the dark doesn't get ideas. How to talk to yourself in two languages so neither one gets lonely. But one thing it taught me that turned out to matter enormously, right now, at this door: how to wait. Humans rush. Humans knock. Humans treat every closed thing as an insult. The Vess treated every closed thing as a courtesy — a chance for the thing inside to prepare itself for being seen.

So I waited. I put my palms flat against the warm coral, spread my fingers the way the root gesture demanded, and I waited, breathing slow, letting the door feel the shape of my intent through my skin.

Nothing happened for a long time. Long enough that Sefu started to say something and Idris elbowed him quiet. Long enough that I could hear the relay itself — a hum below hearing, felt in the teeth and the breastbone, vast and patient, like the building was a lung that had been holding one breath for four hundred years.

Then the veins under my palms brightened.

It moved outward from my hands in rings, light chasing itself through the channels of the door, faster and faster, and the pulse in my chest matched it, and somewhere behind me Petra made a sound — not a word, just a sound, the sound a person makes when a language they've been dreaming finally opens its eyes.

The door didn't swing. Doors that swing are doors built by people who expect enemies. This one *unfurled*, the coral petals drawing back and up and around like a flower deciding morning has come, and the light that came out of it was —

All right. Here is where my wry narration fails me, and I'm going to let it fail, because some things deserve honest prose even from me.

The light was warm. That's all I can tell you that won't sound like madness. It was warm the way the door was warm, the way a kitchen is warm, the way the deep colony's fungal groves glowed at night when everything alive down there was doing its small stubborn work of staying alive. It fell across us and it did not illuminate so much as *recognize*. I stood in it and felt seen — not inspected, not scanned, not catalogued the way Fleet instruments catalogue you, but *seen*, the way my mother used to look at me when I came in from the gardens with dirt to my elbows and something growing in my cupped hands.

"Come in," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I didn't fix it. "Come in, come in."

* * *

Inside was a tower, and the tower was full of voices.

Not metaphorically. I want to be precise about this, because precision was the one gift the deep colony gave me that the Fleet couldn't confiscate. Inside the relay's heart rose a column of light perhaps thirty meters tall, and within that column hung — suspended, drifting, turning slowly like things caught in a gentle current — points of radiance. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one a different color, a different size, a different rhythm of flicker, and each one, when you came close enough, resolved into a voice.

I heard my mother before I understood what I was hearing.

She was singing. One of the work-songs from the mining levels, the ones the deserters made up because the Fleet's hymnals didn't cover the experience of hauling ore in the dark and calling it freedom. Her voice was younger than I remembered it, which is impossible, and exactly right, which is also impossible, and I stood there with both impossibilities in my chest and let them fight it out.

"The archive," Petra whispered. She had gone past tears into something beyond tears, some country on the far side of crying where the landscape is made entirely of attention. "Amara. It's not a record. Records are copies. This is — they're *here*. Every transmission the colony ever sent. Every voice that ever spoke into a Fleet comm channel and was told to log off. Every Vess broadcast the survey teams dismissed as noise. They kept all of it. They kept *us*."

"The dead ones too?" Idris asked. His voice was careful. He'd spent the whole approach saying almost nothing, carrying his guilt like a man carries a broken rib — braced, guarded, afraid to cough.

"I don't know," Petra said. "I don't think the archive distinguishes. I think—" She stopped, and I watched her try to build a sentence that could hold what she was seeing, and I watched the sentence collapse, and I did the decent thing and finished it for her.

"I think the Vess never accepted the difference between a voice and its echo," I said. "My mother wrote that. *They do not believe anything truly spoken is ever lost. They believe loss is a failure of listening.*"

Sefu had drifted off to one side, and I'll say this for the man: he knew when a cathedral wasn't his. He kept his distance, hands in pockets, muttering to himself about power conduits and whether any of this ancient light-show could be jury-rigged into a weapon if Yuen's cutter came back. Profanity as prayer. It worked for him.

Idris touched one of the lights.

I saw him decide to do it — saw the guilt and the hunger wrestle for possession of his hand — and then his fingers passed into the glow, and a voice bloomed around him, and he went white.

"That's—" He swallowed. "That's the colony council. From the year I falsified the manifest. That's the session where they voted on supply requests. I was *there*. I took the minutes." His hand stayed in the light, trembling. "It's not a recording. Recordings play back. This is — it's still happening. Somewhere in here it's always the day of that meeting, and they're always asking for medicine for the lower districts, and the answer is always—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. We all knew what the answer had been.

I moved deeper into the tower, and the voices thickened around me, and I want to describe what it was like but the honest description is: imagine walking into a river made of everyone you have ever failed to save, and the river is glad to see you.

There were children's voices. Lesson broadcasts from the colonial school, back when the school still taught both species' children together, before the segregation orders. There were market calls, arguments, jokes whose punchlines I caught and lost and caught again as I drifted past. There were Vess voices — and this undid me worse than my mother's song, because I had never heard a living Vess speak aloud. Their speech wasn't sound the way ours is; it was closer to weather, to pressure, to the feeling before a storm when the air gets serious. But the archive rendered it, translated it into frequencies a human ear could hold, and it was beautiful in a way that made beauty seem like too small a word, and it was *dying* in a way that made dying seem like too small a word too, because none of these voices were dead. They were kept. There is a difference, and the difference is a door, and someone had built a door.

Petra found me at the center.

Or I found her. In the middle of the tower the light was densest, and there — I don't know how else to say this — there was a shape in the light. Not a figure. An absence that glowed. A place where the archive gathered around something it was protecting, the way water gathers around a stone.

"It's the core," Petra breathed. "The verification key. The treaty protocols — they're anchored here. Amara, do you understand what this means? The relay isn't just storage. It's a *witness*. Anything broadcast from this point carries the authentication of the archive itself. Every voice in this tower would stand behind whatever we say."

"Then we should be very careful what we say," I said.

"Yes." She looked at me, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "And we shouldn't be careful at all."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and I let that be wrong too. Fifteen years I'd lived in the dark keeping my mother's pages dry, practicing signs to no one, tending a garden of engineered fungi because something in me refused to let the last green thing in the deep colony die unattended. And the whole time — the whole *time* — this was above me. Every voice I'd grieved. Every conversation I'd reconstructed from context and guesswork. Every night I'd signed goodnight to an empty room in a language the last speakers of which were supposedly extinct.

They weren't extinct. They were archived. And there is a kind of salvation in that, and there is also a kind of horror, and I stood in the middle of both and let them be true at once, because the deep colony had taught me that too: two true things can share a body. It's uncomfortable. You survive it.

"Amara." Petra's hand hovered near mine, not touching. She'd learned that much about me on the voyage — that touch was a currency I spent carefully. "Your mother. Is she in here?"

I hadn't dared ask. Asking makes hope into a debt.

I reached into the light.

I don't know how I navigated it. Instinct, maybe. Or the archive reaching back — because I swear to you, swear it on my mother's grave and on her garden and on every page of her shaking handwriting, the archive *leaned toward me*. The voices parted. The current bent. And there, in a fold of light near the core, small and steady and flickering in the exact rhythm of the work-song's chorus, was her.

Not her. The shape her voice had left in the world. The same thing, as far as the Vess were ever concerned.

She was singing. She was always singing. Somewhere in this tower it was always the evening of a day when the ore quota had been met and the garden was thriving and her daughter was asleep in the next chamber, and Amara Okafor the elder was singing to the dark because the dark deserved music too, and the Vess relay had caught the sound on some long-dead sensor and had never, not once in fifteen years, let go of it.

I put both hands into the light and I signed to her.

*I come without armor. What I carry is small but true. You may look inside me.*

The light around her brightened. The song swelled — or I imagined it did; I will never know and I refuse to find out — and for one moment, one impossible warm moment, I was six years old again and the dark was full of music and nothing had been taken from me yet.

Then I stepped back, because grief transfigured is still grief, and even cathedrals expect you to leave eventually.

* * *

We regrouped near the base of the column, where the light thinned enough to think in. Sefu reported — grudgingly, awed despite himself — that the relay's transmitter systems were intact, dormant, and enormous. Idris sat with his head in his hands, taking inventory of his guilt, finding it heavier than when he'd packed it. Petra stared up into the tower with the expression of a woman reading a letter she'd waited her whole life for and dreading the postscript.

"There's a problem," she said finally.

Of course there was. There is always a problem. Hope on New Lagos comes with problems attached the way ships come with barnacles.

"The broadcast protocols require a bilingual key," she said. "A human voice, speaking Vess truth, authenticated by both species' systems simultaneously. The Vess side will accept me — my dream-transmissions have been registering in their network for months; I'm apparently already in the archive, which is a sentence I never expected to say. But the human authentication isn't technical. It's *provenance*." She paused, and I watched her gather herself the way you gather yourself before diving into cold water. "The system verifies identity through Fleet records. Biometric, service history, signature match. When it authenticates me, it will pull my full file."

"And?" Sefu said. "You're Fleet-raised. Golden girl. Should be fine."

"My file includes the preliminary extinction certification," Petra said quietly. "Drafted eleven years ago. Signed by Petra Thackeray, junior xenolinguist, attesting that the Vess language showed no surviving speakers and no likelihood of revival." She looked at me, and I made myself hold her gaze, because looking away would have been mercy and mercy wasn't mine to give yet. "When the relay authenticates my voice, it will authenticate that signature too. Every world that receives the broadcast will receive my confession with it. The truth and the proof that I helped bury it, in the same transmission, bound together forever."

The tower hummed around us. Ten thousand voices, waiting.

"Well," I said, after a while. "Sounds honest."

"Amara—"

"No. I mean it." I looked up into the light, at the small steady flicker that had been my mother's song. "You don't get to hand them a clean version. None of us do. If the price of the truth is that it drags your sin along with it, then pay it. My mother paid worse for less."

Petra nodded slowly. "The truce expires soon. Yuen's cutter is out there. Once I begin authentication, there's no pause button."

"Then we'd better decide," I said, "what you're going to say."

Above us, the tower of light turned slowly on its ancient axis, holding every voice the colony ever lost, and somewhere in its depths a woman who had been dead for fifteen years went on singing to the dark — patient, certain, still believing someone was listening.

Soon, Mama, I signed, too low for any of them to see. Soon.
