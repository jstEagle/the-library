## 8. What the Grammar Conceals

There is a particular kind of silence that happens when you've been staring at the same spectrogram for eleven hours and your eyes have started inventing things. I'd reached that silence somewhere around hour nine, when I became convinced that a smudge of interference near the 4.2 kilohertz band was shaped like my mother's handwriting.

I made tea. This is what linguists do instead of screaming.

The Tern's galley smelled like burnt coffee and Tam's soldering flux, which is apparently a flavor he considers foundational. Odile was at the table doing captain-things to a ledger, which mostly meant glaring at it until the numbers apologized. She glanced up when I came in.

"You look like a woman who's losing an argument with a dead language."

"I'm not losing," I said. "I'm being lied to. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"In linguistics? Constantly." I wrapped both hands around the mug because my fingers had developed a tremor I didn't want to name. "Odile, can I ask you something about salvage?"

"That's my favorite subject after money and before sleep."

"When you strip a wreck — say a ship that's been dead two hundred years — how do you know what's original and what's been repaired?"

She set down her pen. That was how I knew she was actually listening; Odile Mbeki did not interrupt pen-work for small questions. "Provenance. Materials tell on themselves. A patch welded on later has different heat signatures, different corrosion. The hull remembers every hand that touched it." She narrowed her eyes at me. "Why?"

"Because the signal is lying to me," I said, "and I think it's lying beautifully."

* * *

Here's what I had, as of that morning: a distress transmission in Ashkari, an extinct language, decoded into something like testimony. Fire. Denial. A colony called Meridian's End that did not exist according to any record anywhere. And a man sleeping three compartments down who spoke the same dead tongue in his dreams, whose memories had been scooped out by someone very tidy.

Here's what bothered me: it was too clean.

Translation work is never clean. Real speech is a mess of false starts, overlapping speakers, dialect drift, hesitation markers, people talking over each other because they're frightened or angry or human. Two hundred years of archival decay should have chewed this signal into lace. Instead I had a coherent narrative delivered in a single consistent voice, grammatically impeccable, emotionally legible. It read like a memoir written by someone who'd had time to edit.

Dead people don't edit. Dead people don't get time.

So I went back to the raw waveform, and I stopped listening to it like a message and started listening to it like a hull.

* * *

The galley had become my laboratory by necessity — the Tern's comm suite was built for haggling with port authorities, not forensic phonetics, so I'd cannibalized half of Tam's spare parts and one of his good amplifiers, for which he'd extracted payment in the form of my dessert rations for a week. Worth it. The amplifier hummed under my hands now like a patient animal.

Ashkari grammar, for those who don't collect dead languages the way other people collect regrets: it's agglutinative, heavily inflected, and — this is the part that matters — it marks evidentiality. Every verb carries a morpheme declaring *how you know what you're saying*. Direct witness. Hearsay. Inference. Dream. There are seven categories. It's a language built by people who were obsessed with the difference between what happened and what was claimed. You cannot lie casually in Ashkari. The grammar makes you confess your sources.

Which meant the signal's speaker had marked every statement with its epistemic source, and the sources were wrong.

Not wrong exactly. *Inconsistent.* The first passage — fire falling on the settlement domes — was marked direct witness. Fine. But forty seconds later, a description of the vote to secede carried the hearsay marker, then corrected itself mid-clause to witness, then back again, in a way no native speaker would stumble through. And the final section, the part about denial, the records being rewritten — that was marked with a category I'd only ever seen in ritual texts. The marker for knowledge held collectively. Knowledge that belongs to everyone or no one.

I ran the formant analysis four times. I checked my own implants' calibration against recordings I trusted. Then I did the thing I'd been avoiding, because if I was right it meant the night was going to be long: I stripped out the dominant voice entirely and looked at what was underneath.

The interference wasn't interference.

Underneath the primary speaker — layered at amplitudes low enough to hide beneath two centuries of noise floor, braided into the pauses like thread through cloth — there were other voices. Dozens. Maybe hundreds. Each one speaking Ashkari, each one carrying its own evidential markers, each one contributing fragments: a sentence here, half a sentence there, a name, a place, a timestamp in the old Ashkari calendar. They weren't background. They were a chorus, and the "single narrator" I'd decoded was actually a composite — a braid, woven from many throats, designed to be heard as one voice unless you knew to pull the threads apart.

I sat back. My tea had gone cold, which felt thematically appropriate.

"Oh," I said, to the empty galley, to the dead, to nobody. "Oh, you clever, terrified people."

* * *

Tam found me there at some ungodly hour, drawn either by the light or by concern for his amplifier. He stood in the doorway holding a wrench he had no use for, the way men hold tools when they want an excuse to stand somewhere.

"You've been talking to yourself for six hours," he said.

"I've been talking to several hundred people. They're just not answering individually."

He looked at the screens. Tam can't read a spectrogram, but he can read a person, and whatever he saw in my face made him drag a crate over and sit down without asking. That's the thing about the Tern's crew I was still learning: they don't ask permission to stay. They just decide you're worth staying for and then act like it was always obvious.

"Explain it like I'm an engineer," he said.

"So imagine someone builds a machine," I said, "and they know the machine will be inspected. Searched. Every component catalogued. So instead of hiding contraband inside it, they build the contraband *into the tolerances*. Into the gaps between parts. An inspector sees a functioning machine. Only someone who takes it completely apart finds the second machine living in the spaces."

"The signal's got a stowaway layer."

"A whole passenger manifest." I pulled up the decomposition. "The loudest voice is a constructed narrator — possibly synthesized, possibly one person reading a script composed from everyone else's words. But the real content, the actual testimony, is distributed across all these sub-voices. Names. Dates. Descriptions. Each fragment too small to trigger content filters, probably. Each one buried in what any automated system would classify as channel noise."

Tam whistled low. "They knew they'd be erased."

"They knew *how* they'd be erased." My throat went tight around the words. "That's the part that gets me. This isn't desperation. Desperation shouts. This is engineering. Someone sat down while their colony was dying and thought: they will rewrite the records within the hour, they will scrub the archives, they will kill us and then kill our names — so we need a message that survives all of that. We need a message that looks like nothing. We need to hide a massacre inside the grammar itself."

"And it worked," Tam said quietly. "Two hundred years, and here you are taking it apart."

"Here I am," I agreed. And then, because the thought arrived fully formed and cruel: "And here's Ilya, walking around with the rest of it locked in his skull. They hid it in him the same way. In the tolerances. In the spaces where no one would look."

Tam didn't say anything to that. He just stayed, which was its own kind of answer, and handed me the last of his ration bar, which in Tern currency is roughly equivalent to a marriage proposal.

* * *

I presented the findings to the crew the next cycle, because keeping it to myself had started to feel like hoarding, and also because I needed witnesses to my awe or I was going to start crying into the flux residue.

Ilya sat across from me with his hands folded, wearing the careful stillness he wore whenever the past came up — a man braced for a door to open onto fire. Odile had her ledger closed, which I'd learned was her version of full attention. Even Juno, the pilot, had abandoned the cockpit for once, curled in the corner seat with the wariness of a cat attending a meeting it distrusts.

"It's not one voice," I told them. "It never was. What I decoded initially — the coherent testimony, the narrator — that's a construction. A braid. Underneath are dozens of individual speakers, maybe more, each contributing pieces. The design is extraordinary. If the Bureau's filters had caught it, they'd have found a few seconds of anomalous audio, easily dismissed as corruption. To recover the whole thing you have to already suspect it exists and know Ashkari well enough to hear the seams. They built a lock where the key was the language itself — and then they let the language die, betting that someday someone would learn it again purely for love."

"Why bet that?" Juno asked. "Sounds like a bad wager."

"Because it's the only wager that can't be beaten," I said. "You can't torture a language back to life. You can't buy fluency in something extinct. The only people who could ever decode this are people who learned Ashkari for no reason except that losing it felt unbearable. They filtered for devotion." I looked at Ilya. "And for whatever it's worth — whoever built that braid used your kind of mind. The structure, the redundancy patterns, the way the fragments cross-reference each other. It's archivist work. It might even be your work."

His hands had gone white where they gripped each other. "I don't remember building it."

"No," I said gently. "But your body does. Watch." I played a ten-second clip of the braided signal, voices layered like sediment. Ilya's breathing changed before the clip ended — a hitch, a catch, the involuntary flinch of a man hearing music from a room he'd been told was empty. "Your nervous system recognizes it. Whatever they cut out of you, they couldn't cut the shape of it out of how you listen."

"They planned for everything," Ilya said. His voice was rough. "Except me surviving."

"Or including it," Odile said. Everyone turned. She shrugged, unapologetic. "Think like a salvager. You build a message that hides in noise, keyed to a dead language, waiting two centuries. Why bother, unless you believe someone will come looking? They didn't just plan to survive erasure. They planned to be *found*. By the right person. Eventually." Her eyes moved between Ilya and me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not suspicion, but the particular respect a scavenger gives a well-made lock. "The dead sent out a hook, and it caught the two of you. Question is whether that was luck or aim."

Nobody answered. The ventilation cycled. Somewhere below decks, Tam's machinery kept up its arrhythmic heartbeat.

* * *

I spent the next watch mapping the braid properly, and the deeper I went, the more the architecture revealed itself, and the more it undid me.

The fragments weren't random. They were ordered — not chronologically, but *evidentially*. All the direct-witness statements clustered in one stratum of the signal. The hearsay, the inference, the collective-knowledge markers each formed their own bands. Whoever composed the braid had organized the testimony by *epistemic type*, so that a future analyst could reconstruct not just what happened but the entire web of how the community knew it — who saw, who was told, what was inferred, what was held in common. It was a map of a society's knowing, compressed into noise.

And threaded through all of it, recurring at intervals like a refrain, was a single phrase. Short. Grammatically simple. Marked in the seventh evidential category — collective knowledge — and repeated so many times it functioned as a spine for the whole structure.

I translated it sitting alone in the galley at the end of the watch, and my hands were steady right up until they weren't.

*We were here. We chose. Remember the choosing.*

Not *remember us*. Not *avenge us*. Remember the *choosing* — the vote, the decision to secede, the act of self-determination that apparently cost two thousand lives. Of everything they could have made their refrain, they chose the moment they exercised agency. As if the crime wasn't the killing. As if the crime was the choice, and therefore the choice was what had to survive.

I put my head down on the table, briefly, among the cold tea and the flux stains, and let myself grieve for people who had died two hundred years ago and loved their own freedom enough to encrypt it.

Then I straightened up, because there was one more thing. The refrain wasn't just structural. When I mapped its repetitions against the signal's timing, they fell at precise intervals — intervals that matched, near enough to make my skin prickle, the carrier rhythm of Beacon Kestrel-9 itself. My beacon. The forgotten transmitter I'd tended for six years, the one nobody ordered, broadcasting its idle maintenance pulse into the dark.

The braid was tuned to it. Or rather — the braid was *addressed* to anything that would eventually hear it through a beacon like mine. The dead hadn't just hidden their testament. They'd aimed it at the neglected infrastructure of the sector, at the lonely stations and unmaintained relays, the places where oversight thinned out. They'd addressed their message to the forgotten corners, betting that forgetting cuts both ways — that what the Bureau neglects, it also fails to watch.

They had planned ahead. Across two centuries, across their own extinction, they had planned ahead — and the plan had a shape, and the shape had a destination, and the destination was not a person.

It was a network.

I pulled up the Tern's chart of Sector Nine's beacon lattice, all the Kestrel-class transmitters scattered along the shipping lanes like unlit candles, and overlaid the signal's origin vector, and the pattern that emerged was so clean it looked less like archaeology than like instructions.

Someone had built a distribution system for the truth, and left it dormant, waiting for a key.

Waiting, I realized, with a cold spreading delight in my chest, for someone to switch it on.
