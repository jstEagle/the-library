## 13. Sanctuary and Strife

There is a particular kind of silence that comes from a ship graveyard, and I have spent enough of my life in them to know it the way other people know their mothers' voices. It isn't quiet. Quiet is just an absence of noise. This is a presence — the accumulated hush of ten thousand dead hulls drifting in slow orbits around a collapsed moonlet, all of them still holding whatever air they held when their crews stopped breathing, all of them creaking on thermal expansion as the distant sun swings behind a wreck and then out again. The graveyard talks to itself. It just doesn't talk to you.

I had parked the *Tern* in the ribs of a decommissioned ore-hauler called, according to the faded registry plate Tam pried off its flank, the *Patient Mercy*. Someone with a sense of humor had named her that two hundred years ago, and now she was hosting a fugitive linguist, a man who was legally a rounding error, and a crew of four idiots who had decided — without consulting me, their captain, the person whose name was on exactly none of the paperwork because salvage captains don't believe in paperwork — to hide here while the Bureau of Harmonious Records combed Sector Nine looking for us.

I sat in my cabin with the log open and did not write in it for a long time.

Here is what nobody tells you about being captain of a small ship: you are not paid to make decisions. You are paid to be the person the decisions happen *to*, publicly, so that everyone else can have their crisis in private. When Tam came to me three days after we'd run dark out of Corvane Station and said, "Odile, we need to talk about the math," I knew before he opened his mouth that the math would be about Ilya Voss, and that I would be expected to solve it with my face.

"Sit down," I said.

"I'm standing."

"You'll want to sit down."

He sat down. That's the thing about Tam Osei. He argues with everything — with gravity, with the fuel gauge, with the fundamental architecture of the drive we both know he loves more than either of his ex-wives — but when I tell him he'll want to sit, he sits. Twenty-two years of flying together buys you that much.

"The Bureau's bounty," he said, "is four hundred thousand credits. Untraceable. No questions about provenance." He turned his slate toward me. "That's eleven years of fuel, six years of parts, and a new spine for the *Tern*'s port strut, which, in case you've forgotten, sounds like a dying whale every time we come out of burn."

"I haven't forgotten the whale."

"And that's just the money," he went on, because Tam has never once in his life stopped at the money. "There's also the fact that they found us at Corvane within thirty-six hours. Somebody talked or somebody will talk. There's also the fact that Rhee — this inspector, Cadence Rhee — she offered Odile a clean deal at the dock, hand over the anomalies, keep your ship, and Odile told her we'd think about it, which means the Bureau thinks we're thinking about it, which means they're patient, which means every day we sit in this scrapyard is a day they get closer."

"We're hiding in a graveyard, Tam. They'd have to be very close."

"They were close at Corvane."

He had me there. I looked at the slate. Four hundred thousand. I made myself look at it properly, the way you make yourself look at a wound, because if you flinch away from numbers they win.

"What are you actually asking me?" I said.

"I'm asking whether we've decided anything," he said. "Because I've been listening to this crew for nine days, and nobody has said the sentence out loud. Everybody's circling it. Juno keeps fixing things that aren't broken. Freya keeps translating like translation is going to save anybody. And Voss—" he shook his head. "Voss walks the corridors at night like he's checking on us. Like we're the ones who lost something. And nobody says the sentence."

"Which sentence?"

"*We could sell him.*"

The word *sell* sat in the cabin between us. Tam didn't look away from it and neither did I, and I will tell you honestly that some part of me — the part that has eaten noodles for a month because the protein printer died and the part that repairs cost more than the ship is worth — that part of me heard the number again and felt it land somewhere soft.

"That's not the only sentence," I said finally. "There's another one. *We could give him up and not even take the money.* Hand him over, walk away clean, no bounty, no Bureau file on the *Tern*. Some people would call that the responsible option."

"Some people," Tam said, "have never met Ilya Voss."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Ilya Voss at that point, nine days into our exile among the dead ships:

I knew that he ate last. Every meal, without fail, he waited until everyone else had taken theirs, and if you asked him why, he said "habit" in a voice that suggested the habit had been installed by someone with a whip. I knew that he slept four hours a night and spent the rest walking the ship, touching bulkheads, learning the *Tern* the way a man learns a language he expects to need. I knew that Freya had taught him maybe forty words of written Ashkari by then, working from her dream-fluency and his fragments, and that watching them work was like watching two people build a bridge from opposite banks of a river, calling measurements across the water.

And I knew — this is the one that got under my skin — I knew that he had started doing maintenance. Nobody asked him to. Tam certainly never authorized it. But I'd come around a corner on day six and found him elbow-deep in a coolant junction, sleeves rolled, following Tam's shouted instructions with the focused competence of a man remembering how to be useful. When I raised an eyebrow at Tam later, Tam shrugged and said, "He asked what needed doing. What was I supposed to say? *Nothing, you're cargo?*"

So that was the problem. You cannot sell a person who has fixed your coolant junction. It's not a moral prohibition; it's something more basic than morality. It's arithmetic. A person who fixes your ship stops being inventory and starts being crew, and there is no exchange rate between those categories. The Bureau's ledger didn't have a line for it. Neither, I'm ashamed to say, did mine, until that moment in the corridor, watching a man with surgically murdered memories re-torque a pipe fitting because it needed doing and he was there.

* * *

I called the meeting for second shift, in the mess, because the mess is where the *Tern* does its real governing. My cabin is for logs and self-pity. The bridge is for flying. The mess — eight seats bolted around a table Juno had salvaged from a luxury liner and never sanded properly, so that it caught your sleeve at exactly the wrong spot — the mess is where we decide who we are.

Everyone came. Freya Eastwick arrived with her slate and sat down like she was attending a lecture she'd been dreading. Ilya Voss stood by the galley hatch until I told him to sit, and even then he sat on the edge of the seat, a man perpetually ready to be dismissed. Juno Reyes, my pilot, my conscience, the youngest of us and the oldest soul aboard, came in last and closed the door, which she only does when she thinks what follows matters.

"All right," I said. "Sentence time. We're going to say the sentences out loud, all of them, and then we're going to vote, and then whatever we vote, we live with it, and nobody gets to resurrect this conversation at three in the morning for the rest of their natural lives. Agreed?"

Nods. Even Voss nodded, though I noticed his hands were flat on the table, pressing down, as if the table might lift off otherwise.

"Sentence one," I said. "We sell him. Four hundred thousand credits, untraceable, the *Tern* gets rebuilt, we all retire comfortable, and a man who has done nothing to us except fix our plumbing goes back to the people who cut his memory out with a knife. Who says it?"

Silence. Tam stared at his own hands. I hadn't expected anyone to say it. I'd needed it said, though — needed it hanging in the air where it could be seen, because a decision made around an unspoken option isn't a decision, it's a drift.

"Sentence two," I said. "We give him up without taking payment. Clean hands, clean file, Bureau leaves us alone. Who wants it?"

Freya spoke first, and her voice was quieter than I'd ever heard it. "You should understand what you'd be giving up," she said. Not to me — to the table, to all of us. She set her slate down and turned it around. On it was the braided testimony, the signal from Beacon Kestrel-9, the thing that had started all of this. "Two thousand people. Meridian's End. They voted to secede — a lawful vote, a documented vote — and within the hour the Bureau erased the colony, the vote, and the people. All of it. And they built this—" she touched Voss's shoulder, lightly, the way you touch a scar you didn't cause, "—they built a man to carry the record and forget he was carrying it. The testimony survived because the dead braided it into grammar that erasure couldn't reach. They planned for this. Two hundred years ago, they planned for someone to find it." She looked around the table. "If you give him up, the testimony dies with the last copy. They planned for that too."

"It's not our fight," Tam said. He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Freya, I like you. I like him. That's the whole problem. But two centuries dead is two centuries dead, and we're alive, and alive people have fuel bills."

"Yes," Freya said. "That's true. I won't pretend it isn't. I'll even say the rest of it: if you put me and Ilya off at the next station, I'll understand. I'll hate it, but I'll understand. You took us aboard when you didn't have to. Nobody owes a stranger a war."

And that — that was the moment, though I didn't know it yet. Because she offered us the exit. She stood in the doorway of the exit and held it open and said *go*, and the strange thing about a door like that, honestly offered, is that once it's open you can see through it clearly, and what I saw through it was a version of myself that had handed over a man who fixed coolant junctions and eaten last at every meal, and I could not live in that version. I could see it perfectly and it did not fit.

Juno broke the silence. She was seventeen when I took her on, running numbers for a smuggler who beat her, and in six years she has never once volunteered an opinion in a meeting unless she was certain. She was certain now.

"When I was fourteen," she said, "somebody sold me. Not figuratively. A man I trusted traded me to settle a debt, and the people who bought me were careful, and polite, and they explained that it was nothing personal, it was just economics." She looked at Tam. "It was personal. It's always personal. The people who do the selling always call it economics, and the people who get sold always remember it as the day the world showed them what they were worth." She turned her slate face-down on the table, a small deliberate gesture, closing her accounts. "I'm not doing that to anyone. Not for four hundred thousand. Not for four million. Vote however you want. That's mine."

Nobody spoke for a while. In the graveyard outside, the *Patient Mercy* groaned as the sun came out from behind a wreck, and the sound came through the hull like the ship clearing her throat.

Then Ilya Voss stood up.

"I want to say something," he said, "and then I want you to ignore it completely, and I want you to know that both of those things are sincere." His Ashkari accent had mostly worn off his Standard by then, but it surfaced when he was tired or frightened, and it surfaced now. "I have been property before. I don't remember most of it — they made sure of that — but my body remembers, and my body is telling me right now that this room is the room where it happens again. So I understand the math. Believe me, I understand the math better than anyone at this table." He looked at each of us in turn. "But I need you to know one thing. If you sell me, I will go quietly. I will not blame you. I will walk to their ship and I will thank you for the meals. And I will spend whatever time they leave me trying to finish memorizing the testimony, because Freya taught me enough Ashkari now that I can hold pieces of it, and if they erase me again, the pieces die with me — but while I am me, I will carry it. That's what I was built for. It's the only thing left that's mine." He sat back down. "Now ignore all of that, like I asked."

"Fat chance," Tam muttered.

I called the vote. Captain's privilege: I voted last, out loud, because I wanted them to hear it.

Tam voted to let Freya and Voss off at a neutral station — no sale, no bounty, just separation. "Middle ground," he said, and had the grace to look ashamed when Juno looked at him. Freya abstained; she refused to vote on her own fate, and I let her. Juno voted no — no sale, no surrender, no separation. *No* meaning: we keep them, we keep the testimony, we see it through.

Voss tried to abstain too. I wouldn't allow it. "You're crew," I said, and watched the word hit him like a physical blow, watched him grip the edge of the table. "Crew votes."

"I vote no," he said, barely audible. "Whatever *no* means. No."

Then it was me. Nine days earlier I'd told Inspector Rhee I'd think about her offer, and I had thought about it, thoroughly, at three in the morning more than once, and here is what the thinking produced: the Bureau pays four hundred thousand for a man because a man is cheaper than a truth. That's the entire business model. Erasure isn't ideology; it's accounting. And I have spent twenty-two years in salvage, pulling value out of wrecks that powerful people wrote off and wanted forgotten, and I have never once — not once — taken a wreck at the insurance company's valuation. I wasn't going to start with a human being.

"No," I said. "All of it. No sale, no surrender, no separation. We carry the testimony, and we see it delivered, and if the Bureau wants to discuss it further they can take it up with the *Tern*'s opinion on the matter, which is currently expressed in four mass drivers and a very stubborn attitude."

Tam laughed — a short, defeated, entirely genuine laugh. "You're going to get us killed, Captain."

"Probably," I agreed. "But richly. Richly and honestly. There are worse deaths. I've seen several."

He held out a moment longer, and then he did the thing that made me love that infuriating man: he reached across the table and turned Freya's slate face-up again, the testimony glowing on it, and said, "Fine. But I want it noted that the port strut still sounds like a whale, and if we die, my ghost is going to haunt this ship complaining about the port strut."

"Noted for the record," I said.

* * *

Afterward, the mess emptied slowly, the way rooms empty after a decision that cost something. Juno went forward to plot us a deeper course into the graveyard. Tam went aft, already muttering about the strut. And Freya stayed behind with Voss, and I stayed behind too, pretending to log the meeting, listening.

"Do you understand what they just did?" Freya asked him softly.

"They chose," he said. "For me. Again." There was no bitterness in it — only exhaustion, and something underneath the exhaustion that might have been wonder.

"No," Freya said. "They chose *with* you. There's a difference. It's the whole difference. Two hundred years ago, someone decided for you what you would carry and what you'd lose. Tonight, five people in a leaking mess hall looked at the price of carrying it and picked it up anyway — together, out loud, by vote. That's the thing the Bureau can't erase, Ilya. Not the testimony. *This.* People choosing. It's the one record they've never figured out how to rewrite."

I kept my eyes on the log and wrote: *Day nine. Crew voted unanimously to continue harboring anomalous assets. Morale: complicated. Fuel: worse.*

And that was when Juno's voice came over the intercom, tight and fast, the voice she only uses when the graveyard itself has changed shape around us.

"Captain. Get up here. Long-range just painted a Bureau survey pattern at the graveyard's edge — three ships, grid search, closing. And Odile—" a pause, the kind of pause that costs a pilot something, "—the lead ship is broadcasting an open channel. They're asking for the *Tern* by name. Inspector Rhee says she wants to talk. She says she's coming in alone."
