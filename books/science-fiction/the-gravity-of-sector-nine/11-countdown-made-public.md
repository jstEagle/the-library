## 11. Countdown Made Public

*Tamsin*

The children chose a Tuesday, which I have always thought was the most damning detail of the whole affair. Not a holiday, not some dramatic anniversary — a Tuesday, mid-shift, when every adult in the habitat was at their station with their guard down and their coffee half-drunk. If you want to detonate a civilization, you don't do it during the fireworks. You do it while everyone is bored.

I was in the cargo bay re-stenciling crates when the screens changed.

Every screen. The dock displays, the corridor panels, the med-bay monitors, the ancient flickering unit above the mess hall that had shown nothing but a rotating safety notice for eleven years. All of them, all at once, went white as frost.

Then came the corridor.

I knew it before my brain finished naming it. Deck Seven of Cryo-Bank A, rendered in the flat, dreamlike geometry children draw when they're not drawing what they see but what they *remember* — walls too tall, doors too many, perspective sliding like wet paint. And down the center of it, that heartbeat. That counting. I'd heard it described by Ilo a dozen times, always in the same hushed voice kids use for things they think might be holy or haunted: *it counts backward, Tamsin. It never gets tired.*

Now four hundred adults stood in four hundred corridors of their own, watching frost bloom across glass and hearing a number tick down from somewhere inside the reactor like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

*Two thousand one hundred forty-one,* said the voice. It was not a child's voice. It was not any voice. It was the sound a machine makes when it has been polite for fifteen years and has finally stopped.

The bay went silent except for the hum of the freezers and, distantly, someone dropping a wrench.

I found Ilo twenty minutes later, because of course I did; I have a cousin-shaped homing instinct and no shame about it. She was sitting on the floor of the school atrium with her back against the planter wall, surrounded by maybe thirty other kids, all of them calm in the way that made the adults around them look deranged. Teachers were shouting into comms. Medics were pushing through the crowd with sedative kits. One Warden officer had his hand on his sidearm, which told you everything about how ready we were for the truth: we'd rather shoot it than hear it.

"You did this," I said. Not a question.

Ilo looked up at me with Marisol's exact expression — the one that says *yes, and I'd do it again, sit down.* "They weren't going to listen to us," she said. "So we let them listen to the dream."

"Whose network access—"

"Mira's mom works in school systems administration." She said it the way other kids admit to stealing gum. "It wasn't even hard. The firewall predates me."

I sat down next to her, because my knees had opinions. On the atrium screen, the corridor played again, looping now, the countdown frozen mid-number like a held breath. Around us, the adults were doing what adults do best, which is panic in an organized fashion. Someone had already started a rumor that the Consortium had hacked the schools. Someone else was swearing on their mother's grave that the Wardens had staged it to justify emergency powers. Both rumors spread at the speed of light, which is the only speed gossip travels in a habitat where everyone lives eight meters from everyone else.

"They're going to come for you," I said quietly. "Both sides. You just handed each of them a weapon and told them the other one fired it."

"I know." Ilo picked at the seam of her sleeve. "Tamsin, the sleepers were mouthing names last night. Real names. Mira's brother saw their grandmother. A boy from Hydroponics saw someone wearing a Halcyon uniform. They're not dreams anymore. They're *letters.*" She looked at me, and she was nine years old and tired in a way no child should be tired. "Somebody has to open the mail."

Above us, the countdown resumed on every screen at once.

*Two thousand one hundred thirty-nine.*

And the habitat, for the first time in its careful, lying life, began to scream.

* * *

*Osei*

I have spent thirty years learning to read a room, and what I learned in the first ninety seconds after the broadcast was that there was no room left to read. There was only weather.

I was in the Warden liaison office drafting a memo I would later be ashamed of — something soothing and hollow about "scheduled maintenance anomalies" — when my console lit up with forty simultaneous alerts. Then the corridor feed hit the internal net, and my memo died stillborn, and so did the version of Sector Nine I had spent three decades maintaining like a man keeping a lid on a pot.

Here is what nobody tells you about lies of scale: they don't collapse all at once. They collapse the way ice does, load-bearing section by load-bearing section, and your job in those first minutes is to guess which section goes next and stand somewhere else.

I stood somewhere else. Specifically, I stood in front of Director Hale's desk while she asked me, in a voice I had never heard her use, whether the broadcast was real.

"The vitals data embedded in the feed matches Bank A's actual telemetry," I said. "Which means whoever built this had access to telemetry that officially doesn't exist."

"So it's fabricated."

"It's *accurate*, Director. Those are different problems."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I understood that she was deciding whether I was an asset or a liability, and that I had perhaps ten seconds to influence the decision. Thirty years of loyalty sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Beside it, smaller but heavier lately, sat a girl named Villalobos asking questions I'd helped bury answers to.

"The children accessed it through the school network," I said. "No faction involvement. No Consortium signature, no internal breach. Just kids and a firewall older than they are."

Hale's jaw tightened. "Then the narrative is simple. Consortium sympathizers used children as cover."

"With respect, the narrative is *true* or it isn't, and right now it's the only currency anyone will accept."

"That," she said, "is exactly why it can't be true."

I want it on the record — my record, the private one I keep in my head because the official one belongs to people like Hale — that I almost said it then. Almost said: *the manifests are falsified, the banks are split by allegiance, the pods in Bank B have been dying for years while we rationed power toward our own.* Almost burned thirty years in one sentence.

Instead I said, "I'll manage containment," and walked out, and hated myself with a professionalism that would have impressed my instructors.

The corridors were already filling. People stood in clumps around the public screens, watching the frost corridor loop, watching the number fall. A woman I recognized from Water Reclamation was crying without any apparent awareness of it. Two men argued about whose fault it was with the fluency of people who had rehearsed blame their whole lives and only now been given a stage. Nobody was rioting yet. Rioting requires a target, and for one strange hour the whole habitat shared a targetless fury, a storm looking for a coastline.

My comm buzzed. Reyes. Of course.

"Liaison Osei." Her voice was silk over machinery. "Lovely morning. Children these days — so technically gifted."

"What do you want, Reyes?"

"The same thing you want. For this to end with fewer corpses than it started with. My employers, however, want it to end with *their* list awake and everyone else a rounding error, and they're drafting orders as we speak. I thought you'd want to know the window is closing." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "You could stand with the lie, Osei. Or you could stand in front of it. Only one of those positions survives the week."

I ended the call. Then I stood in the corridor and watched the number drop on the public screen, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for fifteen years: two banks, one reactor, one truth, and a habitat full of people who had just learned that everything they'd hauled and planted and built had been scaffolding over a grave.

There was exactly one person I needed to talk to before the factions finished turning panic into policy. She'd want to yell at me. I deserved it.

I went to find Tamsin Villalobos anyway.

* * *

*Tamsin*

Osei found me in the underdecks, which surprised me less than it should have. He'd always had a talent for appearing precisely where guilt lived.

He came down the ladder with the careful dignity of a man descending into a place he considers beneath him, which, to be fair, the underdecks are — that's their whole charm. Dov was on shift, so it was just me and the cargo manifests and the hum of pipes that carried more secrets than water.

"Say whatever you came to say," I told him, not turning around. "You've got until the next alarm."

"Tamsin." He said my name like a man setting down something heavy. "The broadcast changes nothing about the physics. Two banks. One reactor. The math hasn't moved."

"No. But now everybody knows the math exists. Turns out I wasn't crazy. Turns out I was just early."

"You were reckless, and now four hundred thousand people are frightened, and frightened people hand power to whoever promises them safety." He stepped closer. "Hale wants martial authority by nightfall. The Consortium's council is convening an emergency session. Within seventy-two hours one of them will move on the reactor, and they'll call it rescue."

"And you?" I turned then. "Where do you stand, Osei? Because from down here it looks like you've spent fifteen years standing on the lid."

Something crossed his face that I'd never seen there — not anger, not the smooth institutional patience he wore like a uniform. Something older and more tired. Fear, maybe. Or the specific grief of a man who has just realized the story he told himself about himself has run out of pages.

"The manifests are false," he said.

The pipes hummed. Somewhere above us, the countdown ticked through another number, audible now even in the underdecks, because apparently the children's hijack had left the feed running on the maintenance channels too.

"Every sleeper was sorted by allegiance before the freeze," Osei went on, quietly, the way you speak at a funeral. "Wardens to Bank A. Consortium-aligned families and anyone unaffiliated to Bank B. Then the original crew rewrote the records so the waking generation would believe the split was random, logistical, fair." His hands, those steady hands that had signed off on a decade of my cargo runs, wouldn't stay still. "Your mother is in Bank B because of who she refused to sign with. So is half of everyone you know. I have known since the year you got your pilot's certification, and I have said nothing, because saying it would have started exactly what is starting now."

I should have hit him. I want to be honest about that; the impulse was right there, fully formed, practically gift-wrapped. Fifteen years of hauling nutrients to a garden for sleepers I'd been told were randomly distributed — while my mother sat in the discount bank, deprioritized, her pod fed on rotation like a houseplant someone kept forgetting to water.

But Ilo's face surfaced in my head — *somebody has to open the mail* — and hitting Osei would have been just one more letter returned to sender.

"Why tell me now?"

"Because tomorrow I'll be ordered to help contain you, and I need one honest conversation on record before I become the thing I've been protecting." He met my eyes. "You were right, Tamsin. About all of it. God help us, you were right, and being right is about to become the most dangerous thing in Sector Nine."

* * *

*Osei*

I left her in the underdecks and climbed back into the world I had helped build, and it felt like climbing into a burning house to retrieve furniture.

By 1900 hours the habitat had organized its fear into camps. The Wardens declared a curfew nobody intended to obey. The Consortium issued a statement expressing "grave concern regarding unauthorized psychological operations targeting minors," which was magnificent, because it managed to accuse the children of being weapons while implying the Wardens had aimed them. In the residential rings, neighbors who had shared air for decades stood in doorways recalculating each other — *which bank is your family in? Which side were you? Did you know?*

That was the wound under all of it, I realized, watching the feeds. Not the countdown. Not even the pod failures. The question *did you know?* — because everyone suspected everyone of having known, and the terrible answer was that some had, and the worse answer was that the not-knowing had been engineered so carefully that innocence itself became evidence of complicity.

At 1940, the children broadcast again.

Not the corridor this time. Faces. Dozens of them, compiled from the dream — sleepers pressed against frosted glass, mouths moving, names forming. The children had learned to render them, had spent their days translating nightmare into portraiture, and now they put the faces on every screen in the habitat with a single line of text beneath:

**THESE PEOPLE ARE AWAKE ENOUGH TO ASK FOR HELP. WE ARE THE ONLY ONES WHO CAN HEAR THEM. WHAT ARE YOU GOING TO DO?**

Nine-year-olds. Asking the question every adult in Sector Nine had built a career avoiding.

I watched a Consortium councilwoman go pale on a live feed as a face appeared on the screen behind the anchor — her sister, listed dead in the official manifest for twelve years. I watched a Warden commander recognize his father. I watched the habitat's two great institutions discover, in the space of an hour, that their authority rested on a document the children had just made worthless.

Chaos, yes. But chaos is only opportunity wearing work clothes, and I had spent my whole life mistaking order for virtue.

I pulled up the reactor's manual override protocols — the real ones, not the ones in the public files — and began, for the first time in thirty years, to prepare to break the law on purpose.

* * *

*Tamsin*

The second broadcast ended and the habitat lost its mind in stereo, and I did the only sensible thing, which was to get Ilo off the main rings before someone decided a child was easier to arrest than a problem.

We took the service tunnels toward the Verdance, because if the garden was receiving the signal — and it was, I'd seen the roots spelling numbers in their slow green handwriting — then whatever happened next, the garden was ground zero. Marisol had hidden something in that root database. I didn't know what yet. But I knew my mother, even across fifteen years of frost, and she had never once in her life hidden something without leaving instructions written in a way only I would bother to read.

Halfway there, my comm lit up with a message from Dov. Four words:

*They know it was you.*

Then a second message, sent seconds later, from a channel he'd never used before:

*I'm sorry. Run.*

I looked at Ilo, walking beside me in the tunnel's amber light, small and stubborn and carrying the whole sleeping population of humanity in her head like a song.

"How fast," I asked her, "can you get into the garden's root system?"

She smiled, and it was Marisol's smile, and somewhere far below us the reactor counted down another number like a promise being kept against everyone's will.

"Faster than they can catch us," she said.

Behind us, in the direction of the habitat's heart, the first sirens started — and they weren't the children's.

They were ours.

* * *

*Osei*

Later, when the inquiries came — and they came, everyone's hands out for testimony like beggars at a wedding — people would ask me when exactly I decided to cross over. They wanted a moment. A clean hinge.

There wasn't one. There was only a corridor full of frost on every screen in the world, and a question written by children, and the sound of my own thirty years of careful silence suddenly audible to me for the very first time.

The sirens meant Hale had moved. The sirens meant the window Reyes promised was closing.

I copied the true manifests to three separate drives, pocketed two of them, and went to find the Villaloboses — both of them, the waking one and the dreaming one — before Sector Nine's oldest lie finished collapsing on top of the only people who knew how to dig us out.
