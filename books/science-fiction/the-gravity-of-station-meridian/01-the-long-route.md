## 1. The Long Route

The freighter *Charitable Intent* had been old when Vesper Hartwell was young, which made it roughly the same age as everything else Vesper trusted.

It groaned through the long dark between Halcyon Reach and the Meridian corridor like an arthritic pilgrim, all prayer and complaint and no speed to speak of. Its corridors smelled of recycled air that had been recycled so many times it had achieved a kind of philosophical exhaustion. In the mess hall, a coffee machine dispensed something brown and sincere. Nobody drank it twice.

Vesper had claimed the corner table by the viewport three weeks ago and defended it with the quiet territorial ferocity of a cat. From there they could watch the trade lanes scroll past — or rather, watch nothing scroll past, because the lanes were silent now, had been silent for years, and there was very little to see in the way of traffic. An occasional ore hauler. A survey drone with a limping signal. Once, memorably, a funeral barge from Thessaly Nine, running dark except for its lanterns, carrying somebody important enough to be mourned slowly.

Vesper watched it go and thought, as always: *somebody's claim.*

Twenty years as an insurance investigator for Consolidated Risk & Reassurance taught you to see the universe as paperwork wearing a costume. Stars were actuarial tables. Comets were liability exposure. And the silent routes — the beautiful, empty, unprofitable silence between the colony worlds — were simply claims waiting to be filed.

Vesper was good at this. It was the one thing about themselves Vesper had never doubted.

* * *

The claim on the table tonight was file CR-4471-Kestrel, and it was, by any professional standard, impossible.

The Kestrel relay had served the outer loop of the Halcyon Reach for sixty-one years without incident. Then, on the fourth of March by station-standard calendar, it had gone dark. Not failed — *gone dark*, which is a different thing entirely. Failed relays die loudly: telemetry screaming, backup systems squawking, a long electronic death rattle that any monitoring station could hear from three systems away. The Kestrel relay had simply stopped, cleanly, completely, the way a sentence stops when someone puts a hand over your mouth.

And then — here was the part Vesper liked, the part that kept the job interesting — it had come back on eleven days later, fully functional, logs intact, reporting nothing wrong at all. As if it had never stopped. As if the eleven days had happened to everyone else but not to it.

"Impossible accident," said the file, in the dry voice of the classification system. Category Seven. Payout authorized pending investigator sign-off.

Vesper read the telemetry for the eleventh time, pen tapping against their lower lip. The relay's own internal clock showed no gap. The maintenance drones reported no gap. The power draw curves were smooth as poured cream across the entire missing fortnight. Somewhere out there was a machine insisting, with total sincerity, that eleven days had not occurred.

Somebody had to pay for that insistence. That was, more or less, Vesper's entire philosophy of employment.

They signed off the claim with a flourish that had worn down over two decades into something more like a tired dash, and set the tablet face-down on the table, and looked out the viewport at the nothing.

"You'll ruin your eyes," said the steward, refilling Vesper's cup with the brown sincerity. Her name was Amara; she'd worked the *Intent* for thirty years and treated every passenger like weather — tolerated, occasionally remarked upon, never taken personally.

"My eyes are insured," Vesper said.

"With us? Ha." She settled into the opposite seat without asking, which was her privilege and one of the few human comforts aboard. "You've been staring at that screen for three weeks like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money. Indirectly. Through a chain of corporate entities I'm contractually forbidden to describe."

Amara laughed the way she did everything, thoroughly and without apology. "You're a strange one, Hartwell. Twenty years riding these dead lanes, closing files nobody reads. Doesn't it get lonely?"

The question landed the way such questions always did — lightly, harmlessly, and directly into the soft place Vesper kept hidden under layers of procedure. They considered several deflections. The witty one. The technical one. The one where they explained actuarial risk pooling until she went away.

"I don't really think about it," Vesper said, which was true, in the sense that a person who never opens a door can honestly say nothing ever comes through it.

"Mm," said Amara, who had raised four children and could hear a locked door from across a room. She patted the table once, stood, and left Vesper alone with the coffee and the stars.

That was the arrangement, anyway. That was the whole architecture of Vesper's life, load-bearing and invisible: work hard, ask precisely the questions the forms required, and never once — not in twenty years, not since the beginning, whatever the beginning had been — ask why the routes went silent. Why the great shipping arteries between the colonies had thinned to capillaries. Why whole sectors that appeared on every chart as *thriving* appeared out every viewport as *empty*. The company didn't ask. The colonists didn't complain, or if they complained, the complaints arrived pre-redacted. And Vesper Hartwell, who could smell a falsified timestamp through three layers of encryption, had somehow never noticed the largest falsification of all, spread across half the sky.

There was a version of Vesper, in some other life, who had asked that question. Vesper sometimes felt them out there, faintly, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue. Then they'd open another file, and the feeling would pass.

* * *

Three days out from Meridian, the *Charitable Intent*'s comms array caught a fragment of children's chatter on an open channel — some school broadcast leaking past its encryption, the way small things leak everywhere. Vesper almost skipped past it. Kids' voices carried nothing actuarial.

But one phrase snagged, and Vesper replayed it.

*"—and then the door was locked, and we were all inside, and the name got said and then unsaid—"*

A teacher's voice cut in, soothing, embarrassed: *"That's just the fever dream, loves, turn your tablets to page forty—"*

Vesper sat very still for a moment.

Then they laughed at themselves, quietly, because it was nothing. Children invented things. Children dreamed strange dreams and stitched them together in the schoolyard the way they always had, on every world, since the first colony ship. It meant nothing. It was not a pattern. It was certainly not, say, structurally identical to the recurring dream-fragments documented in the annexes of six separate Category Seven files across four different systems — fire, a locked door, a name spoken and then somehow *withdrawn*, retracted like a statement from a witness.

Vesper knew those annexes existed because Vesper had filed three of them. Had noted the coincidence each time in the appropriate field. Had written, in the appropriate field, the appropriate words: *no evidentiary significance.*

No evidentiary significance. Twenty years of that phrase, laid down like ballast, keeping the ship of Vesper's attention upright and pointed safely away from anything that mattered.

The children's broadcast hissed into static. Out the viewport, the nothing continued being nothing, vast and patient and full of things nobody counted anymore.

* * *

On the fifth day, the *Intent* made its final burn toward Station Meridian, and Vesper packed their kit with the ritual economy of long practice: slate, stylus, seal-stamp, spare seal-stamp (the first always got lost), one good coat, one flask of tea that Amara had pressed on them at the airlock along with a look that said *eat something green while you're out there.*

Meridian came up on the displays looking exactly like its file photographs, which should have been reassuring and instead produced a small, cold tick of unease that Vesper couldn't source. It was a handsome station — a wheel-and-spindle design from the founding era, ringed with solar sails like a moth mid-molt. The file said *population 11,000, stable.* The file said *founded 61 years ago, charter intact.* The file said the relay Vesper had come to assess was older than the colony itself, which was odd, when you thought about it — a listening post built before there was anyone to listen — but the file said it, and the file was signed, and the signature was notarized, and that was how the universe stayed closed.

The docking clamps took hold with a shudder Vesper felt in their teeth.

"Meridian Control to *Charitable Intent*, you are cleared for berth nine. Welcome back, Investigator Hartwell."

Vesper paused, stylus halfway into its sleeve.

*Welcome back.*

Standard courtesy, surely. Controllers said it to everyone; it was in the hospitality manuals; Vesper had literally helped draft the language standards for port greetings after the Thessaly incident. There was no reason at all for the small hairs on their arms to stand up. No reason to replay the transmission twice. No reason to notice that the controller's voice had hesitated — barely, a quarter-second, the length of a swallowed word — before saying *back.*

"Control, this is my first visit to Meridian," Vester said, and heard their own voice come out lighter than intended, the way voices do when they're carrying something heavier than they look.

A pause. Longer than a quarter-second this time. Long enough that Vesper could hear the station breathing behind it — fans, pumps, the great slow lungs of a habitat holding ten thousand lives aloft in the dark.

"Apologies, Investigator," the controller said at last, warm as fresh bread. "Transcription error in our visitor log. Berth nine is yours. Enjoy your stay."

The channel closed. The *Intent* settled onto its cradle with a groan of relief. And Vesper Hartwell stood in the airlock vestibule with their kit bag over one shoulder, looking out through the glass at the wheel-and-spindle station turning slowly against the stars, and felt — distinctly, unmistakably, for the first time in twenty years of not feeling it —

— like coming home.

Which was ridiculous. Which was impossible. Which was, Vesper told themselves firmly, pulling up the claim file to drown the sensation in procedure, *no evidentiary significance whatsoever.*

The file opened. The relay's founding certificate filled the screen, dated sixty-two years ago, authorizing construction before the colony charter that made the relay necessary.

And at the bottom, in the signature field, in handwriting Vesper knew better than their own heartbeat — because it was their own handwriting, neat and slanted and utterly, impossibly theirs —

*V. Hartwell, Field Investigation, dated fifty-one years before Vesper Hartwell was born.*

The airlock cycled. Somewhere beyond it, Station Meridian waited, patient as a held breath.

Vesper stepped through.
