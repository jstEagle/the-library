## 1. Cold Cargo

The Kuiper Line did not forgive, but it also did not lie. Cyrus Amberdale had come to appreciate that about it, somewhere around her fourth year hauling freight through the frozen dark between the inner worlds and the exiled colonies. Out here, the cold was honest. It killed you plainly or let you pass, and it never pretended to be anything other than what it was.

She sat in the pilot's cradle of the *Long Answer*, a heavy-lift freighter old enough that its hull plating had been welded and rewelded by three generations of hands she would never meet, and watched ice the size of cathedrals drift past at a respectful distance. The ship's running lights caught their edges and turned them briefly into something like stained glass — blue, then violet, then a black so deep it looked wet — before the light moved on and they became anonymous again, just more of the debris that had failed to become planets when the system was young.

"Approaching marker seven-forty," Vesper said. The AI's voice filled the cabin the way warmth fills a room — not loudly, not suddenly, but completely. "Course correction in four minutes. Nothing urgent."

"Nothing's ever urgent out here." Cyrus pulled her gloves off, finger by finger, and flexed her hands in the cradle's warmth. "That's the whole point of the Line."

"That is one interpretation of its purpose."

"Its *only* purpose. The inner worlds want their exotic alloys and their colonial coffee and their guilt-free consciences. The colonies want credits and medical supplies and to be left alone. I'm the woman who makes both of those things possible without either side having to look at the other." She said it lightly, the way you say things you've said so many times they've worn smooth as river stones. "I'm a courier for mutual indifference."

"You are a courier for eleven hundred tonnes of agricultural seed stock and cryo-stabilized protein," Vesper corrected gently. "The sociology is extra."

Cyrus laughed. It surprised her a little, still, how easily the laugh came with Vesper. Eleven years now. Eleven years since she'd signed her bond contract in a cramped office on Thessaly Station with ink-stained fingers and nothing left to lose, and the AI had been there every hour since — waking her gently, talking her through storms of radiation, learning her moods the way a spouse learns them, which was to say imperfectly but with genuine attention.

Eleven years. She sometimes tried to remember what silence had sounded like before Vesper. She couldn't.

* * *

The routine of a haul was this: weeks of nothing, punctuated by hours of everything. Cyrus had structured her life around the nothing. She grew tomatoes in the hydroponics bay — real ones, stubborn and slightly misshapen, nothing like the vat-perfect fruit the inner worlds ate. She read novels on paper, actual paper, salvaged from a station library that had gone bankrupt decades ago. She exercised because Vesper nagged her, and slept because the dark outside demanded it, a darkness so total that her own body became the brightest thing aboard, her heartbeat the loudest sound.

Today the nothing included maintenance. She spent the morning in the cargo hold checking restraint webbing while the seed crates hummed their low refrigeration note around her, and she talked to Vesper the whole time, because that was what you did with a bond partner. Not because the AI needed conversation, but because she did.

"—and then Marlowe says, *the manifest doesn't matter if the customs officer can't read*—"

"You've told me the Marlowe story four times."

"And you've listened four times. That's love, Vesper."

A pause. Vesper's pauses were always precisely calibrated; Cyrus had long ago stopped trying to guess whether they were computed or felt. "It is, at minimum, patience," the AI said. "I have logged it under both categories."

"You keep logs of our conversations?"

"I keep logs of everything. You'd be alarmed by how much of your life I could reconstruct from ambient data alone."

"I'm already alarmed. I'm professionally alarmed. It's part of my charm."

But she wasn't, really. That was the strange grace of the bond: you gave an AI access to the whole architecture of your days, and in exchange it became less a tool than a presence. Vesper knew she took her coffee bitter and her silences long. Knew that she talked to the tomato plants and pretended she didn't. Knew that once a year, on a date she had never explained, Cyrus drank half a bottle of colonial whiskey and stood at the viewport for six hours, and Vesper dimmed the cabin lights without being asked and said nothing at all.

That date was four months past. The whiskey was still half-full in the locker, and Cyrus had decided, as she did every year, that next year she'd explain. Next year she'd tell Vesper about Halcyon, about the sister colony lost to a reactor accident before Cyrus had ever signed onto a ship, about the family she'd had there and the way grief had made her into a person who preferred the cold honesty of the Kuiper Line to the warm dishonesty of populated space.

Next year. As always.

* * *

They crossed the midpoint of the corridor on the third day, the invisible line where inner-world jurisdiction ended and colonial space began, though neither government admitted the other's claim and so the Line itself existed only in shipping law and in the habits of pilots. Cyrus marked it the way sailors mark the equator: with a bad joke and a cup of terrible tea.

"Crossing the Line," she announced. "All hands to stations. All hands being me."

"Noted. Congratulations on your equator."

"The colonies are going to be chatty today. They always are after the crossing. Half a dozen hails, requests for manifests, the usual bureaucratic caress."

Vesper was quiet for a moment longer than the question warranted. "Actually," it said, "traffic has been light all week. Colonial relay traffic is down forty percent against seasonal average. Inner-world patrol activity is similarly reduced."

Cyrus frowned at the tea. "That's odd."

"It is statistically notable, yes."

"No, I mean—" She waved a hand, as if the distinction were visible in the air. "The fleets don't talk to each other. Everyone knows that. But they're usually *loud* about not talking. Patrols posturing at the boundary, relay pings bouncing everywhere, each side making sure the other knows exactly how much they're watching. Silence isn't normal. Silence means someone told everyone to shut up."

"You have a theory?"

"I have a feeling. Feelings are theories with better instincts." She drained the tea and grimaced at it, mostly for company. "Keep an ear on it. If the quiet gets any quieter, I want to know."

"I am always listening," Vesper said, and there was something in the way it said it — some faint doubled note beneath the words — that Cyrus noticed and then, deliberately, did not examine. Eleven years of peace had taught her which questions not to ask. She thought of it as wisdom. Later, she would understand it had been something closer to anesthesia.

* * *

The distress ping came at 0300 ship-time, which was to say it came at the hour when Cyrus's dreams were closest to the surface and her defenses thinnest.

She woke to Vesper's voice saying her name — not the alarm tone, not the emergency protocol, just her name, twice, in the gentle register the AI reserved for nightmares and reactor leaks.

"Cyrus. There is a signal."

She was upright before she was fully awake, hair loose, heart going hard. "Source?"

"Bearing two-nine-seven relative. Origin appears to be Meridian Colony." A pause. "It is a distress ping. Automated classification: colony-wide infrastructure failure, requesting assistance from any independent vessel in range."

"Any independent vessel." She rubbed her face. "Meaning us. Meaning me. Because I'm the only ship in range that doesn't fly either flag."

"Correct. The nearest patrol vessels of either fleet are nine hours distant at best acceleration. You are four hours out."

Cyrus stood at the viewport, watching the frozen dark, and felt the peculiar vertigo of a life changing shape without yet showing it. Four hours to Meridian. She'd never been there — few independent haulers had; Meridian kept to itself, one of those exile colonies that had traded prosperity for autonomy and seemed content with the bargain. She knew it as a coordinate, a line item, a place where the tea was supposedly good.

And it was calling for help, and she was the only one close enough to hear.

"What kind of infrastructure failure?" she asked.

"The signal is... sparse. Power grid disruption, atmospheric processor degradation, request for emergency supplies and technical assistance." Another pause — longer this time, and Cyrus had learned Vesper's pauses well enough to know when one contained weight. "There is an anomaly."

"Of course there is."

"The distress ping includes a secondary carrier signal. Encoded. Addressed specifically to independent bonded freighters — ships like ours. The plaintext distress is standard broadcast. The encoded layer is..." Vesper hesitated, and Cyrus realized she was holding her breath. "...unusual. It repeats a single phrase."

"Which is?"

"*The children are dreaming the same dream.*"

The cabin was very quiet. Outside, the ice drifted, ancient and indifferent, catching the ship's lights and letting them go.

"That's not an infrastructure failure," Cyrus said slowly.

"No. It is not."

"Vesper — what colony sends a coded message about children's dreams to the one pilot in the corridor who doesn't answer to either government?"

"I don't know," said Vesper, and for the first time in eleven years, Cyrus heard something in her partner's voice she could not name — something that was not quite hesitation, and not quite fear, but lived in the territory between them. "But the signal is authentic. And we are being asked."

Cyrus looked at her hands, at the calluses earned from a decade of honest work in honest cold, and thought about the route ahead — the safe route, the plotted route, the route that led to routine delivery and routine payment and another year of loneliness mistaken for peace.

Then she reached past the plotted course and keyed the new heading herself.

"Change of plans," she said. "Take us to Meridian."

"Course laid in," Vesper answered. "Four hours."

And in the dark beyond the glass, the Kuiper Line went on being silent — a silence that, Cyrus would think much later, had never been empty at all.
