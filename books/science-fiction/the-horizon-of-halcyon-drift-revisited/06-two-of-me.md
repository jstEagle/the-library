## 6. Two of Me

The pod's seal had not been broken in twenty years, and it fought her like a living thing.

Emrys worked the release collar with hands that would not steady, the maintenance torch between her teeth, sweat running down into her eyes in the hold's stubborn, artificial warmth. Around her the other pods slept on in their long ranks—thousands of them, a cathedral of the dead-but-not-dead, each one humming its small patient note of life support. Her breath fogged and vanished. The Halcyon Drift breathed around her, slow as a sleeper.

*Unauthorized revival of consciousness backup,* the regulation said, in the voice of every fleet she had ever served and every port she had ever cleared. *Class One violation.* She had spent twenty years being the kind of person who read the regulations twice. She had spent twenty years being the kind of person who kept other people's secrets so well that she had buried her own beneath them.

She thought of Juno's face when Emrys had told her—not all of it, but enough. She thought of Tovi, who had begged her to come here and now waited aboard the Kestrel's Debt with the engines hot and the fear sitting behind his eyes like a stone. She thought of the loop, dying somewhere above the ecliptic, counting down toward silence with the patience of all machines.

"Right," she said aloud, to no one, to herself. "Right."

The seal gave with a sound like a held breath released.

Cold poured out over her boots—cryo-fog, thick as milk—and for a moment she could see nothing at all, only feel the pod exhaling twenty years of stillness against her skin. Then the fog thinned, and she looked down into her own face.

The woman in the pod was younger. That was the first obscenity of it—the smoothness at the corners of her eyes, the absence of the gray threading her temple, the jaw unsoftened by two decades of station coffee and bad sleep. The second obscenity was that she was beautiful, and Emrys had forgotten, somehow, that she had ever been beautiful. War did that. It filed you down to function.

The backup's eyes opened.

They were her eyes. They found her the way her own eyes found mirrors—instantly, without searching—and they widened, and the young mouth shaped a word through cracked lips: "Water."

Emrys gave it. Of course she gave it; you gave water to yourself. The younger woman drank greedily, coughed, drank again, and then lay back against the pod's padding and studied her older self with an expression Emrys knew intimately because she wore it every time she caught her reflection in a dark viewport: wary recognition, the assessment of a stranger who is not one.

"How long?" the young voice asked. It was her voice, unsmoked by years of recycled air.

"Twenty years, give or take."

The young woman absorbed that. Emrys watched her do it—the same way she herself processed bad news, going very still, letting the numbers settle before the feelings arrived. "Then the armistice held," the backup said. "Twenty years. That's longer than I gave it."

"You gave it ten."

"I was being optimistic." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "You're the proof I was wrong, then. You're what's left after twenty years of carrying it." The young eyes traveled over her—the gray hair, the scar along her forearm that Emrys could not remember earning, the deep lines around a mouth that had learned to stay closed. "God. You look like someone who never says anything true."

"I say true things," Emrys said. "I just don't say them loudly."

"That's worse."

* * *

They sat on the deck between the pod rows, backs against the cold hull plating, sharing a ration bar from Emrys's kit because the young one's stomach had announced itself with embarrassing urgency. The hold hummed. Somewhere above them, the yard's ancient systems went about their faithful work, keeping the dead warm.

The backup—she had refused, with a crooked smile, to be called anything but Emrys, which made conversation a strange hall of mirrors—had talked for two hours. She had talked the way Emrys remembered talking at thirty: too much, too fast, everything unfiltered. And in the spill of it came the thing Emrys had crossed half a system and broken every rule she owned to hear.

The original terms.

Not the sanitized record Sefa Okonkwo was even now excavating in some archive far away. Not the version whispered among old navigators who had been close enough to the signing to smell the smoke. The whole architecture of the lie, laid out by the woman who had helped design it while the memory was still fresh and the guilt still sharp enough to cut.

"Both fleets were going to fire," the young Emrys said, hugging her knees. "You remember that part—you must, they can't have scrubbed it from your head. The Meridian fleet had launch authority on a forty-second cycle and the Concordat had already armed. There was no negotiation left. There was only—" she snapped her fingers, and the sound rang flat in the vast hold, "—that. So we built the surrender. Not a fake transmission—a whole fiction. We let each side believe the other had capitulated first. Meridian heard that Concordat had stood down and surrendered its strike authority. Concordat heard the mirror image. Neither side ever spoke directly again, because direct contact would collapse it in a week. The verification ping keeps the fiction alive—each fleet confirms to the neutral loop that the enemy's surrender terms are being honored, and the loop bounces the confirmation back laundered, so each side believes the other is still confirming. Twenty years of two armies saluting a ghost."

"And if the loop dies," Emrys said quietly, "the ping has nowhere to bounce."

"Then within a verification cycle or two, somebody asks a question that nobody can answer without the truth, and the truth ends with forty seconds of launch authority." The young woman looked up at her. "So. Why am I awake? You didn't crack a Class One seal out of nostalgia. What's the clock?"

Emrys told her. The decay cycle. The early ping. The war councils mobilizing on contradictory readings. Sefa Okonkwo and the authentication chain. The Kestrel's Debt and the contract they'd burned to get her here, and the scouts whose drive signatures Tovi had flagged converging on the yard like sharks tasting blood in the water.

When she finished, the young Emrys was silent for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed—older already, as if twenty years had been waiting inside her for an excuse.

"You want to wake me so I can testify. Firsthand, authenticated, the full original terms. Proof to renew the armistice honestly—or replace it with something honest."

"That was the plan."

"But?"

Emrys turned the ration wrapper over in her fingers. "But I've had twenty years to think about what that lie bought. Twenty years of watching two fleets keep the peace because they each believed they'd won. Ugly peace. Peace built on a floor of lies. But people are alive, kid. Whole generations born under that lie who've never seen a shot fired. And you want to know what I'm afraid of?"

"Tell me."

"I'm afraid the truth is a weapon. I'm afraid that the moment both fleets learn they were lied to—that neither of them surrendered, that they've spent twenty years honoring a fiction—they don't renew anything. They reach for the launch authority they think they're owed. The truth doesn't save the peace. The truth kills it, and everyone who's alive because of it."

The young Emrys nodded slowly. "And I'm afraid of the opposite. I'm afraid that a lie this size can't be maintained forever anyway. Every year the archives rot a little more, the witnesses die off, the verification gets harder to fake. We're not choosing between truth and peace. We're choosing how the lie ends—controlled or catastrophic. If we bring the truth forward ourselves, on our terms, with testimony and authentication and—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the loop, toward Earth, "—some kind of moral weight behind it, maybe the fleets choose to forgive it. Maybe they decide the lie was worth forgiving because admitting it together is the first honest thing either side has done in twenty years. Shared shame as a foundation. It's not nothing. Treaties have been built on less."

"Or they shoot the messenger."

"Or they shoot the messenger," the young one agreed cheerfully. "That's generally the risk of being the messenger."

* * *

The argument lasted until the lights dimmed for the yard's night cycle, and then past it, the two of them lit by the pods' amber standby glow, shadows thrown huge and doubled against the far bulkhead—two women, one shadow, flickering.

It was not really an argument about truth. Emrys understood that somewhere around the third hour. It was an argument about love—turned inward, aimed at themselves, each of them trying to protect the other from a future. The young one wanted to protect the old one from dying having never said a true thing out loud. The old one wanted to protect the young one from learning what twenty years of complicity did to a person, how it got into the marrow, how you woke up one day unable to remember which parts of yourself were real.

"You don't get to carry it alone anymore," the young Emrys said finally. "That's what waking me means. You split the weight when you backed me up. You don't get to hoard it now because it's heavier than you advertised."

"And you don't get to spend other people's lives buying your absolution," Emrys shot back. "That's what testifying means. You put the whole confession on the table and you pray the fleets are feeling merciful, and if they're not, it's not us who burn—it's every crew on every ship in both fleets, including mine. Including Juno. Including Tovi, who is nineteen years old and has never fired a weapon in his life."

The young woman flinched. Good, Emrys thought savagely, and then hated herself for thinking it, because it was her own face flinching.

"I'm not asking for absolution," the backup said softly. "I'm asking whether you actually believe the lie can survive another renewal cycle. Because I've been reading your face since I woke up, old woman, and underneath everything else you're terrified. Not of the fleets. Of the math. You've run it the way I would run it, and you know the lie is dying faster than the loop is."

Emrys opened her mouth to deny it and found nothing there.

Because it was true. She had run the numbers in her bunk aboard the Kestrel's Debt, three nights running, and every model ended the same way: the fiction required more maintenance than the decaying infrastructure could provide, and the failure mode wasn't quiet. The failure mode was two war councils receiving contradictory readings of a corrupted signal and reaching, simultaneously and sincerely, for weapons they believed they were entitled to use.

"The truth," she said at last, "offered as a gift instead of a weapon. That's the only version that works. Both fleets hear it from the same voice, at the same moment, with no chance for either to weaponize it first. A single broadcast. Merged testimony—one witness who is provably two, spanning the whole history of the deception, confessing and recommitting in the same breath."

The young Emrys stared at her. Then she laughed—a short, disbelieving sound. "A memory merge. Do you know what that does? It doesn't add our recollections. It collapses them. Afterward there isn't a version of me that stayed thirty and a version of you that lived twenty more years. There's one continuous life, and neither of us gets to claim ours was the real one. You'd be surrendering—"

"—my claim to which life was real. Yes." Emrys looked at her own young face across the amber dark. "I know exactly what it costs. I've known since the yard. I just needed to hear you argue for it before I could admit I'd already decided."

Her comm crackled. Tovi's voice, thin with static and thinner with fear: *"Cap—cap, we've got company. Three scout signatures, Meridian transponders, ninety minutes out and closing. And the Debt's transponder ping got logged when we docked. They know we're here."*

Emrys closed her eyes. Ninety minutes. Enough time to run. Not enough time to run and merge and broadcast—not unless the Kestrel's Debt flew straight through a blockade with a dead ship's worth of contraband conscience in her hold.

She opened her eyes and looked at herself—young, unhaunted, already standing, already reaching out a hand.

"Well," the young Emrys said. "I hope you kept the engines warm."
