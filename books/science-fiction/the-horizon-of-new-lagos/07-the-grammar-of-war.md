## 7. The Grammar of War

The fleets arrived like weather.

Wren watched them resolve on the Kindly Hours' long-range display, two constellations of drive-flare crawling out of the dark on converging vectors: the Concord battle line in its patient wedge, the Meridian flotilla strung out in the loose, aggressive scatter that their admirals called initiative and everyone else called a firing solution waiting to happen. Between them, small and blue and utterly silent on every channel that mattered, hung New Lagos.

"They're not talking," Sefu said. He had not left the nav station in nine hours. His hands rested on the console the way a surgeon's rest on a patient. "I mean it, Wren. They're not talking to each other. Concord's hailing on the old diplomatic bands. Meridian's hailing on theirs. Neither one is listening."

"Because each of them already knows how the conversation ends," Wren said.

He could feel the forged signal like a weight behind his sternum. Somewhere below them, on the derelict archive ship they had christened the Long Memory, Adaeze was broadcasting. She had been broadcasting for eleven days now — Ashanti-Meridian, the dead tongue, the language of their grandmother's grandmother, poured out over the colony in flawless, urgent, invented distress. *We are under attack. We are dying. Stand down. Stand down.* Both fleets had heard her. Both fleets had obeyed her, for a while, the way you obey a scream in a language you don't speak but recognize as pain.

Now the cryptographers had caught up.

The Concord interrogation vessel had sent a single message an hour ago, addressed not to the colony, not to the Meridian flotilla, but to the signal's source itself. Wren had read it three times. It was polite the way an autopsy is polite. *Your transmission exhibits grammatical features inconsistent with the documented corpus of Ashanti-Meridian. Please transmit authentication keys or cease broadcasting. Noncompliance will be construed as hostile deception.*

"They've found the seams," Wren said.

"There are seams?"

"There are always seams." He rubbed his eyes. "She built a language out of a corpse. She's been improvising for eleven days. Every verb she conjugates that the corpus never recorded is a thread a cryptographer can pull."

Ilse appeared at his elbow with a bulb of tea he hadn't asked for and drank from it himself before handing it over, which was her way of telling him it wasn't poisoned and that he was going to drink it regardless. "And when they pull the threads?"

"Then both fleets learn there is no colony in distress." Wren watched the wedge and the scatter creep closer to each other, closer to the blue world. "They learn the only thing holding them apart was a ghost. And then they ask the only question left, which is who fired first — and each of them has spent two years certain it was the other."

* * *

He took the shuttle down to the Long Memory alone. Sefu objected. Sefu always objected now, with a new flatness in his voice that Wren recognized and could not yet answer. Ilse pressed a med-kit into his hands and said nothing at all, which was worse.

The archive ship was a cathedral that had drowned. Its corridors were lined with dead servers, their indicator lights long gone, their racks holding the recorded voice of a civilization that had died in the same accident both fleets now pretended had been an act of war. Wren walked through the dark of it, following the sound.

He found Adaeze in the transmission well, lit by the amber glow of the array's status boards, her hands moving over the input console like a pianist's, like a weaver's. She was speaking as she typed — actually speaking, aloud, in Ashanti-Meridian, feeding the array a continuous stream of the dead language because silence, now, would read as death.

She looked terrible. She looked luminous. She had not slept, by the evidence of her, in days.

"You got their message," she said, without turning. Of course she had; she was tapped into everything.

"I got their message."

"Then you know I have maybe thirty hours before they pull the authentication thread, and forty before they decide a forged distress call is itself an act of war." Her hands never stopped. "I've been building a response. A grammatical defense. I'm claiming dialect drift. I'm claiming the colony's speech evolved in the two centuries since the corpus was sealed. It's plausible. It's even elegant. It's also a house of cards, and they have the wind."

"Adaeze." He said her name the way you set something heavy on a table.

She stopped typing. The array hummed on, carrying her last sentence out into the dark, a dead woman's grammar still in the air.

"Tell me," she said.

"Evacuation's at sixty percent. The colony ships need four more days. If the forgery dies tonight, the fleets close the gap tonight." He made himself say the rest. "If we extend the lie — if I go down there and stand in the transmission well with you and we build the dialect-drift defense together, live, faster than they can break it — we buy those four days. Every hour we hold, another ten thousand people get off that world."

"And if we fail?"

"Then we haven't just failed. We've committed fraud against two armed fleets in a dead language. They'll call it an act of war. They'll be right."

Adaeze turned to him then, and in the amber light her face was their mother's face, worn down to the wire. "You came all this way to tell me to stop."

"I came all this way to help you," Wren said. "That's what terrifies me."

* * *

They worked for thirty-one hours.

They worked the way they had worked as children, before the quarrel, before New Lagos burned the first time — side by side, finishing each other's clauses, their grandmother's language rising in them like water finding its level. Wren built the morphology of the dialect drift; Adaeze built its history, inventing three generations of colony speech with sound changes so consistent she could have taught them. The array sang. The dead language lived, hour by hour, lie by lie, and out in the black the fleets slowed, wavered, held.

At hour nineteen, the Concord cryptographers sent a challenge: a passage of reconstructed proto-Ashanti and a demand for translation in ninety minutes. Wren translated it in eleven, and embellished, and the embellishment was the point — he answered the challenge in a register the corpus never documented, warm, colloquial, grieving, the voice of a colony that had learned its ancestors' tongue at its mother's knee and bent it to new skies. He was so good it frightened him. Somewhere on an interrogation vessel, an examiner was flagging him as a native speaker, and the flag was a lie, and the lie was keeping ten thousand people breathing.

At hour twenty-seven, Adaeze swayed in her chair and kept typing. At hour twenty-nine, her hands began to miss the keys, and she corrected each miss with a small furious sound, and Wren saw the tremor climb her arms. At hour thirty-one, she said a word in Ashanti-Meridian that their grandmother had used only for the dead, and then she said nothing at all, and folded sideways out of the chair.

Wren caught her before the deck did. She was light. When had she become so light? Her pulse was fast and shallow; her eyes fluttered, unfocused, her lips still shaping syllables of a language only two people alive could speak.

"Adaeze. Adaeze, I've got the watch. Sleep. I've got it."

She surfaced just enough to grip his wrist. "Don't let it die," she whispered. "Don't let them die twice."

* * *

He held the watch alone.

He sat in her chair, in the amber light, with his sister's breath shallow against his shoulder where he'd settled her, and he spoke the dead language to two fleets who wanted an excuse to kill each other, and he was good at it, and every hour he was good at it the lie grew another ring like a tree.

And that was when he found it.

He was reaching into the archive for corpus material to shore up the dialect defense — deep layers, the sealed strata neither fleet had properly catalogued — when his search brushed against a directory the index called only *FINAL*. The colony's own last transmissions. The genuine ones. The recordings both fleets had sealed, the ones that would say, in the voices of the dead, exactly what had happened on New Lagos two years ago — and whose accident it had been, and whose order, and whose silence after.

Adaeze's forgery was holding the fleets apart from each other. But it was also holding them apart from *this*. Her beautiful lie, built to stop a war, had become the wall that kept the truth of their parents' deaths in the dark — and kept the guilty safe behind it.

Wren sat with his hands above the console, not touching it. The array hummed. Out in the black, the wedge and the scatter drifted closer, and the evacuation crawled toward its fourth day, and his sister slept the sleep of the spent, trusting him with the watch.

He could extend the lie. Four more days, maybe. Ten thousand more people gone clear. And then the cryptographers would break it anyway, and the truth would come out as fraud, tangled and discredited, and the sealed recordings would stay sealed, and both fleets would go on believing whatever let them sleep.

Or he could open *FINAL*.

He thought of Sefu's flat voice. Of Ilse's silence. Of the flag on some examiner's desk with his name under it. Of the blue world hanging between two armadas, holding its breath.

His hands were still above the console. The array waited. Somewhere below him, ten thousand people were packing their lives into colony ships, and somewhere above him two fleets were deciding, and somewhere in the drowned archive the dead were waiting with the only words that had ever been true.

Wren Oyelaran put his hands on the keys — and began, quietly, to open the directory.
