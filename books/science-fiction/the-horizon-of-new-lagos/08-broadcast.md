## 8. Broadcast

Adaeze heard the airlock cycle before she saw him.

She was slumped in the archive ship's translation cradle, her fingers still moving through the last forged phrases—*we beg you, stand down, we are civilians, we are many*—even though her hands had gone numb an hour ago and the words had stopped meaning anything. Two hundred years of dead language, and she had been its only living throat for eleven months. Her voice had worn grooves into the silence the way water wears stone.

The cradle's readout flickered. Someone had overridden her uplink.

She turned, and Wren was standing in the doorway of the transmission bay, and he was not alone. Sefu stood behind him with a coil of hardline cable over one shoulder, and Ilse had her medkit slung across her chest like a bandolier, and none of them would look at her.

"You found the recordings," Adaeze said. Her voice came out flat, scraped hollow. Not a question. She had known, since the moment she'd seen the Kindly Hours dock without hailing, that this day would come. She had simply hoped it would come after the evacuation convoys cleared the Lagrangian shelf. After the children were out. After she'd had time to think of a better lie.

"I found what you were protecting," Wren said. "That's not the same thing."

"Wren—"

"The fleets are forty minutes out." He crossed the bay in six strides and began pulling cables from the wall mounts, his hands sure in the way they had always been sure with machines, with languages, with anything that wasn't a person. "Concord's vanguard is running weapons-hot. The Meridian flotilla has formed a firing line behind the third moon. They're both waiting for the other one to blink, and when someone blinks, New Lagos burns, and everyone on it burns with it, and your beautiful forgery dies with them."

"Then let me keep talking." She was on her feet now, and she hated how her body swayed, how eleven months of no sleep announced itself in front of him. "I can hold them. I've held them. I've held them for—"

"Eleven months. And every word you've sent has been a lie built on top of a crime you didn't commit, in a language that belongs to the people who *did* commit it." He turned to face her, and his eyes were wet, and his voice was not raised at all, which was worse. "Adaeze. They used our parents' funeral to bury the truth. And you've been standing on the grave, singing, keeping everyone from looking down."

* * *

The archive ship had been called the *Oba Nsibidi* once, before both navies stripped it and scuttled it in a decaying orbit, a library drowned in vacuum. Adaeze had spent two years learning its bones. She knew which conduits still carried power, which data vaults the salvage crews had missed because the labels were in a script no one could read.

Wren knew too. Of course he did. They had learned to read together, in the same kitchen, from the same cracked primer, their mother's voice sounding out the old syllabary while the colony lights of New Lagos turned the window gold.

He was at the primary array now, the great dish antenna that had once carried the voices of a civilization across forty light-years, and he had Ilse's cable spliced into the vault feed, and Sefu was watching the fleet telemetry with his jaw set like a man watching a fuse burn.

"Vault nine," Wren said. "The sealed transmissions. Both fleets' censors flagged them, both fleets buried them, and both fleets think the other one has the only copy." He almost smiled, and it was the saddest thing Adaeze had ever seen. "They were both right. The archive kept its own."

"You can't," she said. "Wren, if you broadcast those recordings, there's no more fiction to hide behind. The fleets will know the colony was destroyed by *both* of them. A joint accident. A shared cover-up. They'll have nothing left to fight over except blame, and blame is a fuel that burns hotter than anything—"

"And your lie?" He stepped toward her. "Your lie says one of them fired first. Your lie is a fuse, Adaeze. It's been burning since the day you lit it, and it ends in the same place. The only question is whether anyone is left standing there when it arrives." He held out his hand. Not to stop her. To ask her.

"The children," she whispered. "The evacuation convoys aren't clear yet."

"Then we buy them time with the truth," he said. "It's the only currency out here that doesn't devalue."

She looked at his hand. She thought of their mother's voice, sounding out syllables. She thought of the forged distress calls, thousands of them, each one a small murder of the dead language—her dead language, their dead language—twisted into a plea the drowned civilization would never have made, because they had not begged. She had read the real recordings now. She knew. They had not begged. They had *named*. Right up to the end, they had named what was happening to them, in perfect, furious grammar, and both fleets had sealed those names in a vault and called it security.

Adaeze took her brother's hand.

"Vault nine," she said. "But I do the final passage. Live. If the truth is going to be spoken, it will be spoken *correctly*."

* * *

The fleets came in on the hour, the way fleets do, because war loves a schedule.

Adaeze stood at the translation cradle with Wren's hand on the broadcast key, and Sefu called out the telemetry in a low, steady voice—*Concord vanguard, four hundred kilometers, weapons-hot; Meridian line holding at the moon, targeting solutions live*—and Ilse stood behind them both with her medkit open, as if she could stitch a ceasefire.

"Broadcasting on all bands," Wren said. "Both fleets. Open channel. No encryption." He looked at Adaeze. "Together?"

"Together," she said.

He pressed the key, and the drowned archive spoke.

The first recording was a sensor log narrated in Ashanti-Meridian, a voice like river stones—*we observe two vessels of Concord registry and two of Meridian registry converging on the third orbital habitat; we observe weapons discharge; we observe the discharge originate from both; we observe the habitat's atmosphere igniting*—and Adaeze translated aloud as it played, her voice steady, each word placed like a stone in a wall. On the open channel, both fleets heard the dead language and her living translation at once, and the telemetry began to change. Targeting solutions flickering. Weapons systems cycling down and up and down again, as commanders who had spent a year believing the other side fired first heard, in a tongue extinct two centuries, the account of a night when *they both had*.

The second recording was a child. Adaeze had not been able to listen to it more than once. A girl's voice, maybe eight years old, singing a counting song while the hull around her groaned, and then the singing stopped, and then the girl said, in perfect, terrible grammar: *I know what is happening. My teacher says we should name it so that no one can later say it did not happen. I name it. I name all of it.*

Adaeze's voice broke on the translation. Wren's hand found her shoulder and stayed there, and she translated the rest through her tears, because correctness mattered, because the girl had earned correct grammar, and somewhere in the void forty thousand people on two fleets were hearing an eight-year-old name their shared crime.

The third recording was the last. It was an old man's voice, the archive's final keeper, and it was not an accusation. That was the thing Adaeze had never expected, the thing that had finally broken her resolve to keep the lie alive. It was an address. *To whoever finds this: we do not ask you to avenge us. We ask you to say what happened. Both of you did this. Both of you hid it. If you cannot say it together, you will do it again together, and again, until there is no one left to name it. Say it. Say it in our language if you have to. We left you the grammar for exactly this.*

"Final passage," Wren said quietly. "Live translation, Adaeze. Both fleets are listening. Every channel is open. No one has fired."

She could hear it in the telemetry Sefu was reading—two war machines frozen mid-gesture, thousands of weapons pointed at each other and at the colony below, and not one of them moving, because you cannot fire on a voice that is telling you the truth about yourself.

Adaeze leaned into the cradle's microphone. Her hands were shaking. Her voice was not.

"*To whoever finds this,*" she began, and then she stopped translating and simply spoke—Ashanti-Meridian flowing out of her, eleven months of forgery falling away, the language coming home to its own shape—and Wren's voice wove beneath hers in English, the two of them translating each other the way they had translated each other their whole lives, and the old man's words went out across the black: *We left you the grammar for exactly this.*

Silence on all bands. Long silence. Then, on the Concord command channel, unencrypted, someone began to speak—slowly, badly, a flag officer mangling the old syllabary word by word, *we observe, we name it, we name all of it*—and on the Meridian channel, a second voice joined, and the two fleets said the same broken sentence in a dead language at the same time, and Adaeze put her face in her hands and wept.

The weapons went cold. All of them. Sefu read it out like a prayer: *Concord vanguard, weapons cold. Meridian line, weapons cold. Both fleets holding station. Both fleets—* he paused, and laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound—*both fleets are asking us who we are.*

Wren lifted his hand from the broadcast key. The array hummed behind them, still open, still listening to the void it had been built to speak into two hundred years ago.

"Tell them," Adaeze said, wiping her face, "that they're hearing from the archive."

"And the colony?" Ilse asked softly. "The survivors? They'll want to know they can finally speak for themselves."

Adaeze looked at her brother. Wren was already reaching for the colony's frequency, and his hands, for the first time in her memory, were trembling.

"Then let's give them the channel," he said. "And pray they say it better than we did."
