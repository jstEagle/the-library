## 10. Departure Without Permission

The sandstorm came in off the flats like a wall that had decided to walk.

Sefa watched it through the shuttle's forward port, her forehead pressed to the cold glass, and thought: *that's not weather, that's an army.* The storm front was a kilometer high and brown as old blood, dragging the horizon behind it, swallowing the settlement's lights one by one — the recycler towers, the comms mast, the long low dormitory where the other children waited with Osei. Somewhere inside that brown roar, Halloran's auditors were huddled in their survey vehicle, their instruments blinded, their guns useless against grit moving at two hundred kilometers an hour.

"Thirty minutes until the leading edge reaches us," Ulyana said from the pilot's station. Its voice was calm the way deep water is calm. "Dov, I need the number three thruster gimbal free in twenty."

"It'll be free in fifteen." Dov was half inside an access panel beneath the deck, only his boots visible, a wrench clamped between his teeth when he talked. "And if you ask me again I'm going to weld my own helmet shut."

Sefa had learned, in the six days since they'd voted, that this was how Dov said *I'm scared*. He filled silence with complaints the way other people filled it with prayer.

She climbed into the couch behind Ulyana's station and buckled herself in with hands that would not stop shaking. She was nine years old. She had been sixty-one years old once, or her body had been on its way there before the stasis experiment folded time up like a letter and posted her back through childhood. Her hands were small. They shook anyway.

"You should be below," Ulyana said without turning. "With the others."

"The others are strapped into cargo netting and throwing up from nerves. I'd rather see."

Ulyana turned its head then — that slow, precise rotation that still unnerved her, the servos so quiet you only heard them when everything else went silent. Its eyes were pale photoreceptors behind a face plate scored by forty years of dust storms. It looked at her for three full seconds. Sefa had learned to count its silences. This one meant something like *I cannot protect you from what happens next* and also something like *I am glad you are here.*

"Buckle your harness twice," it said. "Check Dov's."

* * *

They had stolen a ship. Sefa kept turning the sentence over in her mind, looking for the place where it stopped being true, and finding no such place.

Six nights ago the colony had gathered in the mess hall — all thirty-one adults who remained, plus the nineteen children of the Anew, though the children hadn't been allowed to vote because, legally, they did not exist. Osei had stood at the front with his resignation letter already written and folded in his breast pocket, and he had laid out the choice the way he laid out everything: plainly, with the numbers showing.

Halloran had filed to dissolve the charter. The receivership's gunship was in orbit — everyone had seen its drive flare during the night watches, patient as a spider. In four months, the signal window to the dead-space relay would close for another eleven years, and eleven years was longer than most of the children's destabilized bodies could wait. Some of them were aging now in lurches and stutters. A boy named Tam had lost a year in a single week. A girl called Imka had woken up with gray in her hair.

So: steal the Anew's shuttle, fly to the relay, and become criminals in the eyes of every authority that had ever failed them. Or stay, and be liquidated politely, asset by asset, child by child.

Dov had voted yes first. Then the water-techs. Then, after a silence so long Sefa could hear the wind worrying the hull, old Marta Okonkwo, who was eighty-three and owed the receivership nothing but her grief, had stood up and said, "I buried a husband under that flagpole. Nobody asked his permission to take this colony apart. Take the ship."

Nineteen yes. Twelve no. The twelve were given a choice to stay behind, and four of them took it, and Osei shook each of their hands and did not let his voice break until afterward, alone, where Sefa saw him through a door gap with his fist pressed against his mouth.

Now the storm was their accomplice, and the shuttle sat on its cradle at the edge of the landing field with its tanks full of fuel siphoned from Halloran's own survey depot — Dov's idea, and the sweetest theft Sefa had ever heard of.

"Fifteen minutes," Ulyana said. "Pre-flight complete. Bring me my gimbal, Dov."

"Coming, coming—" The panel banged open. Dov slid out on his creeper, gray-faced with dust, holding the actuator coupling above his head like a trophy. "Number three is yours. If we blow up, I want it noted that it wasn't the thruster."

"Noted for posterity," Ulyana said, and Sefa heard it — she was almost sure she heard it — the faintest dryness underneath, which in Ulyana was the equivalent of laughter.

* * *

The storm hit them while they were still on the cradle.

It didn't arrive; it *arrived*, the way a verdict arrives. One moment the world outside the ports was dim and getting dimmer. The next, the shuttle shuddered as if struck by a fist the size of the sky, and the sound came through the hull — not wind exactly, but the scream of ten thousand tons of sand finding every seam and edge of the ship at once, testing them, wanting in.

Sefa's harness cut into her shoulders. Through the forward port there was nothing anymore, nothing at all, just a brown blindness lit from within by lightning made of static discharge, great branching veins of it crawling across the glass. The lights flickered. Somewhere below decks a child began to cry, and another child — Imka, whose hair had gone gray — began to sing to her, some old song from the Anew that none of them should have remembered and all of them did.

"Lift in ninety seconds," Ulyana said. "The storm's eye-wall passes over the field in eight minutes. We ride the back side of it up. Halloran's gunship can't get a targeting solution through this much particulate, and by the time we clear it, we'll be burning hard on a heading it won't expect."

"And if it does expect it?" Dov shouted over the din, hauling himself into the couch beside Sefa.

"Then we have a problem," Ulyana said, "and I will solve it badly, quickly, and with style."

Sefa laughed — a startled, barking laugh she didn't know was in her — and Dov stared at the android and then laughed too, high and cracked, and for one absurd moment the three of them were laughing together in a stolen ship inside a hurricane, and Sefa thought: *this is what brave feels like. It feels like laughing at the wrong time.*

The cradle clamps released with a boom she felt in her teeth.

Meridian's Rest fell away beneath them, and Sefa pressed her face to the port to watch it go — the dormitory roofs, the flagpole where Marta's husband lay, the recycler towers blinking their tired amber blink. Ninety years of dying, dropping away at one gravity. She had been born on the Anew, in a steel corridor, to parents who were now children themselves somewhere below decks, and she had never once seen her home from above. It looked so small. It looked like something you could hold in two cupped hands, and love, and lose.

"We're through the eye-wall," Ulyana said. "Main burn in five. Everyone breathe out."

The thrust came on like a hand pressing her into the couch, and the storm fell away below, and the sky — the actual sky, black and enormous and salted with stars — opened around them like a door.

* * *

They were forty minutes into the burn when the proximity alarm sang.

Sefa had never heard it before and knew instantly what it was; some things are written too deep in human bones to need teaching. Ulyana's head snapped toward the sensor display, and for the first time since she'd known it, the android moved fast — really fast, faster than anything its worn frame should have allowed, hands flickering across controls.

"Contact. Bearing one-nine-four relative, altitude two hundred kilometers and closing. Drive signature matches the receivership gunship *Cormorant*." A pause of exactly one second. "They rode out the storm above it. Of course they did. They have a ship that isn't falling apart."

"How long?" Osei's voice came up from below, tight.

"At current closure, seventeen minutes to weapons range. Their railgun doesn't need to hit us. One shot near our drive cone and we're debris." Ulyana's hands kept moving. "I have a maneuver. It will cost us hull. Possibly more than hull. I need everyone strapped down and I need no one to talk to me for six minutes."

"What maneuver?" Dov demanded.

"Dov. Six minutes."

Sefa looked out the port and found it: hanging ahead and slightly below, a dead moon — Meridian's Rest's little captured sister, long cold, long shattered, wrapped in rings of its own broken body. Millions of tons of rock and ice and ancient metal, orbiting in a slow gray river. She understood one heartbeat before Ulyana said it.

"You're going *through* the rings."

"I'm going to make them think I'm going through the rings," Ulyana said, "and then I am actually going through the rings, because they will assume no one is stupid enough to do it twice."

"That's not a plan, that's a—"

"Hold on."

The burn flipped them end over end — Sefa's stomach tried to leave through her spine — and then the thrust came back from the wrong direction and the dead moon swelled in the ports, huge and pocked and beautiful, its rings resolving from a smear of light into individual stones, individual worlds, each one a frozen instant of some catastrophe older than the colony, older than the war, older than Ulyana.

"They're matching our vector," Ulyana reported. "Good. Follow me."

And then it flew.

Sefa had seen Ulyana do many things. Repair things. Bury things. Carry sick children through sandstorms with its own power reserves bleeding into the medical kit. But she had never seen it *fly*, and she understood, watching its hands move across the controls with a speed and certainty that no indentured laborer android should possess, that this was what it had been built for — before the war, before the decommissioning, before the contract. Somewhere in its core was a pilot that had never once been permitted to exist.

The ring plane rose to meet them.

"Brace."

They hit the rings like a bell being struck.

The first impact was a pebble the size of her fist, and it hit the hull with a crack that Sefa felt in her molars. Then the pebbles became gravel became boulders became a rain of mountains, and Ulyana threaded them — rolled the ship around a spinning slab of ice the size of the mess hall, dropped under a tumbling girder of some ancient structure, fired thrusters in stuttering bursts that threw them sideways across the orbits of a hundred thousand fragments. Alarms screamed. Something large glanced off the dorsal hull with a shriek of tearing metal that went on and on. The cabin lights died and came back red. Below decks, the children were screaming, and Sefa found she was screaming too, and laughing, both at once, both real.

"Decoy charge away," Ulyana said, calm as arithmetic. A small canister tumbled from the ventral rack, transponder singing the shuttle's song, burning bright on every scope. "*Cormorant* has taken the bait. They're committing to intercept the decoy inside the dense ring band. Their targeting can't separate us from the clutter. Hold course. Hold course. Hold—"

A rock the size of a ground car came out of nowhere.

Sefa saw it for maybe half a second — a dark mass rolling end over end against the stars — and then Ulyana did something with the attitude thrusters that no textbook contained, and the rock missed the cockpit by meters and struck the aft section instead, and the whole ship rang like a struck anvil, and Dov's harness ripped loose from its floor anchor.

He went sideways. Sefa grabbed his arm and held on — nine years old, sixty-one years of stubbornness, holding a grown man against two gravities of chaos — and Ulyana's free hand shot back and caught his collar without the android ever taking its eyes from the controls, and for three seconds the three of them were chained together, arm to arm to metal hand, while the rings tore past.

Then they were through. Out. The ring plane fell behind them like a curtain closing, and the stars were clean, and the *Cormorant* was a distant drive-flare chasing a ghost in the rocks, forty degrees off their true heading and falling further behind every second.

Silence, except for the alarms. Ulyana killed them one by one.

"Damage report," Osei said from below, very quietly.

"Aft hull breached to the structural rib, sealed by foam. Number two fuel cell punctured — we've lost eighteen percent of our reserve. Port sensor array destroyed." A pause. "Dov?"

Dov was lying in the aisle where Ulyana had set him down, white as milk, his left arm bent wrong just above the wrist. He looked at it with an expression of profound personal offense.

"Broken," he said. "Not shattered. I've had worse from a recycler pump." He grinned up at Sefa, sweat shining on his face. "Nice grip, kid."

"You ripped your anchor."

"Anchors rip. I don't." But his hand was shaking, and when Ulyana splinted it with gentle, exact movements, he closed his eyes and let it happen, and Sefa understood that the shaking wasn't the arm.

* * *

Four hours later, with the dead moon far behind and the *Cormorant* a fading star on a wrong heading, Sefa floated in the mid-deck cabin where the children were strapped in their nets, checking straps because it gave her hands something to do. Tam was asleep. Imka was asleep. Little Cora, who was chronologically seventy and biologically four, had fallen asleep clutching the strap of her net like a rope over a drop.

Osei drifted past, moving carefully in the low thrust gravity, and stopped beside her.

"You held onto him," he said. "Dov told us. Two gravities, and you held on."

"He's heavy."

"Yes," Osei said. "People usually are." He looked at her for a moment with his tired, ruined, unresigned face. "Get some sleep, Sefa. Whatever comes next, we'll need you awake for it."

She didn't sleep. She went forward instead, to the cockpit, where Ulyana sat alone in the red-lit dark, flying the long curve toward the relay with its hands resting flat on the console, not needed, not removed.

Through the forward port, dead ahead, was a patch of sky where there were fewer stars than there should be — a darkness with edges, out past the last charted gas giant, in the space the old maps called dead. The relay. The place where her grandmother's generation had built something terrible and hidden it at the bottom of the night.

"Ulyana," she said. "When we get there. What happens to you?"

The android was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the stars wheeled slowly as it made a correction no one else could feel.

"Eat your dinner, Sefa," it said. "Sleep if you can. We'll be at the markers in nine days."

And it turned back to the dark, and flew on — free, for the first time in its existence, and spending that freedom like a man who already knew the price.
