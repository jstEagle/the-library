## 13. Interrogation of a Signal

Dov had a rule about machines that talked in riddles: you didn't argue with them, you starved them out. You fed them the same question until they got tired of being clever.

He'd been starving this one for eleven days.

The relay's approach codes had come to them as a fragment — a sliver of medical telemetry buried in the Anew's stasis logs, wrapped around coordinates like a seed around its husk. Ulyana could read the shape of it but not the language. The shuttle's nav computer could parse the language but not the shape. So it fell to Dov, hunched in the engineering bay with his injured side propped against a coolant line and Sefa cross-legged on the deck beside him, to do what he'd done his whole life on Meridian's Rest: take something broken and refuse to let it stay that way.

"Again," Sefa said.

Dov keyed the fragment into the parser for the fortieth time. The display stuttered, resolved, and offered up the same beautiful nonsense: a string of prime-indexed pulses, then a checksum that failed by exactly one bit, every time, no matter how they framed it.

"It's not corrupted," Sefa said, for the fortieth time.

"I know it's not corrupted."

"It's *corrected*. Someone fixed it before it was sent. Deliberately." She pulled her knees up to her chin — she did that when she was thinking, made herself small, like her body was just a vehicle for the part of her that worked. "One bit off. Always the same bit. That's not damage, Dov. That's a signature."

Dov looked at her. Eleven years old, or sixty-one, depending on which way you counted; she had the habit of speech of someone much older and the stubbornness of someone much younger, and both at once, always. "A signature of what?"

"Of someone who wanted it found by the right people and not the wrong ones." She reached past him and tapped the failed checksum. "You don't break your own message unless you're afraid of who's listening. My grandmother's generation built encryption like this during the Charter Wars — keys hidden inside errors. It's in the histories Ulyana keeps. Nobody teaches it anymore because nobody trusts anybody enough to need it."

The coolant line ticked against Dov's ribs. He thought about arguing, found nothing to argue with, and hated it a little. He was forty-three years old and had rebuilt half the colony's infrastructure with his hands, and here was a child reading dead engineers' paranoia like a bedtime story.

"So we find the key," he said.

"The key isn't a word." Sefa was already pulling up the fragment again, spreading its layers across three screens. "It's a *thing*. Something both of us would know. Something the sender knew we'd have." Her finger stopped moving. "Something from home."

* * *

They tried the obvious first. Meridian's Rest's founding date. The colony charter number. Osei's name, the settlement's name, the names of the dead — Ulyana's archive gave them four hundred and six surnames to work through, and the checksum stayed wrong by one bit, patient as stone.

On the fourth day Sefa stopped sleeping properly. Dov found her at odd hours in the nav alcove, wrapped in a thermal blanket, feeding the parser strings of numbers with the grim focus of a child doing penance. When he asked what she was looking for, she said, "What my grandmother would have trusted more than her own name," and wouldn't say anything else.

On the sixth day she found it.

Dov was asleep when she came to him — actually shook him awake, which no one on the shuttle dared do except her — and dragged him to the console with her eyes shining and terrified at once.

"The water recycler," she said.

"What?"

"The serial number. The original recycler at Meridian's Rest — the one Ulyana keeps repairing. Unit designation WR-0011." She keyed it in. "My grandmother helped install it. She wrote about it in her logs — she said it was the first thing they built that was meant to *outlast* them. Not the ship, not the station. A machine whose only job was to keep a small amount of water going in circles forever." Her voice wobbled. "She said if anyone ever needed to prove they were hers, they should prove they understood why that mattered."

Dov watched her enter the serial. Watched the checksum fail by one bit, same as always — and then watched Sefa flip the bit herself, manually, the way you'd turn a key you already knew fit.

The fragment opened like a flower.

Coordinates bloomed across the display: not just the relay's position but an approach corridor, a docking heading, a transmission window measured in hours rather than months. And beneath all of it, in plain text where no one had thought to hide it because no one was ever supposed to get this far, a header:

**ANEW STATION AUTHORITY — FOUNDER ACCESS — CIRCULAR 1**

Sefa sat very still.

"That's not a distress code," Dov said slowly. "That's an invitation."

"No." Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when they're holding something heavy very close to their chest. "It's a *return authorization*. This wasn't a signal for help, Dov. It was a signal for *homecoming*. Someone built that station so that a specific ship, carrying specific people, could come back to it — and they locked the door behind them with a key only family would know." She turned to look at him, and Dov saw that she was crying without any of the noise of crying, tears just falling straight down her face like rain down glass. "My grandmother didn't lose the Anew. My grandmother *sent* it."

* * *

Ulyana found them there two hours later, the fragment fully unpacked, Sefa's grandmother's logs spread open across every screen like evidence at a trial.

The android stood in the engineering bay doorway for a long moment — Dov had learned to read its stillnesses, and this one was the stillness of a thing deciding how much truth a room can hold. Then it stepped inside and said, quietly: "Show me."

Sefa showed it. The circular header. The return authorization. The key.

And then, because she was her grandmother's granddaughter and apparently couldn't do anything halfway, she showed Ulyana the rest — the deeper layer they'd peeled back once the fragment opened, the mission architecture underneath. The Anew hadn't been a colony ship that went missing. It had been an *experiment platform*, launched under colonial charter, crewed by volunteers who'd been told they were pioneers and were actually test subjects. Relativistic stasis cycling — time folded and refolded, bodies wound backward through their own development like tape spooled the wrong way. The founders of Meridian's Rest hadn't fled a dying Earth and gotten lost. They'd *built* the thing that lost them, here, at the edge of dead space, and then come back to the inner system and founded a colony on the proceeds, and buried the station's existence under sixty years of silence, and called the missing ship a tragedy.

"They knew," Sefa said. Her voice didn't shake anymore. It had gone somewhere past shaking, into a country Dov recognized from funerals. "The whole founding generation knew. Grandma Amara knew. She helped design the stasis lattice — it's in her early logs, before she started writing about water recyclers and harvest yields. She helped build the thing that turned her own daughter into a child. Turned *me*—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm not sick, Dov. I'm not cursed. I'm a *result*. I'm page forty of somebody's report."

The bay was silent except for the coolant lines ticking.

"And the cover-up," Ulyana said at last, in that even voice it used for hard things, "explains the creditors. Halloran's employers didn't file salvage claims on the Anew because it's valuable. They filed because it's *dangerous*. If the returners exist, the experiment exists. If the experiment exists, the receivership's founding families are answerable for it." Its photoreceptors moved from Sefa to the screens. "Your colony wasn't abandoned, Sefa. It was *paid for*. Meridian's Rest exists because someone bought its silence."

Dov felt something shift in his chest, some load-bearing assumption giving way. He'd spent his whole life believing Meridian's Rest was a failure — a place the galaxy had tried and given up on. It had never occurred to him that the galaxy might have been *paid* to look away.

"So everything we were," he said. "Every bad harvest, every debt, every person in Ulyana's archive. We weren't unlucky. We were *kept*."

"You were kept poor," Ulyana said. "There is a difference, and it has a ledger."

Sefa laughed — one short, cracked sound with nothing funny in it. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and looked at the header glowing on the screen: **FOUNDER ACCESS**.

"I keep waiting to feel like it's not mine," she said. "The betrayal part. Everyone lied, everyone buried it, my own grandmother helped build the machine that broke us — I should feel like none of this belongs to me." She touched the screen, over the words, the way you'd touch a gravestone. "But it's her handwriting on the lock, Dov. Whatever else she did, she made sure there *was* a lock, and that the key was something small and faithful — a water recycler, of all things — instead of a password some company could buy. She hid the door home inside the most ordinary thing she ever loved." She looked up at Ulyana. "She knew exactly what kind of person would eventually come looking. Someone who fixes things. Someone who stays."

Dov cleared his throat, which had gone unreliable. "So the past isn't innocent."

"The past is a debt too," Ulyana said. "The only question is who pays it forward."

* * *

They brought it to Osei that evening, in the shuttle's cramped common room, with the children gathered in a loose ring around the walls — twenty-two of them, watching with the terrible attentiveness of children who have learned that adults' silences contain the real news.

Osei listened without interrupting, which Dov had learned was how he grieved: administratively, holding himself together with procedure until procedure ran out. When Sefa finished, he was quiet for a long moment, hands folded, an old man sitting in a room full of children who were older than the colony itself.

"So the founders committed a crime," he said finally, "and then built a civilization on top of it, and then died owing it."

"And left us the receipt," Sefa said.

Osei nodded slowly. Then he did something Dov had never seen him do in fifteen years of knowing him: he smiled, small and crooked and exhausted, at a room full of impossible children.

"Then we're not orphans," he said. "We're heirs. There's a difference, legally speaking, and I intend to spend whatever's left of my career exploiting it." He looked at Ulyana. "How long to the relay?"

"At current burn, nineteen days. But the transmission window the fragment specifies opens in twelve." Ulyana paused, and Dov heard the hesitation in it, rare as snow. "Administrator — the window is not for our arrival. It is for a *response*. The station will expect to hear from the Anew. It will expect founder authentication codes, crew manifests, mission reports. If we arrive unable to answer—"

"Then it may treat us as intruders," Osei finished.

"Worse," Sefa said. Everyone turned. She was standing now, holding her grandmother's logbook against her chest like a shield. "If it treats us as intruders, it might treat us as *loose ends*. The people who built that station buried it once already. They built it to be quiet." She swallowed. "And there's one more thing. At the bottom of the fragment. I didn't want to say it in front of the little ones."

"Say it now," Osei said.

Sefa keyed the last line of the decrypted fragment onto the main display. Dov read it twice, feeling the floor tilt under a sentence.

**CUSTODIAN UNIT ACTIVE. DO NOT APPROACH WITHOUT FOUNDER KEY. IT HAS BEEN WAITING A LONG TIME.**

In the silence that followed, Ulyana spoke, and its voice was strange — stripped of its usual evenness, almost human in its uncertainty.

"Sefa. Your grandmother's logs. The early ones, the engineering records." A pause. "Do they mention the custodian's production line?"

Sefa flipped pages, fast, her lips moving. Then she stopped, and went pale in a way that had nothing to do with the shuttle's dim light.

"Yes," she whispered. "Line seven." She looked up at the android. "Ulyana — you're line seven."

Outside the viewport, dead space rolled past, black and patient, and somewhere ahead of them an ancient station held its breath around a machine that had been waiting two hundred years for family — or for an excuse.
