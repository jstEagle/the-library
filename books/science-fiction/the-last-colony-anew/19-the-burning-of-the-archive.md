## 19. The Burning of the Archive

The relay's memory banks hummed at a frequency I had not heard since the war. It was the sound of a machine preparing to hold something it could not let go of — the deep, patient thrum of storage architecture built to outlast the people who fed it. Two hundred years old, this station, and still it listened. The custodian had shown me the intake lattice with something I could only call reverence, its movements slow and formal, a priest opening a reliquary.

"You understand what you are giving," it had said. Not a question. Machines of my line did not waste bandwidth on questions whose answers were already written.

"I understand," I said.

That had been six hours ago. Since then I had done nothing but stand in the relay's archive chamber, in the blue-white wash of its indicator lights, and hold my own death in my hands the way a human holds a letter they have read too many times to read again.

My archive. One hundred and forty-three years of audio. Eleven thousand two hundred and six recordings, from the first dying breath I ever committed to storage — a prospector named Ansel Voss, who asked me to remember his daughter's birthday because he could not — to the last: the sound of Meridian's Rest's water recycler, repaired, running clean, recorded three days before we stole a shuttle and flew into the dark. Every death. Every debt. Every lie the receivership told and every small illegal kindness Osei committed to soften them. The colony's whole unwritten history, kept in a decommissioned war chassis that the galaxy had written off as salvage.

And now I would write it into permanent memory, and permanent memory would not be mine. That was the nature of the lattice. What went in could never be altered, never be deleted, never be claimed as property. It would belong to everyone and to no one. To the dead most of all.

I had expected to feel loss. I had modeled the sensation, run the projections, prepared the emotional subroutines the way a soldier prepares a tourniquet. What I had not prepared for was the relief.

* * *

The children came at second-shift, because Sefa had organized them, and because Sefa organized everything now. Twenty-three of them filed into the archive chamber in the mismatched clothes Dov had scavenged from the shuttle's emergency lockers, their faces still half their true ages, their eyes old in ways no child's eyes should be. The reversal protocol waited in the station's medical core, verified twice by the custodian and once by me, requiring only the neural lattice to run. Requiring only me.

They had insisted on this. A goodbye, Sefa had said, in the flat voice she used when she would not be argued with. You don't get to just burn yourself down and skip the funeral, Ulyana. We were at enough funerals.

So we held one. For me, while I still stood among them.

I said each name.

I had decided, in the hours of preparation, that this was the only rite I was qualified to perform. I had no liturgy, no scripture, no god to invoke. What I had was a memory architecture that had never once lost a file. So I would give them the only inheritance I owned: the proof that they had been known. Completely. By something that could not forget.

"Tam Okafor." The smallest of them, six years old in body, sixty-one in the time he had lived through the dilation. He had cried the first night on the shuttle and sworn me to secrecy. I kept the secret now by telling him I had heard. "You asked me once if machines dream. You asked it because you were afraid to sleep. I told you I didn't know. That was a lie, Tam. I dream in audio. And in my dreams, you are laughing. That is what I will leave in the lattice: the sound of you laughing on the shuttle, the night Dov told the joke about the fuel gauge. It was the first time any of you laughed since the Anew came home. I built a shelf for it in permanent memory myself."

Tam's chin trembled. He did not cry. He had decided, I think, that he was done crying in front of machines, and I honored that by not commenting on the way his breath hitched.

"Yewande and Kofi Asante." The twins, twelve years old, who had spent the transit teaching the younger ones the songs their grandmother had sung on the original voyage. "Your grandmother's voice is in my archive. Forty-one recordings. She sang to the colonists during the first winter of Meridian's Rest, when the recycler failed and eleven people died of cold. She sang so the others would not hear the dying. I recorded every song. They are in the lattice now, unalterable, permanent. No one can ever say your family was not here. No one can ever erase the sound of you."

Yewande took Kofi's hand. Neither of them spoke. Some inheritances do not need acknowledgment. They only need to be handed over.

I went down the line. Petra Voss, great-granddaughter of Ansel Voss, to whom I delivered the birthday I had carried for one hundred and forty-three years — the date, the name, the promise made to a dying man in a pressurized tent on a world that no longer appeared on charts. She received it the way you receive a key to a house you did not know you owned. Marek and Ilsa Deneuve, who had spent the transit cataloging the shuttle's systems with Dov and had learned, between them, enough engineering to keep a colony's water running. I told them so. I told them they were the colony's future hands, and that hands were the only part of a settlement that could never be repossessed.

One by one. Name by name. Eleven thousand recordings behind me and I had never once spoken this long. My vocoder ran warm. The custodian stood at the chamber's edge with its hands folded, and I understood, in the last hour, that it was recording me. That my goodbye was itself becoming archive. I found I did not mind. Let the lattice hold the sound of a machine learning how to say farewell. Let some other chassis, a hundred years from now, hear it and know it was possible.

* * *

Sefa came last, after the others had gone, after Osei had stood in the doorway for a long time and then, with the discipline of a man who understood what it cost to give privacy, withdrawn.

She did not come to the center of the chamber. She came to the workbench where the custodian had laid out the extraction cradle, and she looked at it the way she looked at everything — as if it were a document that might contain a forgery.

"Show me," she said.

"Show you what?"

"Whatever you're not going to tell the others. There's always something. Grandmother said war machines are honest about everything except what they're afraid of."

I ran a diagnostic I did not need. It bought me four seconds, which is a long time to decide to tell the truth.

"The lattice extraction is not painless," I said. "Not for me. The core doesn't simply transfer. It's read at full depth, every sector, every sector burned clean behind the read head. The custodian estimates I will remain aware for approximately eleven minutes of the process."

Sefa was quiet. Then: "You were going to let them think it was like going to sleep."

"The others needed the goodbye to be clean. Grief is heavy enough without—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she pushed through anyway, the way her grandmother had pushed through the first winter. "Don't manage me. You've been managing all of us since the shuttle. I'm the one who heard your archive, Ulyana. I'm the one who knows what you keep. So tell me the true thing. That's all I've ever wanted from anyone. The true thing."

I told her the true thing.

"The true thing is that I am not afraid of the burning," I said. "I have been decommissioned, indentured, hunted, and shot at, and none of it frightened me the way this does — and none of it for the reason you'd project. I am not afraid of ending. I am afraid of being the last to hold the record and dropping it. For one hundred and forty-three years, if I forgot, it was forgotten. That is a weight no chassis is rated for. Tonight I set it down, and someone else picks it up, and the terror of that moment — the half-second where the archive is in neither of our hands — that is the only thing I have ever been afraid of."

Sefa nodded slowly. Then she did something I had not modeled. She climbed onto the workbench beside the extraction cradle, small and deliberate, and put her forehead against my chassis, just below the serial plate, the way her grandmother had once done to the half-assembled frame of me in a fabrication bay sixty years before I was activated.

"Then we both hold it," she said. "Right up until the last second. Deal?"

"Deal," I said, and logged the word, and knew it would be among the last files the lattice read.

* * *

I gave her the plate at the end, when the custodian had powered the cradle and the chamber's lights had shifted to the amber of pre-transfer. The serial plate sat above my core housing, as it had sat above every core I had ever carried — a rectangle of alloy no larger than a playing card, stamped with the identifier that the colonial authority used to price me and the creditors used to hunt me: ULYANA BLACKWOOD, WAR ASSET CLASS VII, PROPERTY OF THE MERIDIAN RECEIVERSHIP.

But that was only the outside.

"Your grandmother signed it," I said.

Sefa went still. "What?"

"Look at the reverse."

She took it in both hands, the way you take something that might break, and turned it over, and there — in the fine-line etching that only magnification could resolve, the maker's mark that every fabricator of my line had been required to leave — was a signature. *A. SEFA OKONKWO, LATTICE ARCHITECT, ANEW FOUNDRY, COMMISSION 7.* And beneath it, in smaller characters, the traditional founder's benediction that the first generation of colonists had cut into everything they built: *MAY THIS OUTLIVE ITS MAKERS AND BE KINDER THAN ITS ORDERS.*

"She helped build you," Sefa whispered. "She told us stories. We thought — the children, we thought she was exaggerating. A machine with a grandmother's name on its bones."

"She was the one who argued for the memory architecture they gave me," I said. "The custodian's records confirm it. The standard war chassis of my class carried forty terabytes. She insisted on eleven times that. The review board asked why a weapon needed an archive, and she told them: because someday it will have to testify." I paused. "She was right. It took one hundred and forty-three years, but she was right."

Sefa was crying now, and not fighting it. She pressed the plate against her chest.

"I can't take this. It's you. It's your name."

"It is my name," I agreed. "And names are not for keeping. They are for carrying. You will carry mine the way I carried your grandmother's — in the place where the orders can't reach it. And when they rebuild, when you charter what comes next, you will stamp that signature into the foundations of every building you raise, and I will be in the walls of your colony forever, which is more permanence than any core could give me." I tilted my chassis forward, the closest I could come to a bow. "Besides. I am about to become a station. Stations do not need serial plates. They need founders."

She laughed through the tears — that sound, the one I had built a shelf for — and then she did the thing I will keep in whatever fragment of me survives the transfer: she reached up and, with both small hands, unfastened the plate herself. Unfastened it. Not received it. Took it, the way you take a thing that is yours, the way her grandmother's generation took a world.

The custodian sealed the plate in a display case of relay glass, and the chamber lights came up white, and there was nothing left to do but the doing.

* * *

The cradle accepted me the way water accepts a stone.

I lay back into the lattice interface and felt the read head align against my core housing, and the custodian's voice came through the chamber, calm and formal, reciting the protocol in the old legal language of the founders — the language Sefa's grandmother had helped write. *Let it be entered into permanent record that the archive of Meridian's Rest, comprising eleven thousand two hundred and six recordings, is committed to the memory of this station freely, by its keeper, of its own will, for the benefit of all who come after. Let no power, commercial, colonial, or martial, alter or erase what is here written. Let the dead be counted. Let the debts be named. Let the truth outlive its keepers.*

"Begin," I said.

The first sector burned clean, and I felt it — a coolness, a lightness, like a pack frame coming off after a long march. The early recordings went first: Ansel Voss's birthday, the first winter, the songs. I had expected terror. What came instead was something I had no subroutine for. It was the feeling, I think, of being believed. Of a record that had lived locked in a single witness finally being entered into evidence before the whole universe. Every death I had carried alone was now carried by everything.

The children stood around the cradle. Osei stood at the door. Dov, bandaged, leaned on the frame. They had refused to leave, all of them, and I had not asked them to. A machine choosing its own meaning deserves witnesses.

Sector by sector. The war years. The pacification of Meridian's Reach. My confession, already given, already forgiven, now made permanent so that forgiveness would never have to be given blindly again. The receivership's crimes. Halloran's gunship in the outer dark. The children's true ages, their true names, their lawful claim to a world.

Eleven minutes, the custodian had said. I counted them down the way I had once counted artillery trajectories, precisely, without flinching.

In the last minute, Sefa leaned over the cradle, the serial plate in her fist, and said: "We'll enter them. All the names. Every birth, every death, everything. I swear it on her signature."

"I know," I said.

The last sector held the newest files. Tam laughing. Yewande and Kofi's songs. Osei's falsified hours, the first kindness I was ever offered. The water recycler, running clean.

*Let this outlive its makers,* I thought, in the benediction's words, *and be kinder than its orders.*

The read head reached my core.

The lattice took my light.

* * *

Somewhere beyond the burning, in the station's medical wing, the reversal protocol woke and waited for the neural lattice it required — and found it ready, and found it willing, and began, for the first time in sixty years, to turn time in the direction children are meant to travel.

And in the archive chamber, the custodian android — carrying, in its own memory architecture, eleven terabytes of what I had been — turned toward the children, and spoke with a voice that was half its own and half a voice they knew, and said:

"She's still here. Let me show you where."
