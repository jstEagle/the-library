## 5. The Woman Who Signed It

The fragment arrived the way all dangerous things arrive: quietly, wrapped in something innocuous.

Xanthe had been running decryption passes on the children's testimony for six hours when her console chimed—a routine notification from a data-broker she'd used for years, a faceless entity called Lattice that sold salvaged records from decommissioned stations. The subject line read: *Kessari-era archive lot, partial recovery, as-is.* She almost deleted it. She had bought a dozen such lots since Halcyon, and every one had been scrubbed to meaningless static, the Consortium's erasure so thorough that even the gaps where information should be had been filled with plausible noise.

But this one was different. The file header was old—older than the standard encryption protocols, using a cipher generation the Consortium had deprecated two decades ago. Whoever had encoded it had done so in haste, or in hiding, or both. Xanthe fed it through her translation matrix, the one she'd built over thirty years of listening to dead languages, and watched the characters resolve.

It was a shipping manifest. Cargo classification: *biological containment, priority black.* Origin: Kessari Reach. Destination: a Consortium facility whose designation had been redacted—but not thoroughly enough. Beneath the redaction bar, half a character remained visible, and Xanthe's mind, trained on fragments, completed it before she could stop herself: **Ceres-9**.

Her hands went still above the keys.

There was an authorization block at the bottom of the manifest. Standard format: officer name, clearance tier, code sequence. The name field had been burned away with a precision tool—the kind of edit that left no residue, no shadow of what had been removed. But authorization codes were longer than names, harder to burn completely, and whoever had sanitized this document had been careless with the last four digits of the sequence.

**VOSS-7741-■■■■**

Xanthe stared at those four surviving digits for a long time. Long enough for the cabin's recycled air to cycle twice. Long enough for the hum of the Meridian's children playing three decks below to fade into white noise.

She knew those numbers. Not because they were familiar in the way a face is familiar. She knew them the way you know your own heartbeat—without ever having listened for it, without ever having thought about it, and yet with absolute certainty when it appears in front of you.

They were hers.

She pulled up her current authorization profile, the one issued when she'd signed onto the fringe routes fifteen years ago, after whatever came before had been sanded out of her record like frost from glass. Her clearance code began VOSS-7741. The last four digits were different. Of course they were different. Codes rotated. Clearance tiers changed. Twenty years was an eternity in bureaucratic time.

But the prefix didn't rotate. Prefixes were identity. And somewhere in the Consortium's vast machinery, a woman named Voss had signed a biological containment order out of the Kessari Reach, and that woman had carried the same identity-prefix that Xanthe carried now, sitting alone in a rented cabin on Halcyon Station with a decrypted manifest glowing on her screen.

*Priority black.* She knew what that meant. Every xenolinguist knew what that meant, even the ones who pretended not to. Priority black was the classification reserved for cargo that did not officially exist. It was the classification of things too dangerous to acknowledge and too valuable to destroy. It was the classification, historically, of people.

She thought about the word *containment*. Biological containment. Applied to a species. Applied to a civilization that had sung its language into the bones of dying crewmen so that someone, someday, might hear it.

Applied by her.

No—not applied by her. Applied by a woman who shared her name, her code-prefix, her clearance signature. A woman who existed twenty years ago in a part of Xanthe's life that felt, when she reached for it, like reaching into a room where the furniture had been removed and the walls repainted a neutral gray. There were shapes missing from that room. She had always known there were shapes missing. She had told herself, for fifteen years, that the missing shapes were grief—that she had lost people, sometime, somewhere, and the loss had hollowed her out. It was a comfortable explanation. Grief explained everything and demanded nothing.

This explained more, and demanded everything.

Xanthe sat back from the console. Her reflection looked at her from the darkened viewport: a thin woman in her fifties, gray-streaked hair pulled back, eyes that had spent so long reading other species' meanings that they had forgotten how to hold her own. Behind the reflection, Halcyon's lights wheeled slowly, indifferent.

"You signed it," she said aloud, to the reflection, to the woman twenty years gone. "Whatever it was. You signed it."

The reflection said nothing. Reflections never do.

* * *

Ivo Renk stood in the observation blister of the Consortium corvette *Aegis*, watching Halcyon Station turn beneath him like a specimen under glass, and tried not to think about how much he disliked his job today.

The order had come through twelve hours ago, routed through three intermediaries so that no single transmission bore the full weight of it. *Subject: Voss, X. Status: elevated concern. Directive: contain. Method: auditor's discretion.* Contain. Not detain—contain. There was a difference, and Renk had learned the difference early in his career, back when he still believed the difference mattered. Detention created records. Containment created silence. Detention said *we caught her*. Containment said *she was never here*.

He had flagged her queries himself, three days ago, almost as a formality. A fringe linguist pulling records on a de-aged freighter crew—that happened twice a year, usually cranks, usually harmless. But then she'd pulled the second query, and the third, and each one had been precise in a way that cranks were not. She wasn't asking *what happened*. She was asking *what was removed*. And there was only one kind of person who asked that question correctly: someone who already knew what shape the hole was.

Now he watched her ship docked at berth fourteen, small and unremarkable, and he thought about the file on his lap that he was not supposed to have opened. Personnel history: Voss, X. Fifteen years of fringe-route service, unremarkable, competent, deliberately invisible. Before that: a gap. Not a redacted gap—redactions left edges, and edges could be followed. This gap had no edges. It simply wasn't there, the way a river isn't in a desert, the way a voice isn't in a room after the voice has been taken out of it.

Except Renk had once audited the deep vaults on Ceres-9, early in his career, before he'd learned which doors stayed shut. And he remembered—vaguely, the way you remember a dream—a personnel file with the same name on it. Thicker then. He remembered thinking the photograph had shown a younger woman who looked like she was trying very hard not to be photographed.

He closed the file.

"Renk." His comms officer, Adeyemi, stood in the blister doorway, holding a tablet the way you hold a thing you don't want to touch. "Priority traffic from Ceres-9. Vault-direct level."

Vault-direct. Above his pay grade. Above, frankly, his survival grade, though he'd stopped saying that sort of thing out loud years ago.

He took the tablet. The message was short, which was how the worst messages always were:

*Confirm subject has not accessed original records. If acquisition attempt detected, deny access by any means. Subject's prior association with Project ERASURE is classified beyond your tier. Do not investigate further. Acknowledge and purge.*

Project ERASURE.

Renk read the name twice. He had worked the Consortium for nineteen years and he had never heard the project name, which meant either it was so deeply buried that even its name was contraband, or it was so routine at certain levels that no one bothered to classify it. Both possibilities made his stomach hurt in the same way.

*Prior association.* He looked at the words for a long moment. Prior association with the project that had erased—what? The Kessari? The records? The woman herself?

All of them, probably. That was the elegant horror of it. Erasure projects didn't distinguish between the erased and the erasers. They consumed their own instruments. Somewhere in the Consortium's architecture was a woman who had signed orders, done her work, been thanked, and then been edited down to a fringe-route ghost with a fifteen-year service record and a hole where her life used to be. And now that woman was asking questions, and the machinery that had hollowed her out wanted her contained before she noticed the shape of her own absence.

"Adeyemi," Renk said. "When we go over, we're going in plain clothes. No colors, no insignia."

Adeyemi raised an eyebrow. "Sir?"

"We're auditors," Renk said. "We count things. We don't make things disappear. Today I'd like to keep it that way as long as possible." He handed back the tablet. "And pull everything we have on Halcyon's medical intake logs for the past week. Everything. Unfiltered."

"That'll trip flags, sir."

"I know," Renk said. "Trip them quietly."

* * *

Back in her cabin, Xanthe did the thing she was best at in all the worlds: she listened.

Not to the children—not yet. To the manifest. To the silences inside it. Translation, real translation, had never been about the words. Words were the visible wake of meaning; the meaning itself moved underneath, in what was omitted, in what was repeated, in the rhythm of what a speaker chose not to say. She had learned that from the Kessari, although until this week she hadn't known where she'd learned it. Now every skill she owned felt like evidence. Every technique in her repertoire was a fossil of some contact she couldn't remember making.

She ran the manifest's metadata. Creation timestamp: twenty-two years ago, plus or minus sensor drift. Encryption key: generated from a Consortium root certificate that had been revoked nineteen years ago—which meant the document had been hidden, not archived. Archived documents got migrated forward with each protocol generation. Hidden documents got left behind, orphaned in dead systems, waiting for someone like Lattice to scrape them up and sell them to anyone with credits and curiosity.

Someone had wanted this manifest to survive but not to be found. Someone had buried it shallowly on purpose.

Or—she turned the thought over—someone had buried it and then been erased themselves, and the burial was all that remained of them. A dead-drop from a dead hand.

She decoded the cargo description field next. Most of it was standard containment boilerplate, the bureaucratic language of crates and cryo-units. But nested inside the technical specifications was a string of characters that didn't belong—not Consortium script, not any trade cipher she recognized. She isolated it, ran it against her linguistic matrices, and felt the floor of her understanding tilt.

It was Kessari. Degenerated, corrupted, run through machine transcription that had mangled it badly—but Kessari. The same language the children spoke. The same language she dreamed in.

The string translated, roughly, imperfectly, to: *the ones who sign are the ones who are signed away.*

Xanthe closed her eyes.

The Kessari had understood. In their final days—in the days of the culling, the days when the containment orders were being written—they had understood exactly what was happening to them, and they had understood something worse: that the people writing the orders would be consumed by them too. That the instrument becomes the material. That the pen signs itself eventually. They had hidden that truth inside a shipping manifest like a seed inside stone, and it had waited twenty-two years in an orphaned file for the one person damaged enough to recognize it.

Her own authorization code, sitting at the bottom of the document like a signature on a confession.

She thought about the dreams again. The language she woke with in her mouth, syllables dissolving like frost in sunlight. She had assumed the dreams were contamination—decades of signal-work bleeding into her sleep. But what if they weren't contamination? What if they were residue? What if the woman she had been, the woman who signed, had known even then what was coming for her own memory, and had hidden pieces of herself the way the Kessari hid pieces of themselves—in places the editors wouldn't think to look?

In the dreams. In the instincts. In the way she could hear a dead language's grammar in screaming.

Xanthe opened a private file. She titled it with a Kessari character she didn't consciously know the meaning of—one that had surfaced in her dreams often enough to feel like a name—and began to write down everything she now knew, everything she suspected, everything the manifest had shown her. Not for the Consortium. Not for the authorities. For the version of herself that might exist after the next editing pass, if one came. A message in a bottle, addressed inward.

*If you are reading this,* she wrote, *you signed something. You don't remember what. Find the children. They remember for you.*

Outside, Halcyon's lights kept turning. Three decks below, forty children were singing in a language that officially belonged to no one, and somewhere in orbit, a corvette with no markings was preparing to send two people in plain clothes to count things.

Xanthe saved the file, encrypted it, and split it across seven storage nodes with staggered release triggers. Then she pulled up the station directory and searched for one name: the archivist who handled deep-vault transfer requests. Because if the manifest pointed to Ceres-9—if her own signature pointed to Ceres-9—then the next step wasn't to run.

It was to go where the signing had happened, and stand in front of the empty shelf where her life used to be, and make someone tell her why.

The console chimed again. New message. Unknown sender, routed through the same deprecated cipher as the manifest.

Four words, in Kessari, in a hand that trembled:

*They are coming for you.*

Xanthe looked at the message for a long moment. Then, with steady hands, she began to pack.
