## 6. Hollow Archive

Ceres-9 turned in the dark like a thought no one wanted to finish.

Xanthe watched it grow on the approach display for eleven hours, a gray seed of rock wrapped in the pale lattice of the Consortium's memory vault—a structure so old it had been built when the Consortium still bothered to name things honestly. The official designation was *Institute for Continuity Studies*. Everyone in her trade called it what it was: the place where forgetting was manufactured.

She came in quiet. No petition filed from Halcyon, no queries routed through standard channels—she'd learned that much already. Every request she'd made aboard the station had come back wrong in ways too small to prove: timestamps shifted by minutes, cross-references pointing to records that existed only as citations of other citations. The Meridian's voyage log, folded into nothing. A paper trail that led to a locked door painted on the wall.

So she paid a data-broker in Halcyon's lower rings three months' salary to forge her credentials into something older and duller than herself: a mid-tier linguistics auditor, cleared for routine archival review, unremarkable enough to be invisible. She had made invisibility a profession once. It seemed the skills hadn't rotted.

The vault swallowed her shuttle without ceremony. Gravity here was spun up to a gentle third of a g; the corridors smelled of cold metal and ozone and, beneath it, something faintly organic—the climate systems seeded with engineered moss to scrub the air, a fashion from forty years ago that no one had bothered to remove. The moss glowed faintly along the wall seams, a green ghost-light that made the whole station feel underwater.

"Auditor Voss." The reception archivist didn't look up from his console. He was very old, or very well-preserved—hard to tell at this distance—with the translucent skin of someone who'd spent decades away from any sun. "Your clearance covers Tier Three. Retrieval bay is down the spine, second rotunda."

"I'm looking for personnel memory-records," Xanthe said. "Linguistic division. Older acquisitions."

That got his eyes up. Not alarmed—just a flicker, the way water flickers when something moves beneath it. "Memory-records are Tier Five."

"They're mine."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the corridor, a ventilation fan cycled with a sound like slow breathing.

"The vault doesn't distinguish between what people remember and what people are owed," the archivist said at last, and there was something strange in his voice—not quite sympathy, not quite warning. Something wearier than both. "But Tier Five access requires a sponsor signature. Current one."

"And if the person who signed my original file is dead?"

His gaze held hers for a moment longer than procedure allowed. Then he looked back down. "Rotunda two," he said. "The retrieval bays will explain the rest."

* * *

The rotunda was a cylinder of pale ceramic, ringed with alcoves, each alcove holding a single retrieval cradle: a reclined chair threaded with filament leads, above it a lens array like the eye of something patient and blind. Memory-vaults didn't store data the way ships did. They stored *people*—or rather, the difference between what a person was and what they'd been permitted to remain. Every edit left a scar in the neural substrate, and the vault kept the scars the way a hospital keeps amputated limbs: catalogued, preserved, never spoken of.

Xanthe settled into the cradle nearest the door. The filaments found her ports without being guided, which meant they'd been calibrated recently, which meant someone had expected visitors.

The interface bloomed behind her eyes.

*Requesting: VOSS, XANTHE. Personnel acquisition record. Original intake, linguistic division.*

A pause. Then the system spoke in a voice like warm milk:

*Record located. Integrity status: ninety-one percent.*

Ninety-one percent sounded almost reassuring. She knew better. In archival terms, integrity below ninety-five meant the record had been *handled*. Above ninety-nine meant untouched. Ninety-one meant someone had opened her, carefully, with good tools, and put everything back where it belonged—except for the parts they took.

*Display.*

The record unfolded around her like a room she'd lived in all her life and never seen.

Her intake interview: a younger Xanthe, twenty-six years old, sharp-faced and sleepless, talking too fast about phoneme drift in pre-collapse colony dialects. She remembered this. She remembered *choosing* to remember this—it was one of the stories she told about herself, the ambitious young linguist who talked her way onto the fringe routes because the core worlds bored her.

Except now she could see the seams.

They showed as texture, in the way the memory rendered sound. Real memories had grain—ambient noise, stray sensation, the itch of the chair, the hum of lights. This one was smooth as glass at its edges. And at the edges was where the edits lived. Her eyes kept sliding off a figure seated beside the interviewer, a presence her younger self clearly saw and addressed but whose face resolved into nothing, a smudge of authority. Someone had been cut out and the wound smoothed over so cleanly that only a mind trained to hear false phonemes could hear the false silence.

*Isolate removed subject,* she whispered.

*Subject data absent,* said the system. *Adjacent metadata fragment retained: authorization code KX-4471-V.*

KX-4471-V. Her own code. The same string that had surfaced in the decrypted fragment aboard Halcyon, signing off on a quarantine order dated twenty years ago—an order concerning a species the archives insisted had already been extinct for a decade before the date on the document.

She made herself keep going.

*Requesting: full edit history, VOSS, XANTHE.*

*Edit history classified. Sponsor access required.*

*Requesting: number of edits.*

A pause, longer than any before it.

*Nine,* said the system.

Nine. Nine surgeries performed on her past, and she had felt none of them, noticed none of them, mourned none of them. She lay in the cradle with the filaments humming against her skull and tried to understand what a person was if nine pieces of them could be lifted out and replaced with smooth glass, and the person went on walking, working, dreaming in languages she couldn't name—

Dreaming. The word landed like a dropped tool.

*Requesting: dream-state anomalies logged during post-edit recovery periods.*

*No such logs exist.*

Of course they didn't. But the question itself had told her something: whatever the Kessari signal-patterns were doing inside her head, whatever language she dreamed in, it had survived nine edits. Either the editors hadn't found it—or they had, and decided to leave it, the way you leave a fuse burning because you want to see where it leads.

* * *

She found the old archivist afterward, in a maintenance gallery overlooking the deep stacks—kilometers of cold storage descending into Ceres's rock, row after row of sealed canisters, each one holding somebody's amputated years.

"You knew," she said, standing beside him at the rail. "When I asked about memory-records. You knew what I'd find."

He didn't deny it. Below them, a service drone moved along the stacks with a light like a single falling star.

"I've worked here thirty-one years," he said. "Do you know how many integrity reports I've flagged? Records opened, altered, resealed? Thousands. Tens of thousands. Do you know how many investigations those flags produced?" He turned his translucent face toward her. "One. And the investigator was promoted."

"Whose records?"

"Anyone who got close to anything." He said it flatly, a man reciting weather. "Witnesses. Auditors. Archivists." A pause. "Archivists especially. You think the moss is why the air smells sweet? We're all edited here, Auditor Voss. Some of us just have the professional curiosity to check."

"Then check yourself. What did they take from you?"

Something moved across his face—grief trying to find its object and failing, reaching into rooms that had been emptied before it arrived. "I don't know," he said simply. "That's rather the point. But I know the shape of the hole. There's a woman I lunch with every week. Marta, from hydroponics. And every time she laughs, I feel—" His hands flexed on the rail. "I feel like I'm remembering someone else laughing. Like the feeling belongs to a different face. That's what an edit feels like from the inside, if you're unlucky enough to notice. Not absence. *Displacement.*"

Xanthe thought of the smudged figure in her intake memory. Of the quarantine order bearing her code. Of the children on Halcyon Station speaking a dead tongue with living mouths, and the way understanding had risen in her before she could stop it—recognition arriving ahead of permission, like a guest through a door she didn't know she'd left unlocked.

"If I wanted the originals," she said slowly, "the pre-edit substrate. The scars themselves. Where would they be?"

The archivist was quiet for a long moment. The drone's starlight fell and fell among the canisters below.

"Deep stack," he said finally. "Sub-level nine. Cold storage for *contested* records—the ones flagged for review and never reviewed. If your substrate exists anywhere, it's there." He looked at her then, fully, and she saw that his eyes were wet. "I never told you that. Understand? If anyone asks, you found the manifest yourself. Because if they open me again, I don't know what they'll leave behind—and I've grown attached to whatever this is."

"What *is* this?" she asked. "Helping me?"

He smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen in years. "Institutional corruption isn't monsters, Auditor Voss. It's a building. Walls, floors, load-bearing lies. Everyone lives inside it. Everyone maintains it. And every so often—" he tapped the rail, twice, softly, like knocking on a door "—someone finds a crack."

* * *

Sub-level nine breathed cold.

The lift let her out into a dark that the glow-moss didn't reach, where frost furred the canister seams and her own exhalations hung in the air like small ghosts. The contested archive ran in long rows into the black, and the manifest terminal at the entrance glowed a sickly amber, waiting.

*Xanthé Voss,* she entered. *Original neural substrate. Pre-edit.*

The terminal considered.

*Record located. Canister 9-1147-K. Status: flagged for destruction, order pending.*

Pending. Not scheduled—pending, which meant someone upstairs had argued about it, which meant somewhere in the Consortium's machinery her erased self was still a live question, a bone not yet buried deep enough.

She pulled up the retrieval request. Sponsor signature required.

Her forged credentials wouldn't reach this deep. Her real code—KX-4471-V—was burned; using it would light her up on every monitor in the system. She stood in the freezing dark with her breath unraveling in front of her and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that she had perhaps hours before Renk's people traced the broker, the forgery, the shuttle. Hours before the machinery that had edited her nine times decided ten was cleaner.

The terminal waited, amber and indifferent.

And then, unbidden—from some sediment-layer of herself older than the edits, deeper than the glass—rose a string of characters. An override format. Old. Deprecated. The kind of backdoor that got built into systems by their architects and forgotten by their masters.

She didn't know where she knew it from.

That was the point, wasn't it. That was the whole shape of her life: knowledge arriving ahead of permission, a guest through doors she didn't know she'd left unlocked. Whoever she had been, twenty years ago, had built doors into the Consortium's own house.

Xanthe Voss began to type.
