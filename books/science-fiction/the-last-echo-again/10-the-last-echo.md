## 10. The Last Echo

The silence after the broadcast was not silence at all.

Xanthe sat on the deck of the long-range array's control room, her back against a console that still hummed with the heat of transmission, and listened to the network breathe. Somewhere beyond Halcyon Station's thin hull, the signal she had woven—Kessari testimony braided into the carrier wave of every freight channel, every colony relay, every listening post in the Verge—was propagating outward at the speed of light, unstoppable now, unstrippable. To filter it out would mean filtering out everything. The Consortium could shut down its own relays, but it could not shut down the sky.

She knew this because she had built it to be known this way. She remembered building it.

That was nearly all she remembered.

* * *

The first answer came eleven hours later, from a mining platform orbiting a gas giant whose name Xanthe did not know anymore.

The array's passive receivers caught it: a burst of structured sound, brief and trembling, riding the same carrier frequency. Not noise. Grammar. The distinctive nested cadence of Kessari—the rising triple-pulse that meant *we hear you*, the falling fourth tone that meant *we are afraid to answer*, spoken anyway.

One of the children—Sefi, small and solemn, her de-aged hands too clumsy for the console she'd learned to operate in another life—translated without being asked. Her voice was high and clear in the control room's cold air.

"They said: *We thought we were the only ones who remembered.*"

Xanthe looked at her and found, to her quiet horror, that she did not understand the words. Sefi had spoken them in Standard, and Standard had become a foreign country overnight. The shapes of the sentences were there—she could see them, feel their edges—but the meanings slid away like water through fingers held too tightly. She nodded anyway. She smiled anyway. Some things crossed the border without language.

More answers came. A farming collective on Thessaly Reach. A lighthouse-keeper on an asteroid nobody had bothered to name. A whole colony on Marrow, silent for six years, suddenly transmitting in fluent Kessari, as if they had been holding their breath for half a decade and had only been waiting for permission to exhale. Each response arrived with the same structure, the same grammar of grief and relief, and each one passed through Xanthe like light through glass—visible, beautiful, incomprehensible.

She had spent her memories buying the truth its freedom. Every word of Standard she'd ever learned, every human tongue she'd mastered across thirty years of fringe routes and dead beacons—all of it had gone into the encoding, ground fine and packed into the signal's architecture so thoroughly that no algorithm could separate testimony from language. The price of an unbreakable message was the messenger's own voice. She had known that when she made the choice. She could feel the shape of having known it, the way you can feel the shape of a missing tooth.

What remained was strange and spare. She remembered how to walk, how to eat, how to pilot a ship by muscle memory alone. She remembered warmth and cold, hunger and its easing. She remembered faces without names—Sefi's solemn eyes, the tall boy Darien who had once been the Meridian's chief engineer, the archivist Wren from Ceres-9 with ink-stained fingers and a laugh like a struck bell. She remembered *that* she loved them. She no longer remembered why, or when, or what she had called the feeling.

And she remembered the Kessari. That was the cruelest mercy of it. The alien tongue lived in her untouched, pristine, because she had never spent it—had only ever spent the languages wrapped around it. She woke each morning knowing how to say *the sea remembers longer than stone* in a language no living being had spoken before last week, and not knowing how to ask for breakfast.

* * *

On the third day, Sefi brought her a slate.

They sat in the observation blister at the top of Halcyon's spindle, where the stars turned slowly past the glass and the station's rotation made the distant nebulae wheel like slow birds. Sefi set the slate between them. On it, in careful child's handwriting rendered in Kessari glyphs, was a single word.

"*Vessha*," Sefi said. "It means *beginning*. Or maybe *morning*. The old ones used it for both."

"The old ones," Xanthe repeated, and heard the words come out in Kessari, correct and effortless, while the Standard translation floated somewhere just out of reach.

Sefi grinned. "You're getting good."

"I'm getting *empty*," Xanthe said, also in Kessari, and then laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her—at the absurdity of it. Complaining fluently in a dead species' tongue about the loss of her own.

"It's not dead," Sefi said quietly. "You made sure of that." She tapped the slate. "Lesson two."

They worked through the morning. *Vessha*, beginning. *Oleth*, the long dark between stars, but also patience. *Kesseth*—and here Sefi paused, because the word meant both *Kessari* and *to remember aloud*, and the children had decided this was not a coincidence but a definition. A people was the act of remembering itself. Erase the remembering and you had erased nothing; the remembering would simply find new mouths.

Darien came up sometime after second shift and joined without asking, folding his too-small body onto the deck beside them. He had been teaching himself to rebuild the station's atmospheric processors with hands that couldn't yet span the tools, and he talked as he wrote glyphs on the slate—haltingly, in a mixture of tongues, the way all of them spoke now. The Meridian's children were becoming translators in both directions, bridges with a foot on either shore. They remembered Standard. They carried Kessari. And Xanthe, who had once been the greatest xenolinguist of her generation, sat among them as a student, learning her own future one syllable at a time.

There was no humiliation in it. That surprised her most. She had expected shame, or grief, or the gnawing hunger for the self she'd spent. Instead there was something almost weightless about it—being a woman with no past, in a room full of people building one.

"Tell me about her," Xanthe said once, gesturing vaguely at herself. "The woman I was. You knew her."

Darien considered. "She was tired," he said finally. "Tired and very sharp, like a knife someone used too long without sharpening. She looked at us—" his voice caught, the child's throat struggling with an adult's memory— "she looked at us like we were evidence. And then somewhere in the middle of it she started looking at us like we were *children*. I think that's when she decided."

"Decided what?"

"To stop being careful."

Wren, arriving with food trays, snorted. "She was never careful. I read her file. Twenty years of queries the Consortium flagged and buried and flagged again. She was a splinter working deeper the whole time." Wren set down the trays and touched Xanthe's shoulder. "You didn't lose yourself, Voss. You spent yourself. There's a difference. Spent is what fuel is. Lost is what ships are."

Xanthe turned the phrase over in her mind and could not translate it into anything, and did not need to. It was true in the way music is true.

* * *

The answers kept coming.

By the end of the first month, four hundred signals. By the end of the second, thousands—colonies answering in Kessari, then in hybrid tongues, Kessari grammar wearing human words, human sentiment wearing Kessari cadence. New dialects blooming in real time across the network, none of them controllable, all of them descended from the testimony of a murdered people and the stolen voices of forty children. The Consortium issued denials. The denials were answered with more testimony. The Consortium cut relays. The colonies routed around them. You cannot quarantine a language. Xanthe had known that once, professionally, and now she knew it in her bones: a tongue is not information. It is a *population*. It lives in whoever will speak it next.

Ivo Renk sent a single message in the third week. No threat, no offer. Just coordinates and a timestamp and one line, which Sefi read aloud in careful Standard:

*"I have begun to forget which side I was on. Thank you for making it difficult."*

Xanthe listened to the translation twice, feeling the shape of something like forgiveness, or its possibility, and then asked Sefi to teach her the Kessari word for *complicated*, which turned out to be *selveth*, which also meant *a knot that chooses to hold*.

* * *

On the ninetieth day, the deep-space receiver woke them all.

A signal from outside the network entirely—from the dark past the Verge, past the old shipping lanes, from a region of space where the Kessari homeworld had once orbited a star that official records insisted had always burned alone. The signal was old. Decades old, maybe centuries, traveling the long way through dust and silence. It was weak, and repeating, and it was a question.

Sefi decoded it standing, both hands pressed over her mouth, tears running down her face, and when she could speak she gave it to the room in both languages at once, the way they all spoke now, the way the future sounded:

*"Is anyone still there? Is anyone still there? Is anyone still there?"*

The Kessari had sent it before the end. Before the culling. A final call cast into the dark from a dying world, addressed to survivors who did not yet exist—and now the survivors existed, thousands of voices strong, a whole constellation of strangers who had learned a murdered people's tongue in a single season, and the question had finally, after all its years of falling, landed somewhere with ears.

Xanthe stood at the console, surrounded by children and archivists and the accumulated answers of a thousand colonies, and understood that she was the answer. Not her memories—those were spent, scattered through the architecture of a broadcast, doing their work forever. But her voice. What remained of it. The part that had learned *vessha* and *oleth* and *kesseth* and *selveth* one syllable at a time from solemn-eyed children in an observation blister.

She keyed the transmitter herself. Her hands knew how without being told.

"This is Halcyon Station," she said, in Kessari, in the oldest pattern, the greeting-before-language that meant *we are here, we hear you, you are not alone.* "This is everyone. We are still here. We are still here. We are still here."

She sent it three times, because three was the number of hearing, and four was the number of courage, and so she sent a fourth.

Then she stepped back from the console, and Sefi took her hand, and together they waited—for the reply, for the dawn, for whatever voice would come next out of the long dark, learning to speak.
