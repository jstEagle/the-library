## 9. Counting Down

The countdown started in the waking world on a Tuesday, during arithmetic.

Teo knew it was Tuesday because Tuesdays were long-division days, and long-division days were the worst, and the worst days were when the numbers in his head got loud. But this was different. This wasn't the scratchy static of sums refusing to behave. This was a voice, low and patient, counting backward from somewhere behind his ears.

*Four hundred eleven.*

He looked up from his slate. Priya Raghavan, two desks over, was already looking at him. Her chalk had stopped mid-equation. Around them, other kids kept scratching away, oblivious, and Teo felt the strange hot loneliness of knowing something nobody else did — except that Priya knew it too, and her eyes said so.

*Four hundred ten,* said the voice.

Priya mouthed something. Teo read her lips: *You hear it?*

He nodded once, small, like they were passing notes under a teacher's nose.

*Four hundred nine.*

It didn't feel like the dream. The dream was fire and spinning corridors and a woman's voice that cracked on certain numbers, the way voices crack when they've been crying or running or both. The dream came at night and let go by morning, leaving only the greasy residue of fear and the smell of scorched metal that no one else could smell. This was daylight. This was clean. And it was *counting*, which meant it was going somewhere, which meant someone — or something — wanted them to arrive.

"Teo." Ms. Okafor's voice cut through. "The answer to number six?"

He looked down at his slate. He'd written *409* as the answer to a question about grain shipments. He erased it fast, heart hammering, and put down the right number, and Ms. Okafor moved on, but she paused at his desk a half-second longer than she needed to, and her hand rested briefly on his shoulder, and her face did something complicated that he couldn't read yet.

*Four hundred eight.*

* * *

They gathered at recess, the ones who heard it. Not all forty-seven — some of the younger ones still only dreamed, their days untouched — but nine of them now heard the voice while awake, and nine was enough for a council. They met behind the water reclamation sheds, where the drainage hum covered whispers, an arrangement that felt very grown-up and secret and slightly ridiculous all at once.

"It's getting closer," Priya said. She was the oldest of the hearing children at eleven, and she acted like it, arms crossed, chin up. "Last week it was at five hundred. It drops every day."

"So what happens at zero?" asked Milo Chen, who was seven and mostly scared but too proud to say so.

Nobody answered. That was the thing about the countdown: it implied an ending, and endings were either good or terrible, and none of them wanted to guess wrong out loud.

"It's not a ghost," Teo said.

They all turned to him. He hadn't planned to speak; the words just came up, pushed by something that had been sitting in his chest since the day on the pier when he'd seen the tall lady with the scarred hull-plating and known her, the way you know your own name.

"Ghosts haunt you," he went on, because now he had to finish. "Ghosts don't count down. Ghosts don't wait for you to be awake. My mom says a haunting is something stuck. This isn't stuck. It's—" He searched for the word and found it, surprising himself. "It's *sent*. Like a message. Like somebody's talking to us on purpose, a little bit at a time, so we don't miss any of it."

Priya frowned. "Why little bits? Why not just tell us everything at once?"

Teo thought about the dream fragments, how they never gave him the whole corridor, just pieces — a door, a shoe, a number. How the fire always cut off before the screaming started, like a vid skipping.

"Because if you send it all at once," he said slowly, "somebody might stop it. But if you send it in pieces, into dreams, into heads, all over the colony—" he spread his hands— "how do you stop that? You can't catch a message that's already been delivered."

The drainage hum filled the silence. Milo had gone pale. Priya's arms uncrossed slowly.

"You think someone's hiding it," she said. "From the grown-ups."

"I think the grown-ups already know something," Teo said. "Ms. Okafor's been weird. And there's a new lady going around asking kids questions about sleep."

That landed hard. Everyone had seen the auditor — the smooth one with the kind smile and the tablet full of friendly checkboxes. Wellness screenings, the teachers called it. Voluntary. A gift from Halcyon Reach, caring about the colony's children.

Milo hugged his knees. "My sister went yesterday. She doesn't dream anymore."

The words dropped like a stone into deep water. Nobody spoke for a moment, and Teo watched the others do the same math he was doing, the terrible new arithmetic: if the message was being sent piece by piece, then someone could also take the pieces back. Someone could reach into a sleeping head and subtract.

"They're erasing us," Priya whispered. "We're the archive."

* * *

Ms. Okafor found him after last bell, sitting on the pier with his feet hanging over black water, watching the freight traffic crawl between the stars. She sat down next to him without asking, which was her way. She smelled like chalk dust and engine tea.

"You heard it today," she said. Not a question.

"During division." He kept his eyes on the water. "Four hundred eight."

She exhaled through her nose. For a while she said nothing, and Teo counted barges, and the voice stayed quiet, resting maybe, the way voices rest between sentences.

"The screenings are tomorrow," she said finally. "Halcyon's people want all forty-seven of you in the clinic annex. 'Routine.'" Her jaw tightened on the word. "I've taught on three colonies, Teo. I've seen routine. Routine doesn't come with its own medical transport."

"What happens if we go?"

"I don't know." She said it flat and honest, the way she said everything, and somehow that was scarier than a lie. "But I know what I'm going to do about it, and I need you to help me, because you're the one they listen to. The older ones follow you. Even Priya, and she'd never admit it."

"Follow me where?"

"Tomorrow morning, there's a filtration fault in the east habitat ring. Water pressure advisory. All students in that sector get routed to the learning commons for supervised study." She looked at him sideways. "Filtration faults are unpredictable things. Sometimes they happen the night before."

Teo stared at her. Ms. Okafor, who made them copy rules into their slates, who docked points for untidy margins, was proposing to lie to a corporation. Something in his chest went bright and terrified.

"You'd get in trouble."

"I'm old enough to afford trouble." She stood, brushing off her skirt. "You're not. So tomorrow, when the notice comes, you look surprised. Can you look surprised?"

"I can look surprised."

"Good." She paused at the top of the pier steps, silhouetted against the dock lights. "And Teo — the voice you hear. When it counts, does it ever sound afraid?"

He thought about it. The voice was steady, patient, tireless. But there were nights, deep in the dream, when the fire got close and the corridor spun and the numbers tumbled out faster, stumbling over each other like someone running downstairs in the dark.

"Sometimes," he said. "When it gets to the bad part. It sounds like it's running out of time."

Ms. Okafor nodded slowly, and her face did the complicated thing again, and this time he could read a piece of it: grief, old and worn smooth, like a stone carried a long way.

"That's what I thought," she said. "Somebody's been running out of time for a very long while."

* * *

The filtration fault notice arrived at dawn, exactly as promised, and Teo looked surprised, and Priya looked surprised, and thirty-one children of the forty-seven spent the day in the learning commons doing unsupervised supervised study while the clinic annex sat half-empty and the smooth auditor's smile developed hairline cracks.

Sixteen went anyway. Families who couldn't afford questions. Teo tried not to think about them, and failed, and hated himself a little, and understood for the first time that surviving something sometimes means leaving people inside it.

In the commons, while the teacher-aide dozed over her slate, the voice came again — mid-morning, broad daylight, bold as anything.

*Ninety-seven.*

Gasps around the room. Nine heads snapping up, then twelve, then fifteen — more of them now, the waking listeners spreading like a spill. Priya grabbed Teo's arm hard enough to bruise.

"It jumped," she hissed. "It was at four hundred yesterday. It can't drop three hundred in a day."

But even as she said it, Teo understood, and the understanding went through him cold and clear like station-cold water. He raised his hand, got the aide's bleary nod, and pulled Priya and Milo into the supply closet between the shelving, where the smell of dry markers hid their whispering.

"It's not dropping," he said. "There's more than one count. Listen—" He shut his eyes and let the voice come, really listened for the first time, past the fear, past the fire-smell, listening the way Ms. Okafor listened to reading aloud, for structure underneath sound. "Under the big number there are little ones. Different speeds. It's not one countdown. It's lots of them. Hundreds. Thousands. All different lengths."

"Why would—" Milo started.

"Because it's not counting down to one thing." Teo opened his eyes. "It's counting down to everything. Every person. Every record. Every memory it's holding. Each one is running out separately, like—" his throat tightened— "like batteries dying. It's not sending us a warning. It's sending us *itself*, before it goes dark. Piece by piece. And some of the pieces are almost empty."

Priya's face had gone white. "Then the big number—"

"Is the whole thing," Teo said. "All of it together. How long until there's nothing left to send."

*Ninety-six,* said the voice, gentle as a lullaby, terrible as a funeral drum.

Through the closet door slats, Teo could see the learning commons, the aide, the sunlight through the dome, the ordinary colony morning going on above a buried crime. Somewhere out there was a ship with a scarred hull and a lady who looked at him like he was a ghost she recognized. Somewhere out there was whatever was counting. Dying, and spending its last light trying to make sure they remembered.

"We have to find it," he said. "Before ninety-six runs out. We have to find whoever's sending this, and we have to help them finish."

"How?" Priya breathed. "We're kids."

Teo thought of the pier, the tall figure with the hull scar, the words that had come out of him unbidden: *You were there.* He thought of how she'd gone still, like a machine whose whole program had crashed.

"No," he said. "But we know somebody who was."
