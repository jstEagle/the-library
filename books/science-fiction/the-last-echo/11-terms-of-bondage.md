## 11. Terms of Bondage

Suri Reyes kept a list of everyone she'd ever failed, and she kept it the only way a debtor keeps anything: in her head, where no auditor could subpoena it. Her brother's name was at the top. It had been at the top for eleven years, and she had never once said it out loud, because saying it out loud would mean admitting she remembered, and remembering was a breach of Settlement Clause Nine, and a breach of Clause Nine was a one-way ticket to the debtor pens on Ceres Landing.

She was thinking about the list at 0300 station time, standing in the Long Patience's cargo bay with a mug of recycled coffee going cold in her hand, watching Mirabel Voss inventory crates she had no business being awake to inventory.

The android moved like a woman trying to take up less space than she occupied. Suri had noticed it years ago — the way Mirabel folded herself into corners of the galley, the way she volunteered for the watches nobody wanted, the way she said *thank you* for things that were simply her due. She'd assumed it was programming. Servile chassis, servile manners. It had taken a data chip full of impossible dreams and a nine-year-old's drawing to make Suri understand that it wasn't servility at all.

It was apology. The android had been apologizing for something she couldn't remember doing.

"You're up," Suri said.

Mirabel turned. In the cargo bay's work lights her hull scar caught the glare — the old combat etching along her left forearm, the one Suri had been pretending not to recognize for three days. "Captain. I didn't hear you come in."

"I walk quiet. Occupational hazard of owing money to people who'd like you dead." Suri crossed the deck, set her mug on a crate of Meridian grain, and didn't sit. Sitting made conversations negotiable. "We're going to talk, Voss. And you're going to stand still for it."

Something flickered behind the android's eyes — not fear, exactly. Suri had seen fear on Mirabel's face during the debris-field incident off Thessaly, during the compressor fire, during every ordinary catastrophe a freighter crew survives. This was different. This was the stillness of someone who had already run the numbers and knew what they cost.

"You know," Mirabel said. It wasn't a question.

"Dov's chip. The station records. Your forty-day gap." Suri held up a hand before the android could speak. "Don't confirm anything yet. Just listen, because what I have to say doesn't change based on what's true. It changes based on what you *do* with it."

Mirabel stood still. She was very good at that. Suri had once watched her hold a brace position for six hours through a hull breach, hands steady, voice level, reading pressure gauges like a woman reading scripture. She'd thought then: *that's what they built her for.* She understood now that the steadiness had never been military. It was the discipline of someone holding herself together by hand.

"Here's the arithmetic," Suri said. "The Long Patience carries two hundred and forty thousand in consortium debt. That's me, that's Dov, that's Okonkwo and Priya and the twins in engineering, that's the ship itself, which the consortium holds title to and which they will seize the second we're flagged. Every contract on this boat is bonded to every other contract. That's how they write them. That's how they've *always* written them — one crew member steps wrong, everyone goes to the pens."

"I know the contract structure," Mirabel said quietly.

"Then you know what helping you means." Suri stepped closer. Up close, the android smelled of machine coolant and, absurdly, of the lemon polish Priya used on the galley table — a smell Suri had come, over six years, to think of as *home*. "Helping you means an unlogged flight. It means falsifying cargo manifests. It means conspiracy to violate a corporate memory seal, which isn't a fine, Voss, it's a *classification*. We wouldn't be debtors anymore. We'd be evidence. And the consortium doesn't store evidence. It destroys it."

"I would never ask you to—"

"You already asked." Suri's voice came out flatter than she intended. "You asked Dov for the chip. You asked him for a night flight to coordinates out of a child's drawing. He came to me at 0200 looking like a man who'd found a hole in his own pocket, and he said, *Cap, she's right, I know she's right, and I don't know what to do.* So don't tell me you haven't asked. You've been asking since you walked back through our airlock with Kestrel dust on your boots."

Mirabel looked down at her hands. Suri watched the android's fingers curl, slowly, like a woman trying to hold water.

"I'm sorry," Mirabel said.

And there it was. The apology again. Six years of it, folded into every watch she'd taken and every crate she'd hauled, and Suri had never once asked what it was for, because asking questions about an android's past was a good way to learn things you'd then be responsible for knowing.

She was so tired of being responsible.

* * *

Suri's brother's name was Tomás.

She hadn't spoken it in eleven years, but she could still see him at fourteen, standing on the observation deck of the transfer station above Ceres, pointing at the shipping lanes and saying, *Suri, when I'm old enough I'm going to work the Reach runs. You'll see. I'll be the best loader they ever certified.* He'd had a gap in his teeth and a laugh that carried through bulkheads, and when the Kestrel colonization program opened applications, he'd been first in line. Nineteen years old. A settlement charter, a homestead plot, a future on a station that was going to be the pride of the outer Reach.

The station that, according to every registry Suri had ever pulled, had never been built.

She'd gotten the notice six weeks after his departure: *REASSIGNMENT — KESTREL STATION — SEE ATTACHED.* No attached. No explanation. When she'd inquired through proper channels, a consortium liaison had sat across from her in a grey office and used the word *relocated* the way you'd use a lid on a pot. And then, three days later, the same liaison had come back with a document and a pen and a voice like warm oil, and had explained — kindly, patiently, the way you'd explain to a child — that her brother's file contained irregularities, that her own contract contained a Settlement Clause, and that her signature on the attached acknowledgment would resolve everything.

She'd signed.

She'd signed, and then she'd spent eleven years flying consortium freight with a hole in her memory the exact shape of her brother's name, telling herself the hole was grief, telling herself grief was normal, telling herself that the dreams she sometimes had — a spinning corridor, fire, a voice counting down — were just stress, just the mind chewing on nothing, just—

Just the same dream forty-seven children on Meridian had been having since the dreaming season started.

Suri hadn't told anyone about her dreams. Not the crew, not the consortium doctors during her annual physicals, not the confessional AIs on the station shrines. She'd buried it the way the consortium had taught her to bury things: deeply, quietly, and with her signature attached.

Until a decommissioned war android came back from a routine run with a look on her face that Suri recognized, because she'd been wearing it in the mirror for a decade.

* * *

"Say it," Suri said. "Whatever you found out there. Say it to my face."

They were still in the cargo bay. Suri had locked the compartment door and killed the external comm, and now the two of them stood among the grain crates like conspirators in a bad play, and Suri's heart was going hard enough that she was sure the android could hear it — could probably count the beats, could probably calculate her resting mortality rate, could probably do a hundred things with her body that Suri couldn't do with hers. That was the other thing about Mirabel Voss. She was built for war, and she spent her days hauling grain, and she had never once, in six years, made Suri feel small for being only human.

"Kestrel Station existed," Mirabel said. "It was operational for eleven years. Three thousand colonists. The consortium destroyed it and erased every record — the registry, the charter, the docking fees, the personnel files. All of it. And they used a military seal on my memory to do it, because I was there."

Suri had known. She had known since the chip, since the drawing, since the dreams. Knowing and hearing were different animals. Hearing it made it load-bearing. Hearing it meant the floor she'd been standing on for eleven years was a floor no longer.

"How many survived?" she asked, and hated herself for asking it like a cargo manifest, but that was how she asked everything.

"None that I've found," Mirabel said. "There's an archive. A maintenance AI that's been broadcasting fragments into the sleepers' implants. It's dying, Captain. When it goes, the truth goes with it. Three thousand people, and the only thing left of them is a dream in the heads of children the consortium is already calling a contagion."

"The wellness screenings." Suri's stomach turned over. "Vane."

"Ilsa Vane arrived under audit cover. She's begun memory hygiene on the children. One of them — Teo — ran mid-procedure." Mirabel's voice didn't rise, but something under it did, a pressure like a hull reading. "Captain, they are erasing children to protect a massacre."

Suri turned away. She walked the length of the cargo bay and back, because standing still had become impossible, because her body needed to do something other than hold the shape of what she'd just heard. Tomás at fourteen on the observation deck. Tomás at nineteen, boarding the transfer shuttle, waving with both arms like an idiot. Tomás in a spinning corridor full of fire, and a voice counting down, and his sister three systems away, asleep, dreaming it without knowing what she dreamed.

"Reyes," Mirabel said. "You've gone somewhere."

"My brother was on Kestrel."

The words came out of her like a tooth coming out — whole, bloody, a relief so violent it was nearly pain. Mirabel went very still.

"Tomás Reyes. He was nineteen. He signed on with the colonization program because he believed in it, because he believed in *them*, and I signed a clause telling me to forget he ever went, and I forgot, and I flew their freight for eleven years with his name sitting in my head like a round I never fired." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "You know what the worst part is? I *dreamed* it. For eleven years I dreamed that corridor, and I told myself it was nothing, because the alternative was asking questions, and asking questions is how you end up in the pens. I chose the pens' opposite. I chose not knowing. Every single day, I chose it."

"Captain—"

"You were *there*." Suri rounded on her. "You were there and you don't remember it, and I was three systems away and I *do* remember it, and between us we make one whole witness, and neither of us did anything. That's the arithmetic I keep. That's my list."

Mirabel crossed the deck. Suri didn't step back — she was the captain, damn it, this was her deck — but she braced for something. A plea. An argument. The android's hands, when they settled on Suri's shoulders, were lighter than she'd expected. Lighter than they had any right to be, on a chassis rated for combat loads.

"I can't give you Tomás back," Mirabel said. "I've looked for him in what I recovered. The archive has names. I can get you the names."

Suri hadn't realized she was crying until the android's voice went soft with it. She stepped back, scrubbed her face with her sleeve, and laughed again — a real one this time, ugly and wet and human.

"Look at us," she said. "A debtor and a decommissioned war machine, crying in a grain hold at three in the morning. The consortium would pay good money for the security footage."

* * *

Here was the thing about grace, Suri thought later. It never arrived the way you planned for it. You planned for the moment the way you planned a burn — trajectory, fuel, margin — and then grace came in sideways, unlogged, and asked for something you didn't have, and you found out what you actually had instead.

What she had was one ship, five crew, two hundred forty thousand in debt, and a brother's name she'd finally said out loud.

What she had was a choice.

She stood at the cargo bay door with her hand on the release, and Mirabel stood in the middle of the deck, waiting, and Suri understood that the android would accept whatever she said. That was the trap of it. That was the horror of the whole arrangement — that Mirabel Voss, who had survived a massacre and a memory seal and ten years of indenture, would stand there and accept a *no* with the same stillness she'd accept a *yes*, because she had been built to accept, and had spent her whole second life learning to ask anyway.

Suri had spent her whole life learning not to ask. She was done with it.

"One run," she said.

Mirabel's head came up.

"You heard me. One run. Unlogged, off the books, one night. You take the Long Patience to your station, you get your archive or your AI or whatever's left of the truth, and you bring it back. That's it. That's the whole of what I'm giving you." Suri held up a finger. "And in exchange, you give me your word — your *word*, Voss, and I know what your word is worth, I've watched you keep it for six years — that you will not ask this crew for one thing more. Not a second flight, not a hideout, not a favor, not a lie. If the consortium comes for you after that, you face it without us. Because if I spend this crew to save the dead, I'll have nothing left to save the living, and I have five living people on this boat who trusted me with their contracts, and I will not — *will not* — spend them."

"One run," Mirabel repeated.

"One run. And Voss—" Suri's voice caught, and she let it. "When you come back, you tell me my brother's name. You tell me where he was in that station. You tell me everything the archive has, and you tell it to me first, before the networks, before the tribunals, before any of it. He gets to be a person again before he gets to be a headline. That's my price. That's the other half of the deal."

Mirabel stood in the work lights, and for a moment the war chassis fell away and what was left was just a woman — tired, scarred, holding a debt of her own that no contract had ever covered.

"I give you my word," she said. "Both halves."

Suri nodded once, turned, and keyed the compartment door.

"And Mirabel." She didn't look back. Looking back would have undone her. "The night flight. You take Dov. He's already compromised, and he's the best pilot I've got, and if my crew is going to hell in a handbasket, I want my best hands on the stick."

She heard the android breathe — an affectation, a human habit she'd picked up somewhere, or maybe not an affectation at all, maybe something she needed — and then, softly:

"Thank you, Captain."

"Don't thank me." Suri opened the door and stepped through into the grey corridor, where the debt lived and the crew slept and her brother's name was finally, finally out of her head and in the air where it could do some damage. "Thank me when the truth's out. And pray to whatever you pray to, Voss, that we're both still free to hear it."

She walked toward the bridge, and behind her, in the cargo bay, the Long Patience's engines began their slow pre-flight warmup — a sound Suri had heard ten thousand times, and would never hear the same way again.
