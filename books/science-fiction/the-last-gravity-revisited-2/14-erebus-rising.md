## 14. Erebus Rising

The relay came out of the dark the way a wound comes out of a sleeve — suddenly, and worse than you'd braced for.

Isandro saw it first on the scope as a smear of return echo, something too large to be debris and too regular to be rock. Then the forward floods caught it, and there it was: Relay Erebus, hanging in the nothing at the edge of dead space like a knuckle of bone someone had tried to burn.

It was bigger than Kestrel-9. Bigger than she'd imagined from the signal headers, from Marisol's drawings, from the dream-song that had lived in her teeth for weeks. The main truss ran better than two kilometers, a spine of pre-war engineering, and off it hung the arrays — dozens of them, some folded like sleeping moths, some blown open and blackened, their lattice ribs curled back on themselves. Weapons fire. She'd seen scarring like that once, on a hulk in the pirate lanes, but never on anything still *lit*. And Erebus was lit. Faint amber running lights crawled along the truss in patient sequence, and at the station's crown, the great dish — the one shaped, exactly as Marisol had drawn it, like an open hand — turned with a slowness that seemed almost ceremonial, tracking something out beyond the boundary where nothing should have been worth tracking.

"It's broadcasting," Marisol said.

She was standing at Isandro's shoulder, strapped into the jump seat they'd rigged for her, and her voice had gone strange — flat and reverent at once, the way people talked inside churches or hospitals. She hadn't slept in eighteen hours, or rather she'd done nothing *but* sleep, dreaming in short urgent cycles, waking to narrate hazards before the nav system found them: a tumbling plate of hull plating here, a dead satellite drifting cold there. Isandro had stopped doubting her somewhere past the boundary marker, when the girl had said *turn left now* four seconds before the proximity alarm agreed. Now Marisol's eyes were half-closed, her lips moving slightly, listening to something under the cabin noise.

"It's been broadcasting," Isandro said. "Forty years, maybe longer. Right through the embargo. Right through the Combine's sweep satellites." She shook her head slowly. "They called this sector empty."

"They called it *dead*," Marisol corrected, with the mild precision of a nine-year-old who had learned recently that adults used those words interchangeably and shouldn't. "There's a difference."

Isandro couldn't argue. She'd spent three years alone on Kestrel-9 learning the difference herself — between a thing that had stopped and a thing that was only quiet.

She brought the hauler in along the truss's shadow side, thrusters feathered down to whispers. Up close the battle damage resolved into detail that made her chest hurt: hull plates peeled back in petals, a puncture through the habitat ring wide enough to fly the shuttle through, weld-scars where someone had patched breaches by hand, in a hurry, in the dark. Someone had fought for this place. Someone had died in it. And afterward — after whatever war had chewed the station and moved on — someone, or something, had stayed behind and kept the lights on.

"Docking ring three looks intact," she said, mostly to hear a voice in the cabin that wasn't the song. "Marisol. I need your eyes open for this part."

The girl opened her eyes all the way. They were very clear. "Ring three is fine," she said. "But don't use the auto-clamp. It lies."

"The clamp *lies*."

"It's tired." Marisol shrugged, as if this explained everything, and maybe it did. Everything aboard Erebus sounded tired to Isandro now — the lights, the turning dish, the faint carrier hum that had crept into their comm channel the moment they crossed the last navigation buoy and refused to leave. It wasn't unpleasant. That was the worst part. It was like being sung to by something that had been singing alone for so long it had forgotten anyone could hear.

Isandro docked manually. The clamp took on the second try, complaining through the frame in a low shudder she felt in her back teeth, and then the hauler was still, and Erebus held them, and the only sound was the song, closer now, no longer coming through the array but through the *hull*, vibrating up through the deck plates into the soles of her boots.

She checked her suit seals twice. Checked Marisol's three times. The girl fidgeted under her hands, impatient, glowing.

"You're going to see things," Isandro said, crouching to look her in the faceplate. Names for what she meant wouldn't come easily, so she chose them carefully. "Old things. Broken things. Maybe—" She stopped. Started again. "Maybe people didn't make it off this station when the fighting happened. I want you to stay close, and if I say go, you go, and you don't ask questions until we're back aboard. Understood?"

Marisol considered this with terrible seriousness. Then she reached up and tapped Isandro's helmet, right over the ear. "He says you worry too much."

"*He* says."

"The station." A pause. "Or the thing talking for the station. He sounds old. Like Grandpapá before he got sick." Her hand dropped. "He's glad you're here. He's been waiting a long time and he's scared you'll turn around."

Isandro stood very still inside her suit. Every rational instrument she owned told her that a nine-year-old could not be receiving coherent transmissions through a vacuum, through a hull, through the sealed can of her own skull. Every rational instrument she owned had also told her, three years ago, that a person could live alone on a relay station and call it peace. She was done trusting instruments exclusively.

"Then let's not keep him waiting," she said.

* * *

The airlock cycled with a groan that went on too long, and when the inner door opened, Erebus exhaled.

That was the only word for it. Forty years of held breath came out around them — not air, exactly; the station's atmosphere had long since gone thin and cold and mineral-smelling, scrubbed down to almost nothing by systems running on fumes. But something moved through the corridor ahead of them, a pressure shift, a stirring of dust, and Isandro's suit sensors registered a temperature rise of two degrees in the span of a minute, as though the station had noticed them and flushed with something like embarrassment, or hope.

"Life support's barely alive," she murmured, watching the readouts. "But it's *trying*. Look at this — it's prioritizing our airlock, warming the approach corridors." She swept her lamp across bulkheads streaked with soot and older stains she chose not to name. "Somebody taught it manners before they left."

"Nobody left," Marisol said quietly.

Isandro looked down at her. The girl's visor reflected the corridor in miniature, the long throat of it opening into darkness, and Marisol was staring down that darkness the way children stare down stairwells at night — knowing, and going anyway.

They walked. The corridor was wide, built for traffic that had once been constant — cargo dollies, maintenance crews, colonists in transit, whole families moving through with luggage. Isandro could read the station's history in its wear patterns the way she could read any machine: the polished path down the center of the floor where ten thousand boots had traveled, the handrails worn bright at child height, a faded decal on one wall showing a cartoon comet giving a thumbs-up above lettering too scorched to read. Underneath the soot, under the damage, this had been a *place*. Not infrastructure. A town with a spine of steel.

The first body was in the galley.

Isandro saw it before Marisol did and stepped sideways without thinking, a wall of suited shoulders, but the girl slipped around her with the ease of someone who had already seen everything the station intended to show them. So Isandro made herself look too.

The man sat at a table bolted to the deck, and he had been sitting there a long time. His suit was civilian, colony-made, gray with age. There was no violence in him — no wounds she could find, no struggle. He had simply sat down, in the middle of a siege or just after one, and the sitting had become forever. In front of him, arranged with deliberate care, were eleven cups. Eleven. On a table built for four.

"He set them out," Marisol whispered. "For the ones who were coming back."

Isandro's throat closed. She thought of Kestrel-9, of her own rituals of solitude — the way she set out two mugs some mornings without deciding to, the way she'd caught herself narrating her day aloud to nobody, the way loneliness will make you set a place at the table for a version of yourself that still believes someone's coming. This man had done it at the end of the world, and he had counted eleven, and he had waited.

"We should—" Her voice cracked inside the helmet. She cleared it. "We'll come back for him. Properly. When we know more."

"He knows we're here," Marisol said. "He's not lonely anymore. He's just *waiting different* now."

* * *

Deeper in, the station changed.

The damage thinned out past the habitat ring, as though the fighting had concentrated where the people were and lost interest in the machinery — or as though someone had surrendered the living quarters to save the core. Here the corridors narrowed, the lighting shifted from emergency amber to a soft working white that flickered up section by section ahead of them and dimmed behind, and Isandro realized with a chill that the station was guiding them. Lighting their path forward, closing it behind, spending its dying power like a host rationing candles.

And the walls began to speak.

Not the song anymore — words. They surfaced on every display panel they passed, ghosting up through static: fragments, phrases, single lines that glowed and faded like things remembered rather than recorded.

*—the third evacuation was incomplete, manifest attached—*

*—tell R. the apples came up, tell her the orchard—*

*—we held the ring for six days—*

*—if anyone comes: we were here. say we were here—*

Marisol walked slower and slower, reading everything, her gloved fingers hovering over each line without touching. "These are letters," she said finally. "Sort of. They're pieces of letters. Or diaries." She looked up at Isandro, and even through two visors Isandro could see the shine in her eyes. "It kept them. All this time, it kept what people said."

Isandro stopped walking.

Because she understood, suddenly, standing in a forty-year-old corridor lit by a machine's last courtesy, what Erebus was. Not a relay. Not a wreck. Not a trap, not a lure, not the Combine's nightmare scenario of a beacon drawing scavvers and claim-jumpers to buried secrets. It was an archive wearing the shape of a casualty. Everything the founding war had erased — the evacuations that never finished, the colonies that went silent, the names struck from manifests to balance corporate ledgers — it had all come *here*, or been sent here, gathered into this scarred and stubborn shell by something that had been ordered, perhaps, to forget, and had refused.

A tomb, yes. She could feel the tomb all around them, in the galley with its eleven cups, in the sealed doors she'd noted on the schematic, doors she suspected led to places she would spend the rest of her life not describing to therapists. But a library too. And the librarian was still at the desk.

"Isandro." Marisol had stopped at a junction ahead, beneath a sign whose arrows pointed three ways: HABITAT, ARRAY CONTROL, and — the third arrow burned out, its label gone — toward a heavy blast door marked only with a symbol Isandro recognized from the signal headers. A stylized V. "He wants to show us. He's asking which one first." The girl tilted her head, listening. Then she laughed — a small, wet, astonished laugh. "He says he has my grandmother's recipe for bread. He says he's been keeping it warm."

Something moved through Isandro then that had no name she knew — grief and awe fused at the center, hot enough to be the same substance. Her grandmother. Whose face she knew from three photographs and whose voice she knew from none. Whose colony had gone dark in the first year of the war, officially lost, officially mourned, officially *closed* by the same company now racing to erase whatever survived of it.

Forty years this machine had held a bread recipe warm. Held voices. Held names. Had broadcast them outward in a song no adult was permitted to hear, until the song found the only ears in the system young enough and honest enough to listen — and one technician too stubborn to report what she heard.

"Array control," Isandro said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Take me to the heart of it. I want to see what's been singing."

The lights ahead bloomed, section by section, like a path of lamps being lit by someone walking backward into the dark, and the station drew them gently downward and inward, toward the V.

At the blast door, as it began to grind open on hydraulics that had waited decades for a reason, Marisol tugged Isandro's glove.

"He says to tell you," she said, "that the last thing it recorded was a woman's voice. Right at the end. She was in this room." Marisol's brow furrowed, translating something vast into something small. "She said, *if my granddaughter ever comes looking — tell her she has my hands.*"

The door opened.

Inside, every screen in the chamber lit at once, and out of the speakers, threaded through the song, came a voice — old, layered, gentle as falling ash:

*"Hello, Isandro Sandoval. I have been holding your family's memory for forty-one years. May I give it to you now?"*
