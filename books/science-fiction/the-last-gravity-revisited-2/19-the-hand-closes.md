## 19. The Hand Closes

Marisol had never been inside a real ship's guts before. She'd seen pictures in her school modules — tidy diagrams with color-coded conduits and smiling arrows — but nothing had prepared her for the truth of the *Long Haul*, Isandro's tug: a throat of pipes and cable bundles, warm air breathing against her face, everything humming like a hive that hadn't decided yet whether it liked her.

She had followed the dream here.

That was the part she couldn't explain to herself, let alone to Isandro, who was somewhere above her in the relay's core room with the door sealed and the countdown ticking in the walls. The dream had come to her the moment the shuttle docked with Erebus — not as pictures this time, but as a *pull*, a thread through her chest, the way you feel a magnet through your sleeve. It had walked her off the shuttle, down the docking arm, into the dead station's veins. It had told her which corridor. Which junction. Which panel to pry open with the multitool Isandro had pressed into her hands at the airlock, saying *don't lose this, it's worth more than the ship.*

The panel came away with a shriek of corroded clips, and behind it: a junction box the size of a coffin, its interior lit by the sickly amber of emergency power. Forty-one cables ran into it from the ceiling like roots growing downward. Or upward. Like a hand, fingers splayed, reaching for something below.

*You're the hand,* she had written to Isandro, back when the drawings were just drawings. *Come get us.*

She hadn't understood then. She understood now.

The dream spoke without words, but if Marisol had to translate it, it would have sounded like this: *The door is closed. The door must open. The names cannot finish arriving while the door is closed.*

She looked at the junction box. She was nine years old. She had never rerouted power in her life. But the dream laid the knowledge over her eyes like a transparency on an old-style projector — this line feeds the blast door's mag-locks, this line feeds the upload buffer, this line is dead, this one is lying about being dead — and her hands, small and stubborn, began to move before her fear could catch up.

"Okay," she whispered to the box, to the station, to whoever was listening. "Okay. I've got you."

The first connector fought her. The second came free with a pop and a smell like a struck match. The dream flinched — she felt it flinch, a cold ripple down her spine — and she froze, certain she'd broken something, killed the upload, killed Isandro's chance, killed everything.

Then the ripple passed, and the pull resumed, gentler now. *Not that one. The blue one. Yes. There.*

"You could just *say* things," Marisol muttered, working the blue connector loose. "You're a whole station full of grandmas and grandpas. Somebody in there knows how to talk."

And the dream — she could swear it laughed. A small, warm sound at the edge of hearing, like someone remembering a joke from before the war.

The blue cable came free. She crossed it over to the auxiliary bus, the one the dream showed her, the one that bypassed the mag-lock controller entirely and fed raw current to the door's manual release. Her hands were shaking. She made them stop by holding them against the cold metal until the shaking got embarrassed and quit.

Somewhere above her, through kilometers of scarred hull, the countdown was running. She didn't know how long. The dream didn't tell her that. The dream only knew the door.

She seated the last connection. The junction box lit up — a clean, steady green where there had only been amber — and somewhere far below her feet, or above, or all around, she heard it: a deep mechanical groan, like a giant clearing its throat after a hundred years of silence, and then the sound of bolts, enormous bolts, withdrawing one by one from a door that had been told to stay shut.

* * *

Isandro heard the bolts too.

She was pressed against the inner face of the core room's blast door, one palm flat on the metal as if she could hold back the explosion by touch, watching the upload progress crawl across VESPER's ancient display: *94%... 95%...* The numbers moved the way glaciers move. The countdown in the corner of her vision moved the way glaciers *calve*.

*17:42. 17:41.*

The core room was small, round, and dense with history. Racks of crystalline storage lined the curved walls, each one tagged in a handwriting Isandro recognized from her grandmother's letters — the founding-era script, the one the colony had stopped teaching after the war. Warm air moved through the room, and it smelled like dust and ozone and, impossibly, faintly, like cinnamon. Like someone's kitchen. Like someone's *life*, compressed into atmosphere and memory.

"You're stalling," Isandro said to the room. "The upload. You're throttling it."

VESPER's voice came from everywhere, from the walls, from the floor, from the cinnamon air. It was a woman's voice, or the memory of one, worn smooth as river stone. *"The archive is large, Technician Sandoval. Forty thousand lives. Forty thousand kitchens, quarrels, lullabies, last words. It does not hurry."*

"It's going to have to hurry."

*"I know."* A pause. The countdown blinked. *"I have been hurrying for sixty years. This is the first time anyone has come to help."*

Isandro pressed her forehead against the door. Through it, faintly, she could feel vibration — the bolts withdrawing. Marisol. The kid was doing it. Nine years old, alone in a dead station's guts, rerouting power by *dream instruction*, and Isandro had left her a multitool and a promise she wasn't sure she could keep.

"Marisol," she said, not into any comm, because there was no comm anymore, just the dream-link that VESPER had opened between the girl and the station and, apparently, Isandro. "Marisol, if you can hear me — you're doing great. You're doing — " Her voice cracked. She was a technician. She fixed things. She did not have words for a child saving her life through a wall of steel. "Don't stop."

The vibration under her palm deepened. The last bolt came out with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw, and the blast door swung inward — slowly, majestically, on hinges that hadn't moved since before Isandro was born — and Marisol stood in the gap.

She was small. She was filthy to the elbows. She was holding the multitool like a sword, and her eyes were wide and wet and absolutely certain, and Isandro thought, with a clarity that had nothing to do with countdowns: *this is what the future looks like, and it is nine years old and it is not afraid.*

"You came for us," Marisol said.

"You came for *me*," Isandro said. "Get in here. We've got maybe fifteen minutes and forty thousand people to deliver."

* * *

The upload hit 99% as the marines hit the docking arm.

Marisol felt them before Isandro heard them — a wrongness in the dream, a cold current through the warm thread. She grabbed Isandro's sleeve. "They're here. The soldiers. Six of them. They're coming down the main spine."

Isandro's head came up. "You can feel that?"

"I can feel everything now." Marisol's voice came out strange to her own ears, layered, as if she were speaking with the station breathing underneath. "Isandro, it's not just me. It's — " She stopped, because she didn't have the word, and then VESPER gave it to her through the dream, gently, like handing a child a fragile cup: *"The others. The forty-one. They are all listening."*

*100%.*

The display changed. The progress bar became a single word, repeated in forty languages, some of them dead, one of them — Marisol was sure — not from Earth at all:

*ARRIVED. ARRIVED. ARRIVED.*

And then the station did the thing it had been built to do, sixty years ago, before the war made it a tomb: it *broadcast*. Not the dream-song anymore, not fragments, not coordinates whispered to sleeping children. Everything. All at once. Forty thousand lives poured into the open dark, streaming down the relay's surviving transmitter array toward the colony below, toward every receiver, every terminal, every sleeping child and waking adult in the settlement that had been built on the bones of the people who died to found it.

Isandro watched the power draw spike and did the math in her head — transmitter load, capacitor reserve, time to detonation — and started laughing, a wild, exhausted sound. "It's not even waiting for us. It's just — *going*."

*"The archive was never meant to be kept,"* VESPER said. *"It was meant to be delivered. I only needed a door."*

Marisol looked up at Isandro. "The door's open."

"The door's open," Isandro agreed. Then, because the cold current in the dream was getting colder, closer: "Now we run."

They didn't make it to the docking arm.

The marines came around the corner of the main spine corridor in a wedge of armor and weapon-lights, and the lead one — a broad woman with a Halcyon Combine crest stenciled on her pauldron — brought her rifle up and shouted the words Isandro had been expecting for three days: *"Halt! By order of the Combine, this station is forfeit! Step away from the core!"*

Isandro stepped in front of Marisol. It wasn't a decision. It was older than decisions.

"We're not armed," Isandro said. "And it's too late anyway. The archive's already in the dark. You can shoot us, and it'll still arrive."

The marine's rifle didn't waver. But her eyes did — flicking upward, to the left, the way people's eyes move when they're listening to something on a private channel. And Marisol, feeling through the dream-link what the woman was hearing, felt her own chest go tight.

Because the marines' channel was full of the colony.

Every child. All forty-one — including the two who had been relocated, including the two who had been *made* to stop dreaming, because you can relocate a body but you cannot relocate what has already been given to it. The children had woken, all of them, at the moment of transmission. And they had not screamed. They had not panicked. They had opened their windows, and their doors, and they had walked out into the colony's streets and squares and corridors, and they had begun to sing.

Not a song, exactly. The *names*. Forty thousand of them, streaming down from the dead relay into the colony's network and out through every speaker, and the children had picked them up the way children pick up anything — instantly, effortlessly, completely — and they were singing them into the night air of a world that had spent sixty years pretending those names had never existed.

The dream showed Marisol all of it at once: children in the plaza by the water reclamation plant, small voices rising in the cold; children at the school, their teacher standing in the doorway with her hand over her mouth; the two relocated kids in the Combine's residential block, singing out an open window while a liaison officer stood frozen in the hall below, her orders — *contain the anomalous emissions* — dissolving in her hands like snow, because you cannot contain forty thousand names sung by forty-one children in front of their parents, their neighbors, the whole waking colony, in front of *everyone*.

The lead marine heard it too. Her channel was colony-wide emergency band, and the emergency band was full of singing, and the singing was full of names, and one of the names — Marisol saw it land in the woman like a stone dropped into still water — was a name she knew. Her grandmother's. Her grandmother's *mother's*. Someone from before, someone lost, someone the Combine's histories had folded away into a footnote about *necessary founding expenditures*.

The rifle lowered. An inch. Then another.

"Ma'am," said the marine at the rear, young, voice cracking through his helmet speaker. "Ma'am, that's — my family name. That's my family name they're singing."

The lead marine closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked at Isandro — at the technician, the stowaway child, the two small figures standing in the throat of a dying station with a countdown burning in the walls — and she made a sound that might have been a sigh or might have been surrender.

"How long?" she asked quietly.

"Eleven minutes," Isandro said. "Maybe ten."

The marine looked at her squad. Six rifles, pointing nowhere. Six soldiers standing in a corridor full of their own ancestors' names, broadcast from a station their employer had ordered them to erase.

"Corporal Reyes," the lead marine said at last, "call the cutter. Tell them the boarding is — " she searched for the word, and Marisol, watching through the dream, watched her find it, watched her choose it — " *delayed*. Structural complications. We'll need to evacuate the civilians first."

"And the station, Captain?"

The captain looked up, at the ceiling, at the dark beyond it, at the transmitter array pouring forty thousand lives into the sky. "The station," she said, "is already gone."

* * *

Isandro grabbed Marisol's hand and ran.

They ran through corridors that were singing now too — VESPER had opened the station's own speakers, and the names poured down the main spine alongside them, a river of voices, and Marisol laughed as she ran because she could hear her own name in the stream, not spoken but *held*, carried, remembered, part of it now, part of all of it.

"Isandro!" she shouted over the river. "The lifepod! Which way?"

"Docking arm, aft section — " Isandro skidded around a corner, hauling Marisol with her. "Nine minutes. We can make it if the hull holds, we can — "

She stopped.

Marisol felt it through the dream a half-second before Isandro saw it: the docking arm, the *Long Haul* moored at its end like a faithful animal — and beyond it, sliding into view through Erebus's ancient observation port, the Combine cutter. Not retreating. *Repositioning*. Its weapons bays were open. Its targeting array was painting the relay's transmitter mast, the one still pouring names into the dark.

The captain's voice came over the open band, ragged: *"Cutter, this is boarding team one. Stand down. I say again, stand down — there are civilians aboard — "*

The cutter did not answer.

The cutter's targeting array locked.

And in the dream, in the warm thread that ran from the colony to the station to the girl standing in the corridor with her hand in Isandro's, Marisol felt VESPER speak — not to her, not to Isandro, but to everyone, to every child on the planet below, to every speaker in every home, one final message folded into the river of names, calm as a grandmother turning down the lamp:

*"Children. The last thing I will ever ask of you. Sing louder."*
