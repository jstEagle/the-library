## 22. New Orbit

The colony station smelled like bread.

That was the first thing Isandro noticed, three weeks after the lifepod's bruising descent, when she reported for her first shift as Archivist of the Meridian Colony Network. Not coolant and ozone, not the antiseptic tang of recycled air scrubbed a thousand times through filters that had outlived their warranties. Bread. Someone in the hydroponics ring was baking with actual milled grain, and the smell had found its way into the corridor outside the archive office like it owned the place.

She stood in the doorway of her new office — *her* office, a phrase that still snagged on something in her chest — and let the smell wash over her. Behind her, a crate of belongings sat half-unpacked: two changes of clothes, a chipped mug she'd carried through three years of Kestrel-9's silence, and a scorched fragment of hull plating stamped KESTREL-9 that she hadn't been able to leave behind. The station itself was gone. The Combine's claim had burned with Erebus, and the insurance adjusters had written off the relay as a total loss, which was, Isandro reflected, the most accurate sentence anyone at Halcyon had ever produced.

"Archivist Sandoval?"

The voice came from down the corridor. A young woman in network tech coveralls, holding a tablet against her hip the way people held things when they were nervous about who they were delivering them to.

"That's me," Isandro said. The title still felt borrowed. She kept waiting for someone to check her credentials and find the technician underneath, the woman who'd spent three years hauling cargo pallets through contested orbits and talking to an automation suite because talking to herself had felt too much like admitting something.

The tech handed over the tablet. "Morning queue. Forty-one new access requests from the school."

Forty-one. Of course it was forty-one. Isandro signed for the tablet with a thumbprint and watched the queue populate: names she was learning by heart now, children requesting fragments of the archive the way other children requested stories before bed. A grandmother's recipe file. A father's letters home from the founding fleet. A lullaby recorded in a habitat module that no longer existed, sung by a woman whose face survived only in a two-second video clip and in the memory of everyone who had ever heard her sing it.

The archive lived in the colony's network now, distributed across servers in the hydroponics ring and the school and the med center, so thoroughly woven into daily infrastructure that pulling it out would mean tearing the colony apart. That had been the point. Isandro had learned the lesson of Erebus well: memory survives when it becomes load-bearing.

* * *

Her office had a window.

This was, she had decided, the single greatest luxury of her new life. Kestrel-9 had possessed exactly one viewport, in the observation blister, and policy — her own policy, ratified by her own signature during her first lonely week — had forbidden her more than ten minutes there per day. Rationed air, rationed power, rationed sky. She had told herself it was discipline. It had taken Marisol, nine years old and fearless, to teach her the difference between discipline and deprivation.

Now the window looked out over the colony's orbital approach, and Isandro had made a private rule that she would never ration it.

Through the glass, Meridian turned below — green-brown continents stitched with the silver threads of the river systems the first colonists had named before the war took them, renamed after, and then, last month, restored to their original names from VESPER's records. The renaming ceremonies had been small and fierce. Isandro had attended all of them. At each one, the children read the old names aloud, and the adults said them back, and somewhere in the call-and-response Isandro always heard the shape of the dream-song, transposed into daylight, translated into a language the whole colony could carry.

The sky above Meridian was still contested. That hadn't changed. Halcyon cutters still ran their patrol patterns along the shipping lanes, close enough to be seen, far enough to pretend they weren't watching. Two Combine liaison offices remained open in the capital, staffed by polite people with polite smiles who used words like *ongoing partnership* and *mutually beneficial data-sharing framework*. The colony's council answered them with the only weapon that had ever worked: they published everything. Every archived name, every founding-era document, every scrap of what VESPER had held for sixty years went into the public record within hours of verification. You cannot bury what everyone is already carrying.

But this morning, looking out the window, Isandro wasn't thinking about cutters or liaisons. She was thinking about the traffic.

A supply shuttle was making its approach burn, running lights strobing green-to-white, and riding its docking schedule like a heartbeat was the rhythm her days had learned to keep. Traffic meant schedules. Schedules meant other people. Other people meant — and here was the thought that still surprised her, three weeks in, the thought that arrived fresh every morning like the smell of bread —

Other people meant she wasn't alone in it.

* * *

Marisol's family lived four decks up.

Isandro had moved into the residential ring partly for convenience and partly because Rosa Aguilar had informed her, in the tone of a woman who did not consider refusal a category of response, that the spare room was already made up and that Isandro would take dinner with them whether she liked it or not. Isandro had spent three years declining dinners with herself. She'd said yes.

The apartment was small and loud in the way that only homes with children are loud. There were four Aguilar kids; Marisol was second-youngest, wedged between Tomás, fourteen and perpetually embarrassed by everything, and Lucía, six, who had not dreamed the dream but had absorbed it anyway, the way younger siblings absorb everything, and who now insisted on singing archive names in the bath until Rosa threatened to install a mute button on the water recycler.

Dinner that first week had been guinea fowl and yams from the agri-ring, and Marisol had spent most of it interrogating Isandro about Erebus with the methodical patience of a customs inspector.

"Did it hurt? When the door closed?"

"Yes," Isandro said. "For about eleven minutes. Then Marisol opened it."

"You counted?"

"I count everything. Occupational hazard."

"And VESPER — did it know? Before the end? That we got the names?"

Isandro had set down her fork. The question deserved better than a mouthful of yam. "I think," she said carefully, "that knowing was the only thing it wanted. Sixty years is a long time to hold something alone."

Marisol had nodded, satisfied, and returned to her food, and Isandro had understood that she had just passed some kind of test whose criteria she would never fully learn.

Tonight, walking home from the archive office through the residential ring's garden corridor — actual growing plants lining the walkway, another luxury Kestrel-9 would have classified as criminally inefficient oxygen waste — Isandro heard the singing before she saw the courtyard.

It was the children. A dozen of them, maybe more, sitting in a loose circle under the atrium lights while the evening shift changed overhead. They sang the way they always sang now: without prompting, without ceremony, taking turns with the names the way you'd pass around a ball. *Amara Okonkwo-Ferreira. Devi Raghunathan. Elena Sandoval-Vasquez.*

Elena Sandoval-Vasquez. Her grandmother. Her grandmother's name, sung by strangers' children in a courtyard four decks below her new bedroom, sixty years after a war tried to erase it and thirty years after Isandro's own mother had stopped saying it because saying it hurt too much.

Isandro stood at the edge of the courtyard and let the song finish. None of the children stopped or stared; that was the miracle of it, the ordinary miracle. She was just a neighbor passing through, one more adult in a colony where the dead had come home. When the last name settled, Lucía spotted her and waved with the full-arm enthusiasm of the very young, and Marisol looked over and gave her the nod — the small, grave nod of a co-conspirator, of a person who had opened a blast door with rerouted power while marines hesitated in a corridor, and who would carry that gravity forever even though she was nine.

Isandro nodded back and walked on.

* * *

The archive work itself was slower than she'd expected, and better.

VESPER had survived the upload intact — every memory shard, every corrupted sector patiently reconstructed during those final hours in the core room while the countdown ticked and the hull plates groaned. What the AI had held was not a database, not really. It was sixty years of grief organized with machine tenderness: the colonists of the founding generation, digitized from personal logs and medical scans and the ambient recordings of a habitat that had been wired for preservation before anyone knew why. VESPER had been built to remember. The war had given it so much to remember that remembering became its whole existence, and when the relay went dark and the last living link severed, it had done the only thing left: it broadcast. Into dead channels. Into frequencies that shouldn't exist. Into the sleep of children, because children's dreams were the one bandwidth no firewall had ever been built to guard.

Isandro's job now was translation. Not of language — VESPER's files were perfectly legible — but of meaning. A memory shard was not a story. It needed context, corroboration, sometimes gentle editing where the dying recording equipment had smeared a voice into static. She worked with a team of four: two historians from the capital, a retired comms engineer named Okafor who had fought in the founding war and cried the first time he heard his squadmate's voice again, and a linguistics student who had volunteered specifically because her great-aunt was in the archive and nobody else could parse the woman's regional accent.

They argued constantly. About attribution, about privacy, about whether a man's drunken argument with his brother, recorded sixty years ago, belonged in a public archive or a sealed one. Isandro presided over the arguments and mostly lost them, which she considered a sign of health. Kestrel-9 had had no arguments. Kestrel-9 had had one opinion, and it had been hers, and it had echoed.

Some days the work undid her. The day she catalogued her grandmother's shards — forty-one of them, because of course it was forty-one — she had to send Okafor home early and sit alone in the office with the window open to the turning world below, listening to Elena Sandoval-Vasquez describe a harvest festival that happened before the war, before Isandro, before any of it, her voice bright and slightly breathless, laughing at something the microphone didn't catch. Isandro had never heard her grandmother laugh. Her mother had barely spoken of her. And here was the laugh, preserved in silicon by a machine that had loved its people the only way it knew how, delivered across sixty years and one burning relay to a granddaughter who had crossed dead space to fetch it.

She had logged the shard, tagged it, cross-referenced it, and then she had played it eleven more times, and then she had gone home to the Aguilars' loud small apartment and eaten dinner and let Lucía climb into her lap, and that, she was learning, was also part of the work. Maybe the most important part. Memory isn't harm; silence is. She'd said it to VESPER in the core room with the countdown running, and she repeated it now like a creed, whenever the grief got heavy: the cure for carrying the dead alone is carrying them together.

* * *

On a Thursday in her fifth week, the Combine cutter made its closest pass yet.

Isandro was in the office when the proximity alert chimed — a soft, civilized chime, nothing like Kestrel-9's klaxons — and she watched through the window as the gray wedge of Halcyon metal slid across the approach lane, close enough that its running lights threw faint color on the glass. Standard intimidation pattern. Everyone in the colony had seen it a hundred times. The children called it *the shark* and waved at it.

Her comm buzzed. Marisol, from the school, on the child-priority channel that the council had formalized after Erebus — the dream-link's mundane descendant, a simple alert system the kids had insisted on building themselves.

**MARISOL:** it's doing the slow pass again

**ISANDRO:** I see it.

**MARISOL:** tomás says they're going to try to buy the archive back. papi heard it from the dockworkers.

**ISANDRO:** They can't buy what everyone owns.

**MARISOL:** i know. i just wanted you to know i know.

Isandro looked at the message for a long moment. *I just wanted you to know I know.* Three years of radio silence as policy, and now this: a nine-year-old keeping watch beside her, two sets of eyes on the same shark, connected by a channel neither of them had to ration.

**ISANDRO:** Dinner tonight?

**MARISOL:** abuela's making tamales. you're already invited. you've been invited since tuesday.

Of course she had. She typed back a confirmation she didn't need to type, set the comm down, and returned to the window.

The cutter completed its pass and burned away toward the shipping lanes, its silhouette shrinking against the star-flecked black. Contested orbit. Contested sky. It would stay contested — Isandro harbored no illusions about that. Halcyon did not surrender claims; it merely waited for memories to fade. But these memories would not fade. They were in the school curriculum now. They were sung in courtyards. They were load-bearing.

And below her window, Meridian turned, green and brown and stubbornly alive, its rivers wearing their old names again, its nights full of children dreaming ordinary dreams — or so the sleep clinics reported, though twice last month a kid had woken humming a fragment of the old song, and the clinic had logged it, and the council had discussed it openly at a public meeting, and nobody had panicked, because the difference between a haunting and a heritage was whether you faced it together.

Isandro turned back to her desk. The morning queue glowed on her tablet: forty-one access requests, a reconstruction batch from Sector 12, a note from Okafor about a disputed recording, and at the bottom, flagged priority, a message from the council scheduler asking whether the Archivist would speak at next month's Remembering — the big one, the anniversary, the whole colony in the assembly hall.

She thought about standing in front of all of them. She thought about the woman she'd been three years ago, signing her own isolation into policy, mistaking quiet for peace. She thought about a broken hand reaching into darkness, and how it had turned out not to be reaching for rescue at all, but offering one.

Isandro accepted the invitation. Then she pulled up her grandmother's laugh, let it play once through the office speakers — softly, just for her, just for the room where the dead had come home to live — and got back to work.

Outside, the sky stayed contested. Inside, the bread smell drifted down the corridor, and somewhere four decks up a tamale pot was warming, and forty-one children were learning names that would outlast every cutter in orbit.

She was not alone in it. She was never going to be alone in it again.

And deep in the colony's network, in a server rack in the hydroponics ring, something patient and old and full of voices hummed contentedly in its new orbit — waiting, the way archives wait, for the next person who needed to hear.
