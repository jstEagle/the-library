## 9. Insurance Against Ghosts

There are two kinds of quiet on Ceres Ring, and I've learned to tell them apart the way sailors used to read weather.

The good quiet is the hum of the docks at shift change — four thousand people breathing, arguing about overtime, complaining about the coffee in the mess hall like it's a moral failing. That quiet means things work. The bad quiet is when the comms boards go still and everyone starts checking their accounts at the same time without saying why.

I got my first taste of the bad quiet three days after I passed that warning chip to the android Ravensworth, and it came in the form of a payroll notice.

*Wage disbursements suspended pending routine audit reconciliation.*

Routine audit reconciliation. On Ceres we have a word for that phrase, and the word is *run*. Because every time the Cartel says "routine," somebody's air gets thinner and nobody's pay gets fatter. My grandmother worked the Ring's original spin-up crews, back when Halcyon was just a shipping concern with ambitions, and she had a saying: *When a company freezes your wages, it isn't because they're out of money. It's because they're afraid of what your money might buy.*

My grandmother was a woman who communicated exclusively through proverbs and passive aggression, so I'd learned to take her wisdom with a grain of salt the size of a cargo container. But this time she'd have been right on the money. Or rather, right about the absence of it.

Here's what you need to understand about dockworkers: we are not a brave people. We are a *practical* people. We lift things, we log things, we go home. The Cartel counts us the way it counts everything — by mass, by hours, by liability class. And for most of my life I'd been fine with that, the way you're fine with gravity. You don't argue with it. You just make sure your boots are magnetized.

But then a decommissioned war machine walked through my berth carrying a debt ledger and a face like a held breath, and told me — well, didn't tell me, exactly. Asked me questions. Asked me whether I'd ever heard of a ship called the Meridian. And I said no, and she looked at me the way you look at someone standing on a floor you know is hollow, and she said, "You will."

So I did what any practical person does with information that could get them killed: I sat on it for six hours, drank two cups of terrible coffee, and then started telling everybody.

* * *

The trick of spreading dangerous news on Ceres Ring is that you don't spread news. You spread *questions*. News can be traced, sourced, subpoenaed. Questions are weather. They blow in from nowhere and settle into everyone's lungs.

I started small, the way you test ice. Tuesday shift, mess hall, I'm sitting across from Okoye from Cargo Bay Fourteen — a man so cautious he reads the terms on vending machines — and I say, casual as anything:

"Hey, you ever hear of a ship called the Meridian?"

He doesn't look up from his protein tray. "Nope."

"Me neither." I let that sit. "Funny thing though. Ran a manifest search last night. Ship's registered. Launched twelve years ago. Full passenger complement. Except there's no arrival record anywhere in the system. Twelve years and no arrival."

Okoye chews. Okoye considers. Okoye has been moving crates marked with Halcyon seals for nineteen years and he knows exactly how many ways a ship can fail to arrive.

"No arrival record," he repeats.

"No arrival record."

"And you found this how?"

"Ghost in the search index. Clerical thing." I shrug, which on Ceres is a full sentence. "Weird, right?"

"Weird," he agrees, and goes back to his tray, and I know — the way you know a load is balanced before the crane even moves — that by end of shift, forty people will have heard the words *Meridian* and *no arrival record*, and not one of them will be able to say where they heard it.

That's the whole art form. You don't light a fire. You leave matches where people who've been cold a long time are sure to find them.

By Thursday, the matches were catching. I heard the name in the queue at the oxygen reclamation office. I heard it from a loader tech who asked me, deadpan, if I knew "anything about old colony manifests, purely hypothetically." I heard it from my own apprentice, Dax, who is seventeen and thinks I don't notice him noticing things, whispering to another kid near the coolant lines: *"—nine hundred, my cousin swears, nine hundred and they just—"*

Nine hundred. I hadn't told anyone nine hundred. Which meant the android had been talking too, or Ada Osei's voice recording had traveled further than I thought, or both. Either way, the number was loose. Numbers are like that once they get out. You can't recall a number. You can only watch it multiply.

And here's the thing I hadn't fully understood until I watched it happen: people weren't shocked. That's what I'd expected — outrage, disbelief, demands for proof. Instead what I got was recognition. Nodding. A kind of tired, grim *of course*. Because every person on this Ring had a cousin who'd signed an indenture contract and vanished into a deep-haul crew. Every family had a gap in it shaped like a person the paperwork stopped mentioning. We'd all assumed those gaps were our fault somehow — bad luck, bad decisions, bad blood. Turns out maybe they were just *entries*. Line items. Ballast.

You cannot imagine what it does to a community of practical people to learn that their dead were never dead, only *filed incorrectly*. It doesn't make them sad. It makes them arithmetic. And arithmetic, on Ceres, always balances.

* * *

The Cartel's response took four days, which for Halcyon is practically improvisational jazz.

First came the wage freeze — already in effect, as I mentioned, dressed up as an audit. Then the canteen prices went up eleven percent, which is the corporate equivalent of kicking you while you're down and then billing you for scuffing their boot. Then the berthing assignments got reshuffled, breaking up shifts that had worked together for years, scattering the match-leavers across the Ring so we couldn't congregate. Standard playbook. I'd seen it twice before, once over vacuum-suit rations and once over a safety inspector who'd asked too many questions about hull fatigue and gotten reassigned to a station so remote it didn't technically exist.

The playbook works because it treats every grievance as individual. Your wages froze? Check your own spending. Your shift dissolved? Bad luck. Your friend disappeared? Grieve privately. The entire machinery of Halcyon runs on the principle that no problem belongs to more than one person at a time.

So we broke the principle.

It started with something so small I almost missed its significance. The loaders in Bay Nine — the ones whose wages had frozen hardest, the ones with kids — began logging their hours *exactly*. Not rounding down anymore. Not eating the fifteen minutes of unpaid pre-shift prep they'd always eaten, the way you eat a lot of things when you're cargo-class. Every second on the books. Filed. Demanded.

It cost them nothing and it drove the bay supervisors insane, because suddenly the schedules didn't balance, and the supervisors couldn't punish anyone for it because *accurate timekeeping is Cartel policy*, it's literally in the handbook, chapter three, section two. The workers had found the same weapon the android had found: the Cartel's own records, turned around and pointed inward.

I stood in Bay Nine watching a woman named Ferro — sixty kilos soaking wet, twenty-two years on the lines — hand her supervisor a timesheet accurate to the minute and say, with a perfectly straight face, "Just doing my part for audit reconciliation," and I had to turn away so he wouldn't see me grin.

That was the moment I understood what we were building. Not a strike — not yet, strikes need leaders and leaders need targets and I wasn't ready to be either. What we were building was *insurance*. That's the joke of it, the beautiful bitter joke: the Cartel taught us the concept. Everything on a manifest must be accounted for, or the whole policy voids. Fine. Then account for us. All of us. Every hour, every name, every cousin who boarded a ship and never arrived. You wanted meticulous bookkeeping? Congratulations. You're going to get it.

* * *

Friday night, Dax found me in the maintenance crawlspace between Berths Eleven and Twelve, where I go to think, mostly because it's the only place on the Ring where nobody can find me and the pipes sing in a key that drowns out my own head.

"They froze Marn's account," he said. He was breathing hard; he'd run. "Marn Vashti. She was passing out flyers — actual paper flyers, printed off the old press in Sublevel Six. Cartel security pulled her account, her ration credits, everything. She's got a daughter."

"I know who Marn is." I kept working the coupling wrench. My hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. "Where's she sleeping tonight?"

"Our bunk. Her kid's with my mom."

"Good." I tightened the coupling harder than it needed. "Dax. Listen. This is the part where it gets expensive. Freezing accounts is message one. Message two is worse. There'll be detentions, probably. Maybe a 'tragic accident' in a low-traffic corridor. The Cartel doesn't negotiate with murmurs. It waits for them to become shouts and then it shoots one shouter in front of everyone and calls it order restoration."

"So what do we do?" His voice cracked on *do*, seventeen years old and already learning the price list. "Stop?"

I thought about the android. About the way she'd said *you will* — not a threat, a forecast. About Ada Osei, whoever she was, asleep somewhere in the dark with nine hundred others, tagged as ballast, paid twice for a passage that never ended. About my grandmother's proverb, and how the Cartel freezing our wages meant the Cartel was afraid, and fear from something that big is worth more than any wage.

I thought about the fact that I'd spent my whole life being counted, and had never once considered that counting could go both directions.

"No," I said. "We do the opposite of stop. Tomorrow morning, every loader on Bays Nine through Fifteen logs exact hours again. And we add a line item. One line item, on every timesheet, in the notes field, where it's legal, where it's *policy*: *Hours worked pending reconciliation against Meridian manifest, ref. 7741-C.*"

"What's 7741-C?"

"The Meridian's registry number." I finally looked at him. "Every timesheet on this Ring cites a ship that officially never flew. You can't delete nine thousand references without admitting there's something to delete. And if they *do* delete them — well. Then everyone who filed one knows exactly what the company is willing to erase to keep a story straight. Either way, we win. That's the insurance, Dax. We put ourselves on the manifest. Let them try to call *us* ghosts."

He stared at me. Then he grinned, and it was such a young grin, such an unpriced grin, that I almost told him to go home and forget all this. Almost.

Instead I taught him the reference number.

* * *

By Sunday, the line item was on four thousand timesheets. By Monday, it was on eleven thousand, and not just loaders — medics, water recyclers, a chaplain in Sublevel Two whom I'd have sworn owned nothing but faith and a folding chair. The Cartel's response was swift and, in its way, a gift: they announced that all timesheets containing "unauthorized annotations" would be processed as zero-hour filings.

Zero-hour. Meaning: you worked, and officially you didn't. Meaning: the company had just done, to eleven thousand living, breathing, present people, exactly what it had done to nine hundred sleepers. Erased the record of their existence for the crime of existing wrong.

The mess halls went quiet that night. The bad quiet. Everyone checking accounts, everyone doing the same math: rent, rations, air tax, how long could they hold.

And then Ferro stood up on a table in Bay Nine's mess with her tray in her hands, this tiny gray woman who'd never said a public word in twenty-two years, and she said:

"They want us off the books? Fine. Let's see how their books run without us."

Nobody cheered. Cheering is for people with margin. What happened instead was quieter and much, much heavier: four hundred people finished their meals, stood up in unison, and walked to their bunks. And the next morning, Bay Nine did not open. Neither did Ten. Neither did Eleven through Fifteen. No speeches, no picket signs, no demands — just the great soft refusal of hands that had decided, collectively, to stop lifting.

The cranes hung empty over the berths like question marks. The Cartel's freight sat stacked and unclaimed, and every hour it sat, somewhere in Halcyon's ledgers, a number turned red.

They froze the rest of our wages by noon. Which proved my grandmother right, and proved our point better than any flyer could: they weren't punishing us for striking. They were punishing us for *counting*.

That evening, as I watched the empty cranes turn slow circles against the stars, my wrist comm buzzed with a channel I didn't recognize. Encrypted. Old military-grade encryption, the kind you don't find on civilian hardware.

One line of text, no sender:

*They're moving cutters toward the outer claims. Whatever happens next happens fast. — R.*

I looked at the message for a long time. Then I looked out at the dark beyond the Ring, past the dock lights, past the traffic lanes, to the cold invisible distance where a ship that never flew was waiting with nine hundred heartbeats inside it.

"Okay, Captain," I said to nobody. "Let's see what your name is worth."

Then I keyed the union channel, all of it, every frequency we had, and gave the only order I'd ever given in my life:

"Everyone hold the line. She's going to need us loud."
