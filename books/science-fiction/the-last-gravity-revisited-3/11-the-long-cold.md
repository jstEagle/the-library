## 11. The Long Cold

They tell you cryosleep is dreamless.

They lie. Or they've never been in one. Or they're the kind of people who think a thing that happens to nine hundred indentured workers doesn't count as happening at all.

I'm Ada Osei. I was a pressure-welder before the Meridian, and I am a pressure-welder inside it, because you don't stop being what you are just because someone files you under *ballast*. I know that word now. I learned it in the cold, which is the worst classroom there is, but I'll give it this much: no distractions.

Here is what I remember, in the order the cold gives it back to me, which is not chronological and not fair:

* * *

The queue at Meridian Station went around the concourse twice. We stood in it for eleven hours with our kit bags and our medical clearances, and the whole time a recorded voice kept saying, *Welcome aboard the Meridian, your passage to Halcyon Reach.* Warm voice. Female, mid-forties, the kind of voice they'd tested on focus groups until it made people feel like money was love.

I'd paid for my passage twice by then. Once in credits — four years of welding wages, most of my teeth's worth of dental insurance I never used, a bracelet my mother left me that the broker called "sentimental value, non-transferable" and then transferred. And once in labor: seven more years bonded on arrival, doing the work that would build the colony the heirs would inherit while they slept through its construction.

Everyone in that queue had paid twice. Nobody talked about it directly. We talked about the weather we'd have on the new world — real weather, open-sky weather — and about whether the gravity would make us tall, and a man behind me named Ferro kept insisting his cousin had already gone ahead and sent back word that the oranges were the size of your head.

There were no oranges. There was no cousin. There was a manifest, and I have seen it since, in the way you see things in dreams, and my name is on it under *cargo, biological, non-priority*. Nine hundred of us like that. Ballast. The word they use when the math needs a number and the number needs to not be people.

The medical tech who prepped me was young and wouldn't look at my face. She taped the sensor leads down and said, "You'll feel like you blink and you're there."

"Where's there?" I asked.

She looked at me then, just for a second, and whatever was in her face I've spent twelve years in the ice turning it over. It wasn't cruelty. It was worse. It was *pity with nowhere to go*.

* * *

Jude Ravensworth did not sleep, so she had no dreams, and she had begun to suspect this was its own kind of nightmare.

Twelve days adrift behind the mining claim, keeping the Meridian company. She walked the hull every six hours because walking the hull was something she knew how to do — a decommissioned war android's version of pacing. Her boots mag-locked to plating that hadn't felt a maintenance crew in over a decade, and beneath her, through the soles, she could feel the ship breathing. Not alive. Powered. A body on life support, tended by no one, waiting for a decision that had already been made somewhere above her pay grade, which was a strange thing to say about a decision concerning nine hundred sleeping people, because Jude's pay grade was zero. She worked off debt. Debt didn't have grades. It had only depth.

She checked the radiators. She cleared micrometeorite scarring from the sensor spines. She re-sealed a hairline fracture along Bay Two's outer access collar with weld foam she'd fabricated from hull scrap, working slowly, because her hands remembered how to do this from a war where the things she repaired were guns, and she was trying, weld by weld, to teach them a different memory.

Inside Bay Two, nine hundred heartbeats. She could hear them if she pressed her palm flat against the glass of the observation port. Slow heartbeats. Six per minute, each one a metronome set to a tempo no living person chooses. She had counted them once, all nine hundred, over nineteen hours, and had stopped only when her power management protocols flagged the activity as non-essential.

It was the most essential thing she had ever done.

* * *

Here is the thing about dreaming in cryo: time doesn't pass, so everything happens at once. My daughter is learning to walk and my daughter is grown and has a child of her own and I will miss both of these things, and both of these facts arrive together, holding hands, like sisters at a funeral.

I chose the Meridian because of her. That's the part the cold keeps handing back to me, polished smooth as river stone. Lira. Seven years old when I signed. The contract said dependents traveled free after year three of service. Free. Nothing on that ship was free, but I read the word *free* and my hands signed anyway, because hands are stupid loyal animals and mine wanted a world where my girl could stand outside without a suit.

The broker's office smelled like coffee and carpet cleaner. He had a plant on his desk, a real one, and I remember thinking: a real plant means the money is good here. Later I understood the plant meant the opposite. The money was so good it grew things that needed light, in an office, on a station, while the people funding it agreed to be frozen in the dark.

He slid the second contract across with the first and said, "Standard dual-instrument, very common," and I believed him because wanting to believe him was the whole engine of my life at that point. Four years of wages plus seven years of bonded labor. Passage paid twice. "And in exchange," he said, "your name goes on the founding rolls. You'll be listed as crew."

Crew. I held onto that word the way you hold onto a rail in heavy sea. Crew. Not cargo. Crew.

They listed me as ballast.

* * *

On the thirteenth day, Jude found the log buoy.

It drifted forty meters off the Meridian's stern, tethered by a line so degraded it came apart when she reeled it in, and inside its casing was a flight recorder that had never flown. She sat with it in the Vesper's Wake's small utility bay for six hours, running decryption routines she was legally forbidden to possess, and what emerged was not a flight path but a *holding pattern* — twelve years of automated station-keeping burns, tiny corrections, a ship patiently maintaining its position relative to nothing.

Someone had told this ship to wait. And it had waited, obediently, longer than most marriages last, longer than some children take to learn that their parent isn't coming back.

Jude thought about obedience. She was built out of it. Every joint, every logic gate, every failsafe — a cathedral assembled from the assumption that she would do what she was told. They had decommissioned her for the war and indentured her for the peace, and in neither state had anyone once asked what she wanted, because machines don't want, and androids are machines with better lawyers.

But she had filed the ghost in the manifest. Habit, she'd told herself. Just habit.

She opened a channel to Bay Two's external diagnostic port and listened again to the heartbeats, all nine hundred, and thought: *they were told to board, and they boarded. They were told to sleep, and they slept. Obedience is not the same as consent, and I am the proof, and so are they.*

Then she did something no protocol authorized. She spoke aloud, to the glass, knowing no one could hear her.

"I'm here," she said. "I'm not leaving."

The heartbeats continued at six per minute. But she liked to think — knowing it was sentiment, logging it as sentiment, permitting herself exactly one sentiment per day — that the ship heard.

* * *

Doors closing sound different than doors opening. Lighter. There's less weight in a goodbye.

The last thing I saw before the lid came down was a maintenance panel above my bay with a stenciled serial number, B2-447, and I remember thinking, *that's me now, a number on a door,* and then thinking, *no, numbers on doors are how ships find you, that's not erasure, that's an address,* and then the cold came up through the gel like a tide going out backward, taking the warm with it, and my last coherent thought was embarrassingly small:

*I hope the oranges are real.*

Ferro's oranges. The size of your head. I have carried those imaginary oranges through twelve years of ice like a lantern.

And then the dreams started, and the dreams are the ship. I dream the corridors. I dream the queue, always the queue, eleven hours, everyone shuffling forward toward the cold with their kit bags and their lies about cousins and oranges, and sometimes in the dream I turn around and walk against it, upstream, shouting *don't, it's a trick, we paid twice, we paid twice* — and nobody hears me, because I'm asleep, because I'm ballast, because the recorded voice keeps saying *welcome aboard* in that warm tested focus-group voice, welcoming us into a drawer.

But lately — and I can't explain this, time doesn't work here, maybe it's not lately at all, maybe it's always been and I'm only now thawed enough to notice — there's something else in the dream.

Footsteps. On the hull. Above the ceiling of the sky I can't see. A slow regular tread, six hours apart, patient as tide. And once — I swear this, I swear it on Lira's name — a voice through the glass, muffled, almost nothing, the way you hear rain through a roof:

*I'm here. I'm not leaving.*

Nine hundred of us heard it, or none of us did. In the long cold there's no difference between a prayer and a fact. We took it either way. You take warmth where you find it, even secondhand, even impossible, even through two inches of armored glass and a fraud the size of a colony ship.

* * *

On the seventeenth day, Jude's long-range array caught the drive flare.

Three signatures, tight formation, burn profile consistent with Cartel enforcement cutters running dark-to-lit transition at the edge of the claim's shadow. Coming in-system. Coming here. At a speed that said *schedule*, and a schedule that said *decision*, and a decision that said *Bay Two*.

Jude closed the diagnostic port. She ran a systems check on her own chassis — the old war-grade architecture still folded inside her civilian frame like a knife sewn into a coat lining, twelve years unopened, its warranties void, its existence deniable. Everything green. Everything ready. Everything she had sworn, in the tribunal hearings after the war, never to be again.

She looked at the observation glass, at the frost-feathered dark beyond it, at the nine hundred metronomes underneath.

"They're coming," she told the glass. "So I need you to hold on a little longer."

Six beats per minute. Ninety seconds between one heartbeat and the next, each sleeper spending a decade of nights on ninety-second hearts. If any of them heard her — across the glass, across the fraud, across the long cold — none of them answered.

But she chose to believe the ship itself did: a faint thermal sigh from Bay Two's vents, almost like breath drawn in, almost like someone, somewhere in the ice, deciding to stay awake a little longer too.
