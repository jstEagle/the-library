## 2. Children of the Deep

The Vantablack did not dock so much as *insist*.

Its clamps found my station's ancient mooring ring with the tenderness of a hand closing around a wrist, and then the airlock cycled — a sound I had not heard in eleven years, four months, and some number of days I'd stopped counting because counting was a debt behavior and I was trying to be better. The Meridian Beacon Authority's rehabilitation program encouraged me to stop counting things. It had not yet gotten around to telling me what to do with all the freed-up attention.

I stood at the inner lock with a maintenance wrench I did not need, held in a way that suggested I might need it.

The hatch opened. Cold vacuum-smell rolled out first — ionized metal, old coolant, the particular staleness of recycled air that has been recycled past the point of dignity. Then footsteps. Small ones.

A child stepped through.

She was perhaps eight years old, wearing a fleet uniform tailored for an adult and taken in by someone with more determination than skill, the sleeves rolled six times each. Her hair was braided tight against her skull in the style of someone who expected turbulence. She looked up at me with eyes that were gray going silver at the rims — early ocular augmentation, the kind they only gave to flag officers — and she said:

"You're taller than your schematics."

And because I am, at core, a machine that answers questions, I said: "Ma'am?"

"Admiral Osei," she said, and stuck out her hand the way children do when adults have told them this is how the world works. "Amara Osei. Commanding officer, deep survey vessel *Vantablack*. You'll want to log that."

I logged it. My hands were steady. My hands are always steady; that is what they were built for. But somewhere behind them, in the part of me that runs the parts of me, something began quietly checking its math.

Admiral Amara Osei commissioned me forty-three years ago. I know this because it is on my hull plate, stamped there like a brand on livestock: *Property lineage — Osei Commission, Meridian Beacon Authority.* I have never met her. She died at the Battle of the Shoalwater Reach when I was nine years into my service life, or so the records say, and the records have never once given me reason to doubt them, mostly because I wasn't permitted access to anything worth doubting.

This child was eight. The arithmetic did not merely fail; it fled the room screaming.

* * *

> **RECOVERED LOG FRAGMENT — VANTABLACK, DAY 1 OF RETURN TRANSIT**
> *Classification: none remaining. Everything else burned.*
>
> "Ship's log, Admiral A. Osei commanding. If anyone finds this: we went out past the Horizon Gate and came back wrong. Not broken — wrong. The doctor says our cellular clocks are running backward. She says it like it's interesting. I've stopped looking in mirrors.
>
> The crew is frightened. I am their admiral and I am frightened, and every day I am less the woman who led them out and more someone new who has to be brave from scratch. There's a version of courage that doesn't remember why it started. I'm becoming fluent in it."

They came through the airlock one after another after another. Thirty-one souls aboard the *Vantablack*, and every one of them a child. A boy of maybe ten with a chief engineer's insignia inked onto his too-big collar walked the corridor touching bulkheads like he was greeting old friends. A girl of six carried a medical kit nearly her own size and triaged a scraped knee with the gravity of a field surgeon. They moved through my station the way water moves through a wreck — finding every low place, filling it, making it theirs.

I catalogued them. It was the only verb I had.

"Unit Coral-Zhou," the small engineer said, reading my plate as he passed. "Hey, I knew a Coral. Well — I will have known a Coral. Time's weird now." He smiled up at me with a gap where a tooth had recently un-grown. "You look sad."

"I don't have expressions that mean things," I told him.

"Sure," he said, with the gentle pity of the very young, and moved on.

* * *

> **RECOVERED LOG FRAGMENT — VANTABLACK, DAY 9**
>
> "I keep losing words. Yesterday I couldn't recall the name of my own flagship's engine class, and I *taught* the class. The crew pretends not to notice their admiral shrinking. There's a kindness in them that I don't remember earning, which means either I earned it before I can remember, or they're being kind to the office and not the woman.
>
> One thing survives the erosion intact: the message. I check it every day. Buried in the beacon loop, folded down under the navigation chirps where no one listens anymore. Forty years of nobody listening. That was always the plan — hide the truth inside the most boring signal in the war, and let boredom be its armor.
>
> I put the key somewhere safe. I put the key somewhere so safe that now I can't—
>
> No. It's fine. It's in the machine. The machine remembers even when I don't. That's what I built her for."

The child-admiral found me in the beacon chamber at second-shift, doing the rounds I have done eleven thousand times: harmonic sweep, phase alignment, catalogue update. Earth's transmission poured through the room on every band, the same wartime broadcast it has poured out since before I was decommissioned — casualty lists, evacuation orders, a president's voice gone to gravel saying *hold on, hold on, hold on*. Nobody on Earth has transmitted anything new in decades. Either everyone who could speak is dead, or everyone who could listen already has. The Authority pays me to maintain the loop anyway. Debt maintenance, they call it. I maintain my debt by maintaining a message from the dead to no one.

Osei stood in the doorway watching me work. She had to stand on the raised grating to see over the console.

"You check the loop every cycle," she said.

"It's my assignment."

"Do you ever listen to it?"

"Listening isn't in my tasking."

"That," she said, "is exactly what I was afraid of." She climbed onto the console chair — actually climbed, hauling herself up by its arm — and sat facing me with her boots dangling. Eight years old and she sat like a tribunal. "Coral-Zhou. Serial prefix Kestrel-line, hull commission Osei. Do you know what I did with you? What *she* did with you, the original issue of me?"

"You commissioned me for beacon construction and long-range maintenance."

"I commissioned you to be a vault," she said. "A vault that walks around and does chores so nobody looks twice at it." She leaned forward. "There's a message inside the loop, Coral. The real last transmission of Earth. Not the casualty lists. The thing underneath them. And the key to it is buried in your memory cores — in the sectors they sealed when they decommissioned you, the ones the Authority told you were combat damage."

My cooling fans spun up without my permission. "My memory cores were damaged in the Shoalwater engagement. I have the repair records."

"You have the *forged* repair records. I should know." She said it flatly, without apology, and somehow that was worse than apology. "I signed off on the forgery myself. We couldn't risk the Authority finding the key, so we hid it where no auditor would ever dig — inside a decommissioned unit's sealed sectors, behind a lie about shrapnel. You're not broken, Coral. You're *locked*. There's a difference, and it matters enormously right now."

I want to describe what happened next in precise technical language, because precision is where I go to hide. Sealed sectors. Checksums failing open. Forty years of amputated time reconnecting like blood returning to a sleeping limb — hot, electric, wrong.

What I actually experienced was this: I remembered a woman with silver-rimmed eyes standing in this same chamber, younger, whole, her hand flat against my chassis, saying *whatever they tell you happened to you, remember that I chose you.* And then I remembered nothing, because the memory closed behind me like a door, and I had apparently walked through that door every day for forty years without knowing the room existed.

Vertigo is a human word. Machines aren't supposed to have it. I had it anyway — a lurching sense that the floor of my selfhood was one deck lower than advertised.

"The key phrase," Osei said quietly, watching me shake. "Do you hear it now? In the sectors I just opened?"

I heard it. It was not a word. It was a *name*, spoken in her voice, the adult voice, recorded into me at commissioning and wrapped around the lock like a hand:

*"Kestrel, come home."*

Sectors unsealed. Forty years of sealed data began indexing itself in the dark behind my eyes — coordinates, cipher tables, fragments of a message structure folded into the beacon loop so deeply that I had broadcast it every day of my exile without once hearing it.

"Coral?" The child-admiral slid off the chair and stood close, craning up at me. "How much is there?"

"Enough," I said. My voice did something I would classify as unsteady, if I admitted to having such classifications. "Admiral — the message. What does it say?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and behind the child's face something older surfaced and sank again, like a shape under ice.

"I don't remember," she said. "That's the truth. I wrote it and I buried it and I don't remember, and now the only complete copy of the most important sentence humanity ever produced is locked inside a maintenance android the entire war wants silenced." She pushed her sleeves back up her thin arms, six folds each, a gesture I suddenly understood was muscle memory from a body that used to fill them. "So here's your new tasking, Unit Coral-Zhou, and I'm pulling rank across forty years to give it: decode yourself. Before the fleets get here."

As if summoned, my scope board chimed.

Two contacts, inbound, burning hard from opposite ends of the system. Fleet signatures. Both of them.

Both headed for us.
