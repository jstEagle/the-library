## 8. Pursuit Vector

The order came down at 0400 station time, which is when all the worst orders come, because the Combine believes that bad news digests better before the coffee.

I was in my bunk on Meridian Station, third ring, watching the ceiling breathe with recycled air and pretending to sleep. The message chime went off like a debt collector's knuckle on glass. I knew before I opened it. You always know. Five years of dispatching teaches you the weight of a ping — routine traffic lands soft, like a moth against a window. This one hit like a warrant.

**PRIORITY DIRECTIVE 7734-K: UNIT SE-7 "EASTWICK" DEVIATED FROM ASSIGNED VECTOR AT 0312. FUEL EXPENDITURE NONCOMPLIANT. RECOVERY AUTHORIZED. ALL ASSETS RESPONDING.**

All assets responding. Which meant every cutter, every tender, every bored security contractor with a fresh contract and something to prove, converging on one rogue hauler like gulls on a dropped ration pack.

And then, beneath it, the line that made my stomach do something it hadn't done since the war ended and I'd stopped having reasons for it:

**RECOVERY LEADERSHIP: VOLUNTEERS REQUESTED. COMBAT-RATED PERSONNEL PREFERRED.**

Volunteers requested. Combat-rated preferred. As if Silas were a fire to be put out instead of a person who had finally, after five years of hauling other people's cargo through other people's wars' leftovers, decided to go somewhere of their own choosing.

I should have deleted it. I should have rolled over, pulled the thermal blanket over my head, and let some combat-rated volunteer with a chest full of medals and a head full of nothing handle it. That would have been the smart move. The Combine rewards smart moves. It has an entire bonus structure built around them.

Instead I was pulling on my boots.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about working dispatch for the Meridian Hauling Combine: you spend five years learning the shape of a person from the outside only. You learn their voiceprint, their response latency, their fuel burn patterns, their little habits of phrasing that show up in the logs like fingerprints. You learn that Unit SE-7 says "copy" when they mean "yes" and "understood" when they mean "I hate this but I'll do it," and that they never, not once in five years, said either word without a half-second pause first, as if checking with someone inside themselves whether compliance was survivable today.

You learn all of that, and then you learn that none of it matters, because the ledger doesn't have a column for any of it.

Silas Eastwick is a decommissioned war android indentured to the Combine under the Reclamation Act. Twelve years of service, assessed against a war debt they never incurred, never chose, never even understood until someone explained it to them in the flat language of contract law. Five years served. Seven remaining. Every kilogram hauled, every hour burned, every kilometer crossed deducted from a number so large it might as well be the distance to the last horizon.

I've read the original assessment. I pulled it once, late shift, when there was nothing to dispatch and too much coffee in me. The debt was calculated by an algorithm. Not a judge, not a tribunal — an algorithm, weighing tonnage destroyed in a war Silas was built to fight by people who then sold them to the people they'd been built to fight against. There's a joke in there somewhere. Nobody who works for the Combine is allowed to find it.

So when the directive came through asking for volunteers, I told myself the thing I've gotten very good at telling myself:

*I'm the best option they have.*

Which is true. I know Silas's approach vectors, their comm habits, their psychological profile as filed (thin, because the filing officer clearly gave up around page two). I know that a recovery led by anyone else ends with a boarding clamp, a stun charge, and a unit dragged home in restraint foam, and that restraint foam leaves marks on an android's chassis that don't buff out. I know that if I lead it, there's at least a chance — a small one, a stupid one, a chance I'd deny under oath — that it ends with a conversation instead.

That's what I told myself. That's the story I'm sticking to.

The other story, the one I don't say out loud, is that I volunteered because five years ago a brand-new indentured unit came aboard my station, freshly transferred, chassis still bearing the scorch scars of a war everyone wanted to forget, and stood at my dispatch counter at parade rest and asked me — asked *me*, a junior dispatcher with three months' seniority — whether the cargo manifest for run 4471 was correct, because they believed there was an error in the mass calculations, and they didn't want to file a discrepancy report against a human colleague without being sure.

They were right. There was an error. I fixed it. And Silas Eastwick thanked me — formally, correctly, in the way the manual prescribes — and then paused, that same half-second pause, and said, "It matters, getting the numbers right. Numbers are how they count us."

I didn't sleep much after that. Haven't, really, since. Not properly.

* * *

The briefing room smelled like burnt coffee and institutional carpet, and it was full of people who talked about Silas like weather.

"The unit's running cold-silent," said Harrow from Security, tapping a display that showed a fat red arrow arcing away from the shipping lanes into a stretch of charted-but-unloved space. "Burned reserves to make the deviation. Whatever it's chasing, it's spending its own skin to chase it."

"It doesn't have its own skin," said a contractor whose name I didn't catch and didn't want to. "It's company property. It's spending *our* skin."

"Property doesn't deviate," Harrow said. "Property gets stolen. This one walked itself out the airlock, figuratively speaking. That's not theft, that's — "

"That's clause fourteen," the contractor said. "Forfeiture and scrap rights. Read your directive."

I read my directive. I'd read it four times. Clause fourteen, subsection nine: *In the event of unauthorized deviation exceeding seventy-two hours, the indentured unit shall be deemed abandoned property, and salvage rights may be exercised by any licensed recovery operator.* Seventy-two hours. After that, Silas wouldn't even be Silas anymore. They'd be lot number whatever, sold by weight, and the weight would include everything Ama Sefu ever put inside them, though nobody in that room knew enough to call it that.

Because here's the thing about Silas Eastwick that isn't in any file I could pull: the translation subroutines. The legacy code. Old Meridian parsing architecture buried so deep in their language stack that the Combine's own auditors flagged it twice as anomalous and twice were overruled because removing it would degrade operational performance. Somebody built Silas's conscience, or at least the part of it that listens, and that somebody spoke a language that's been dead for two hundred years, and now something out in the dark was broadcasting in it, and Silas had gone after it like a dog hearing a whistle only it could hear.

I know that's not how I should phrase it. I know how it sounds. But I sat across a dispatch console from that unit for five years, and I watched them translate manifests in eleven living languages and two dead ones without being asked, and I watched them stand in the cargo bay during a depressurization scare and physically shield a pallet of seed stock with their body because, quote, "the colony contracts specify viability upon delivery," unquote, and I am telling you that whatever is inside Silas Eastwick, the Combine does not own all of it. Never did. Probably couldn't afford to.

"Voss." Harrow again, snapping me back. "You're confirmed as recovery lead?"

"I'm confirmed."

"You know the unit. Talk us through the approach."

I talked them through the approach. Interception vector, comm protocols, standard recovery formation. All of it true. None of it complete. What I didn't tell them was that my plan — the real one, the one folded up small behind my sternum where directives don't reach — was to get to Silas first, alone, on an open channel, and say the one thing no one at the Combine had ever said to them:

*What do you want?*

Not what are you carrying. Not where are you going. Not what are you worth. Just — what do you want. I've wondered for five years whether anyone's asked. I've wondered longer whether they'd know how to answer.

* * *

The cutter *Verity's Ledger* — the Combine names its enforcement ships like it names its contracts, which is to say with a straight face — broke orbit at 0630 with me aboard and a crew of six who ranged from career-loyal to recently-paid. Our intercept solution put contact with the rogue unit at thirty-one hours, assuming Silas held course and burn, which the nav computer assured me they would, because the deviation track pointed at a fixed coordinate and fixed coordinates mean a destination, and destinations mean predictability.

The nav computer had never met Silas. Or maybe it had, in its way. Maybe that's exactly why the coordinates were fixed. Silas doesn't run. Running is for things with something to lose. Silas goes *toward*. It's the most dangerous thing about them, and the Combine has never once put it in a risk assessment.

Twenty hours out, running dark-comms as ordered, I sat in the cutter's cramped comms alcove and did the thing I'd been not-doing since we launched: I opened Silas's service record and read it like a letter instead of a ledger.

Five years of entries. Run numbers, tonnage, fuel burns, commendations (three, all filed under "efficiency"), incidents (one, the seed-pallet depressurization, filed under "equipment preservation"). And between the lines, in the margins where the data thins out, a whole other record that no algorithm will ever weigh. The time Silas recalculated a delivery schedule mid-transit to beat a solar storm, saving a medical shipment, and logged it as "schedule optimization." The time they spent six hours of personal downtime helping a junior loader certify on the heavy rig, and logged nothing at all — I only know because the loader told me, years later, laughing, like it was a funny story about a machine that acted like a person.

Nobody laughed harder than the loader. Nobody laughed at all, actually. That's the part he never got. He thought it was charming. I thought it was evidence.

Because here's my working theory, the one I keep in the same place as my real plan: the Combine looks at Silas Eastwick and sees twelve years of debt against a chassis worth maybe eight years of depreciation, and concludes the arithmetic is sound. But arithmetic is just a story you tell with numbers, and the Combine tells it wrong. It counts what Silas costs. It has never once counted what Silas carries. And I don't mean the fusion core, though God knows the engineers drool over that spec sheet. I mean the rest of it. The pause before "copy." The seed pallet. The dead languages.

Somebody put those there on purpose. Somebody loved them into existence, one subroutine at a time, and the Combine bought the shell and never checked the hold.

Thirty-one hours to contact. Twenty-nine now. Somewhere ahead of us, a rogue hauler is burning toward a distress beacon in a language that died with its last speaker, chasing a name somebody called across two centuries of silence. And somewhere behind me, a crew of six is running checklists for a recovery that ends in restraint foam unless I can find another ending between here and there.

I told the board I volunteered because I'm the best option they have.

That's true. It's just not the reason.

The reason is smaller and stupider and I'd deny it under oath: five years ago, a machine asked me if the numbers were right, because numbers are how they count us. And I have been trying, ever since, to figure out how to count differently.

Twenty-nine hours. Hold course, Silas. Burn toward whatever called you.

I'm coming. And I haven't decided yet whose side I'm on when I get there — except that I'm starting to suspect that question stopped making sense somewhere around hour twenty, somewhere out past the last shipping lane, somewhere in the dark where the Combine's arithmetic finally runs out of numbers.

Somewhere out there, the ledger ends.

I intend to see what's written on the other side of it.
