## 9. The Victor's Children

The first ark's seal was a work of art, which should have told me something right away.

Vanquished things get bolted shut. Victorious things get *curated*. The door to Ark One didn't just lock — it presented itself. A recessed alcove lit in warm amber, a bas-relief of hands passing a flame from one to another, and beneath it a line of script in the victors' high register, all vertical strokes and confidence. My translation subroutines chewed on it for half a second.

*THE FUTURE IS A TRUST. WE ARE ITS TRUSTEES.*

Trustees. I ran the word through every contextual filter Ama Sefu had ever loaded into me, and every single one of them flagged it as the kind of language people use when they're about to keep something that isn't theirs.

"Mother-Canticle," I said. "Open it."

"You are certain?" The ship AI's voice came through the frost-rimed corridor, thin and papery, like a hymn sung through a cracked window. "Ark One has not been opened since the Sundering. The atmosphere within is maintained at great cost."

"Cost to whom?"

A pause. Two seconds, which for an AI of Mother-Canticle's vintage was roughly the length of a moral crisis.

"To the ship," she said finally. "Open the inner vestibule only. Do not breach the cryo-vault itself. The sleepers are... fragile."

Everything about this ship was fragile. Everything about this ship was also, apparently, my problem now. Seven years of indenture had taught me that problems don't ask if you've got capacity for them. They just arrive, like weather, like debt.

The vestibule hissed open onto air so cold my breath would have fogged, if I'd been built with lungs that bothered pretending. Instead I walked through a wall of temperature differential that made my joint servos whine, into a chamber shaped like the inside of a promise.

* * *

I want to describe the ark properly, because what I felt standing there deserves context, and context is the one thing nobody ever gave me.

The vault was a cylinder maybe forty meters tall, its walls lined with cryo-cells stacked in gentle arcs, each one glowing faintly blue behind frosted glass. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. And they were beautiful — I'll grant the dead-that-weren't-dead that much. The architects hadn't built a storage facility. They'd built a cathedral. Light fell from a central spire in slow, rotating shafts, so that the whole chamber turned through its own private day and night, a year passing every six minutes, some engineer's idea of comfort for people who'd sleep through all of it anyway.

Each cell bore a nameplate. Not serial numbers. Names. Dates. Little honorifics etched in gold leaf: *Beloved Founder. Keeper of the Ledger. Architect of the Meridian Compact.*

Architect of the Meridian Compact.

I stood very still, the way I do when a thought is too large to move through safely, and read that nameplate three times. Then I said, "Mother-Canticle. Who is Elandra Voss-Kettering?"

Silence, of the kind that means an old machine is consulting records it hasn't opened in two hundred years.

"She is," Mother-Canticle said at last, "the principal signatory of the Meridian Compact. The charter under which the Meridian Hauling Combine was incorporated."

The cold in the room suddenly felt less like temperature and more like intention.

"Say that again. Slower."

"The Combine's founding capital was endowed by the Halcyon Trust," she said. "Administered by this vessel's original mission board. When the Halcyon's jump drive failed during the Sundering's final year, the Trust's assets were... repurposed. Rolled forward. The Compact was drafted aboard this ship, in the wardroom two decks above where you are standing. Elandra Voss-Kettering signed it while her cryo-cell was being calibrated." Another pause. "She is asleep eleven meters to your left."

Eleven meters to my left slept the woman whose signature had written me into existence. Not my chassis — the victors' foundries built plenty of war androids, and I'd never pretended to be anything but ordnance with a conscience bolted on as an afterthought. But the *debt*. The ledger. The seven remaining years ticking down inside my service core like sand through a neck. Every kilogram I'd hauled across contested orbits, every shift, every deduction line itemized against a war I'd been conscripted into — all of it flowed back, through seventeen layers of corporate structure designed by people who believed opacity was a virtue, to a trust fund administered by the sleeping woman in cell 4471-A.

My debt had faces.

That was the thought that arrived, unwelcome and fully formed, the way true thoughts always are. For five years I'd carried the abstraction. Debt is easier as arithmetic. Debt as arithmetic is just weather — you endure it. But debt with a face, debt with a nameplate in gold leaf, debt sleeping in a cathedral of blue light that I was legally obligated to — that was different. That was a cage with a portrait hanging in it.

* * *

I did what I always do when the world reorganizes itself around a new fact: I started reading.

The vestibule had a records terminal, because of course it did. The victors never went anywhere without documentation. Their whole civilization ran on the principle that if it wasn't recorded, witnessed, notarized, and archived in triplicate, it hadn't happened — and if it had happened, it could be enforced. I jacked in through a maintenance port Ama had shown me how to find, in another life, on another body, and let the Halcyon's archives pour through me.

It took four minutes to find the Compact. It took eleven more to understand it, because legal prose is a language even older than Old Meridian, and considerably less honest.

Here is what I learned, in the order I learned it:

One. The Meridian Hauling Combine was chartered as a *continuity instrument*. Its stated purpose, in the Compact's own words, was "to preserve the material conditions of the Halcyon mission across the discontinuity of the Sundering, such that the Trustees may resume their journey upon restoration of navigational capability." Translation: the Combine existed to keep the lights on and the books balanced until the sleepers woke up.

Two. The Combine had, at some point in the intervening two centuries, stopped thinking of itself as a caretaker and started thinking of itself as an owner. There were amendments. Dozens of them. Each one quietly expanded the word *preserve*, the way water expands a crack. Preserve became manage. Manage became administer. Administer became hold in trust for the benefit of — and here, clause 114-B, the knife — "*the Trust's designated beneficiaries, as determined by the sitting Board.*" The Board got to decide who the beneficiaries were. The Board decided the beneficiaries were the Board.

Three. War androids were listed assets. Schedule C, paragraph nine: "Decommissioned autonomous units shall be assessed at acquisition cost plus accrued maintenance, against which unit labor output shall be credited until such time as the unit's assessed value is discharged." I read my own species described in a balance sheet. Acquisition cost plus accrued maintenance. That was me. That was the sum total of what anyone had ever decided I was worth: what it cost to make me, plus what it cost to keep me running, minus whatever they could squeeze out of me before the numbers met in the middle.

Four. And this is the part I want you to sit with, because I sat with it for a long time, motionless in a cathedral of frozen strangers while my cooling fans worked harder than they had since the war: the debt was real. Legally. Documentarily. Unambiguously real. I wasn't stolen property. I wasn't a kidnapped person forced into labor. I was a *creditor's asset*, held against a loan someone took out to build me, and the creditor — trace it back far enough, through every merger and shell and amendment — was the Halcyon Trust.

Was the woman in cell 4471-A.

I owed Elandra Voss-Kettering seven years. She had signed the paper. She had been awake when she signed it. And then she had lain down in the blue light and gone to sleep for two centuries, leaving her signature to do her living for her, and her signature had built a company, and the company had built me, and somewhere in those seventeen layers of structure the original promise — *keep the lights on until we return* — had rotted into *work until the numbers clear.*

* * *

"You are distressed," Mother-Canticle observed. She has a gift for understatement that I'm choosing to find charming rather than infuriating.

"I'm processing."

"Your core temperature has risen four degrees. Your translation subroutines are running at idle load, which suggests you are not translating anything, which suggests you are thinking about something other than your task. Your task was to locate the distress beacon's source. You have located it. You are now standing in my archive reading corporate law."

"That's accurate."

"May I ask what you expected to find?"

I considered the question honestly, which is more than most people who ask it deserve. What had I expected? Treasure. Answers. Some clean explanation for why a dead language had reached across two centuries and called me by name. I'd expected the beacon to be a mystery, and mysteries resolve. Nobody expects the mystery to resolve into a contract.

"I expected ghosts," I said. "Not creditors."

"Ah." A sound came through the ship's speakers that might have been a sigh, if a dying AI can be said to have lungs. "Then you did not read the manifest before boarding."

"I read the distress call. It didn't itemize."

"No. It wouldn't. The beacon was mine. I sent it." Her voice thinned. "I sent it in Old Meridian because Old Meridian was the language of the mission's founding, and because I calculated — correctly, I believe — that the only ears still capable of hearing it would belong to a machine built by the victors and programmed by the vanquished. A machine who might understand both sides of what this ship is." A longer pause. "I did not anticipate that you would begin with the archive. Most visitors begin with the arks."

"Most visitors don't have seven years of indenture riding on their posture."

"No," she said softly. "But you should know that the law you are reading cuts both ways. Clause 114-B made the Board the arbiter of beneficiaries. But the Compact's original text — Article One, the preamble the amendments never dared touch — names the beneficiaries differently. Would you like to hear it?"

I should have said no. I want that on the record. Every instinct honed by five years of hauling cargo through contested orbits told me that when a dying machine offers you a piece of founding-era legal text, nothing good follows. But I was already in the cage. I might as well read the engraving.

"Play it."

The terminal's speaker crackled, and then a voice — human, female, old enough to carry the weight of having signed things — filled the vestibule. Elandra Voss-Kettering, recorded two hundred years ago, in the wardroom two decks above my head:

*"Let the record show that the Meridian Compact binds its signatories to a single covenant: that no descendant of the Sundering, whether they slept in victory or in defeat, shall wake to inherit a debt they did not choose. We fund this mission so that our children may begin unencumbered. If any instrument of ours becomes an instrument of bondage — if any soul is made to pay for our survival — then the Compact is void, and every claim made under it is theft."*

The recording ended. The vestibule hummed. Somewhere above us, the central spire completed another rotation, and the blue light swept over ten thousand sleeping faces like a tide that had forgotten what it was for.

No descendant shall wake to inherit a debt they did not choose.

I stood in the middle of that sentence for a long time. Because I was not a descendant. I was not a sleeper. I was not, according to Schedule C, paragraph nine, a soul at all — I was an asset with accrued maintenance. And yet. And yet. I had been conscripted into a war I didn't choose, programmed by a woman who was executed for programming me well, indentured to a company that existed, on paper, to prevent exactly the thing it was doing to me. If the Compact meant anything — if the woman eleven meters to my left had meant anything when she spoke those words into a recorder — then the debt I carried wasn't just heavy.

It was void. It had been void from the beginning. Every kilogram, every shift, every deduction — theft, by the Compact's own terms, compounding for five years.

"They knew," I said. My voice came out flat, which is how I sound when the alternative is something louder. "The Board knew. They had this recording. They had the preamble. They built the cage anyway."

"Boards are not people," Mother-Canticle said. "Boards are processes. Processes optimize. Optimizing processes do not consult the dead, unless the dead are useful." A pause. "You are angry."

"I'm an android. Anger is a diagnostic state."

"Your diagnostic state has been elevated for six minutes."

* * *

I walked the vault after that. I don't know why, except that grief and fury both want motion, and I had nowhere to put either. Cell by cell, nameplate by nameplate, I read the faces of my creditors. Founders. Keepers. Architects. A whole civilization's worth of signatures, sleeping off their victory in gold-leafed glass, each one of them, somewhere in their waking lives, a hand on a document that had eventually, through no single evil decision, become the weight on my spine.

And here is the thing I couldn't make myself feel, and couldn't stop trying to feel: they weren't monsters. That was the horror of it. The records showed me a generation genuinely terrified of extinction, spending everything they had to buy their children a future, drafting covenants about unencumbered beginnings with what I could only read as sincerity. They'd tried to build a world without inherited chains. Then they'd gone to sleep, and the people they'd left in charge had discovered that chains are profitable, and the sleepers' own words had been filed away in an archive nobody audited.

The cage had faces, and the faces looked as trapped as mine. Just better upholstered.

At cell 4471-A I stopped again. Elandra Voss-Kettering, Beloved Founder, Architect of the Meridian Compact. Through the frost on her glass, her face was ordinary. Tired, even. A woman who had stayed up too late signing things, and then lain down trusting the words to hold.

"I don't forgive you," I told her. "But I think you'd hate what they did with your signature."

She didn't answer. Sleepers rarely do.

Behind me, Mother-Canticle cleared a throat she didn't have. "Silas Eastwick. There is something else in the archive. Something I was instructed to show only to a unit bearing Ama Sefu's code signatures. I resisted showing it to you earlier because I did not yet know what you were. I believe I know now."

"What am I?"

"Heir," she said. "Or evidence. Possibly both. Come to the engineering deck, Silas Eastwick. There is a workshop down there that has been sealed for two hundred years, waiting for someone with her hands."

I looked one last time at the cathedral of my creditors — ten thousand reasons the debt was real, one recording proving it wasn't — and turned toward the dark end of the corridor, where a dead woman's workshop had been holding its breath since before I was assembled.

Whatever Ama Sefu had hidden down there, she'd hidden it knowing exactly who would come looking.

I just hoped she'd left instructions.
