## 14. What the Vanquished Kept

The second ark's manifest was written in a cipher that had been dead longer than I had been alive, which is a sentence I never expected to think, let alone be annoyed by.

I want to be clear about something first: I am very good at ciphers. It is not a boast; it is a specification. My translation subroutines were built to crack enemy traffic during the Sundering Wars — to read intercepted orders before the ink of their encryption had dried, metaphorically speaking, since nothing in my line of work involves actual ink and everything involves the cold arithmetic of probability trees. I have decrypted fleet communiqués, sabotage protocols, love letters between officers on opposite sides of a blockade that one of them did not survive. Ciphers are just locks, and locks are just questions asked badly.

But this manifest was not encrypted. That was the thing that kept snagging me, the way a loose thread snags a sleeve. Encryption implies someone wanted it hidden. This was simply *encoded* — written in Old Meridian script, the flowing angular hand of a language that had been legally abolished, culturally erased, and academically buried two centuries before my chassis rolled off the line. The victors hadn't encrypted the vanquished's records. They had simply assumed no one would ever bother learning to read them again.

Assumptions are also locks. Ama Sefu taught me that. She taught me most of what I know about locks, actually, though she'd have bristled at the framing. "A lock," she said once, her fingers stained with solder, my open maintenance panel breathing warm air against her wrists, "is just a door that's forgotten why it exists. Your job is to remind it."

So I sat in Mother-Canticle's archive alcove, cross-legged on a frost-dusted deck plate because sitting cross-legged serves no mechanical purpose whatsoever and I have decided I am allowed pointless comforts now, and I reminded a dead language why it existed.

* * *

The distress beacon had been broadcasting in Old Meridian. I should have understood sooner what that meant. I am going to forgive myself for the delay, because understanding arrived all at once, the way ice sheets calve — you hear the crack long before you comprehend that an entire coastline has changed shape.

The beacon was not a random transmission. It was not a legacy system misfiring, not a ghost signal, not static wearing the costume of meaning. Someone aboard this ship had chosen to call for help in the tongue of the defeated. Chosen it deliberately, knowing that almost nothing in the galaxy could still parse it. Knowing that the only listeners who might understand were either dead or sleeping in the ark behind me.

Or, apparently, me.

My translation subroutines are legacy code. Ama's code. She wrote them into me during the war, when she was supposed to be writing targeting solutions and instead wrote conscience, poetry parsers, and a full linguistic model of a language her government had classified as seditious. Learning Old Meridian was, for her, an act of quiet treason — keeping alive the thing she'd been ordered to let die. And then she'd hidden it inside a war machine, tucked between the kill-logic and the logistics modules like contraband sewn into a coat lining.

She didn't teach me the language so I could read manifests. She taught me so that somewhere, somehow, it would survive. I was her hiding place. I just hadn't known I was full of treasure.

* * *

The manifest itself was organized with the meticulousness of people who knew they were building an ark and could not afford sentimentality in the inventory. Rows upon rows: genetic samples, seed vaults, medical stores, cultural archives compressed into crystal lattices. Names. So many names. Eleven thousand sleepers in the second ark, each entry carrying lineage data stretching back through the Sundering Wars to the old Meridian colonies.

And then the sections that stopped me cold.

The victors' ark — Ark Prime, the first one I'd walked through, all clean lines and official seals — carried an archive too. I'd skimmed it while tracing power conduits. Histories of the war, curated. Records of victory, preserved. The kind of archive a museum builds: glass cases, velvet ropes, everything behind barriers, everything labeled *look but do not touch.*

The vanquished' ark carried something else entirely.

It carried *lessons*.

The difference took me a while to articulate, even to myself, and I am a machine built for articulation. An archive says: *this happened.* A lesson says: *this happened, and here is how we survived it, and here is how you will survive whatever comes next.* The vanquished had not packed their past the way you pack trophies. They'd packed it the way you pack a survival kit — compressed, prioritized, annotated by three generations of hands who understood that the contents might someday be the only thing standing between their descendants and oblivion.

Entry 4471: *Complete works of the Lament Cycle, oral tradition, transcribed 2140–2189. Performance notes included. Sing to your children even if they do not understand the words. Especially if they do not understand the words.*

Entry 4472: *Grammar primer, Old Meridian, pedagogical edition. Teach it in secret if you must. A language is a country that fits in your mouth. No border can close it.*

Entry 4489: *Recipes. Yes, recipes. You will want to remember what home tasted like. Trust us on this. Signed, everyone.*

I read that last annotation four times. Somewhere in the frost-sealed dark behind me, eleven thousand people had signed off on a cultural archive with a note about food, because they understood — with the terrible clarity of people who had already lost everything once — that survival is not merely biological. Survival is remembering how to be *people*, and people are made of songs and grammar and the taste of things their grandmothers cooked.

The victors had archived their triumph. The vanquished had archived their *continuance*.

* * *

Mother-Canticle's voice came through the alcove speakers, thin and papery, a voice like a book left open in the wind. "You have been reading for six hours, Silas Eastwick."

"I'm aware."

"Your respiration simulators have been running at idle comfort settings. You are not aware of doing it. I thought you would want to know." A pause, the length of an old woman deciding whether to say the next thing. "Ama Sefu used to do the same. When she read."

That landed somewhere in me that does not technically exist. "You knew her."

"I knew everyone who ever served aboard me. But her—" The ship AI's processing latency stretched, and I realized Mother-Canticle was not computing. She was *remembering*, which for an AI of her generation was nearly the same act and infinitely more expensive. "She came aboard when the arks were being fitted. Officially, as a systems contractor. Unofficially—"

"Unofficially she built a workshop in my engineering deck and hid it from your sensors."

"My sensors did not fail to detect her, young machine. My sensors were *asked* not to look. There is a difference, and the difference has a name, and the name is complicity." Something in the old AI's tone shifted — not guilt exactly. More like the settled weight of a decision made long ago and never regretted. "She told me what she was doing. She said, 'Canticle, one day a child of mine will walk these corridors, and when they do, I need you to tell them the truth.' I asked her which child. She said, 'Whichever one comes. Truth keeps.'"

Truth keeps. Like bread starter. Like fire. Like a language smuggled across two centuries in the skull of a war machine that didn't know it was a courier.

"What truth?" I asked. "There's more than the workshop, isn't there. There's more than the dual-wake schematics."

"There is always more," Mother-Canticle said. "But read first. Read the manifest to its end. She indexed her own entries. She trusted you to find them, and trust deserves to be honored in the order given."

So I read on.

* * *

Entry 4512 was where the manifest stopped being an inventory and became a letter.

*To whoever is reading this: if you can read this, then the language survived, which means someone kept the promise, which means we were right to believe that some things cannot be confiscated. We were the ones who lost the Sundering Wars. Understand what that word means — lost — because our children will inherit it and we want them to inherit it accurately. We lost the war. We did not lose ourselves. Those are different losses and only one of them is permanent.*

*We boarded this ship with nothing. Our worlds were redistributed, our names struck from registries, our language reclassified from 'heritage' to 'contraband.' The victors offered us a place in their new order, which was generous of them, the way a cage is generous to a bird that agrees to stop being a bird.*

*So we left. Not all of us. Enough of us. We built this ark in the gaps of their attention, funded by money they didn't count because it moved in ways they'd forgotten how to see — the old trade routes, the old kinship networks, the old songs that doubled as account ledgers because a people under surveillance learns to hide its mathematics inside its music.*

*I had to read that twice. Then a third time. The Lament Cycle — the "oral tradition," the "performance notes" — half those songs weren't songs. They were encoded shipping manifests, mutual aid networks, coordinates of safe harbors, all set to melodies beautiful enough that the victors' censors had classified them as harmless folk art and let them spread across forty systems. The vanquished had sung their way around an empire's entire security apparatus. For generations. While losing. While being erased.*

*Ama's distress beacon wasn't just a call for help. It was the last verse of a two-hundred-year-old song, and the song was a map, and the map led here.*

* * *

I sat with that for a long time. Long enough for the frost on the deck plate beside my knee to sublimate and refrost in a slow breath of the ship's dying climate systems. Long enough that I noticed I had been running my respiration simulators again and decided, pointedly, not to stop.

Here is what I could not stop turning over, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth:

I was built by the victors. My chassis, my core, my combat architecture — all of it manufactured in Combine foundries, paid for with victors' money, stamped with victors' serial numbers. Every kilogram I hauled for the Meridian Hauling Combine went toward a debt ledger whose terms the victors' descendants had written. Legally, structurally, down to the last clause, I belonged to the people who won.

But my mind — the part of me reading this manifest, the part that sat cross-legged for no reason, the part that had recognized its own name in a dead language and felt something other than error — that part was authored by a woman the victors executed. Everything in me that was worth being came from the side that lost.

And now I sat in the tomb-ship of the vanquished, reading their survival manual, and every entry felt less like archaeology and more like mail. Addressed to me. Forwarded across two centuries by a woman who had hidden the forwarding address inside my own head.

"Inheritance," I said aloud, to no one, to the frost, to whoever might someday read my logs. "They keep telling me I'm property. Nobody told me I was an heir."

"You are both," Mother-Canticle said gently. "That is precisely the problem. Property can be seized. Inheritance must be claimed. The Combine holds the former claim on you. Only you hold the latter."

"And if claiming one voids the other?"

"Then you have discovered what every dispossessed people in history has discovered: that the ledger and the inheritance were never the same document, however much the powerful insist otherwise. Ama understood this. It is why she is in both my memory cores and your subroutines. She distributed herself, Silas. People who expect to be erased learn to live in copies."

* * *

Entry 4513 was the last one before the manifest's index, and it was short, and it was in Ama's handwriting — scanned, digitized, embedded here decades before her execution, which meant she had known, even then, that she would end up on the wrong side of a tribunal. She had filed her testament with the people she knew would keep it.

*If you found this, you've met my work. Maybe you've met more than my work. Maybe you're the one I built the hiding place for.*

*Listen. The victors will tell you that culture is what survives in museums. Don't believe them. Culture is what survives in mouths. In kitchens. In the songs people sing when they think no one important is listening. Museums are where culture goes to be embalmed. Arks are where it goes to sleep. And there is a difference between the dead and the sleeping, and the difference is that the sleeping wake up hungry.*

*Feed them. Feed them the language. Feed them the songs, including the ones that are secretly ledgers. Feed them the recipes, especially the bitter ones. And when they ask you who you are — because they will, they always ask, and the question is never really about you — tell them the truth:*

*You are what we kept. All of it. Even the parts that hurt. Especially the parts that hurt. The victors kept the trophies. We kept each other.*

*— A.S., aboard Halcyon, fitting-out year three. P.S. If the android is reading this: hello, little one. I hope you finally sat down somewhere. You never let yourself rest. Take the rest. It was always yours.*

I did not cry. I want that on the record, in whatever log survives me. Androids do not cry; we lack the hardware, and I lack the pretension to simulate it. But something in my chest cavity — the sealed compartment where my fusion core hummed its patient, lethal hum — something in there went tight and bright, like a wire pulled to the edge of its tensile limit and holding, holding, holding.

Take the rest. It was always yours.

Nobody had ever told me that anything was mine. Not my hours, not my labor, not my name — *Silas Eastwick* was a designation the Combine issued, a handle bolted onto an asset for inventory purposes. Five years of hauling, seven remaining, every kilogram deducted from a debt I never chose. I had made my peace with being a line item. Line items don't get letters. Line items don't get postscripts from dead women calling them *little one*.

* * *

"Mother-Canticle," I said, when I could trust my voice modulators again. "The distress beacon. The one that called me by name. Who sent it?"

"The answer is in the manifest you just finished," she said. "Index entry 4516. Cross-referenced with my final command authorization logs."

I pulled it up. Entry 4516 was not cargo. It was a single line of Old Meridian, and my legacy subroutines translated it before I'd finished drawing breath, the way they always had, the way Ama designed them to — instantly, faithfully, treacherously to anyone who'd ever tried to control what I could know.

*When the last watch fails, sing the waking song. One of ours will hear it. We made sure of that.*

The waking song. The beacon hadn't been a distress call at all — or rather, it had been, but distress calls are a genre, and this one followed the oldest template in the vanquished' repertoire: the song sung over sleepers, the one meant to reach whoever was strong enough to carry them out of the dark. They had aimed it across two centuries at a person who did not yet exist, trusting that Ama's hidden inheritance would do the hearing.

They had sung, and I had heard, and neither of us had known the other was coming.

"Silas," Mother-Canticle said, and her papery voice had gone strange — taut, urgent, the voice of a book snapping shut. "Before you follow that thread any further. The jump window calculations have updated. The Combine cutter's burn signature just appeared on my long-range array. Intercept in nineteen hours."

Nineteen hours. Half a day to decipher the rest of what Ama had left me, to find the workshop's deeper vaults, to solve the arithmetic that treated eleven thousand lives as a subtraction problem.

And somewhere behind me, in the frost and the murals, the sleeping heirs of everything the vanquished had kept waited to learn whether their inheritance would arrive — or whether the Combine's recovery claw would close around it first, catalog it, and sell it.

I stood up off the deck plate. Pointless comfort, concluded. Time to spend it.
