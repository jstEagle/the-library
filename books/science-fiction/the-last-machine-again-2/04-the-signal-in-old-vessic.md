## 4. The Signal in Old Vessic

Sefa kept the night watch because the night watch was honest. Daylight shifts on the Long Quiet were full of small negotiations — Toma wanting power rerouted to the fabricators, Ivo wanting quiet, Odile wanting the nav console left exactly as she'd left it, down to the angle of her coffee cup — but at 0300 ship's time there was only Sefa, a cup of cold tea, and the long patient hiss of the universe talking to itself.

She had learned to love that hiss. Eleven years of running dark between ports that pretended not to see you taught you the difference between noise and voice. Pulsars ticked like bad clocks. Magnetars groaned in frequencies that made her back teeth ache when she ran them through audio for no reason except homesickness. The background radiation of the universe itself murmured at the floor of every spectrum, a sound like snow falling on snow, if snow could fall forever.

And then, once or twice a year, something spoke.

Most of the time it was nothing. A ghost echo off a debris field, a freighter's automated beacon still squawking its position years after its owners had gone bankrupt or dead or simply stopped answering. Once, memorably, it had been a colony's weather satellite looping a harvest festival broadcast from before the war — three hundred hours of fiddles and laughter and a woman calling children in from fields that were probably ash now. Sefa had listened to all of it. She'd told no one. Some things you carried alone, the way Odile carried whatever she carried down in the hold every night, and the way Ivo carried the two names he never said aloud but which lived in the way he checked the crew manifest each morning, counting them like a man counting coins he expected to be stolen.

So when the pattern arrived at 0347, Sefa did what she always did first: she doubted it.

It came in on the low band, buried under a solar storm front rolling off the Kessel Shoals, and at first she took it for interference — a rhythmic stutter in the static, four pulses and a gap, four pulses and a gap, so regular it looked manufactured and so faint it looked accidental. She isolated it. Cleaned it. Ran it through the discriminator twice, because the discriminator had a habit of finding patterns in chaos the way grieving people found faces in clouds.

The pattern held.

Four pulses. Gap. Four pulses. Gap. Then, after the ninth repetition, a longer sequence — dense, structured, syllable-like bursts with the cadence of speech. Not code. Not telemetry. *Speech.*

Sefa sat very still, her tea forgotten, and felt the hair rise along her forearms.

Because she knew that cadence. Not consciously — not at first — but somewhere below thought, in the place where a person keeps the sounds of their childhood. Her grandmother had sung lullabies in a language the government had struck from the registers. Her grandmother had prayed in it, quietly, with the windows shuttered, because in the resettlement camps after Meridian you didn't speak Vessic where anyone could hear. By the time Sefa was ten, her grandmother was dead and the language was officially extinct, catalogued in naval archives as one of the war's cultural casualties, a footnote in a report about a hospital ship called the Amaranth.

Old Vessic. The tongue of a people who had been passengers, medics, refugees — and then, in a single broadcast-slaughtered hour, statistics.

The language of the dead. And here it was, arriving out of the dark on her board at 0347, asking — because that was what distress patterns did, they asked — to be heard.

* * *

For eleven minutes Sefa did nothing at all. She sat with her hands flat on the console, listening to the pattern repeat, and let herself believe it and disbelieve it in alternating waves, the way you test ice by putting your weight on it and taking it off again.

The signal source was deep in the Kessel Drift, past the contested corridors, past the beacon chains the navy had seeded during the war and never bothered to decommission. Distance meant the transmission was old — light-lag old, possibly decades old, a scream arriving long after the mouth that made it had closed. That was the merciful explanation. Signals didn't have to come from living senders. Automated distress beacons could repeat for forty years on battery reserves designed to outlast hope.

But automated beacons didn't speak in full sentences.

She pulled the spectral analysis up again and looked at the structure of the longer sequences. There were pauses in them. Deliberate ones — the kind speakers leave for breath, for emphasis, for the listener to catch up. There were repetitions with variations, the way a person repeats an instruction when they're afraid it wasn't understood. And underneath the structure, when she ran the raw waveform through audio, there was texture: the soft irregularities of a human throat, a human mouth shaping words around fear.

Not a machine. A recording, maybe. Or —

Or someone alive, out there in the Drift, speaking a language that had been murdered along with everyone who spoke it.

Sefa looked at the report field on her console. Protocol was simple and absolute: anomalous transmissions in contested space were logged, flagged, and reported to the captain within the shift. The navy remnant had standing orders about signals in Old Vessic specifically — she knew this because the standing orders were part of why she'd taken this job and then spent eleven years being careful. Any transmission in a proscribed language was presumed to be either a loyalist trap or a remnant of the Amaranth incident, and both categories were to be reported to fleet command through secure channels, which meant reporting it to the people who had spent a decade making sure the Amaranth stayed a footnote.

If she filed the standard report, the signal would go into a queue. The queue would go to a liaison. The liaison would go to a naval intelligence office whose job, as far as Sefa could tell from eleven years of watching ports close and archives seal and families get relocated away from certain memorial sites, was to make sure the war ended the way the winners needed it to end: quietly, with no one left who remembered how it actually sounded.

Her hand hovered over the report field.

Then she opened a private partition instead — a sliver of storage she'd carved out years ago behind a maintenance log, big enough for music, for letters she never sent, for the recordings of her grandmother's lullabies that she'd reconstructed from memory and was almost certainly getting wrong. She routed the incoming signal into that partition, raw and unfiltered, every repetition, timestamped, unaltered.

It wasn't destroying evidence. It wasn't even concealing it, really — the main log would show the contact, would show the band, the bearing, the duration. Anyone auditing her shift would see a comms officer who detected an anomaly and reported it, just late, just after she'd finished verifying it. Verification took time. Verification was diligence. No one had ever been disciplined for diligence.

The lie she told herself was smooth and professional and it fit perfectly, and she hated how well it fit.

* * *

The signal repeated for another two hours. Sefa recorded all of it.

Somewhere in the second hour she started trying to translate, and that was when wonder began, very slowly, to curdle.

She didn't speak Old Vessic. Almost nobody did; the generation that had grown up in the camps had been schooled in Standard, and the elders had died with their prayers shuttered behind windows. But her grandmother had taught her fragments before the end — not lessons, nothing so dangerous as lessons, just words attached to things. The word for water. The word for bread. The word for *come inside*, said a thousand times on winter evenings. And a handful of phrases from the lullabies, which Sefa had later realized, with a shock that still visited her some nights, were not entirely lullabies. They were elegies. Her grandmother had been singing the names of the dead to children too young to ask questions.

She played the longest sequence against those fragments, over and over, matching cadences, hunting for cognates the way you'd dig through ash for anything that hadn't burned.

She found three.

The first was *vessa* — water, life, the word her grandmother used for everything that sustained. It appeared early in the sequence and repeated, and its placement made Sefa's stomach tighten, because in distress grammar — and distress grammar was universal, she'd handled enough maydays in Standard to know its shape — the earliest repeated element is the subject. Who is speaking. What is in danger.

*Vessa* in that position didn't mean "water." It meant the speaker was identifying themselves as the thing that sustains. Medics. Healers. People whose whole profession was keeping others alive.

The second fragment was harder, and she almost wished she hadn't found it. It matched a phrase from the third elegy-lullaby, the one her grandmother sang slowest: *kel-avess*, which her grandmother had translated once, reluctantly, when Sefa was fourteen and stubborn, as *"we did not run."*

It appeared in the middle of the sequence. Twice.

The third fragment she found last, near the end, and it was the smallest word in the whole transmission and the one that broke her: *mira*. Come inside. The word for opening your door to someone in the cold.

It was the final word of the sequence. Before the silence. Before the pattern reset and began again — four pulses, gap, four pulses, gap — like a heart that refused to accept it had already stopped.

Someone out in the Kessel Drift was broadcasting in the language of a slaughtered people, identifying themselves as healers, saying *we did not run*, and ending with *come inside* — an invitation, a plea, a door held open across decades of vacuum.

Sefa pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw constellations.

* * *

At 0612, ship's time, the signal changed.

She almost missed it, because the change was subtle — the four-pulse heartbeat faltered, skipped, resumed with a different interval. Then the long sequences stopped coming altogether, and what remained was the pulse, slower now, degraded, the carrier wave fraying at the edges like cloth worn thin.

Dying. Whatever was broadcasting was dying, or its power was, and there was no way to tell the difference from here, and no way to care about the difference either, because both meant the same thing: the door was closing.

Sefa stood up. Sat down. Stood up again.

Protocol said report. Protocol said flag it, route it, wash her hands of it, let it descend into the queues and liaisons and sealed archives where the war's loose ends went to be tied off and buried. Protocol had kept the Long Quiet alive for eleven years. Protocol was the reason forty-one people slept in the hold below instead of lying in graves with their names read aloud — though sometimes, in her darker arithmetic, Sefa wondered whether sleeping backups and graves were separated by anything more than temperature.

But protocol also said that a distress call was a distress call. That was the oldest protocol there was, older than navies, older than wars, older than the Meridian atrocity and every atrocity before it. You heard someone calling for help, you answered. Every sailor in history had agreed to that, on every ocean and every void, and the agreement was the only thing that made any of it bearable — the idea that no matter how far from home you died, someone, someday, would hear you.

The navy had turned that agreement into a crime when the language of the dying became proscribed. Sefa had watched them do it, port by port, archive by archive, and she had kept her head down and her logs clean for eleven years because survival was also an agreement, the one the Long Quiet had made with itself.

Now the two agreements were standing in the same room, and one of them was bleeding.

She keyed the intercom to the captain's cabin, then paused, finger hovering, and did the last dishonest thing of her watch: she checked that the private partition was sealed, that the raw recordings were hidden behind the maintenance log, that her secret was safe before her duty began.

Then she pressed the key.

"Captain. Sorry to wake you. I've got an anomaly on the low band — repeating distress pattern, origin deep in the Kessel Drift." A breath. Tell him the shape of it, she thought. Let him decide what the shape means. "Sir, the transmission is in Old Vessic."

The silence on the other end lasted four seconds. She counted them. In that silence she heard Ivo's chair creak, heard the specific quality of a man going from asleep to awake the way a ship goes from dark to battle stations — all at once, everywhere.

"Say again?" he said finally.

"Old Vessic, Captain." Her voice was steady. She was proud of it and ashamed of it in equal measure. "Extinct since the Amaranth. And sir — it's structured. Full sequences. Whoever sent it wasn't running an automated beacon."

Another silence, longer. When Ivo spoke again, his voice had the flat carefulness of a man walking on ice over deep water.

"Have you reported this to fleet channels?"

"Not yet, sir. I wanted to verify the pattern first." Diligence. The lie fit so well she barely felt it anymore. "Verification's ongoing."

"I'm coming to the bridge," Ivo said. "Wake Odile. And Sefa—" a pause, and she braced, because Ivo missed nothing, had survived two sons and a war by missing nothing— "keep the raw feed. All of it. Don't let anything overwrite it."

"No, sir," Sefa said, and discovered that this, at least, was no lie at all. "I already have."

She cut the channel and sat back, and on the console before her the pulse from the Drift beat on — four pulses, gap, four pulses, gap — slower now, fainter, a door held open by arms that were trembling.

Behind a maintenance log, in a partition no audit had ever found, sat two hours of the dead speaking. And somewhere below her feet, in the cold of the hold, forty-one sleepers lay dreaming in the same language — and Sefa had never thought of that connection until this exact moment, and now she could not stop thinking about it, and the thought arrived with the weight of a heading already plotted:

*They would know what it says.*

She stood, wrapped her hands around her cold tea as if it were something warm, and went to wake Odile Pemberton, who talked to the sleeping every night in a tongue the galaxy believed was buried — and who was about to learn that the grave had started answering back.
