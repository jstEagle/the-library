## 7. Course to Kessel Drift

The chart of the Meridian Reach had not changed in eleven years. The stars hadn't moved — not meaningfully, not at the distances that mattered — but the overlays had. War-era traffic corridors still glowed on the Long Quiet's plotting table like scars that refused to fade: amber lanes where convoys had burned, red hatching where minefields had been seeded and never swept, gray zones marked with the old naval shorthand for *we do not answer distress calls from this volume*.

Odile stood over the table with her hands flat on its cold surface and let the ship's hum come up through her palms, through her wrists, into the bones of her forearms. Somewhere below her feet, forty-one backups slept in their blue-lit cradles, and one of them — Rell, gunner's mate, second death, Vessic-born — had twitched toward a signal in a language that was supposed to be ash.

*Course to Kessel Drift,* Sefa had said, as if it were a place you could simply go.

As if there were a way to go there that didn't pass through everything Odile had spent a decade refusing to remember.

She pulled up the route library. Most of it was hers — eleven years of careful, cowardly cartography, every lane chosen to skirt the war's graveyards by the widest possible margin. The Long Quiet had made its living hauling ore and seed-stock and machine parts along the edges of the map, never through the middle, never through the places where the names of dead systems were still spoken in hushed voices by people who'd lost someone there.

Kessel Drift sat in the middle of all of it.

She typed the coordinates anyway. Her hands did it before she'd finished deciding to, and that frightened her more than the destination did.

* * *

Toma found her an hour later, when the preliminary plot was done and the fuel numbers were ugly. He came onto the bridge carrying two cups of something he called coffee and everyone else called engine-room swill, and he set one down beside her elbow without asking, which was his way of saying he knew she hadn't slept.

"Show me," he said.

She turned the plot toward him. The route ran out of the shipping lanes at Junction Nine, cut across the Tarnow Exclusion — gray zone, unswept mines, no rescue — threaded the debris shoals off Corvane, and then dropped into the long dark between beacon chains where nothing had been maintained since the armistice talks collapsed. Nineteen days of transit if they pushed the drives harder than Toma would like. Twenty-three if they didn't.

"That's the pretty version," Toma said.

"There isn't a pretty version."

"No." He drank his swill and studied the table the way he studied failing machinery: without flinching, cataloguing everything wrong with it. "There's this bit here." He tapped the Tarnow Exclusion. "You've routed us through the middle of it."

"It's the only corridor wide enough for a freighter our mass. The edges are worse — the minefields drift, they've had eleven years to wander into the lanes."

"I know why you routed us through it, Odile. I'm asking if *you* know."

She looked at him. He wasn't challenging her. That was the thing about Toma — he never challenged, he only asked questions whose answers he intended to stand next to you while you gave them.

"My hands plotted it," she said. "I checked the work afterward. It's sound. It's just — I haven't flown that corridor since before the war ended. I flew it during."

"You were navy."

"I was signals," she said, and heard how carefully the word was shaped, how much it left out. "We went everywhere. Nobody shoots at a relay tender."

"Somebody shot at the Amaranth."

The name landed in the room like a dropped tool. Neither of them picked it up. On the plotting table, the route's amber line pulsed softly, patient, waiting for her confirmation.

"The drift fields off Corvane are dense," she said instead. "I'll hand-fly the approach. You'll want to be on the coils the whole time — we'll be shaving debris at relative velocities that will make your teeth ache."

"My teeth already ache. It's the coffee." He set the cup down. "Ivo wants to know when we jump."

"When the vote's confirmed. Not before."

"He already voted yes himself. You know that."

"I know that he voted yes before anyone asked him to. That's different from the crew following him." She turned back to the table. "Get some rest, Toma. I'll have the final solution by second watch."

He paused at the hatch. "Your hands," he said. "What did you mean, they plotted it?"

She kept her eyes on the chart. "Nothing. Go on."

But after the hatch cycled shut she looked down at her own fingers, resting on the edge of the table, and admitted what she'd been refusing to admit since the signal came in: she had typed the coordinates for Kessel Drift from memory. Not from the archive. From memory. A relay station decommissioned nine years ago, its charts purged from every public database, its approach vectors existing now only in the heads of people who'd been ordered never to speak of it — and her hands had produced them whole, smooth as water, while her mind stood aside and watched.

The body keeps old loyalties. She'd read that somewhere once, in a book about trauma, back when she thought trauma was something that happened to other people, people who screamed in their sleep instead of people who woke calm and tidy and unable to say why the morning tasted like copper.

* * *

Second watch. The bridge dimmed itself around her, lights dropping to night-mode amber, and Odile flew the first leg alone because she'd asked to and because no one had argued. Ahead, the shipping lanes thinned. Traffic transponders that had crowded the display for years fell away one by one until the Long Quiet was the only moving thing on the scope, a single warm point crawling across a cold field.

This was the part she loved. She'd never said so out loud — loving anything about the dark felt like a betrayal of everyone who'd died in it — but this was the part. The emptiness with rules inside it. Vector, burn, correction. A language with no room in it for grief.

"Course locked," she told the ship, though the ship needed no telling. "Junction Nine departure burn in four minutes."

The drive answered with a rising note she felt in the deck plates, in the chair frame, in her sternum. The stars ahead began, very slowly, to smear.

She flew the exit from the lanes by hand. There was no reason to — the autopilot could thread Junction Nine in its sleep — but her hands wanted the yoke, and she had learned, over eleven years, that it was cheaper to give them what they wanted than to spend the watch fighting them. They moved with a certainty that had nothing to do with her conscious mind, feeding in corrections half a degree ahead of need, and she watched them work the way you'd watch a stranger do something beautiful and slightly terrible.

Halfway through the burn, her right hand left the yoke of its own accord and keyed a frequency into the comms panel. 121.7 emergency-relay band. The band every naval vessel in the Reach had monitored during the war; the band where the order had gone out.

She stared at the number glowing on the panel for three full seconds before she killed it.

Her heart was going hard. The ship drove on, serene, indifferent, its engines singing their one long note into the dark.

*Okay,* she told herself. *Okay.*

The body keeps old loyalties. Fine. But whose? Hers — the signals officer who'd spent three years living on that band, whose ears had been tuned to it so long the frequency was probably etched somewhere in the meat of her? Or the crew's — the forty-one sleepers in the hold below, whose memories lived in storage lattices built from the same architecture as her own nervous system, humming away under her feet like a second heartbeat?

She didn't know. That was the worst of it. Eleven years of flying this ship and she still couldn't always tell where she ended and they began.

* * *

The Tarnow Exclusion announced itself the way all the war's leftovers did: with silence where there should have been noise. No beacon chatter. No weather satellites. No automated traffic control squawking advisories in three languages. Just the gray hatching on her plot and, outside the viewports, stars that looked exactly like every other star, which was somehow the most obscene thing about it.

She brought Sefa up for the sensor watch because Sefa asked, and because leaving her alone with the signal recording was becoming its own kind of risk. The comms officer sat at the secondary station with her headphones around her neck and her eyes moving constantly between the scope and Odile's hands.

"You fly like you're angry at it," Sefa said.

"I'm not angry at it."

"At something."

Odile fed in a correction. Off the port bow, at extreme range, the scope painted a return: tumbling, irregular, metal-density. A hull fragment, maybe. Maybe larger. In the Exclusion you logged them and you didn't go look.

"During the war I flew this corridor fourteen times," Odile said. She hadn't planned to say it. The words came up the way blood comes into your mouth when you bite your tongue — suddenly, metallic, unstoppable. "Relay tenders ran it every six weeks. We carried the fleet's voice from the shipyards to the front. Every ship, every squadron commander, every order — it all passed through tenders like ours, through racks like the ones I serviced, through hands like mine."

Sefa said nothing. She was good at that. She understood that some sentences needed to finish themselves alone.

"The last run, we carried an order I'd translated myself. Priority-black. I translated it, authenticated it, and passed it up the chain, and thirty-six hours later the Amaranth stopped answering on all bands." Odile's hands stayed level on the yoke. She'd had eleven years of practice keeping them level. "Two thousand wounded aboard. Medics, nurses, men who'd already given what they had to give. The order classified them as legitimate interdiction targets because of cargo they were carrying that they didn't know they were carrying."

"And you flew this corridor after that."

"After that I flew it twice more, because the war doesn't stop for what you know. Then the broadcasts started — the massacres, the ones that took Old Vessic off the air — and the crew and I made a decision together, and we went dark, and I never flew it again." She adjusted the burn by a fraction. "Until my hands decided otherwise last night."

"Is that what happened?"

"That's what happened."

Sefa turned back to her scope. A long moment passed, filled with engine-note and the tick of cooling relays.

"Rell moved again today," she said finally. "When I played the signal in the hold. Not just neural response this time — Toma says the lattice drew power. A real spike. Like someone turning over in bed."

Odile felt that in her chest, in the same place the frequency had lived. "We're bringing the war's dead a message written in their own language, through territory they died in, to a wreck they may have served on. Of course they're turning over."

"Do you think they want to wake up?"

"I think," Odile said slowly, "that wanting has very little to do with it. I think something is calling them home, and home is a scuttled hospital ship full of dying voices, and we're the only vehicle anyone's offered them in eleven years." She exhaled. "And I think I understand exactly how they feel."

* * *

They cleared the Exclusion on the fourth day without losing so much as a sensor nodule, which Odile chose to regard as the universe withholding judgment rather than granting mercy. The debris shoals off Corvane were next, and those she would hand-fly, and she spent the watch before them doing something she hadn't done since her first year aboard the Long Quiet: she opened the sealed partition of her own memory and let the old approaches out.

Not the official ones. Those were gone, purged, denied. The others — the ones you learned by dying a little each time, the smuggler's geometry, the pilot's folklore passed mouth to mouth in ready rooms and tender galleys. Which shoal veins ran deep enough for a hull the Long Quiet's size. Which drift nodes were stable and which were lying. Where the old picket line had anchored, and therefore where its wreckage would still be falling, in long slow curtains, eleven years later.

Her hands wrote it all into the plot. She watched them do it and felt, for the first time in years, something that wasn't grief or guilt or the flat gray discipline of getting through another day.

It felt like being fluent again.

* * *

On the sixth day, nineteen hours out from the shoals, the scope woke up.

Odile was asleep — actually asleep, in her bunk, the first real sleep in five days — when the proximity alarm walked her out of it. She was on the bridge ninety seconds later, hair unbound, flight suit half-sealed, and Sefa was already at the sensor station with her headphones clamped tight and her face drained of color.

"Contact?" Odile said.

"Not a contact. A broadcast." Sefa pulled one earpiece free. "Directional, tight-beam, aimed at us specifically. Someone knows exactly where we are and wanted us and no one else to hear it." She hesitated.

"Play it."

Sefa played it. The bridge speakers carried a voice — clipped, formal, accented in the flat naval standard that every academy in the Reach drilled into its officers — reading from what was obviously a prepared text:

*"Unidentified freighter on approach to Corvane shoals. You are entering a restricted memorial volume. State registry, cargo, and destination within six hours or be subject to boarding inspection under remnant authority statute seven. This is the corvette Vigilant. Acknowledge."*

Silence came back after it, vast and immediate, the silence of a ship that had said its piece and was now watching.

Odile stood very still. Behind her eyes, unbidden, rose the memory of a briefing room twelve years ago, a young lieutenant with a scarred jaw asking her to repeat a translation twice because he couldn't believe what he was hearing — a decent man, a careful man, the kind the war used up fastest — and she realized she was already certain, with the sick certainty of the body, whose voice that was.

She knew the Vigilant. She knew its captain's habits, its patrol patterns, its complement. And somewhere aboard it, older and grayer now, was a man who had once trusted her voice completely.

"Sefa," she said. "Get Ivo. Wake Toma. Then open a channel — audio only, no handshake, no telemetry."

"What are we going to tell them?"

Odile settled into the command chair and put on the calmest face she owned, the one she'd worn through debriefings and tribunals and eleven years of ports that wouldn't meet her eyes.

"We're going to tell them," she said, "exactly enough truth to make the lie invisible. Patch it through."

The channel opened with a soft chime, and somewhere out in the dark between the stars, a corvette named Vigilant waited to hear a voice it might recognize.
