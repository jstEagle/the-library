## 24. Casimir's Choice

The order came through at 0400 station time, which Casimir had learned long ago was when the navy liked to do its killing — in the dark hours, when the corridors of the *Vigilant* were empty and the only witnesses were the night-shift comms officers who had long since stopped reading what they forwarded.

He stood at the message console in his quarters, still dressed, because he hadn't slept. He rarely slept anymore. The ship hummed around him the way it always did, that deep mechanical respiration he'd once found comforting and now heard as something closer to a held breath.

**PRIORITY FLASH — FLEET COMMAND, LOYALIST SEVENTH**

*VESSEL: UNREGISTERED FREIGHTER, DESIGNATION "LONG QUIET" OR SIMILAR HULL PROFILE.*

*STATUS: HOSTILE. CARRYING SUBVERSIVE MATERIAL AND PERSONS OF INTEREST.*

*ORDER: INTERCEPT AND DESTROY. NO BOARDING. NO SURVIVORS TO BE TAKEN ABOARD. WITNESS PROTOCOLS SUSPENDED BY DIRECTIVE 9-DELTA.*

*ACKNOWLEDGMENT REQUIRED WITHIN ONE HOUR.*

Casimir read it three times.

The words didn't change. That was the problem with orders; they never changed no matter how many times you read them hoping they'd soften into something else. He sat down slowly on the edge of his bunk, the mattress sighing under him, and looked at the small framed thing on his shelf — a photograph of a younger man in a signals officer's uniform, standing beside a woman with dark hair pulled back too tightly, both of them squinting against the floodlights of Corvane Station's docking apron. Eleven years ago. Her name had been Odile then, too. Some things survived even the war.

He thought about Directive 9-Delta. Everyone in the remnant fleet knew what it actually meant, whatever the legal language said. It meant: *there are things on that ship we do not want testified about.* It meant: *the war is supposed to be over, and over means buried.* It meant: *you will fire on a freighter and you will not ask why, because asking why is how men like Voss end up in the brig.*

Captain Voss. Casimir closed his eyes briefly. Voss would deliver this order with relish. Voss believed the war had been stolen from them — not lost, *stolen*, by politicians and mediators and people who talked about amnesty as if amnesty were a gift rather than a wound left open. Voss kept a list of ships that had run the blockade of conscience, and the *Long Quiet*, if the intelligence was right, was near the top of it now.

And Odile Pemberton was somewhere aboard her.

Casimir got up, crossed to the washbasin, and splashed water on his face. In the mirror, a forty-six-year-old lieutenant looked back at him, gray coming in early at the temples, a scar along his jaw from a decompression drill gone wrong in another life. He looked tired. He looked like a man standing on a crack in the ice, listening to it spread.

*Acknowledgment required within one hour.*

He dried his face. He straightened his collar out of habit. And he did the only thing left to a man whose duty and conscience had finally stopped pretending to be the same thing.

He went to find the captain — and he asked.

* * *

Voss received him in the wardroom, which doubled as the tactical briefing space aboard the corvette. She was already there, of course. Captains of the loyalist remnant slept the way sharks swam: continuously, and with their mouths slightly open. She stood over the holotable, watching the track projection crawl across it — the *Long Quiet*'s predicted course, a thin amber line threading toward the Kessel Drift and beyond it, toward the neutral corridor where the mediation world hung like a lantern nobody was supposed to look at.

"Lieutenant." She didn't look up. "You have my order."

"I do, Captain." Casimir came to attention, then made himself stand easy, because rigid posture invited correction and he couldn't afford corrections right now. "I'm requesting verification before execution."

That got her eyes up. They were pale, almost colorless, the kind of eyes that made junior officers feel audited. "Verification."

"Yes, ma'am. The order cites subversive material and persons of interest. It doesn't specify what material. It doesn't name the persons. Under the Remnant Code of Engagement, Article Fourteen, a destruction order against an unregistered vessel requires either positive identification of hostile armament or documented evidence of prohibited cargo. We have neither. We have a hull profile and a rumor."

"We have Fleet Command's word."

"Fleet Command," Casimir said carefully, "is the party the alleged subversion implicates. That's a conflict of interest, ma'am. If the freighter is carrying what I think it may be carrying, then the verification chain itself is compromised, and Article Fourteen exists precisely for that case."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere behind the bulkhead, a coolant pump cycled, patient as a heartbeat. Two ensigns at the far console were very deliberately not listening, which meant they were listening with every nerve they owned.

Voss stepped around the holotable until she was close enough that he could smell the stimulant gum she chewed, bitter and chemical. "You served with the navigator of that ship," she said. It wasn't a question. His service record was an open book to her; she'd read it aloud to him once, in this same room, in a voice like a scalpel, the day he'd transferred aboard. "Odile Pemberton. Signals officer, Meridian Command. You've requested her file twice since we picked up the trail. You think I don't watch my officers, Casimir?"

"I assume you watch everything, Captain. It's why you're good at your job."

"Don't flatter me." Her mouth barely moved. "Here's what I know about you. You're the best tactical officer I have. You're also the officer most likely to hesitate at the firing solution, and hesitation in a destruction order gets my crew killed. So let me make this simple for you." She tapped the table, and the amber line of the freighter's course flared red. "That vessel is carrying memory-backups of war dead. Dozens of them. It's carrying a fragment of an armistice accord that certain parties in Fleet Command would very much prefer stayed lost, because half an accord is more dangerous than none — it's a promise with the signature torn off, and promises like that start wars all over again when people go looking for the other piece. Command has ruled. Witness protocols are suspended. There is nothing to verify, Lieutenant, because verification is exactly what we've been ordered to prevent."

"Then the order is self-incriminating," Casimir said quietly. "Ma'am."

Something flickered behind those colorless eyes — not anger, or not only anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman hearing her own doubt spoken back to her in someone else's voice and hating him for it.

"You're confined to quarters," Voss said. "Pending review of your fitness for command duties. Ensign Brava will assume tactical."

"Brava is nineteen years old."

"Brava follows orders." Voss turned back to the holotable, dismissing him, but she spoke one more sentence to the amber light, almost too low to hear. "You were a good officer once, Casimir. Whatever you think you remember about that woman — memories are just another kind of cargo. Some of it should stay in the hold."

He saluted her back, because procedure was the last honest thing he had left, and walked out of the wardroom with two marines falling into step behind him, escorting him the forty meters to his own cabin as if he might bolt for an escape pod in transit.

The door sealed behind him with a sound like a held breath released wrong.

* * *

Confined quarters, Casimir discovered, were a strange kind of freedom. Nobody watched a locked door as closely as they watched an unlocked one. Within six hours he had mapped the ship's rhythms through the wall: the marine rotation outside his hatch changed at odd intervals — sloppy, or hurried, or both; the ship's internal comms carried a half-second delay on the bridge relay that meant someone was routing around standard channels; and Ensign Brava's voice, young and taut, came through the ventilation ducting whenever the bridge ran open-mic during maneuvers, which Voss permitted because she believed in ambient discipline.

He sat on his bunk with a maintenance tablet — the one concession they'd left him, stripped of network access, useful only for diagrams and personal logs — and he wrote, because writing was how he'd always thought, in the old signals way, everything reduced to clean lines of transmission and reception.

*Received: order to destroy.*
*Signal integrity: perfect.*
*Source authenticity: verified.*
*Meaning: corrupted in transit between the hand that wrote it and the hand that reads it.*

That was the whole disease of the remnant navy, he thought. Every order passed through so many hands, so many years of fear, that by the time it reached a trigger finger it had degraded into something its authors wouldn't recognize. The Meridian War hadn't been started by monsters. It had been started by messages like this one — authentic, verified, and rotten at the core — executed by decent people who told themselves that integrity meant fidelity to the signal rather than to what the signal *did*.

He had spent eleven years believing fidelity was enough. He'd translated intercepts during the war, sitting in a booth on Corvane Station, turning other people's dying words into actionable intelligence, telling himself the booth was neutral ground. Then the Amaranth. Then the broadcast massacres, when the fleets learned to weaponize the very channels medics used to call for help, and neutrality died in a single week of screaming frequencies.

He had met Odile Pemberton after that. After. When she was already half-ghost, already speaking in the flat careful voice of someone who had heard something in a signal booth that she could never unhear. He'd never asked her what it was. He'd been afraid the answer would implicate the booth, and the booth was where he lived.

Now he knew. Or he suspected he knew, and suspicion was worse, because suspicion demanded action and action had consequences and he was sitting in a locked cabin while a nineteen-year-old aimed his guns for him.

The ventilation ducting crackled. Brava's voice, thin through the metal: "*—contact bearing two-seven-niner, matching profile, range closing—*" and then Voss, clipped: "*—hold solution. Hold. I want them in the debris field first, where there's nowhere to run—*"

Casimir set down the tablet. He pressed his palms flat against his knees and breathed the way they'd taught him in survival training, four counts in, seven counts out, the rhythm that kept a man functional inside a burning cockpit.

They were going to do it. Not in some abstract future — soon, possibly within days, in the shoals off Kessel Drift, they were going to put torpedoes into a freighter full of sleeping dead and a woman who had once shown him, of all things, how to fold a paper bird in the shadow of a signal tower, laughing for the first time in months, saying *look, even folded wrong it flies.*

He looked at the shelf. At the photograph. Two young officers squinting into floodlights, the war still ahead of them, believing — genuinely, stupidly, magnificently believing — that the signals they carried were going to end it.

*Directive 9-Delta. Witness protocols suspended.*

Witnesses. That was the word that kept catching in him like a fishhook. An order that feared witnesses was an order that knew it was guilty, and a navy that issued such orders had stopped being a navy and become something older and dumber — a fist, swinging in the dark, hitting whatever moved.

There was a procedure for this. Of course there was; the remnant had inherited ten thousand pages of peacetime law it no longer believed in. Article Thirty-One: any officer with reasonable grounds to believe an order unlawful could demand independent verification through the mediation channels — the same channels, Casimir noted with a joyless smile, that Directive 9-Delta had suspended. The law had been amputated to fit the crime. But the amputation had been done by directive, not by treaty amendment, which meant that formally, on paper, in the dustiest archive of Corvane Station, Article Thirty-One still existed.

A request for verification. Filed properly. Through proper channels. It would take days to process — days the *Long Quiet* might use to reach the window. It would also put his name on a list, and Voss's list was shorter than the archives and traveled faster.

It didn't matter. Nothing about his career mattered anymore; that realization had arrived sometime in the night, quiet as frost, and instead of terrifying him it had made everything strangely simple. A man with nothing left to lose transmitted very clearly.

He took out the tablet again. He began to compose the request in formal language, each clause load-bearing, each citation exact, the old signals discipline flowing through his hands — say it cleanly, say it completely, say it so that no one downstream can claim they misunderstood.

*To Fleet Adjudication, via open channel, per Article Thirty-One:*

*I, Lieutenant Davor Casimir, serving aboard the corvette Vigilant, hereby request independent verification of Destruction Order 7-4471 on grounds that said order appears designed to prevent the verification it presupposes...*

Outside his door, boots shifted. Inside the walls, the ship breathed. On the holotable he could not see, a thin amber line crawled toward a window that would open once and close forever.

He signed it. He encrypted it the old way — the way Odile had taught him, the pre-war cipher that the remnant's monitoring software had officially forgotten existed — and queued it for transmission at the next comm-relay alignment, ninety minutes out.

Then he lay back on his bunk, hands laced behind his head, and listened to the *Vigilant* turn toward the shoals, and understood that he had just fired the only shot available to him — not a torpedo but a question, launched down a channel everyone pretended was dead, aimed not at the freighter but at the rot inside his own fleet.

Ninety minutes. Then the whole ship would know what he'd done.

Through the ducting, faint and young and terribly obedient, Ensign Brava's voice reported contact solutions confirmed, weapons hot, awaiting the word.

Casimir closed his eyes and waited for the alarm.
