## 28. Testimony in Transit

The hold no longer smelled like sleep.

Rell noticed it the first time she walked its length after waking — that absence of cold-metal stillness, replaced by something warmer, humaner: sweat and tea and the faint chemical sweetness of forty-one cryo-cells cycling down to standby. The bunks stood open now, lids raised like the ribs of a great patient breathing again. People moved between them. Living people who had been dead for eleven years, touching the frames of their own coffins as if confirming they'd ever fit.

She stopped at bunk thirty-one. It was hers. She remembered lying down in it — remembered the needle, the technician's apology, the last thought before the dark: *the Amaranth is still broadcasting.* She had gone under mid-sentence, carrying a sentence into the dark like a coal cupped in both hands.

Now the coal had become a fire, and the fire needed feeding.

"Rell." Toma's voice from the hatchway, rough with fatigue. "Recording bay's ready. Sefa's rigged the array to take long-form deposition. Whatever you've got in you, it can hold it."

"Whatever's in me," Rell said, "is eleven years of other people's dying."

Toma leaned against the frame. The engineer had aged badly and beautifully — grease in the creases of her knuckles, gray coming in at the temples like frost on a window. "Then we'll spend it well," she said. "That's the whole trick of grief, isn't it? Spending it well."

* * *

They converted the aft survey bay into a witness chamber because it was the only room aboard with acoustic isolation, and because its viewport faced the long dark between stars, which felt appropriate. Sefa ran cabling along the deck seams herself, refusing help twice, then accepting it from an awakened medic named Ovid who had once been her instructor at the Vessic signals academy and who now looked at her like a man reading his own obituary written in better handwriting.

The array hummed up. A red light steadied above the witness chair.

"Test format is simple," Sefa said, her voice carefully level. "Name, rank if you had one, date of death if you know it. Then everything. We're not editing. The council gets all of it or none of it matters."

Rell sat first. Not because she wanted to but because someone had to break the seal, and she had been the one to wake first, and waking first was a kind of rank.

The red light burned. She began.

"I am Rell Ansa-Mariq. I was a triage coordinator aboard the hospital ship Amaranth, Meridian Registry 4-Alpha. I died on the fourteenth day of the bombardment, though 'died' is a generous word for what happened to us."

She talked for six hours.

She told them about the ship's lights going amber, then red, then out. About the medics who kept operating by hand-lamp while the hull rang like a struck bell. About Captain Osei ordering the armistice terms recorded into the hull plating itself — etched, not transmitted, because transmissions were being hunted — half the accord in Old Vessic, mirrored script, so that even if the ship were scuttled the message would survive in its bones. About the order that came down from fleet command, the one Odile Pemberton had translated, designating the Amaranth's corridor as a free-fire zone, and how the medics in the surgical theater had heard the designation read over an open channel and understood, in the same breath, both that they were condemned and that someone, somewhere, had signed their deaths in tidy language.

"We recorded our testimony while we waited," Rell said. "Forty minutes of air left, and we spent them talking. That's what people don't understand about the end. You don't pray. You don't scream. You *testify*. You say: this happened, I saw it, remember me correctly. And then the dark came, and then—" she touched her own sternum, where the backup port sat beneath the skin, "—then nothing, until a freighter full of strangers decided we were worth the power."

She looked directly into the lens. "Remember us correctly. That's all the dead have ever asked."

* * *

After Rell came the others, one by one, hour after hour, watch after watch, while the Long Quiet burned fuel it could not spare through contested space.

Ovid spoke about holding a stranger's heart in his hands and feeling it stop, and about the fleet courier who arrived three days late with a ceasefire order that would have saved everyone in his ward. Maren Kesh, the Amaranth's purser, testified to the supply manifests — the deliberate starvation of the medical corridors, numbers upon numbers, the bureaucracy of murder rendered in clean columns. A young corpsman named Dill, who had been nineteen at his death and was nineteen still behind the eyes, described the sound the hull made when the torpedoes found the reactor housing, and said he had never told anyone because there had never been anyone to tell, and now there were forty strangers and a camera and he wept through half of it and the crew let him, and when he finished, Toma put a mug of tea in his hands without a word, and that was the whole ceremony.

Odile did not testify. Not yet. She sat outside the bay during every session, back against the bulkhead, listening through the open door like a penitent at a confessional whose priest was the entire murdered crew. Rell saw her there each time and said nothing. There would be a floor in a mediation hall soon enough, and Odile Pemberton would stand on it and name herself, and that testimony would be the hinge on which everything turned. The dead could wait for it. They had gotten good at waiting.

But the fuel gauge did not wait.

Toma flagged it on the third day: consumption running nine percent over projection, then twelve. Forty-one resurrected people ate, drank, breathed, argued, mourned, and every exhalation cost mass and every kilogram cost delta-v, and the jump window mathematics did not care about resurrection. Ivo was gone — his shuttle, his sacrifice, his silence on the comms band that no one spoke aloud but everyone heard — and Captain's duty had fallen by default onto a ship that ran on consensus anyway. The consensus was: keep recording. Cut rations, cut heat in the ring sections, cut everything except the testimony.

"Grief is expensive," Toma said, standing before the engineering display, pointing at the falling curve. "Turns out it costs about nine percent over projection. But we are not stopping. We'll burn the furniture before we burn the archive."

"There is no furniture," Sefa said.

"Then we'll burn my spare coil housings, which I have been hoarding against exactly no rational emergency, and I will grieve them properly."

A laugh went around the compartment — thin, exhausted, real. Rell had forgotten what laughter sounded like in a room full of the living-dead. On the Amaranth, in the last hours, there had been no laughter. Only testimony. She decided the freighter's version was better.

* * *

On the fifth day, the backups who had been medics asked to do something no one had anticipated: they asked to record *forward*, not backward.

"Not just what happened to us," Ovid explained, standing in the corridor outside the witness bay, his voice pitched low so the sleeping-off-duty crew wouldn't hear. "What happens *now*. The war didn't end with the Amaranth. It ended — is ending — with a freighter full of ghosts flying a peace accord through a shooting gallery. If the council hears only the massacre, they'll vote amnesty out of guilt. Guilt doesn't hold. Memory holds. So we record the whole arc. The crime *and* the repair. Let them see that the people who were killed came back and chose this — chose the delivery, chose the risk, chose the ones who killed us sitting across a table."

Maren Kesh nodded. "An accusation alone makes defendants. A story makes witnesses. We need the council to be witnesses, not defendants."

Rell took the idea to Odile, who listened with her arms crossed and her jaw tight, the way she did when something true was being said near the wound she carried.

"You want to document your own rescue," Odile said.

"We want to document our own *choice*," Rell said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point. Anyone can be rescued. Choosing to walk back into the war you died in — that has to be on the record too."

Odile was quiet a long moment. Then she uncrossed her arms. "Sefa will give you whatever bandwidth you need. And Rell — when we reach the mediation floor, I'm going to confess what I translated. What I did. When I do, I'd like your testimony playing behind me. All of it. Unedited. If they're going to forgive me, they should do it with the sound of the Amaranth in their ears."

"That," Rell said, "is the first honest thing anyone in fleet uniform ever asked of us."

"I haven't worn the uniform in eleven years."

"No," Rell agreed. "You wore the silence instead. Same tailoring."

* * *

The sessions continued. The ship grew strange with purpose.

In the galley, awakened crew taught the living crew old Vessic mourning songs, and the songs got recorded too, layered under the formal depositions like roots under a tree. In the hold, Dill slept in his old bunk with the lid open because closed lids reminded him of the dark between death and waking, and Toma rigged a small reading lamp above it without being asked. In the witness bay, the red light burned so many hours a day that Sefa replaced the indicator twice and finally wired it to run dim, saying, "It shouldn't shout. Testimony should sound like a person, not an alarm."

Fuel dropped. Heat in the ring sections dropped. Tempers frayed and mended and frayed again. The Long Quiet became a vessel with two cargoes: the accord in its vault, and the voices in its memory — and everyone aboard understood, without anyone saying it, that the second cargo was heavier, and more precious, and that they would spend every last liter of reaction mass getting it to the table.

On the seventh night, Rell stood alone in the hold, at bunk thirty-one, recording one final piece on her handheld — not for the council, for herself.

"If we make it," she said quietly, to the little lens, to the empty bunks, to the dark beyond the hull, "I want it known that dying was not the worst part. The worst part was the silence afterward — eleven years of a universe that had deleted our language and called it peace. And the best part was this: a rusted freighter, a crew of strangers who owed us nothing, burning their own future to carry our voices home. Whatever else the record shows, show that. Show that mercy is a navigation decision. Someone chose a heading toward us. That's how the war ends. Not with surrender. With headings."

She stopped the recording. Behind her, soft footsteps — Odile, drawn to the hold the way she always was, unable to stay away from the place where her guilt slept and no longer did.

"They're asking for a resupply," Odile said. "There's a beacon station on our line — Calloway's Rest, independent, neutral-ish. Toma says we can top off enough to make the window comfortable instead of miraculous."

Rell studied her. "You don't like it."

"I don't like needing anything from anyone on this route. Neutral stations sell to both sides." Odile's eyes went to the open bunks, forty-one of them, empty and lit. "But we don't have a choice. Nineteen days was yesterday. It's less than that now."

"Then we refuel," Rell said simply. "And we keep recording until the tanks are full and the archive is fuller."

Odile almost smiled. Almost. "You've gotten bossy since you died."

"I was always bossy. Death just gave me seniority."

* * *

Calloway's Rest resolved out of the dark eight hours later — a spindle of light and scaffolding hung off a captured comet core, running lights flickering in patterns that Sefa, at the comms board, read with a slow frown.

"Docking channel's open," she reported. "Harbormaster's friendly. Too friendly. He's offering fuel at rates that don't exist anymore, and he asked — casually, very casually — what class of ship we are and how many souls aboard."

Toma, passing behind her, snorted. "Everyone's friendly when they smell profit."

But Rell, standing in the hatchway with her handheld still warm in her pocket, watched Odile's face go still and pale in the light of the console, and understood that the navigator had done the arithmetic already — the same arithmetic Rell had learned on the Amaranth, watching supply ships arrive too promptly at coordinates that were supposed to be secret.

Somebody always knew where the dying were. The only question was who had sold the address.

"Take the fuel," Odile said at last, quietly. "All of it, fast, and nobody goes aboard except Toma and the payment chit. And Sefa—" her voice hardened into something from eleven years ago, something that had once read death warrants in a beautiful language, "—keep every antenna hot. If anyone's selling us, I want to hear the price before the bill arrives."
