## 31. The Window Opens

The corridor to the threshold was called the Needle, and it had earned the name.

Odile flew it with her hands bare against the cold of the console, the way she'd flown it eleven years ago when the Long Quiet still carried a naval registry and she still carried a rank. The charts called it a transit lane. The charts lied. It was a throat of space between two shoals of collapsed debris — the bones of a shipyard the war had eaten whole — and every meter of it was littered with things that could kill them: hull fragments spinning on dead inertia, coolant ice flashing to vapor in the starlight, a derelict tug the size of a cathedral turning end over end like something drowning.

"Two hundred seconds to threshold," Sefa said from comms. Her voice was steady the way a wire is steady — taut, thin, singing under load. "Window opens in one-forty."

Odile did the arithmetic without wanting to. Sixty seconds of margin. She'd flown corridors with less. She'd flown corridors with none, once, over Kessel Drift, with a hull breach aft and Toma screaming at her not to do what she was about to do. But that had been before. That had been when the only thing in the hold worth saving was forty-one sleepers and her own guilt.

Now the hold was full of voices.

She could hear them through the open comm line — Rell's voice foremost, low and cracked, translating the armistice's final clauses for the mediation council's relay buoy while the other forty sat strapped into the cargo cradles they'd slept in for eleven years, awake now, alive now, recording testimony in a language three fleets had tried to burn out of existence. The dead were talking. The dead were going to testify. All Odile had to do was keep the ship in one piece for two hundred more seconds.

"Toma," she said. "Talk to me about the drive."

Toma's voice came back through a wash of static, hoarse, half-laughing, which was how Toma sounded when things were worst. "Drive is a miracle and a lie, Captain. Port injector's running hot. If you ask her for more than point-six thrust in the last stretch, she'll give it to you, and then she'll take the rest of the day off permanently."

"How long is the rest of the day?"

"Forever, Odile. The rest of the day is forever."

"Then we ask her for nothing." Odile eased the yoke two degrees port, threading between a slab of armored plating and a cloud of frozen rivets that glittered like a swarm of furious bees. "Point-five until the final approach. We coast across the line if we have to."

"And if the vanguard catches us coasting?"

"They won't." She said it because someone had to say it, and because saying it made it slightly more true. "Ivo bought us the hours. We spend them well."

Nobody answered that. Nobody ever answered that anymore. Since the beacon, since the betrayal, since Ivo had walked down the ramp of the shuttle bay in his oldest coat with his sons' names folded in his breast pocket, nobody on the Long Quiet said his name out loud. They carried it instead, the way the hold used to carry the backups — carefully, in the dark, waiting for a window.

* * *

The loyalist vanguard came up on the scope at 04:11 ship-time, and Odile knew it before Sefa called it, because the scope had a grammar and she'd learned to read it in the war: the way contacts resolve, the way formation shapes itself around intent. Three signatures, burning hard out of the Shoals' deep shadow where the resupply contact had sold their position. Frigates, two of them. The third —

"The third one's bigger," Sefa said quietly. "Heavy hull. That's a cruiser, Odile. That's *Vigilant*-class or worse."

"It's not the Vigilant." Brava's voice cut in from the relay channel — Ensign Brava, transmitting from the corvette whose crew had torn itself in half rather than fire on them, running dark somewhere behind the loyalists with whatever remained of Casimir's people. "Vigilant is still burning. Half of her, anyway. We've got casualties, we've got fires, and we've got maybe forty souls who refused the order and are coming after you at best speed. Which is slower than theirs." A pause, filled with the sound of someone deciding whether to say the next thing. "The cruiser is *Terrible Certainty*. Admiral Voss's flagship. She jumped two days ago. You were never going to outrun her. You were only ever going to arrive first."

"Arriving first counts," Toma said.

"Arriving first is everything," Odile said, and put the Needle's mouth in front of them, and dove.

* * *

Flying the Needle at combat speed was less navigation than conversation — a sustained argument with physics conducted in corrections small enough to feel but not see. Odile gave herself to it the way she hadn't given herself to anything since the war: wholly, without irony, without the second voice in her head that narrated her failures. There was no room for narration. There was the slab of hull plating yawing into their path and there was her wrist rolling the yoke and there was the Long Quiet groaning like a living thing as the gravics compensated, cargo straps singing, and somewhere below her feet forty-one resurrected voices going silent all at once because even the dead knew to hold their breath when Odile Pemberton flew.

"Thirty seconds to window," Sefa said. "Ninety seconds to threshold."

Margin collapsing. She watched it collapse and did not flinch. Behind them, on the scope, the Terrible Certainty's escort frigates were doing something ugly and elegant — splitting wide, one to each side of the Needle's mouth, so that any ship emerging would emerge into a crossfire. They weren't trying to catch the Long Quiet anymore. They were trying to make the threshold a door that opened onto a wall of guns.

"They're bracketing the exit," Sefa said. "If we come through—"

"I know what they're doing."

"They're betting we'll turn back."

"They're betting wrong." Odile's hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook, later, alone, in the dark of the navigator's bunk. "They think we're carrying cargo. They think we're smugglers with a stolen message. They don't know what's in my hold." She keyed the all-ship. "Rell. How close are you to finishing the relay?"

Rell's voice came back threaded with static and something older than static — the particular grain of a voice that had died once and been kept. "The testimony uploads complete in four minutes. The accord's full text, both halves, verified against my memory of the original negotiation. After that—" a breath that Rell didn't strictly need anymore, taken anyway, out of habit or defiance, "—after that it doesn't matter what happens to this ship. The record exists in three places. Kill us and you kill witnesses who already spoke."

"That's the spirit," Toma muttered.

"One minute to window," Sefa said. "Forty seconds to threshold."

* * *

Here is what Odile thought about in those forty seconds, flying the last stretch of the Needle with the drive at point-five-eight because she'd asked Toma's forgiveness rather than Toma's permission:

She thought about the first time she'd crossed this threshold, eleven years ago, running dark with a crew that had voted to become ghosts. She'd looked back at the closed window of normal space and felt nothing but relief — relief that the war was behind glass, that silence was a kind of armor, that she could carry her dead in the hold and her shame in her chest and never have to set either down in front of another living soul.

She thought about how wrong she'd been. Silence wasn't armor. Silence was just a longer way of dying.

She thought about Ivo, somewhere behind them in a shuttle too small for the fight he'd chosen, drawing the vanguard's eyes, buying minutes with the only currency he had left. *Get them through,* he'd said, in the last transmission they'd let him send. *Get the words through. My boys never got words. They got an order and then they got nothing.* She thought: I am carrying your words, old man. I am carrying everyone's words. The whole drowned language of a murdered world, riding in a freighter's hold, and I will put it down on the far side of this line if I have to carry the hull there on my back.

"Window opening," Sefa said. "Ten seconds. Nine."

The scope bloomed. Ahead, past the Needle's last turn, the threshold hung in space like a held breath given shape — a lens of bent starlight where the corridor's geometry thinned to paper, the jump point that opened twice a month onto the neutral deeps where the mediation council waited. Through it: amnesty, testimony, an end. Around it: two frigates, guns hot, waiting for them to be brave enough to come through.

"Five seconds."

"Odile," Toma said, very calm, "the injector just went red."

"Hold together," Odile told the ship, or the drive, or herself. "Four. Three."

The frigates moved. Both at once, converging on the mouth of the Needle with the smooth mechanical certainty of machines executing an order no one aboard had the conscience to refuse — and Odile saw, in the last half-second before it happened, exactly what they intended: not to destroy the Long Quiet in transit, which might damage the threshold, but to seal it. To cross the jump point themselves, first, and close the corridor behind them. To lock the window shut with their own hulls and leave the Long Quiet and its forty-one witnesses stranded in contested space with a cruiser inbound.

"They're jumping first," Sefa breathed. "They're going to block the—"

"No," Odile said, "they're not."

Because the scope showed her one more thing, one small thing, one grace note written in the debris of everything the war had broken: a fourth signature, small and battered and burning far too fast for its frame, rising out of the Shoals' shadow on an intercept vector no sane pilot would fly. A shuttle. An old man's shuttle, running without transponder, without escort, without hope, straight down the throat of the gap between the two frigates — broadcasting, on every open channel, in clear, in Old Vessic, the first words of the armistice he'd memorized from Rell's translation.

*Ivo,* she thought. *You beautiful, stubborn, impossible—*

"Window open," Sefa said. "We are go. Odile, we are GO."

Odile Pemberton put the Long Quiet's nose on the threshold, asked the dying injector for everything it had left, and held her breath.
