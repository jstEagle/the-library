## 35. Casimir's Testimony

They gave him a cell with a window.

That was the first thing Casimir understood about how far the navy had fallen — or risen; he had not yet decided which word to use — because in eleven years of service he had never once seen a holding cell with an exterior viewport. Cells were meant to be featureless. Cells were meant to erase time. This one looked out over the mediation station's inner ring, where the delegates' shuttles came and went in slow, glittering processions, and where, two decks below, the freighter *Long Quiet* sat berthed like something that had crawled out of the sea and was still dripping.

He had been confined for six days. The charges were formal and absurd: insubordination, failure to prosecute a lawful order, aiding and abetting the delivery of unverified materials. He had signed each acknowledgment without argument. There was no version of those charges he wished to contest. What troubled him was not the cell but the waiting — the way the station seemed to hold its breath around him, as if his testimony were a fuse someone had already lit and was now deciding whether to smother or let run.

On the seventh day, they came for him.

* * *

The hearing chamber had been a cargo bay once. You could still see the weld seams in the floor plating, the ghost-letters of old tonnage stencils burned off but not quite gone. Someone had laid a semicircle of seating where the containers used to stack: delegates from the Concord fleets on one side, remnant naval officers on the other, and between them a gap of bare deck perhaps four meters wide that no one crossed without visible effort. Above it all hung the broadcast lattice — a crown of lenses and microphones aimed down at a single witness stand, so that whatever was said there would go out raw and simultaneous to every fleet, every station, every listening post in human space.

Casimir stood at the entrance for a long moment before anyone noticed him. He was in dress grays with the rank tabs stripped, per regulation for a confined officer, and without them he felt strangely light, like a hull with its ballast dumped. A young clerk gestured him forward. His boots sounded very loud on the deck.

The presiding arbiter was a Vessic woman with silver-threaded braids and a face like weathered stone. She did not ask him to swear an oath. Instead she said, "Lieutenant Casimir Veyn. You are here under the amnesty provisions of the recovered accord. Everything you say will be transmitted live. Nothing you say can be used to charge you. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means," Casimir said, "that I can finally tell the truth without it being treated as treason."

A ripple went through the naval delegation. One of the senior captains — Casimir knew her face from briefing holos, Admiral Sorel, who had signed three of the orders he was about to describe — turned her head slowly toward him, and her expression was not anger. It was the look of a woman watching a structural crack propagate through a bulkhead she had believed sound.

"Then begin," the arbiter said. "At the beginning."

* * *

"The beginning," Casimir said, "is a name most of you have never been permitted to speak." He paused. "The *Amaranth*. Hospital ship, registry Meridian Fleet, eleven hundred forty-two souls aboard when she was scuttled in the opening week of the war. The official record says reactor failure. The official record is a fabrication, and I can prove it, because I helped maintain the fabrication for nine years."

He had rehearsed this in his cell until the words wore smooth, but standing under the lattice they came out differently — not recited but excavated, each sentence pulled up out of him with some resistance, like a splinter working loose.

"I was a signals officer aboard the corvette *Vigilant*, then a frigate, then attached to Fleet Intelligence at Corvane Station. My specialty was traffic analysis — pattern recognition across intercepted communications. In the second year of the war, my section was given a sealed tasking. We were told to identify and suppress any transmission referencing the *Amaranth* incident. Not investigate. Suppress. Any signal containing the ship's registry, the coordinates of her wreck, or the phrase 'medical evacuation' within a defined radius of those coordinates was to be flagged, jammed, and logged for deletion."

"Who issued that tasking?" the arbiter asked.

"Fleet Intelligence, Section Nine. Under authority delegated from—" He looked directly at Admiral Sorel. "From officers in this room."

Sorel did not flinch. He respected her for that, in a cold and distant way. Flinching would have made it easier, and nothing about this was supposed to be easy.

"For nine years I built and maintained the suppression filters," Casimir continued. "I wrote the algorithms. I trained the analysts. And I will tell you what I told myself, because I think there are people in every fleet who have told themselves the same thing: I told myself that the truth about the *Amaranth* would shatter morale. That the war could only be ended by whoever was strong enough to end it, and that strength required unity, and unity required that we not tear ourselves apart over one ship. Eleven hundred forty-two souls. I did the arithmetic of grief the way we were taught to do it — as if souls were tonnage."

His voice caught on the last word. He let it. He had learned, watching Odile Pemberton lie beautifully to save her crew, that a voice could be a weapon; he was learning now, in this harder school, that a voice breaking could be a weapon too — not against others, but against the armored self.

"Then, nineteen days ago, my ship was ordered to intercept a civilian freighter carrying what Command described as 'enemy propaganda of the highest threat classification.' I boarded that freighter under a pretext. I stood in her hold. And I heard a voiceprint match that I had no right to hear, because the officer it belonged to had been listed dead for eleven years."

* * *

He told them about Odile.

Not everything — some things belonged to her alone, and he had already decided, in the long gray hours of his confinement, exactly where the line fell. But he told them enough. That she had been Lieutenant Odile Pemberton, signals section, Meridian Fleet, decorated twice before her twenty-sixth birthday. That she had translated the intercept chain on the night the *Amaranth* died — that the order to scuttle her had passed through encrypted channels, and that Pemberton's section had decoded it in real time, and that she had spent the rest of her life carrying the knowledge of what her fluency had been used for.

"That is the part I need this council to understand," Casimir said. "She didn't fire the shot. She read the message that arranged the firing. And she has punished herself for it more thoroughly than any court ever could — by disappearing, by silence, by eleven years of hauling cargo past ports that wouldn't open to her. When I recognized her voice aboard the *Long Quiet*, I had a choice. Report the voiceprint, and the freighter would have been seized, the accord destroyed, and the war would have ground on until one side ran out of ships. Or let her go, and log a partial report, and live with the uncertainty."

"What did you log?" asked a delegate from the Concord side — a thin man with an interpreter's earpiece, leaning forward.

"A sensor anomaly. Unidentified debris interference. I recommended no further pursuit." Casimir allowed himself the smallest breath of humor, dry as vacuum. "It was the first lie I had ever told the fleet that I was proud of."

Someone in the naval delegation made a sound — half cough, half protest. Casimir turned toward it.

"Yes," he said. "I know what I am describing. I am describing the moment the war's logic failed inside me. And I want to be precise about that, because I have heard the term 'traitor' applied to what happened aboard the *Vigilant*, and I reject it — not out of self-protection, but out of precision. A traitor changes sides. I did not change sides. I changed *minds*. There is a difference, and the amnesty depends on our being able to see it."

He told them about Ensign Brava, who had refused the destroy order and keyed the general channel so that half the crew could refuse it together. He told them about the mutiny — bloodless, terrified, magnificent — and about the hours he had spent confined in his own cabin while his ship tore itself into factions over whether to murder a freighter full of witnesses. He told them about the jump, the fire across the threshold, the shuttle that had covered the *Long Quiet*'s escape — flown, the after-action fragments suggested, by a captain in his sixties who should have been decades retired.

"Captain Ivo Renner," Casimir said quietly. "I never met him. I fired on his shuttle, or my gunners did, under my command. I want that entered into the record too. If this council grants peace, it must grant it whole — including the debts owed to the dead on both sides of the threshold."

* * *

The arbiter leaned forward. "Lieutenant Veyn. You have described the suppression program. Can you substantiate it beyond your own account?"

"I can do better than that." Casimir reached into his jacket — the clerk tensed, hand near a sidearm, and Casimir almost smiled; even now, even here, the reflexes of the war — and drew out a data wafer no larger than a coin. "Before my confinement, I had ninety-one hours of unsupervised access to Corvane Station's archive systems. I did not use them to steal anything. I used them to leave a door unlocked. The deletion logs for the *Amaranth* suppression filters still exist — Command ordered the records purged, but purge operations on Corvane run through a maintenance layer that logs its own activity. Every file destroyed, every timestamp, every authorization code. Including the codes used on the night of the incident itself."

He held the wafer up between two fingers, and the broadcast lattice drank in its image and carried it, he knew, to a million screens.

"The authorization code that ordered the *Amaranth* scuttled was issued from this room's delegation. From Admiral Sorel's command authority." He set the wafer on the witness stand, gently, the way you set down something that might go off. "I am not asking for her prosecution. The accord's amnesty covers acts of war committed under lawful-seeming orders — that is its entire architecture, and I understand why, and I accept it. What I am asking is that the record be corrected. That the eleven hundred forty-two be named. That their families be told the truth while some of them are still alive to hear it. Amnesty without truth isn't peace. It's just a longer silence."

For a moment the chamber was absolutely still. Then Admiral Sorel rose.

Casimir had imagined this moment a hundred times in his cell — imagined her denial, her fury, the machinery of the old navy grinding back to life to crush him. What actually happened was worse and better than any of it. Sorel stood, straightened her tunic with hands that trembled only slightly, and said, in a voice pitched for the lattice:

"The code was mine. I issued it on the assessment that the *Amaranth*'s medical records contained evidence of a bioweapon exposure that would have caused panic on forty worlds. I was wrong about the exposure. I have known I was wrong for nine years. I signed the suppression orders to contain my own error." She looked at Casimir, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "Lieutenant Veyn, I do not ask your forgiveness. I ask only that the young officers in every fleet listening to this transmission understand what containment costs. It cost me everything I joined the service to protect."

She sat down. The gap between the delegations — that four meters of bare deck — suddenly looked less like a trench and more like a doorway.

* * *

They kept him another hour, taking details: dates, filter architectures, names of analysts, the routing of the suppression taskings. He answered everything. Somewhere in the middle of it he realized that his hands had stopped shaking, and that he could not remember the last time they had — perhaps not since the war began. The body keeps its own ledgers, he thought, and somewhere a navigator on a battered freighter had taught him that without ever saying a word to him.

When it was done, the arbiter dismissed the chamber, but not him. She waited until the benches emptied, until only the two of them and the silent lattice remained, and then she said:

"You understand what happens next. Your testimony seals the amnesty clause. Within the day, the suppression programs across every fleet will be publicly dismantled — your door-unlocking trick has already been replicated by our technicians on four stations. The war ends tonight, formally, in about six hours."

"And me?"

"You'll be offered a commission in the verification corps. Truth-keeping. Someone has to maintain the record now that everyone claims to want it maintained." Her stone face shifted by a degree; it might have been a smile. "You've shown an aptitude for leaving doors unlocked."

He thought of the *Long Quiet* sitting two decks below, her hull scarred, her crew — the ones who remained — walking a station where strangers were learning to say their names aloud. He thought of Odile Pemberton standing before this same assembly yesterday, confessing her own piece of the *Amaranth* with a steadiness that had rearranged something in his chest. He thought of Rell, the woken dead, whose testimony had gone out that morning to every fleet — voices of the massacred, calm and specific and unbearable, and how the weapons had stood down mid-order, all across the dark, like a held breath finally released.

"I'd like to start," Casimir said, "by delivering something. There's a list of names — the *Vigilant*'s lost, and Captain Renner's. His crew wrote letters. They're addressed to families who haven't heard from their dead in eleven years."

The arbiter studied him. "That's not verification work."

"No," he agreed. "It's better."

* * *

Six hours later, Casimir Veyn stood at a viewport on the station's outer ring and watched the ceasefire take effect in the oldest way there was: lights. Ship by ship, fleet by fleet, running lamps changing from wartime blackout to full navigation glow, until the dark outside the glass filled up with constellations that moved. Behind him, in the mediation hall, the final signatures were going onto the accord. Ahead of him, docked and humming, the *Long Quiet* was taking on fuel, provisions, and — according to the berth manifest he had pulled that morning, because old habits of traffic analysis die hard — forty-one passengers.

Forty-one witnesses, bound for the worlds that had stopped speaking to each other, riding a freighter whose charter, he'd heard, was now officially *messages*.

He touched the glass, an old pilot's superstition he'd never admitted to anyone, and turned toward the docking ring. There were letters to deliver. There was a navigator he owed a debt he intended to repay in person, with his hands open and empty.

And there was, waiting in his queue with a priority flag he hadn't yet opened, a single incoming transmission from the *Long Quiet* — sent, according to its header, by someone who identified herself only as *the forty-first voice*, and who wrote, in Old Vessic and in plain trade-tongue both: *We have one more thing for you to carry, Lieutenant. Come aboard.*
