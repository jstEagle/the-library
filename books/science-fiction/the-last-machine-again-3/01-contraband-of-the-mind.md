## 1. Contraband of the Mind

The coolant lines of the *Perpetua* ran cold against Mara's bare forearms, and that was the first good sign of the night. Cold meant the lines were live, meant the dock inspectors had no reason to think anyone had been inside them in six months, meant the seals Mara had installed last quarter were still holding their little lies.

They worked by touch more than sight. The maintenance bay of Ceres Station's Berth 44 smelled of ozone and old water and the sweetish chemical tang of coolant that had been recycled too many times—smells Mara had long ago stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing your own blood. Above, through the grating, boots passed and passed again. Customs walked the deck in threes. Never twos. Twos meant one of them was new and being taught; threes meant routine, and routine was the most valuable commodity on Ceres.

Mara fed the first chip into the junction housing at the elbow of the primary line. It was the size of a fingernail clipping, wrapped in dielectric film, and it held—so far as Mara knew or cared to know—a stranger's wedding. Or a stranger's funeral. Or the last living recollection of a woman who had died on Ganymede and whose daughter, now on Ceres, had paid a great deal of ration scrip to have that recollection smuggled across the war line. The manifest Mara had written said *industrial coolant filters, twelve units, nonperishable*. The truth was smaller and heavier than that, the way truth usually was.

Twelve chips. Eleven in the lines, and the twelfth—

The twelfth was the one Mara was not thinking about. That was the discipline. You did not think about the cargo. You thought about torque settings, about the exact pressure of a seal, about the inspector named Brannoc who took his bribe in uncut tea rather than scrip because scrip could be traced and tea could not. You thought about the work. The work was the wall you built between yourself and everything you carried.

*Never open what you carry. Never be remembered.* Mara had written the rules on the inside of their own skull so long ago they felt like bone.

The last chip went into the secondary junction, and Mara sealed the housing, wiped it down with a cloth that carried the bay's own grime so the metal would look appropriately untouched, and climbed out of the coolant trench with the ease of someone who had done this four hundred times and would do it four hundred more. Their hands were steady. Their hands were always steady. It was the rest of them that sometimes, in the empty hours, felt like a housing with nothing sealed inside.

* * *

Brannoc found them at the cargo bay, as scheduled, as inevitable.

"Filters," he said, looking at the manifest without reading it. He was a heavy man gone soft in low gravity, with the pale, unlined face of someone who had spent thirty years underground and would spend thirty more. His eyes did the reading his hands wouldn't.

"Twelve units," Mara agreed. "Nonperishable."

"Lot of traffic through 44 lately."

"Lot of coolant failing lately. The station's older than both of us, Inspector."

Brannoc almost smiled. He held out his hand—not palm up, nothing so vulgar—and Mara pressed the tin of tea into it in a handshake that lasted half a second too long. The tin was sealed, unmarked, and contained enough leaf to buy his silence for a quarter, and his signature on the inspection pad for the length of time it took to write it.

He ran his scanner over the cargo bay anyway. That was part of the ritual too; a bribe without a scan looked like a bribe, and a scan without a bribe looked like an accusation. The scanner chirped its flat, stupid satisfaction at the coolant lines. Dielectric film defeated it the way water defeats a scream.

"Clean," Brannoc said, and signed.

"Always," Mara said, and hated how much it sounded like a promise.

* * *

The client was waiting in a tea house two levels down from the docks, one of those establishments that existed in the gaps between jurisdictions—licensed by no one, frequented by everyone, smelling of steam and recycled citrus. The client had taken the corner booth with his back to the wall, which told Mara three things before they'd even sat down: he was new to this, he was frightened, and he had been told to be frightened by someone who understood fear as a tool.

He was young. Mid-twenties, maybe, with the soft hands of a habitat technician and the hollowed eyes of someone who hadn't slept since the money changed hands. He kept his jacket zipped to the throat even in the tea house heat, and Mara could see the outline of a scrip chit against his chest, worn smooth from handling.

"You're the courier," he said. Not a question. A hope, spoken aloud so it couldn't be taken back.

"I'm the person you were told to meet," Mara said, and sat. "Do you want the tea or the transaction?"

"The—" He blinked. "Both?"

"Tea first. Transaction second. It's better for the nerves, and your nerves are the most dangerous thing at this table."

They ordered. He drank his too fast and burned his tongue and didn't notice. Mara watched him the way a surgeon watches a wound—not with cruelty, but with the professional assessment of how much damage was already done and how much more could be prevented.

"My mother," he said, when the cups were empty. "She's on Ganymede. She's—" His throat worked. "She has the long fading. The doctors say the memories go in the order they arrived. Childhood first. She's already—" He stopped, started again. "I recorded everything. Every call, every visit, everything she told me about her life before the purges. I put it all on one chip. I just need it to reach her while she still—" He couldn't finish it. He didn't need to.

Mara had heard this story in a hundred variations. A mother. A brother. A lover conscripted by one fleet and stranded on the other side of a war that had been cold for twelve years and cold the way deep space is cold—not dead, only waiting. The Belt was full of people the war had cut in half, and Mara was the needle that sewed a few of them back together, one chip at a time, and took no stitches for themselves.

"Berth 44," Mara said. "The *Perpetua*. Coolant filters. Your chip is in the secondary junction. It will be on Ganymede in eleven days, and it will be handled by someone who has never heard your name and never will."

He fumbled the scrip chit out of his jacket. It was more than Mara had quoted. Mara noticed the excess, weighed it, and slid a third of it back across the table.

"You overpaid."

"I can't— I need it to arrive. I need—"

"It will arrive. It always arrives. The extra doesn't make it arrive faster, it just makes you poorer, and you'll want that scrip when your mother's doctors start charging for the good care." Mara stood. "One question. Did you tell anyone what's on the chip?"

"No one."

"Did anyone help you encode it?"

"A friend. She doesn't know what—"

"Does she know the route?"

"No."

"Good." Mara picked up the chit. "Then she can't remember anything that matters. That's the whole trade, in the end. What people don't know, they can't give up." They paused at the edge of the booth, and something—not kindness exactly, something older and more tired than kindness—made them add: "When she's gone, keep the chip's twin if you made one. Play it sometimes. Memory's not just for the dying."

He stared at them like they'd spoken in a language he almost knew.

Mara left the tea house without being remembered. That was a skill too. You dressed forgettably, you walked at the pace of the crowd, you gave your face nothing to hold onto—not anger, not joy, not the small telltale warmth of a person who has somewhere to be. You became, in the presence of others, a gap in the record. Twelve years of practice. Twelve years since the purges had taught Mara that being noticed was the same as being marked, and being marked was the same as being gone.

* * *

The berth was in the old residential ring, a coffin-sized room with a bunk, a heater that clicked like a slow clock, and one porthole that looked out onto the black flank of Ceres itself, all shadow and rock, turning its long turn around the sun. Mara had lived in it for six years and had never once put anything on the walls. Nothing on the walls meant nothing to catalog, nothing to trace, nothing for an investigator to read like tea leaves. But tonight, coming in, the bareness looked less like discipline and more like what it was.

Mara ran the day's logs out of habit. The *Perpetua*'s systems had recorded the coolant work, the customs inspection, the tea house meeting—the ship logged everything, because ships did, because the fleets required it, because the war required it, and Mara's job was to make sure the record said nothing. They scrubbed the tea house entry first, replacing it with a maintenance cycle. They flattened the thermal profile of the cargo bay. They rewrote Brannoc's inspection into the same shape as the eleven inspections before it, so that anyone reading the ship's history would find twelve identical, boring, unremarkable nights, and boredom was the best armor ever forged.

Then they sat on the bunk with the log terminal still glowing, and did the thing they did every night, and had never once examined.

They deleted the day.

Not the falsified entries—the falsified entries stayed, that was the architecture of the lie. They deleted the true ones. The working copies, the raw captures, the little accidental truths the ship's systems had gathered like dust: the exact weight of the scrip chit, the sound of the client's voice cracking on the word *mother*, the temperature of the coolant against their arms. Gone. Wiped clean, every night, so that Mara's own life existed nowhere except in Mara's head, and even there only in the form they permitted: work, work, work, sleep.

Somewhere a woman on Ganymede was forgetting her childhood in the order it arrived. Somewhere her son was lying awake in a bunk on Ceres, lighter by a chit of scrip and heavier by hope. And here was the courier between them, the needle, the gap in the record, sitting in a room with nothing on the walls, deleting their own day out of habit.

Mara told themselves it was tradecraft. It was tradecraft. It was also the only way they knew to live.

They lay down. The heater clicked. Ceres turned its long turn.

In the morning there would be the Ganymede run—eleven days through the debris corridors, the slow smuggler's route, the long cold silence between the fleets. In the morning there would be work.

But that night, before sleep, the terminal chimed. A message from Dessa Voss, the fence who moved half the Belt's quiet cargo and all of its quiet rumors. No pleasantries. Dessa didn't waste words on pleasantries.

*Got an intercept you need to see. Dockside, tomorrow, 0800. It's got your name in it, Mara. Your real one.*

Mara lay in the dark and read it twice.

The heater clicked. Somewhere below, a woman was forgetting, and somewhere above, the fleets waited in their cold formations, and here, in a bare room on a bare rock, a person who had spent twelve years making sure no one remembered them sat holding a message that said someone, somewhere, had.

Mara did not sleep.
