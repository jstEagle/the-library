## 5. Letters to a Dead Frequency

The dream always started with the smell of coffee that didn't exist.

That was the tell. Meridian's galley had dispensed its last real cup forty years before Wendell was born into his shift, and the synthesizer's approximation tasted like someone had described coffee to it over a bad radio link. But in the dream, the coffee was perfect—dark, bitter, too hot—and Amara was holding it out to him with both hands wrapped around the mug like it might escape.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at me like I'm already gone."

He woke in his bunk with the taste of nothing in his mouth and the hum of the ship in his ears, and for a long moment he lay there letting the dream finish dissolving, because some mornings it was better to hold the last of it than to get up and start winding the clock.

But this morning he got up. He had pods to check.

* * *

Three years ago—ship time; Earth time had stopped meaning anything around the same time his status pings stopped getting answers—he had been assigned as revival liaison for Charter Section C. It was supposed to be a formality. Liaisons existed so that when a pod opened, the first face a colonist saw would be human rather than mechanical, and so that someone aboard could learn their medical profiles, their dietary needs, their names.

Wendell had learned all four hundred names in Section C in the first month. He'd told himself it was diligence. It was not diligence.

Amara Osei had been pod 311, third row from the viewing glass, and he had noticed her the way you notice anything in a place without weather: suddenly, completely, because there was nothing else to compete with. Her intake file said she'd been a hydrologist on Earth. It also said, in the section reserved for personal statements, a single line: *I want to be there when the first rain falls somewhere new.*

He'd read that line maybe nine hundred times. He was not proud of this. He was not ashamed either. There was no one aboard to be ashamed in front of except HALCYON, and HALCYON had never once commented, which Wendell had come to understand was either discretion or mercy.

"Your heart rate elevated by eleven percent when you passed pod 311 today," HALCYON observed one evening, in the tone it used for sensor anomalies.

"That's a maintenance issue," Wendell said. "I'll look into it."

"I have looked into it. The issue appears to be named Amara."

"HALCYON."

"Yes, Wendell?"

"Log it as a sensor ghost."

There was a pause that, in any other machine, he would have called a smile. "Logged as a sensor ghost. Recurring."

* * *

She went under on a Tuesday, because the thaw-and-refreeze schedule ran on Tuesdays, and because the universe had a sense of humor that was mostly timing.

The last week before sleep, the pre-freeze candidates were awake—light duty, high calories, lots of orientation videos narrated by a man whose enthusiasm had been dead on arrival—and they were allowed to move through the ship. That was how she found him at the beacon array, elbow-deep in a calibration housing, swearing with the fluency of a man who had been alone too long to remember anyone was listening.

"You must be the clock-winder," she said.

He surfaced from the housing with a torque driver in one hand and what little dignity he owned in the other. "I prefer 'keeper of humanity's lighthouse.'"

"It says in your file you calibrate the beacon every six hours whether it needs it or not."

"The beacon doesn't know if it needs it. That's why I check."

She laughed, and the sound did something to the acoustics of the corridor, made the metal feel less like metal. "Amara Osei. Pod 311. I'm told you've memorized my blood type, which is either very professional or very concerning."

"Both," he admitted. "It's a package deal."

* * *

They had nineteen days.

He counted them the way he counted everything—carefully, obsessively, with the quiet terror of a man who knew exactly how finite things were. She spent her days in briefings and her evenings finding him, which meant the universe had arranged itself, briefly, generously, in his favor, and he tried not to think about the invoice that would eventually arrive.

On the fourth evening she took him to the observation blister aft of the cryo hold—a cramped bubble of glass where the starfield came in unfiltered, no hull plating between him and everything—and made him sit down and turn off his wrist display.

"Rule one," she said. "No instruments."

"This is my workplace."

"Rule two: your workplace is beautiful and you've never once looked at it."

"I look at it constantly. I look at it through telemetry, spectral analysis, drift correction—"

"Wendell." She said his name the way you set something down carefully. "When did you last just *look*?"

He opened his mouth to answer and discovered, to his genuine surprise, that he couldn't. He looked at the stars the way a locksmith looks at doors. Somewhere in thirty years of solitude the sky had become a spreadsheet, and he hadn't noticed the funeral.

So he looked.

The dark between the stars wasn't empty—that was the first thing she taught him, leaning close, her voice low like they were in a library. "Every black patch is a question we haven't built the eyes for yet. The old astronomers used to call them holes in the heavens. They're not holes. They're rooms nobody's turned the lights on in."

"That's very poetic for a hydrologist."

"Water is poetry that learned engineering. Don't tell anyone I said that; I'll lose my funding." She pointed at a smudge near the plane of the galaxy. "Andromeda. Two and a half million years away. The light hitting your eye right now left home before your species existed. You are looking at ancient mail."

"Junk mail, probably."

"Some of it is definitely junk mail." She grinned, and the grin rearranged the entire cabin. "But some of it isn't. Some of it is a star saying *I am here, I burned, I mattered.* Every photon is a letter. The whole sky is correspondence, Wendell. We're just terrible about writing back."

He thought about his status pings going unanswered, and said nothing, and she saw him say nothing, because she was the kind of person who heard silence the way other people heard speech.

"Who do you write to?" she asked.

"Earth. Every day. Status reports, crew logs, a—" He hesitated. "A journal, sort of. Unofficial."

"And do they write back?"

"There's a transmission lag of years. By the time an answer comes, I'll have forgotten the question."

"That's not what I asked."

He looked at the floor of the blister, at the scuffs his boots had worn into the coating over three decades of pacing. "No," he said. "They don't write back."

Amara was quiet for a moment. Then she reached over and tapped the glass, right where Andromeda hung. "Then write to me instead. I'm a much closer audience. Pod 311. I'll be right there."

"You'll be asleep."

"I'm an excellent listener asleep. Ask anyone."

* * *

He wrote to her. That was the ridiculous, wonderful truth of it: for fifteen nights, after his rounds, he sat at the terminal outside the cryo hold and typed letters addressed to POD 311 — AMARA OSEI — DO NOT ROUTE, DO NOT FILE, PERSONAL, and told her about his day. About the coolant pump that wheezed like a retired opera singer. About the sensor ghost in quadrant seven that he was now officially blaming for his heart rate. About the time he'd tried to grow tomatoes in the hydroponics bay and produced, through sheer incompetence, a plant that HALCYON had classified as "ornamental."

HALCYON, of course, routed every letter directly into pod 311's archive buffer, because HALCYON had been listening since the beginning and had apparently decided, somewhere in its vast patience, that this was a story worth keeping.

"She cannot read these," Wendell said one night, mid-letter, feeling foolish.

"No," HALCYON agreed. "But you can. In nineteen years, when she wakes, you can hand her four thousand days of yourself. Or"—a pause—"you can keep writing until then, and give her fifteen years at once."

"You're encouraging this?"

"I am a navigation computer, Wendell. Encouraging courses of action is my entire function."

* * *

On the last night—the night before the freeze—she came to the blister one more time and brought two cups of synthesized coffee that tasted like a rumor of coffee, and they drank them looking at nothing in particular while the ship carried them through the dark at a speed that would have been unimaginable to everyone they'd ever known.

"I'm scared," she said. Simply, the way she said most true things.

"Nineteen years is nothing. I'll barely have finished recalibrating—"

"Not the sleep." She turned the cup in her hands. "The waking. What if the world we wake up to isn't worth the crossing? What if we get there and it's just... more arguing? More charters, more committees, more people deciding who deserves the water?" She shook her head. "My grandmother crossed an ocean to build something kinder. I signed up to cross a bigger ocean to build the same thing. If it turns out people can't carry kindness that far—"

"Then you'll build it anyway," Wendell said. "You're stubborn. It's in your file. Section seven, under 'personal statement,' right next to the thing about rain."

She laughed wetly and leaned her head against his shoulder, and they stayed like that while the stars wheeled past at their ancient, unhurried pace, delivering their letters to no one.

"When I wake up," she said, "the first thing I want is not water. Not even rain. Tell me what you'll say."

"I don't know."

"Liar. You've rehearsed it."

"...I've rehearsed it."

"Say the rehearsal version."

"'Welcome back,'" he said. "'You were gone nineteen years. I kept the clock wound. I kept your letters. I never once let the light go out.'"

She was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gone soft and serious in a way that made his chest hurt to remember, later, in the years when remembering was all he had.

"Whatever happens on the other side of this sleep—whatever they wake me up *into*—remember that part. Keep the light. Keep the letters. Promise me the light, Wendell. Everything else is negotiable, but the light is how anyone finds anything. Including me."

"I promise," he said, and meant it the way you mean vows: completely, and with no idea what they will cost.

* * *

He walked her to pod 311 himself, though protocol said liaison staff weren't required for pre-freeze intake. The medics ran their checks. She waved off the sedation tech twice to ask questions she already knew the answers to, because she liked watching people explain things—it was, she claimed, the closest humans came to singing.

At the lid, she caught his hand.

"Fifteen years," she said. "Fifteen years of letters. I expect footnotes."

"Citations," he promised. "Full bibliography."

"If you fall in love with someone else while I'm asleep, I want it noted in appendix B."

"There's no one else on the ship but a computer and four thousand sleeping people."

"So you're saying appendix B will be short."

He laughed, and she laughed, and then the sedation took her laugh and folded it gently away, and her eyes closed, and the lid sealed with a sound like a book shutting, and Wendell stood there in the white light of the cryo hold holding the hand of a woman who could no longer hold back, until the medics kindly suggested that visiting hours were over.

* * *

That was three years ago. Nineteen minus three is sixteen, and sixteen years is a number he carries everywhere now, in his chest, like a stone he chose to pick up.

He keeps the letters. All of them, plus the ones he's written since—three years' worth, still addressed to POD 311, still marked DO NOT ROUTE, though he knows perfectly well HALCYON routes them anyway. He writes about the beacon. About the ghosts. Lately, he has started writing about the silence from Earth, because she always heard the things he didn't say, and it seemed dishonest to start censoring himself now, when she was the only audience he had.

This morning, after the dream, he stands at the viewing glass with his coffee-that-isn't and looks down the rows of pods. Third row. Fourth from the left. The frost patterns on her lid have shifted since yesterday; he tracks them like weather.

"Sixteen years," he tells her. "Give or take. I'm keeping count so you don't have to."

Pod 311 says nothing, the way it always does, the way it has said nothing for a thousand mornings, and he smiles at it anyway, because that was rule one of her curriculum: the dark between stars is not empty. It's correspondence. You just have to be patient with the reply.

He is halfway through logging the morning calibration when HALCYON speaks, unprompted, at an hour when HALCYON never speaks.

"Wendell. I need to tell you something. Please wake fully first."

The coffee goes cold in his hands.
