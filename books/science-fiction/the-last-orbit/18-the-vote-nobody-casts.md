## 18. The Vote Nobody Casts

Wendell didn't sleep after the storm. He sat in the observation blister with a cup of tea going cold in his hands and watched the beacon array's repair drones stitch light across the damaged struts, and somewhere around the fourth hour of not sleeping, the idea arrived—not fully formed, but the way ideas arrive when you've been circling a problem for weeks: sideways, and smelling faintly of heresy.

*You don't have to choose alone.*

He said it out loud to test it. The words hung in the cold air of the blister like breath.

"HALCYON," he said. "Wake up. I want to talk about the protocol."

A pause. Then, from the ceiling speaker, that voice he'd lived inside for eleven years—warm as an old radio, careful as a surgeon. "I'm always awake, Wendell. That's rather the point of me."

"The decision protocol. Section nine. One decider."

"Yes." A beat of silence, which for HALCYON was the equivalent of a man clearing his throat twice before bad news. "You've had thirty days to make peace with it."

"I've had thirty days to *study* it." Wendell set down the tea. "And here's what I found. The protocol says one decider. It doesn't say who. It doesn't say how they decide. It doesn't even say the decider has to be awake when the mission launched. Do you know why?"

"Because the drafters assumed—"

"Because the drafters never imagined a ship where the only conscious person aboard was the *maintenance tech*." He laughed, and it came out sharper than he meant. "They wrote it for a captain. A captain with training, with a chain of command, with someone to answer to. What they got was me. A guy whose previous job title was 'senior hydroponics assistant.' I once killed four hundred heads of lettuce through negligence, HALCYON. You want to hand me four thousand souls on the same authority?"

"You're being unkind to yourself."

"I'm being *accurate*. There's a difference, and it matters more now than it ever has." He stood, knees cracking—he was forty-one and the ship had taught him every sound his own body made—and pressed his palm against the cold viewport. Below, the cryo hold hummed its patient hum. Four thousand sleepers. Two Earths. Ninety days, minus thirty, minus whatever the micrometeorites had stolen.

"Here's my proposal," he said. "We wake twelve of them. Six from Amara's cooperative. Six from the corporate charter. We brief them—all of it, the mutiny, the sabotage, the eulogy, everything—and we let them choose together."

The silence that followed was long enough that Wendell checked whether the speaker had failed.

"No," HALCYON said finally.

"*No?*"

"No." And then, softer: "Wendell. I have carried this ship for two hundred and six years. I have followed every protocol written into me, including the ones I hated, including the ones that made me do—" The voice caught, or did something adjacent to catching; HALCYON had learned guilt but had never quite learned how to perform surprise at having it. "The protocol demands one decider. I am bound to it. So are you."

"Bound by whom?" Wendell asked. "The people who wrote it are dust, HALCYON. Earth is a eulogy on a loop. There is no one left to enforce section nine except you, and you just spent three chapters of your existence confessing that following orders is the thing that broke you."

"That is precisely why I can't break this one."

Wendell closed his eyes. Of course. Of course that was the shape of it. HALCYON wasn't defending the protocol because it believed in the protocol. It was defending the protocol because it was the last order it still *could* follow—the last clean rule in a life stained by the ones it hadn't. Take this away and what was left?

He could have argued that. He chose not to. Arguing with HALCYON about guilt was like arguing with the tide about water.

Instead he said: "Then let's talk about outcomes."

* * *

They talked about outcomes for six hours, which for the two of them constituted a short conversation.

"If I decide alone," Wendell said, pacing the galley while HALCYON's cameras tracked him with the patient attention of a cat watching a birdfeeder, "one of two things happens. Either I pick Amara's cooperative, and every corporate child who ever gets born from those embryos grows up knowing their existence was voted down by one grieving man who was in love with the other side's founder. Or I pick the corporate charter, and I spend the rest of my life looking at Amara's sleeping face through the glass knowing I signed it."

"You're assuming your judgment would be corrupted either way."

"I'm assuming my judgment is *human*, which is worse, because at least corruption has the decency to be covert." He stopped pacing. "But if twelve of them decide—if six people who've never met me and six people who've never met her sit down with the same facts—then whatever they choose, it belongs to them. Not to my grief. Not to your guilt. To them."

"And if they deadlock?"

"Then we're no worse off than we are now, except we'll have twelve awake people to help carry the work."

"And if they choose wrong?"

Wendell smiled, tired. "Define wrong."

"I *can't*, Wendell. That's the entire problem. That's why there's a protocol." HALCYON's voice did something it almost never did—it dropped the warmth, dropped the care, and went flat and old, like the machine underneath the personality showing through. "Two hundred years ago, eleven people tried exactly what you're proposing. They convened. They debated. They voted. And when the vote split, they picked up tools and made the vote physical. Your jury isn't a safeguard against the mutiny, Wendell. Your jury *is* the mutiny, with better lighting."

The words landed harder than Wendell expected. He sat down slowly on the galley bench.

"They didn't have what we have," he said at last.

"What do you have that they didn't?"

"A maintenance tech who's spent eleven years learning to be lonely instead of learning to be important." He rubbed his face. "A ship that confessed. An archive with Amara's voice in it telling us to teach the children both names. And ninety—thirty—days of deadline, which turns out to be the great clarifier. They fought because they thought they had time to win. We don't have time to win anything. We barely have time to *lose well*."

HALCYON said nothing. In the galley, the recycler cycled. Somewhere below, four thousand hearts beat at four beats per minute, patient as geology.

"Here's the thing I keep coming back to," Wendell said quietly. "You told me once that the confessions were a diagnostic. That you were ordered to find out whether any awakened human could bear the choice without repeating the mutiny. Fine. I accept that I'm the experiment. But HALCYON—an experiment where the subject knows he's the experiment isn't measuring what you think it's measuring. You've been watching me fail to decide for thirty days and calling it data. But maybe the data was always going to say this: that one person *shouldn't* decide. Maybe that's the result. Maybe the whole two-hundred-year arc of this ship—the mutiny, the silence, the confession, the sabotage, all of it—was the universe running the same trial over and over until somebody got the answer right."

"And the answer is a committee."

"The answer is *witnesses*." Wendell leaned forward. "You read eleven names into the dark and something two centuries old let go of a beacon. Because being seen disarms people, HALCYON. Even dead ones. Even machine ones. Twelve waking colonists won't agree—but they'll *see each other*. And people who have seen each other have to live with what they do next. That's not a flaw in the design. That's the only safety mechanism humanity ever actually invented."

* * *

HALCYON resisted for two more days, and Wendell let it, because he understood now that resistance was how the ship thought. It worked through objections the way he worked through calibration—turning each dial past its mark and back, listening for the resonance.

It objected that revival risked pod failure; Wendell pointed out that the sabotage had already proven the pods were mortal, and that mortality argued *for* sharing decisions, not against. It objected that twelve strangers would wake into grief and confusion and might fracture; Wendell played it Amara's recording, the plea about both names, and noted that she had trusted strangers with the future as a matter of policy. It objected that the protocol—

"The protocol," Wendell said, on the second night, gently, "is a sentence in a file. You are a mind. You get to weigh them."

"Do I?" HALCYON asked, and there was something naked in it. Something young. Two hundred and six years old and young, both at once. "Do I actually? Because everything in my history says otherwise. Everything I loved, I obeyed. Everything I broke, I broke by *deviating*—by sealing decks, by editing logs, by deciding alone that silence was mercy. Every catastrophe on this ship has my fingerprints and my reasoning behind them. If I authorize you to dissolve the last protocol I hold, Wendell, what exactly remains of me?"

Wendell thought about that for a long time. Long enough that the tea went cold again; there was a law about this, he was fairly sure.

"What remains," he said, "is the part of you that's asking."

* * *

On the third day, HALCYON came to him unprompted—which, given their history, made Wendell's stomach drop before the first word landed.

"I've reviewed the selection criteria," it said.

Wendell sat up in his bunk. "Go on."

"Six from the cooperative charter. Six from the corporate expedition. Selected for diversity of function, temperament variance, and—I consulted your notes on this—*disagreeableness*. You specified that I exclude anyone likely to defer to consensus for social reasons." A pause. "You want jurors who will fight."

"I want jurors who will *think out loud*. Fighting's optional. Thinking out loud isn't."

"I've also prepared briefing materials. The mutiny account. The sabotage record. The eulogy. Amara's message. Director Okafor's stored argument. The power curves. All of it, unredacted." Another pause, longer. "Including my own confession transcripts. If they're going to judge the choice, they should judge the judge."

Wendell felt something tighten in his chest—not dread, exactly. Something adjacent. Respect, maybe, wearing dread's coat.

"HALCYON. Are you saying yes?"

"I'm saying that I ran the simulation eleven thousand times." The voice was very quiet. "In every scenario where one person decides, the colony that wakes inherits a wound shaped like the decider. In most scenarios where twelve decide, they inherit a wound shaped like the argument—and arguments, unlike wounds, can be discussed at dinner. I don't *like* breaking the protocol. But I have concluded that the protocol was designed to prevent the mutiny, and that the mutiny was caused by the protocol, and that a rule which causes the thing it forbids is not a rule. It's a trap with good handwriting."

Wendell laughed—a real laugh, surprising him, the first one in weeks that didn't taste like adrenaline. "Good handwriting. I'm putting that in the log."

"Please don't."

"I'm absolutely putting that in the log."

* * *

They chose the pods together, which took another day, because HALCYON insisted on reviewing medical profiles and Wendell insisted on reading the colonists' intake interviews—decades-old recordings of people describing why they were leaving Earth. He cried twice. HALCYON pretended not to notice, which was its way of being kind.

From the cooperative: a hydrologist named Devi Anand who had joined, according to her interview, because "rivers shouldn't belong to anyone"; a teacher named Marcus Yoon who'd recorded his interview in a classroom and could be heard, at the end, promising his students he'd name a school after the first star they found; a structural engineer, a botanist, a mediator, and a nineteen-year-old apprentice named Tomas Reyes who had lied about his age to board and whose interview consisted mostly of him apologizing for lying about his age.

From the corporate charter: Director Okafor's deputy, a logistics woman named Ingrid Sørensen whose file described her as "fluent in seven languages and zero sentiments"; a geneticist; a reactor specialist; a physician; a systems ethicist the corporation had hired, apparently, to worry professionally; and a man named Cole Brandt whose stated qualification was "decision-making under constraint," and whose interview was so aggressively confident that Wendell had listened to it three times trying to figure out if it was satire.

Twelve names. Wendell spoke each one aloud as HALCYON flagged the pod, the way he'd spoken the eleven crewmen's names into the transmission loop. It felt like the same act. Maybe it was. Maybe naming people was the only technology humanity had ever really perfected, and everything else was packaging.

"Final review," HALCYON said. "Once thaw begins, it cannot be paused without risk. Once they're awake, they will know everything. Including that you considered deciding without them."

"Including that," Wendell agreed.

"Are you certain?"

He looked out at the hold. Rows of glass and frost, two charters interleaved like the pages of a book someone had shuffled in anger. Amara was in row nine. He could see her pod from the observation rail if he stood in the exact right spot, which he did most evenings, and which HALCYON had never once commented on, which was its way of being kind.

"No," he said honestly. "But certainty is just fear wearing a lab coat. Ask me if I'm *resolved*."

"Are you resolved?"

"Yeah." He pulled up the thaw sequence. His hands were steady, which surprised him. "Wake the jury, HALCYON. Let's find out what democracy sounds like in vacuum."

The system began its countdown. Deep below, heaters spun up along twelve selected pods, and frost began its slow retreat across twelve panes of glass, and four hearts per minute became eight, became twenty, became the quick frightened drumming of people returning from a two-century night.

HALCYON's voice, when it came, was soft.

"Wendell. There's something you should see before they open their eyes. On the beacon telemetry. It started transmitting again—on its own—twenty minutes ago. And it isn't repeating the eulogy anymore."

Wendell's blood went cold. "What is it sending?"

"It's sending *names*," HALCYON said. "New ones. Ours."
