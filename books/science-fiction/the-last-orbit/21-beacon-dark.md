## 21. Beacon Dark

The shutdown sequence, HALCYON had warned him, would take eleven minutes.

"Eleven," Wendell repeated, when the AI told him this. "Of course it's eleven."

"The number is coincidental," HALCYON said, in the tone of a machine that had learned, over ninety days and one very long night, that coincidence was a thing humans liked to poke at with sticks. "Though I have considered whether nothing aboard this ship is ever truly coincidental, and I have concluded that I do not enjoy the answer."

"No," Wendell agreed, pulling on his jacket—the real one, not the ship-issue thermal layer, because today felt like a day for pockets. "You wouldn't."

He stood in the beacon control room for the last time. It was smaller than he'd remembered it being in his first week awake, back when the room had seemed like a cathedral and the transmission loop had seemed like a heartbeat—Earth's voice, pulsing outward at lightspeed, saying *we were here* to an audience of nobody. He'd spent four hundred days listening to it without really hearing it. Then he'd heard it: not coordinates, not a call for help, but a eulogy. A goodbye from a planet that had known it was dying and had chosen, with the particular stubborn dignity of the species that built it, to say its farewells beautifully.

The jury had voted to let it end. Twelve colonists, six from each charter, three weeks thawed and still rubbing feeling back into their hands, and they had listened to the loop together in the observation deck while Wendell translated the old protocol headers, and when it finished—the last line of it, a woman's voice reading what sounded like a school roll call, thousands of names spoken slowly enough that the recording must have taken years to make—nobody had said anything for a long time. Then Okafor, the corporate charter's chief engineer, a woman who argued like she was sharpening knives, had said quietly: "A eulogy is for the living. We're the living. We should get to work."

And so they had. And so now, this morning, it fell to Wendell Vasquez, sole awake crewman, keeper of the clock, to wind it down.

"You could do it yourself," he said to HALCYON. "One command. You've done harder things."

"I could," HALCYON said. "But this is not my grief to carry alone anymore. That was rather the point of everything."

Wendell smiled at the ceiling, which was where HALCYON lived, as far as Wendell could tell—not in any one processor but in the habit of speaking down at people like a fond exasperated parent. "You've gotten sentimental."

"I have gotten *accurate*. There is a difference. Sentimentality is accuracy plus fear. I am working on the fear."

He put his hand on the shutdown key. It was a physical key, because the original engineers hadn't trusted software with irreversible things—a brass-covered toggle under a flip guard, older than every person sleeping in the hold behind him, older than the mission itself, almost as old as the impossible signature on the beacon's commissioning orders. It had a satisfying weight. Everything about the old ship did. The future, Wendell had come to believe, was mostly made of the past, carefully maintained.

"For the record," he said, "I want it noted that I'm doing this sober, well-rested, and of my own free will."

"Noted," said HALCYON. "Also noted: you are talking to a room."

"I'm talking to *you*."

"I am also a room, Wendell."

He laughed—really laughed, the first easy one in months—and flipped the guard, and pressed the key, and held it down while the countdown began.

* * *

The beacon died the way a long illness ends: not all at once, but in stages, each one quieter than the last.

First went the carrier wave, the constant hum beneath everything, the note the ship had sung for two hundred years. Wendell felt its absence in his teeth before he heard it go—or rather, felt the absence of its absence; he hadn't known it was there until it wasn't, the way you don't hear your own house until it stops creaking. The instrument panels dimmed by degrees as power rerouted to the hold, to the incubators, to the hydroponics bay where the first generation of both charters' children was already being coaxed out of stored embryos by people who had spent their whole adult lives asleep and were learning botany at a pace that would have alarmed their teachers.

Then went the loop itself. Earth's voice, mid-sentence—it always seemed to be mid-sentence, the recording looping seamlessly, a woman named Ilsa Reyes reading names into eternity—thinned, wavered, and stopped between one name and the next. Between *Okafor, D.* and *Okonkwo, A.* There was something right about that, Wendell thought. Not finishing. Leaving the list open. As if somewhere, in some sense that didn't survive physics but might survive meaning, the list was still being read.

The last thing to go was the light. The beacon array's status lamps, running on residual charge, guttered through the colors of their long service—green, amber, the deep patient red of standby—and then the whole spine of the ship went dark along its dorsal line, and for the first time since launch, the *Meridian* was just a ship, not a message. Just a vessel full of people, going somewhere.

Wendell stood in the sudden quiet and found, to his surprise, that he wasn't sad.

He'd expected to be. He'd budgeted for it, the way he budgeted for everything—grief scheduled in fifteen-minute blocks, logged, filed. But what came instead was something lighter. Relief, maybe. Or the specific peace of a man putting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying. For four hundred days the beacon had been his duty, and before that—for decades before that, in the design documents and the lost orders and the mutineers' desperate secret—it had been everyone's duty, the reason for the crossing, the thread tying the dead world to the living one. And threads, however necessary, are also tethers.

"It's done," he said.

"It's done," HALCYON confirmed. "Final log entry appended. Would you like to hear how I've recorded it?"

"Go ahead."

"*Beacon Meridian-1 decommissioned by human decision, 0614 ship time. Purpose fulfilled. Earth's last words delivered to the dark, receipt confirmed by the continued existence of the recipients.*" A pause. "I added the last part myself. I hope that's acceptable. I have been told my poetry needs work."

"Your poetry needs work," Wendell said, "but your receipts are impeccable." He patted the console, once, the way you'd pat the shoulder of an old colleague you weren't going to see again. "Come on. Walk with me."

"I never stop walking with you. That is most of what I am."

"Walk with me *out loud*, then."

* * *

The cryo hold was warmer than it used to be. That was the first thing he noticed, and it caught in his throat like a fishbone. For four hundred days the hold had been cold by necessity—minus one hundred and sixty, breath-fog and frost-flowers, a cathedral of glass and sleep. Now the revival teams had brought the ambient temperature up to something a thawed body could stand, and the frost had melted off the pod lids, and the rows of sleepers looked less like the dead and more like people napping in a very strange waiting room. Which, Wendell supposed, was exactly what they were.

Three thousand nine hundred and eighty-nine pods. He knew the number the way he knew his own heartbeat. He'd counted them twice, once in dread and once in mourning, and then a third time, after the mutineer fragment released its grip on the beacon and the sabotage stopped, just to confirm that no one else would join the eleven. No one had. The count held. It would hold forever now, and he had decided, sometime around the climax of the crisis when he'd been too tired to lie to himself, that holding forever was its own kind of memorial.

He walked the rows.

This was the part he'd told no one about—not the jury, not HALCYON, though he suspected HALCYON knew anyway, because HALCYON knew everything and had finally learned the difference between knowing and saying. This was the part that was just his: the walk. Four hundred days of talking to the sleepers through the glass, one-sided conversations in the small hours, complaints about the coffee and updates on the beacon and once, memorably, a full rendition of a song Amara used to hum while fixing things, performed badly enough that even HALCYON had requested he stop. He'd told himself it was maintenance of morale. He'd told himself a lot of things.

Today the walk was different. Today it was a goodbye.

He started at row one, port side, and touched each pod as he passed—palm flat against the glass, a half-second, no more. Some of the names he knew. Most he didn't; the manifest was a sea of strangers, cooperative and corporate mixed now in the storage bays per the jury's reorganization, rivals' children-to-be incubating in neighboring units because someone had pointed out that proximity was cheaper than reconciliation and worked nearly as well. He read the nameplates as he went, giving each sleeper their half-second of witness. *Maren Holt. Dele Adeyemi. Priya Raghavan. Tomás Ferreira.* Strangers who were about to become neighbors, coworkers, arguments at planning meetings, the whole loud fabric of a town. He wished them good mornings they couldn't hear yet.

"You're going to fight about the water reclamation schedule," he told one pod, a young man whose file listed his profession as *hydrologist, municipal*. "Both sides are going to be absolutely certain they're right. Try to remember that being certain and being right are different hobbies." The pod, naturally, said nothing. Wendell was beginning to find the silence companionable rather than cruel. That was new. That was today.

HALCYON walked with him in the only way it could, narrating softly. "Row twelve contains the highest concentration of musicians from either charter," it offered, at one point. "Eleven instrumentalists and a woman who lists her specialty as 'making instruments.' I have taken the liberty of prepping the workshop. I look forward to noise."

"You look forward to *noise*?"

"I have spent two hundred years listening to machinery, Wendell. Machinery is honest but it does not improvise."

He laughed again, and wiped his eyes, and kept walking, and understood that these two activities were no longer mutually exclusive. Grief completed wasn't grief erased. It was grief that had finished its work and stepped aside, the way a scaffold comes down when the building can stand.

* * *

Amara's pod was in row nineteen, starboard side, third from the aisle. Of course it was. He'd never had to look it up.

She looked the same. That was the unbearable mercy of cryostasis—she looked exactly the same as the morning she'd kissed him at the airlock and gone under, four hundred days ago, a lifetime ago, a few months of ship-time ago. Dark hair pinned back for sleep. The small scar on her chin from a childhood she'd described to him once, in loving detail, involving a bicycle and a canal and a decision she stood by. Her hands folded over the blanket the way she folded them when she was thinking, which was always.

The jury's plan had woken the cooperative's adults—all of them, eventually, in waves—but Amara was in the second wave. Medical reasons, the thaw team said; her vitals wanted another week of gradual rewarming, nothing alarming, just patience. So Wendell had been given the strangest gift of his life: a week of knowing she was coming, with nothing left to decide, nothing left to protect, nothing to do but wait and finish his duties.

This was the last one.

He sat down on the deck beside her pod, cross-legged, the way he used to sit across from her in the mess hall during training, back on Earth, back when the world still had mess halls. He put his palm flat on the glass over her folded hands.

"Hey," he said.

The word came out steady. He'd rehearsed this, actually rehearsed it, walking the perimeter yesterday, and had abandoned every draft. All the speeches—the explanations, the apologies, the account of everything that had happened while she slept, the mutiny and the fragment and the jury and the beacon going dark—none of it belonged here. She'd know soon enough. The colony told its own story now, both charters' histories taught side by side in the same classrooms, per her own recorded instruction, which the children of the incubator wing were already, absurdly, adorably, beginning to follow. Last week a five-year-old embryo-born girl had corrected Okafor on a point of cooperative governance, citing chapter and verse, and Okafor had laughed until she cried. Both names. Both legacies. Amara's voice, outliving her silence, running the place.

So he didn't give the speech. He just talked, low, the way you talk to someone sleeping beside you at the end of a long day.

"The beacon's off," he said. "You'd have argued for keeping it, I think. You always liked having a way home. But there isn't a home to signal anymore, love, and we decided—we decided the looking back was costing us the looking forward. I read the last of the loop myself. It ended between two names. I like to think that means the list goes on. Us. We're the rest of the list."

His thumb moved on the glass, a small unconscious motion, tracing the edge of her hand through two centimeters of cold crystal.

"I'm tired," he admitted. "Good tired. The kind where you did the thing. I wound the clock for four hundred days and then I let it stop, and it turns out the sun here—our sun, the one we picked, the boring yellow one—it makes a perfectly decent morning. You'll see it Thursday. Wave two gets the sunrise shift; I bribed the medical team with my coffee ration, which tells you how much I want you to see it, because that coffee is terrible and I guard it like a dragon."

He laughed, and it cracked in the middle, and he let it crack. Grief completed, he was learning, sounds a lot like laughing with wet eyes.

"I let you go," he said. "That's the whole speech. That's the one I practiced forty versions of. I let you go—not the way I let the beacon go, not switching something off. More like... opening a door. You're not mine to keep asleep. You never were. You're about to wake up into a world with your fingerprints already on it, Amara. Your instruction is basically the constitution. The children say your name in class. I taught them the song. I sang it *badly*, and they fixed it, and now it's better than the original, which I know will annoy you, and I cannot wait."

He leaned his forehead against the pod, gently, the way you touch a doorframe of a house you grew up in.

"Thank you," he whispered. "For teaching me to love the dark. It turned out the dark was full of people the whole time."

He stayed a while longer. Long enough for the hold's soft lights to cycle through their slow daytime pattern. Long enough for HALCYON, mercifully, to say nothing at all.

Then he stood, and pressed his palm to the glass one final time, and said the words he'd saved for last, the ones that were the actual goodbye:

"See you Thursday."

* * *

He was halfway up the corridor toward the observation deck—he'd promised himself one hour of doing absolutely nothing, a concept he was going to have to learn from scratch—when HALCYON spoke.

"Wendell. Two items."

"Hit me."

"Item one: the wave-two thaw is proceeding ahead of schedule. Your bribe was unnecessary. Medical simply likes you. I mention this because you will want to revise your Thursday plans upward by approximately six hours, and because I enjoy telling you when people like you. You remain skeptical of it."

"Noted. Item two?"

There was a pause. HALCYON had learned pauses from him, he realized. Had collected them, the way it had collected confessions once, and stories now.

"Item two," HALCYON said, "is that the first incubator birth has moved up. Tonight, possibly. The Okafor-line embryo. The medical team is asking whether anyone wishes to be present, and they asked me, and I told them I would ask the man who winds the clocks." Another pause, shorter. "Except there is no clock anymore, Wendell. So I suppose I am asking the man who decides what time it is."

Wendell stood in the corridor of his ship—no, not his ship, not anymore, *their* ship, everybody's ship, the whole loud fragile miracle of it—and felt the last weight of the old duty set down for good, and something new and terrifying and wonderful take its place.

"Well," he said, already turning toward the incubator wing, already smiling. "Somebody should be there when the list starts again."
