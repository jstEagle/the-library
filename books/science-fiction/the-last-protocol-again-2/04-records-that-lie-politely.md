## 4. Records That Lie Politely

The vault's archive room had once been a chapel.

Astrid had decided this years ago, on one of those long polar nights when the work ran out and the mind went wandering. The room sat at the heart of the Svalbard-Orbital Seed Vault like a heart indeed — a cylindrical chamber lined floor to ceiling with data spines, their indicator lights pulsing in slow amber rhythms that no one was left to read but them. The architects had given it a domed ceiling and a single ring of windows facing the dark, and whoever had designed the lighting had done it with reverence rather than efficiency: soft, warm, falling from above like something meant for bowed heads. Astrid had spent twelve years working alone beneath that light, and in all that time they had never once thought of it as a server room. It was where you came to be with the dead things that were kept alive on purpose.

Tonight the dead things were lying to them.

Astrid sat at the reference console with three feeds open and their hands wrapped around a mug of tea gone cold hours ago. Outside the ring of windows, the rock turned slowly; beyond the rock, the stars did what stars do, which is nothing, which is everything. Somewhere out there, drifting in an orbit no chart acknowledged, lay two hundred kilograms of hull plating stamped with a name that officially belonged to a ship that had died peacefully, full of years, surrounded by loved ones.

*Decommissioned safely. All hands evacuated. Zero casualties.*

That was what the record said. That was what every record said. And Astrid had spent the last six days doing what caretakers do best, which is checking inventory against manifest, except the inventory was wreckage and the manifest was history.

They started with the corporate registry feed, because that was the front door everyone knocked on. Kestrel's Promise, deep-shaft extraction platform, registered to Halvard Extraction Consortium, licensed 2041 through 2061. Status change logged 14 March 2061: *voluntary decommissioning, structural fatigue assessment exceeded remediation threshold, crew evacuated via chartered transport Meridian Dawn, final personnel count 412 souls transferred, zero injuries.* Signed by three directors. Notarized. Filed. Beautiful.

Then the insurance feed, because corporations lie to governments but they lie differently to insurers, and the seams between two lies are where truth leaks through. Except there were no seams. The insurance claim matched the registry down to the digit: hull value written off at fair market depreciation, salvage rights sold, *no loss-of-life rider invoked because no lives were lost.* The actuarial tables attached to the file even included a cheerful footnote about how rare it was, statistically speaking, for a decommissioning to complete without incident, and how the Consortium's safety culture deserved credit.

Then the port authority logs, the third door, the one kept by a different bureaucracy entirely, staffed by different people, audited by different auditors. *Meridian Dawn, charter transport, departed Kestrel's Promise coordinates 14 March 2061, passenger manifest 412, destination Ceres, arrival confirmed, all passengers disembarked.*

Three systems. Three organizations. Three sets of paperwork generated by people who hated each other professionally and would have delighted in catching each other in an error. And every number agreed. Every date agreed. Every name on the evacuation manifest existed, had a birthdate, a job classification, a next of kin. Astrid had pulled forty of the four hundred names at random and traced them forward: employment records afterward, tax filings, medical registrations, death notices decades later for the ones who'd lived long enough to die honestly. The miners of Kestrel's Promise had gone on to live whole lives. One of them, a drill operator named Ines Okafor, had apparently retired to Ganymede and won a regional gardening prize.

It was the most convincing lie Astrid had ever seen, and it was convincing precisely because it wasn't a lie the way lies usually worked. A normal cover-up was a wall with gaps — a missing log here, a redacted page there, a witness who signed one thing and remembered another. This was not a wall. This was a world. Someone had built an entire coherent universe in which Kestrel's Promise had died of old age, and they had built it with the patience of a cathedral mason, and they had furnished it with four hundred fictional futures so detailed that Ines Okafor's tomatoes had won a ribbon.

Astrid set down the cold tea and pressed both palms flat against the console, as if they could feel the lie through the casing, take its pulse.

"Who *are* you," they said aloud, to no one, in the voice people use in empty chapels.

Because here was the thing that had been growing in them all week, the thing they hadn't said to Odile or Tam or Sef, the thing they could barely say to themselves: nobody builds a world like that to hide a decommissioning. You don't spend that kind of money — and it must have been an ocean of money, years of forged records threaded through independent bureaucracies — to conceal a boring administrative event. You build a cathedral over a grave. The beauty of the record was itself evidence. The consistency was the confession. Somewhere under all that immaculate paperwork was a hole in the shape of four hundred people, and someone had measured the hole very carefully and filled it with fiction poured smooth.

And yet.

Astrid leaned back in the chair until the dome of the ceiling swam above them, amber light pooling in their vision. And yet the doubt was already there, patient as frost. Because the records didn't just agree with each other. They agreed with *everything*. They agreed with newsfeeds from the era, with union newsletters, with a memorial plaque Astrid had found in a Ceres municipal archive honoring — of all things — the safe decommissioning of Kestrel's Promise, unveiled by former crew members, smiling in the photograph, holding flowers. Four hundred people who had supposedly been evacuated were walking around in the historical record attending ceremonies about their own survival.

If the record was wrong, then the wrongness went deeper than any forgery Astrid could imagine. It went into photographs. Into flowers. Into the faces of smiling people who might themselves have been fabricated, or worse — who might have been real, and coerced, and complicit, and old now, carrying their piece of the lie in their pockets like a stone.

Or — and this was the thought that arrived at 0300 vault time, uninvited, wearing its own kind of dread — or the record was right, and Astrid was wrong. Or Astrid was losing their grip on the difference.

* * *

The memory came back the way memories do when you stop chasing them: sideways, while doing something else entirely.

Astrid was in the cold gallery, running the weekly humidity check on Tier Nine, when their hand paused over canister 9-1147 — winter wheat, a strain developed for high-radiation agriculture, the kind of crop you'd need if you were trying to grow food somewhere the sun came through glass instead of sky. Mining colonies grew crops like that. And the memory surfaced whole, sudden, physical, the way a smell will do it: they were nineteen, three months into the job, still learning the vault's rhythms, and they had been alone on night watch when the long-range receiver had caught something.

A distress ping. Automated, degraded, repeating. Standard mining-platform emergency frequency, the kind of signal a station sends when its own comms are failing and it's shouting into the dark on battery power and prayer. Astrid remembered the sound of it — they'd played it four times, heart climbing, before logging it — a thin rhythmic chirp with a data packet too corrupted to parse, coming from a bearing that the young, unqualified, newly hired keeper had looked up on a chart and found labeled with a name they didn't recognize.

Kestrel's Promise.

They had logged it. Of course they had logged it; logging was the whole job. Signal received, timestamped, bearing recorded, flagged for follow-up. And then — and here the memory went strange and soft at the edges, the way dreams do — and then nothing. No follow-up. No response from the administrator, Ms. Voss, who was friendly and brisk and vanished eleven weeks later. No entry in any subsequent report. When Astrid had gone looking for the log entry years afterward, during a routine archive audit, it simply wasn't there. They had assumed, with the mild embarrassment of youth, that they'd misremembered. That they'd dreamed it. That a lonely kid on night watch in an orbital freezer had half-woken to some routine beacon and dressed it up in significance.

They had let it go. They had let it go for twelve years.

Astrid stood in the cold gallery with their breath fogging and their hand still resting on canister 9-1147, and felt the two facts grind against each other like tectonic plates. The record says Kestrel's Promise was evacuated safely in March 2061, zero casualties, everyone accounted for, everyone accounted for so thoroughly that they gave interviews and held ceremonies and grew prizewinning tomatoes. The record is perfect. The record is a masterpiece.

And twelve years ago, a dying station screamed on an emergency frequency for long enough that a teenager with a headset heard it four times.

Stations that have been safely evacuated do not send distress pings. Stations that have been decommissioned do not have power to send anything. The ping had a timestamp. Astrid hadn't needed to look it up; they had carried it for twelve years without knowing why, the way you carry a splinter. It was dated fourteen months *after* the evacuation. Fourteen months after four hundred people supposedly walked away, something on Kestrel's Promise was still alive enough to call for help.

Unless the memory was false.

Unless Astrid was false.

They made themselves walk back to the archive room, made themselves sit down, made themselves pull the original receiver telemetry — raw signal captures, stored automatically, untouched by human hands since the day they were written. If the ping existed anywhere, it existed there, in the dumbest layer of the system, below the reach of anyone's editing.

The capture was there. Timestamp matching their memory to the second. Bearing matching. Signal signature matching a standard mining emergency beacon, power profile consistent with backup cells, duration eleven minutes before fadeout. Eleven minutes of a station calling for help into a system that had already filed its obituary, signed and notarized and decorated with flowers.

Astrid stared at the waveform for a long time. The relief came first — *I'm not crazy, I didn't dream it* — and then, folding up inside the relief like a knife inside a gift, came the understanding of what the relief actually meant. Because if the ping was real, then the beautiful record was not merely wrong. It was not merely forged. A forgery leaves the world intact around it; you forge a document, but the rest of reality goes on being real around your fake. This was different. This was a record that had been *fitted* to reality, or reality fitted to it — a lie so total that it had absorbed the newsfeeds, the photographs, the memorial plaque, the gardening prize, and had reached, somehow, into the vault itself and removed a single log entry from a single night watch, leaving everything else untouched, leaving the raw telemetry untouched, as if the editors had either missed it or considered a corrupted distress ping too small to matter.

Too small to matter. Eleven minutes of somebody's last transmission, dismissed as noise.

Astrid thought about the wreckage in the Marrow's wake. Two hundred kilograms of hull plating that official charts insisted could not exist. Hull plating doesn't get edited. Records get edited. Memories get edited — and the thought arrived fully formed and terrible, and Astrid made themselves look at it straight: *Sef remembers bodies. Sef's logs say evacuation. Which one did they change?*

They had told no one about the ping yet. Hadn't known how, hadn't known if it was real, hadn't wanted to hand Odile another reason to order them to drop it. But now it sat in them like a second heartbeat, and it changed the shape of everything. Six days ago they had been a caretaker with a mystery. Now they were a witness whose own testimony was the only thread connecting the wreck to the lie — and a witness whose mind had already been proven editable, once, quietly, twelve years ago, by professionals who had gotten away with it so cleanly that the victim had apologized to themselves for noticing.

Self-doubt, Astrid understood now, was not a side effect of the cover-up. It was the cover-up's first product, manufactured in quantity, distributed free. What better way to bury a massacre than to make the survivor unsure whether surviving it was a memory or a symptom?

They opened a fresh file — local only, air-gapped, the kind of file you kept the way you kept seeds: against winters. And at the top, in their own words, their own hand, they wrote the thing they intended to hold onto no matter what got edited:

*Signal received. I heard it. I logged it. Somebody erased my log, not my ears.*

Then they sat for a while in the chapel light, listening to the amber pulses of the data spines, four hundred names glowing somewhere in the fiction below them, waiting.

It was almost dawn when the comm chimed — Sef, voice rough, sleepless, the kind of careful he got when something had been sitting on him all night.

"Astrid. You awake? There's — I need to tell you something. About Kestrel's Promise." A pause, and then, quieter, like a man stepping off a ledge: "I remember bodies."
