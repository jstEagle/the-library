## 17. What Odile Did

Astrid found the lie in the cargo manifest before they found it anywhere else.

That was the thing about Odile — she thought in manifests the way other people thought in sentences. Every deal she'd ever cut lived somewhere in the Marrow's ledgers, and she was too old-school to trust anything but her own hand-inked entries in the paper book she kept chained under the pilot's seat. Astrid had been transcribing those books into the shielded core for three days now, backfilling the crew's history into analog storage alongside four hundred dead miners, and it was Tam who had said, offhandedly, over cold reconstituted tea: *Funny, that. Odile logged a canister draw two nights ago. Heavy one. Didn't say nothing to me about it.*

Astrid had smiled at first. Thought maybe Odile was moving seed stock as ballast for the run Sef was plotting through the contested orbits. Then they'd pulled the entry itself, and the smile had died somewhere on the way to their mouth.

*Canister 4471 — drawn. Contents: archival core (decoy). Destination: broker relay, Meridian Yards.*

Odile had written *decoy*. In her own hand. In a ledger she thought no one else read.

Astrid sat with that word for a long time in the vault's reading alcove, the one with the porthole that framed the dark curve of the seed levels below, where the truth of Kestrel's Promise slept in cold iron at minus eighteen degrees. *Decoy.* Which meant there was an original. Which meant Odile had built a copy — or had Tam build one without telling them — and sold the copy to the corporation's broker. And the price of a decoy good enough to fool people who could scan for mnemonic contamination wasn't credits. It couldn't be credits. The corporation didn't pay for things anymore; they paid in permission. In windows. In *amnesty*.

Astrid pulled the rest of the manifest apart line by line, and there it was, buried in a fuel requisition dated six hours out: a burn plan for the Marrow that didn't match any route Sef had shown them. A route that started from the vault's shadow and ended past the outer relay buoys — a clean lane, a *bought* lane, the kind of corridor you only got when someone very powerful had been paid to look elsewhere for exactly ninety minutes.

And then, because Astrid had spent twelve years learning to read this vault the way monks once read scripture, they noticed what wasn't in the manifest at all. No line item for Odile's own weight. No berth allocation. No name.

The escape window Odile had bought was for three people.

The Marrow was rated for five.

* * *

They found her in the docking cradle, underneath the Marrow's belly, running a torque wrench over a landing strut like the strut had personally offended her. She worked the way she did everything — fast, economical, swearing at the machinery in a low continuous mutter that was less anger than conversation. She heard Astrid's boots on the grating and didn't look up.

"If you're here to tell me the portside strut's singing again, I know. She always sings when it's cold."

"Canister 4471," Astrid said.

The wrench stopped. Just for a second — a hitch in the rhythm, half a heartbeat — and then it resumed, steady as ever. But Astrid had grown up watching this woman's hands the way other children watched faces, and a hitch was a confession.

"Don't know what you're talking about."

"You wrote *decoy*, Odile. In your own book. You don't write *decoy* unless—" Astrid stopped. Started again, quieter. "You sold them something. What did you buy?"

Odile straightened up out of the strut well, slow, her spine popping in the cold, and finally looked at them. Her face did the thing it always did when she was deciding how much truth a moment could hold: nothing moved except her eyes, which went flat and calculating and then, briefly, tired. Very tired.

"A window," she said. "Ninety minutes of nobody looking. That's what it cost, and that's what I got."

"And the price?"

"The price was paid." She turned back to the strut. "Go help Tam seal the core housing. We lift in six hours."

"Odile." Astrid came down into the well beside her, close enough that they had to tilt their head back to keep her face in view. They were taller than her now — had been since they were sixteen, a fact she'd never once let them forget and never once seemed to mind. "There's no berth allocation for you. There's no weight entry. I've read your books since I was fourteen years old and you have never once failed to log yourself, because you say a captain who doesn't count herself is already lost. So either you've changed the way you keep your books—"

"I haven't."

"—or you're not coming."

The wrench clattered against the deck plating. Not thrown — dropped, which from Odile was worse. She braced both hands on the strut and stood there with her head bowed, and for a long moment the only sound was the vault breathing around them: coolant pumps, the tick of metal contracting in the deep cold, the vast patient silence of a building built to outlast empires.

"Ninety minutes," she said finally, to the strut, to the floor, to no one. "That's what a distraction buys. Ninety minutes where every gun, every scanner, every pair of corporate eyes in this system is pointed at me instead of at a little ship running dark past the buoys. You know what they offered me last week? Amnesty. New names. Debts gone — all of them, Astrid, every debt, including the ones I never told you about. All I had to do was hand you over." She laughed, short and ugly. "I told them I'd think about it. And while I was thinking, I realized thinking was a luxury. So I made a counteroffer instead."

"You volunteered."

"I *negotiated*," she snapped, and now she turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and forty years old with exhaustion. "There's a difference. Volunteers die for free. I got a ship's worth of clear sky for it. I got Tam and Sef out past the relays with the real core and a journalist relay that still owes me a favor from '09. I got four hundred names broadcast to every colony in the belt. That's not dying for free. That's the best trade I've made in my whole rotten life."

"You're staying behind so we can run."

"I'm staying behind so the *truth* runs." She jabbed a finger at the deck, at the vault beneath it, at the cold iron heart of the place. "You think I care whether I'm on that ship? I'm fifty-eight years old, I've got scar tissue where my left kidney used to be, and I owe money to people who use the word *restructure* the way surgeons use the word *amputate*. The universe has been trying to collect on me for decades. Fine. Let it collect. But it doesn't get to collect on *you*. Not you. Not after everything."

"After everything what?" Astrid's voice cracked on it, and they hated that, hated the child's sound coming out of their adult throat. "After everything *what*, Odile? Say it. You raised me. You fished me out of a hiring queue when I was nineteen and unqualified and you lied to the administrator to get me the post, and you never once told me why. Twelve years. You don't get to spend yourself like fuel and call it a trade. You don't get to make me the survivor *again*."

Something moved behind Odile's face — the flinch of a woman hit in a place she'd armored carefully. When she spoke, her voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"You think I don't know what it does to a person? Being the one who lives?" She looked away, toward the porthole, toward the dark. "I've buried two crews, Astrid. Two. I know exactly what it does. And I swore on the second set of graves that I would never, ever be the reason some kid had to carry that weight alone." Her jaw worked. "So yeah. This is me keeping the oath the only way left. Somebody has to stand in the light so the others can move in the dark. That's the job. That's always been the job. Captain's the one who stands in the light."

"No," Astrid said.

"Astrid—"

"*No.*" Louder than they meant, loud enough that it echoed off the hull plating and came back sounding like someone else entirely. "You want to talk about trades? Fine. Here's mine. The core needs a keeper. Not a courier — a *keeper*. Someone who knows the vault systems, someone who can open the legacy transmitters, someone whose hands are calibrated to these locks because they've been turning them for twelve years. That's me. You need me on that ship or the broadcast dies in the dark with everyone else. And if I'm going — if the plan only works with me alive and transmitting — then I am not leaving this vault while you stay behind to be shot at for my sake. I refuse. I will chain myself to the transmitter. I will vent the docking ring. I will do whatever I have to do, Odile, because you taught me the one lesson you probably never meant to teach: *nobody gets left to pay alone.*"

Silence. The long, ringing kind.

Odile looked at them for a long time — really looked, the way she rarely did, the way that made you feel inventoried, cataloged, known down to the serial numbers. And then, slowly, horribly, she began to laugh. Not the ugly laugh from before. Something rawer. She pressed the back of her hand against her mouth and laughed until it wasn't laughing anymore, until her shoulders shook, until she had to grip the landing strut to stay upright, and Astrid stood there in the cold blue light of the docking bay and understood that they were watching fifty-eight years of solitary command crack down the middle.

"You absolute—" She couldn't finish it. She wiped her face with her sleeve, roughly, like the tears were a mechanical fault. "I built a perfect plan. Ninety minutes, clean lane, everybody lives but me. One perfect selfish thing, and you found it in a *ledger*."

"You taught me to read the books."

"I should have taught you to cook."

"You tried. I burned water."

That startled a real laugh out of her, wet and broken, and then she was quiet again, and the quiet between them was different now — not a wall but a shared room. She reached out and took Astrid by the collar of their jacket, the same gesture she'd used since they were nineteen, the grip that meant *listen to me now.*

"Here's what I can't give you," she said. "I can't promise you'll live. I can't promise any of us will. The wipe team has a pulse weapon pointed at this rock and a broker who knows my face, and the only currency I have left is being predictable — being exactly the stubborn old wreck they expect me to be, right up until I'm not." Her grip tightened. "But I can promise you this: whatever happens in the next ninety minutes, nobody on this crew pays alone. Not you. Not me. Not Tam, not Sef. If I stay in the light, then you stay in the light too — the transmitter, the broadcast, all of it, side by side, even if we're a hundred kilometers apart. That's the new plan. That's the only plan I've got left. Can you live with that?"

Astrid thought about the four hundred names sleeping in the cold below them. About the administrator's sister, born in a tunnel, dead in nine seconds of falling. About a message encrypted in a seed manifest: *you're the backup.* About every version of loyalty they'd ever been handed — Odile's silence, the corporation's amnesty, the broker's fortune — and how each one had turned out to be a cage with better upholstery.

"Yes," they said. "But I'm changing one thing."

Odile's eyes narrowed. "Of course you are."

"When the pulse comes — when they hit us with everything — I'm broadcasting through it. Not around it. *Through* it. Tam says the shield rig costs me twelve years of stored memory, and I've decided that's cheap. Because you taught me something else too, Odile, even if you never said it out loud: the captain stands in the light. And right now, the light is a transmitter, and the captain is me."

For a moment Odile just stared. Then she let go of Astrid's collar, smoothed the wrinkled fabric flat with one rough palm — a small, absurd, maternal gesture that undid Astrid more thoroughly than any argument could have — and picked her wrench back up off the deck.

"Six hours to lift," she said, turning back to the strut, voice steadying into command the way a river steadies into its channel. "Tell Tam the shield rig becomes priority one. Tell Sef his lane just got shorter and uglier, and he's going to fly it anyway, because he's Sef. And tell him—" her voice caught, barely, a hairline fracture in the steel, "—tell him I said he flies like his mother taught him, and his mother was a menace, and I miss her."

Astrid nodded and turned to go.

"And Astrid." They stopped. Odile hadn't looked up; her voice came from inside the strut well, muffled, almost gentle. "The decoy core. If they scan it before the exchange, it buys us nothing. If they scan it *after* — after the broadcast lands, after the whole rotten story comes apart in their hands — then every minute they spend staring at a box of lies is a minute the truth travels free." She tapped the wrench twice against the hull, a small bright bell of a sound. "Let them have their prize. It's the only gift I've ever given a corporation that I won't regret."

Astrid walked back up the grating toward the vault's cold heart, where four hundred names waited in the dark and a transmitter older than the war waited above them, and behind them they heard Odile resume work on the strut — steady, rhythmic, unafraid, or performing unafraid so well that the difference had stopped mattering.

Six hours. Somewhere out in the black, a broker was admiring a box full of nothing, and a wipe team was loading a pulse weapon, and a journalist's relay was holding a fragment of truth like a lit match held out in wind.

Ninety minutes, Odile had promised. Ninety seconds might be all they got.

Astrid went to find Tam, and began to count.
