## 3. Records of Nothing

The archive room of the Meridian Colonial Administration smelled the way all official memory smelled: recycled air, ozone from the cooling stacks, and a faint chemical sweetness that Wendell had never been able to identify but had long ago stopped questioning. It was the smell of things being kept, and kept carefully, and he had always found it comforting.

He did not find it comforting now.

He sat at one of the public research terminals in the back corner, the one with the flickering display that nobody else used because it was slower than the others, and he had chosen it for exactly that reason. Slower meant older pathways. Slower meant less caching, less smoothing, fewer polite algorithms deciding what he wanted to see before he asked. He had spent thirty years as this colony's xenolinguist and twenty of them quietly learning where the soft places were in any information system—the seams where data had been handled by human hands rather than generated whole. You could tell a great deal about a record by how it had been touched.

Ilsa's phrase sat behind his eyes like a splinter. *Senneth varra ool.* He had said it aloud on the ridge without thinking, an old reflex, a greeting to no one, and the child had repeated it back to him with the flat, unselfconscious accuracy of a mirror. Not an approximation. Not a child's garbled version. Exact—down to the vowel length on the second syllable, down to the way *varra* should have carried a faint trill at its edge that most human mouths couldn't produce without years of practice.

Vessh. The word itself rose in him with the weight of something submerged for decades, surfacing with water streaming off it. The Vessh. The tall grey-grazed people of the red plain south of the settlement, the ones who had built towers like organ pipes and sang their names instead of writing them, the ones who—

Who did not exist.

That was what every archive said. That was what his own training materials said, back when he'd arrived as a young specialist with two degrees and a head full of dead Earth languages. There was no entry for the Vessh in the colonial bestiary. No entry in the first-contact protocols. No entry anywhere. Meridian had been catalogued as uninhabited at the time of settlement—a rare and valuable thing, an empty world—and the red plain had been zoned for agricultural expansion within the decade.

And yet Ilsa Voss, seven years old, daughter of hydroponics technicians, had spoken *senneth varra ool* as if she'd been saying it her whole life.

Wendell flexed his hands over the keyboard and began, methodically, the way he began everything: with the index.

* * *

The search took four hours, and he made himself do it properly, the way he would have demanded of any graduate student. Cross-referenced. Multi-lingual. Phonetic variants included. He searched for *Vessh*, for *vesh*, for *vess*, for the phonetic renderings in three transcription systems, for the old survey-team spellings that might have mangled the name through human ears—*Veshai*, *Ve'She*, *Fesj*. He searched the biological registries under plausible taxonomic descriptors: sapient, non-human, indigenous, pre-colonial. He searched the land-use records for the red plain itself, thinking that if the species had been scrubbed from the narrative records, perhaps something might survive in the boring bureaucratic sediment beneath—survey coordinates, soil classifications, easement disputes.

Nothing. Every query returned the same serene, absolute answer: no results.

It was the serenity that began to trouble him.

Wendell had spent his career in archives, both physical and digital, and he had developed what his late wife used to call his nose for rot. Real records were messy. They contradicted themselves. A colony's historical database thirty years deep should have had duplicate entries with conflicting dates, orphaned references pointing to files that had been merged or lost, survey notes misfiled under the wrong year, a hundred small scars left by ordinary human error. Messiness was the fingerprint of truth. A record that had simply accumulated, honestly, over decades, wore its history the way a working coat wore its stains.

Meridian's archive had no stains.

He ran a metadata audit on the historical section—not the contents, just the structure, the bones beneath the skin. It was the kind of request that would have required authorization from a department head in most administrations, but Meridian was small and understaffed and the archival system trusted its senior specialists, and Wendell Sandoval's credentials opened doors that younger researchers didn't know existed.

The audit crawled across the screen, line by line, and Wendell felt the temperature of the room change before he understood why.

Every file in the colonial history section—all eleven thousand, four hundred and six of them—carried the same creation timestamp. Not the same date. The same timestamp, to the second. 0347:22:09 UTC, standard colonial reckoning, a time in the small hours when no archivist worked, no administrator filed reports, nothing human happened at all. Eleven thousand files, spanning supposedly forty years of colonial life—settlement logs, harvest reports, school records, weather data, the collected bureaucratic heartbeat of a living community—and every single one of them had been born in the same silent minute.

Rewritten. All of it. Swept and re-laid like new pavement over old blood.

His hands were very still on the desk. He noticed that he had stopped breathing through his nose, some old habit of concentration, and made himself start again.

*All right,* he told himself, in the dry internal voice he used for panicking students. *All right. Timestamps can be corrupted. Migration errors happen. Systems get rebuilt; databases are consolidated; a catastrophic storage failure ten years ago could have forced a full reconstruction.* It was possible. He wrote down the alternative explanations, one by one, in the neat shorthand of his field notebooks, because writing things down was how he kept himself honest. Storage migration. Index rebuild. Deliberate standardization by a fastidious archivist—he had known one or two, gods rest them, who would happily re-stamp a continent for the sake of tidiness.

Possible. All of it possible. And none of it explaining why the rewrite had been so thorough, so total, so perfectly seamless that not one file, not one footnote, not one cross-reference had survived with its original birthmark intact.

He pulled up the oldest document in the system: the founding charter, signed by the first Administrator, dated before the colony's first sunrise. Its metadata said it had been created at 0347:22:09 like everything else. A document claiming to be sixty years old, wearing a birthday from last decade.

Scholarly curiosity, Wendell thought, is a beautiful thing right up until it turns into dread, and the transition is invisible from inside. You're following a thread. You're being rigorous. You're checking one more source, running one more query, and then you look up and realize your heart has been hammering for ten minutes and you cannot remember deciding to be afraid.

* * *

He went home that night and slept badly, which was normal, and dreamed of nothing, which was also normal, and woke before dawn with the phrase still lodged in him. *Senneth varra ool.* In the grey light of his kitchen he drank tea that had gone bitter and let himself say the next part out loud, alone, to the humming refrigerator:

"If the children remember the Vessh, then the Vessh were real."

It sounded absurd spoken into an empty room. But it was sound methodology, and he clung to it the way other men clung to railings. Memory did not arise from nothing. Language did not self-assemble in the dreams of children who had never been taught it. Either someone had taught them—and he would find the teacher, the smuggled recording, the grandparent's secret—or the knowledge came from somewhere deeper than teaching, somewhere the archives' pristine silence could not reach.

Either way, the archive was lying. And a lying archive meant a liar with administrative power, and that meant the lie had been policy.

The next morning he returned to the terminal and did the thing he had been avoiding, the way you avoid pressing on a bruise to confirm it's there. He looked up his own file.

Wendell Amadeus Sandoval. Specialist, Xenolinguistics, Colonial Survey Corps, attached to Meridian. Clearance level appropriate to his station. Service commendations: two. Performance reviews: annual, routine, uniformly adequate. He read his own recorded life with the detachment of a man reading a stranger's obituary—there was the arrival, thirty-one years ago, young and credentialed and full of the arrogance of the well-trained; there were the early assignments, the communication infrastructure audits, the translation-standard work for the beacon systems.

There was the gap.

Fourteen months. Between his third-year review and his fourth, between one dated entry and the next, there was a hole in his own history exactly fourteen months wide. No disciplinary note explained it. No leave record covered it. No medical entry, no transfer, no sabbatical. The file simply went dark for fourteen months and resumed as though nothing had happened—as though he had spent those months in suspended animation, or as though whoever maintained the file had decided those months were better off unwritten.

He stared at the dates until they blurred.

He tried to remember his own fourteenth month of that period. His fourth year on Meridian. What had he done? Where had he lived? He reached back and found—nothing. Not fog, not blur, but nothing, a smooth wall where recollection should have been, the mental equivalent of the archive's identical timestamps. He remembered his fifth year vividly. He remembered his third. Between them lay a corridor with no doors.

Thirty years he had walked past that wall and never once pressed his hand against it. Thirty years he had attributed the vagueness to middle age, to grief after Marta died, to the ordinary erosion of a life heavy with work. But now, sitting in the humming archive with his own service record open on the screen, he felt the wall for what it was: manufactured. Load-bearing. Installed.

Someone had edited him the way they had edited the archive. Thoroughly. Seamlessly. With love, almost—with the terrible care of people who wanted the patient to heal without scarring.

Wendell closed the file. He sat for a long moment in the corner of the archive room, an old man among shelves of immaculate lies, and felt the last comfortable assumption of his profession fall away beneath him. He had spent his life believing that records were the skeleton of truth—that whatever men said, the paperwork remembered. He had built his career on that faith. And here was the proof, stamped in identical timestamps and fourteen-month silences, that the paperwork could be taught to forget on command.

The Vessh had been erased. He had been erased. The children, somehow, had not been.

He thought of Ilsa's face on the ridge, solemn and certain, repeating a dead language like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing. Someone had missed the children. Or someone had decided the children didn't matter, that dream-talk was harmless noise, that no one would listen to seven small voices speaking a language that officially had never existed.

They had made one mistake, whoever they were. They had left the language alive in the only vessels too small to be worth censoring.

Wendell stood, gathered his notebook, and walked out of the archive with the measured pace of a man who knew he was now carrying something dangerous. He needed to hear the children again. All of them. Every phrase, every fragment, transcribed and cross-checked against the grammar he still carried in his hands like a scar.

Because if the dreams were what he feared they were—if they were not invention but inheritance—then somewhere under the red plain, or inside the beacon he had tended for thirty blind years, the Vessh had left something behind.

And it was starting to speak.
