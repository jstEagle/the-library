## 2. The Message That Wasn't Hers

Xanthe did not sleep that night.

She told herself it was the storm — the katabatic wind off the ice cap had been screaming against the vault's northern face for six hours, a sound like a living thing dragging its nails across the hull. She told herself it was the tea she'd brewed too strong out of habit, because for eleven years strong tea had been her companion through long watches and there was no one to tell her the habit no longer served anyone. She told herself many things, lying in her narrow bunk beneath the shelf of seed catalogs she'd brought from Earth and never read twice, but none of them explained why her hands would not stop trembling.

Nine seconds. Gravity had failed for nine seconds across the entire system, and in those nine seconds something had woken up beneath her feet.

At 0300 station time she gave up on sleep, dressed in layers, and took the maintenance lift down into the vault's deepest level — deeper than the seed stacks, deeper than the cryo-galleries where four hundred thousand cultivars slept in their silver sleeves at minus eighteen Celsius. Down past all of that, down the spiral stair that only appeared on schematics if you already knew to look for it, to the chamber Dr. Ilse Varga had called, in her dry and unamused way, *the parlor*.

The beacon filled the room like a sleeping animal.

It was older than the vault itself, older than the Concord's charter, older than any of the tidy bureaucratic fiction that had placed a seed repository on Svalbard-9 in the first place. Officially — and Xanthe had read the official record until she could recite it — the beacon was decommissioned. A relic of the survey era, its transmitter core stripped, its antenna array a dead lattice against the polar sky. Her job description mentioned it exactly once: *Keeper will conduct quarterly visual inspection of legacy hardware.* For eleven years she had climbed down here every ninety days with a flashlight and a clipboard, had walked around the great dark ring of it, had noted *no anomalies*, and had gone back up to her seeds and her weather logs and her silence.

Tonight the beacon was not dark.

A single filament of light ran along its inner circumference, pale gold, pulsing at roughly the rhythm of a resting heartbeat. Frost had sublimated away from the ring in a perfect circle, as though the machine were breathing warmth again after years of holding its breath. And on the small maintenance terminal bolted to the wall beside it — a terminal Xanthe had personally verified was unpowered, three months ago, with her own hand on the switch — a line of text glowed:

**INCOMING — ADDRESSED: THE KEEPER**

She stood in the doorway for a long time, listening to the wind and to her own pulse, which had picked up the beacon's rhythm without asking her permission.

"Nobody's addressed me anything in eleven years," she said aloud, because the vault was the kind of place where you talked to yourself or you stopped talking entirely, and she had chosen talking early on. "Nobody knows I'm here."

The text did not change. Machines are patient in a way people never manage to be.

She crossed the room. Her boots sounded very loud on the deck plating. Up close, the beacon smelled faintly of ozone and, absurdly, of rosemary — some old coolant compound Varga must have favored, a scent memory from before Xanthe's time. She sat down at the terminal the way you sit down at the bedside of someone who has been ill a long while, and she looked at the prompt, and she thought about all the reasons not to touch it.

The Concord patrol that passed through this sector twice a year logged beacon status automatically. If the beacon was transmitting, they would know. If they knew, they would come. If they came—

If they came, then eleven years of being nobody would end, and Xanthe Winterbourne had built her entire life on the load-bearing wall of being nobody.

Her hand hovered over the key. Below the prompt, a second line had appeared, smaller, almost gentle:

**DEAD-MAN'S SWITCH — ORIGIN: I. VARGA — RELEASED ON SYSTEM EVENT**

Xanthe closed her eyes. Ilse Varga. The name arrived in her mind with its whole history attached, the way names do when you've lived alone with them long enough. Her predecessor. Keeper of Svalbard-9 for nineteen years before her. Dead nine years now — officially of cardiac arrest, alone in the same quarters Xanthe occupied now, found two days after the fact by a supply crew who had seemed more annoyed than grieved. Xanthe had inherited her office, her quarters, her hand-labeled jars of dried herbs, and a single piece of advice scrawled inside the cover of the master logbook: *Trust the machines more than the men.*

She had always assumed it was the paranoia of an old woman who'd been isolated too long.

She pressed the key.

* * *

The message was audio. That surprised her; she'd expected text, coordinates, something clinical. Instead the terminal's small speaker crackled and produced a voice — low, accented, tired in the way of someone conserving energy for things that mattered. Xanthe knew the voice. Every keeper knew the voice, because Varga had recorded the orientation modules, and Xanthe had listened to them so many times during her first disorienting year that Varga's cadence had become a kind of company.

But this was not the orientation voice. This was Varga frightened, and Varga frightened sounded like Varga angry with the fear sanded off.

"If you're hearing this, then the event happened, and I'm past caring what they do to my reputation." A pause, a breath. "I don't know how long I have. So listen once, and listen properly."

Xanthe pulled her chair closer. The wind screamed above. The gold light pulsed.

"The gravity stutter is the key. It happens on a cycle — nine seconds, every eleven years, a resonance in the system's mass distribution that nobody publishes because it makes the orbital insurers nervous. I found it in the survey archives my third year here. The beacon doesn't run on our power grid. It runs on the stutter. That's why they left it. That's why they pretend it's dead. It's a lock, and the stutter is the clock that turns it."

A sound on the recording — footsteps? A door? Varga's voice dropped lower, faster.

"Cassini Station. You'll have heard the official account by now, whatever year this reaches. Belt saboteurs, a stolen freighter, three thousand dead, the whole liturgy. I helped write parts of that account. That's why I'm telling you the rest. There is flight recorder data — real data, not the sanitized release — that shows the attack vector came from a Concord corvette running dark under a Belt transponder. The Cassini lie isn't that the station died. The lie is who killed it."

Xanthe's breath fogged in the cold air of the parlor. She realized she had both hands pressed flat against the terminal's housing, as if steadying herself against a swell.

"I couldn't move it myself. They watch me — they've watched me since I filed my first query, and I was naive enough to file it through official channels. So I broke it apart. Coordinates, keys, fragments, distributed to keepers and archivists across the system, each of us holding a piece none of us can read alone. If you're the keeper of Svalbard-9, you hold the primary fragment and the timing key. Nineteen days from release, the fragment self-erases. That's not cruelty, that's design — a truth with an expiration date can't be stockpiled by governments. It has to be *carried*."

The recording crackled. When Varga spoke again, her voice had changed — quieter, and underneath the weariness something that Xanthe recognized, because she heard it in her own voice sometimes on the bad nights. Regret with nowhere to put itself.

"I'm sorry to hand you this. Whoever you are, you wanted to be forgotten — that's why they give this post to people like us. But forgotten people are the only ones left who can carry something like this. Remember that. They made us invisible because we were inconvenient, and invisibility cuts both ways."

A long pause. Then, clipped, urgent, as if she could hear her time running out even then:

"Coordinates follow. Relay node at Ceres — the neutral array, the old diplomatic band, pre-war protocols. Broadcast window closes on day nineteen. And the phrase you'll need to open the Ceres handshake — write this down, don't trust your memory —"

The speaker hissed. Static bloomed, swallowed the voice mid-word, and for three full seconds the parlor held nothing but white noise and the pulse of the gold ring. Then the static cut, cleanly, deliberately, and the terminal displayed a single line:

**FRAGMENT 1 OF 4 — PARTIAL — INTEGRITY 61%**

Below it, in plain characters, the wreckage of the message's ending: a string of coordinates Xanthe didn't recognize, a timestamp counting down — **18 DAYS, 21 HOURS, 44 MINUTES** — and one word, repeated twice, as if the corruption had doubled it out of emphasis:

**CASSINI. CASSINI.**

Then the screen went dark, and the gold ring dimmed to a faint ember, and Xanthe sat alone in the cold with the shape of a sentence she hadn't fully heard pressing against the inside of her skull.

*The lie is who killed it.*

Three thousand people. She remembered the Cassini attack — everyone remembered it; it was the wound the whole war bled from. She'd been twenty-six, still wearing a Concord navigation officer's uniform in those days, still believing in the neat moral architecture of her government. She remembered the footage. The funerals. The speech where First Consul Adeyemi had sworn that the Belt would answer for it, and the answering, and the eleven years of answering since.

And now a dead woman in her own basement was telling her it had been staged. Telling her with flight recorder data. Telling her that Xanthe herself was holding a piece of the proof, and that the proof would rot in nineteen days unless she carried it somewhere.

"No," Xanthe said to the dark room. It came out steadier than she felt. "No, absolutely not. I tend seeds. That's the job. Seeds don't get people killed."

The beacon pulsed its ember-light, indifferent. Above her, the wind began, very slowly, to die.

* * *

She spent the morning doing the most normal things she could construct. She checked the potato trials in Gallery Four. She logged the barometric pressure as the storm collapsed. She recalibrated a humidity sensor that didn't need it, twice. At noon she ate reconstituted stew at her workstation with the master logbook open in front of her, staring at Varga's handwriting — *trust the machines more than the men* — and trying to build a version of events in which last night had been a malfunction, a ghost in forty-year-old wiring, a dream she'd had standing up.

The problem was the timestamp. Malfunctions don't count down. Malfunctions don't say *Cassini* twice.

At 1406, the proximity alert chimed.

Xanthe went very still, spoon halfway to her mouth. The alert was not rare — supply drones, ice-haulers passing far out, the occasional meteor — but the classification code attached to this one was one she had never seen fire in eleven years: **ORBITAL APPROACH — POWERED — CONCORD REGISTRY**.

She pulled up the feed. A ship hung sixty kilometers off the vault's position, descending in a careful, deliberate spiral. Patrol-class, maybe forty meters, gunmetal grey with the Concord's blue-and-white roundel on the flank. Not one of the twice-yearly automated supply runs. Not a survey drone. A crewed vessel, running a standard approach profile, and — her stomach tightened as she read the telemetry — broadcasting a polite, formal, utterly unprecedented hail on the vault's administrative channel.

**CONCORD PATROL VESSEL STEADFAST — REQUESTING DOCK CLEARANCE AND INSPECTION ACCESS PER CHARTER ARTICLE 12. REPEAT: INSPECTION ACCESS.**

Inspection. In eleven years, no one had inspected anything. The Concord had forgotten Svalbard-9 so thoroughly that her annual requisition forms were processed by an algorithm, and she suspected half the time the algorithm approved them by accident. Charter Article 12 was a wartime provision — she remembered it from her service days, remembered it the way you remember the teeth of a trap you once escaped. It allowed military inspection of any charter facility *upon indication of anomalous activity*.

Anomalous activity. Nine seconds of missing gravity, and a beacon that had been dead for decades suddenly breathing gold light into the polar dark. Someone had noticed. Someone had always been noticing, maybe — maybe Varga's watchers had never stopped watching, had simply waited for the lock to turn.

Xanthe set down her spoon. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had gone calm — the specific, terrible calm of a body remembering how to be afraid in an organized way. She stood, straightened her jacket, and looked at herself in the polished steel of the galley cabinet: a middle-aged woman with frost-chapped cheeks and seed dust on her sleeve. Nobody. Invisible. Exactly what they'd made her, and exactly what Varga had said would save her.

*Invisibility cuts both ways.*

She opened the channel. "Svalbard-9 to Steadfast. This is Keeper Winterbourne. You're cleared for soft-dock at the north gantry. Please advise party size and purpose." Her voice came out flat, bored, faintly irritated — the voice of a woman interrupted during inventory season. She was impressed with herself. She was also, underneath the performance, aware of a cold fact settling into place like a seed into soil:

Whatever happened in the next hour, the woman who had existed before today was over.

"Acknowledged, Keeper," the channel returned, crisp and courteous. "Party of two. We have some questions about your beacon."

They landed twelve minutes later. Two officers in Concord grey descended the gantry stairs into the entry hall where Xanthe waited with tea laid out — three cups, a hostess's instinct deployed as camouflage. The senior officer was a captain, silver at the temples, with a smile calibrated to reassure and eyes that did nothing of the kind. He introduced himself as Captain Renn Hale, Concord Diplomatic Corps, which was a strange thing for a patrol ship's boarding officer to be, and he shook her hand with warmth that arrived precisely on schedule and departed the same way.

"Lovely facility," Hale said, accepting the tea. His gaze moved over the entry hall — the logbooks, the sealed doors to the galleries, the stairwell door at the far end marked MAINTENANCE — DEEP LEVELS in faded paint. It rested on that door perhaps half a second longer than it needed to. "Must be quiet work. Lonely."

"It suits me," Xanthe said.

"We detected a power signature from your sublevel during the gravity event. Transient. Very brief." He smiled again, sipped his tea. "Old equipment does strange things when the spacetime gets shaken, doesn't it?"

"Everything down there is decommissioned," Xanthe said, and discovered, with a small internal shock, that the lie came out smooth and warm and completely steady. "Half of it predates my posting. I inspect it quarterly and mostly I find rust."

Hale looked at her for a moment. Somewhere behind his courtesy, something was weighing her, sorting her, filing her. Then he smiled a third time and said, lightly, "Of course. Rust. Well — we'll just have a look around, verify your logs, and be out of your hair before supper."

He was already moving toward the maintenance stairwell as he said it, and behind him his junior officer had drifted, casually, terribly, toward the security panel that governed the gallery locks — and Xanthe understood, with the clarity of a woman stepping off a cliff, that the inspection had begun the moment they'd docked, and that everything she loved about her life was now something she would have to defend room by room.

Down in the parlor, thirty meters below her boots, a dead woman's message sat glowing in the dark, counting down.

Eighteen days.
