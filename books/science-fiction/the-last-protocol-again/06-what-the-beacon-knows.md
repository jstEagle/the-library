## 6. What the Beacon Knows

The decryption took three days.

Not because the cipher was difficult — though it was, a lattice of rotating keys that fought them like a living thing — but because neither of them could bear to rush it. There was something almost sacred in the slowness of it, in watching Dr. Ilse Varga's voice assemble itself line by line out of eleven years of silence, like a woman climbing out of her own grave one rung at a time.

Xanthe worked in the beacon chamber because she couldn't work anywhere else. The room had been hers for a decade: the hum of the antenna array overhead, the amber glow of the status board, the smell of ozone and old insulation that she had long ago stopped noticing and now noticed constantly, as if hearing her own heartbeat for the first time. Odile sat across from her at the maintenance console, her arm still bound in the improvised sling Xanthe had fashioned from thermal blanket material, her fingers moving over the interface with the restless precision of someone who could not stand to be idle even wounded.

They barely spoke. When they did, it was in the shorthand of two people sharing a task too large for small talk.

"Layer nine," Odile said.

"Running."

The console chewed on it. Outside the reinforced window, the Svalbard-9 night rolled past in its slow way — the glacier blue-black under starlight, the aurora breathing green above the ridge line, indifferent and ancient. Somewhere under all that ice, four hundred thousand seeds slept in their nitrogen dark, waiting for a spring that might never come. Xanthe found herself thinking about that a great deal lately. About things kept safe for futures they would never see.

"Xanthe." Odile's voice was strange. "Look at layer nine."

The progress bar had stopped. Not stalled — *stopped*, cleanly, and where the garbled static of the previous eight layers had been, there was text. Clean, deliberate, addressed.

**TO THE KEEPER OF SVALBARD-9. IF YOU ARE READING THIS, THE BEACON WOKE WHEN IT WAS SUPPOSED TO. I AM SORRY FOR WHAT I AM ABOUT TO GIVE YOU.**

Xanthe's hands were cold. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

"That's her," she said. "That's Varga's voice."

"You knew her?"

"I knew *of* her. She left before I arrived. Eleven years ago." Xanthe swallowed. "They told me she'd been reassigned. There was a file. It was very short."

Odile said nothing to that, but her jaw tightened in a way Xanthe was beginning to recognize — the pilot's silent ledger of grievances against Concord bureaucracy, an account that apparently never closed.

The message continued to unfold beneath their hands.

*My name is Ilse Varga. I served this vault for six years, and before that I served the Cassini Station science directorate for nine. On 14 March, standard reckoning, Cassini Station was destroyed with the loss of two thousand three hundred and eleven lives. You know this event by another name. Everyone does. They call it the opening shot of the war.*

*I am going to tell you what actually happened, and then I am going to give you the proof, and then I am going to ask you to do the hardest thing anyone has ever asked of you, which is to carry a truth through a war until it finds daylight.*

*Here is the truth: there were no Belt saboteurs aboard Cassini Station.*

Xanthe heard herself breathe. Heard Odile stop breathing altogether.

*The attack signature that Concord cited — the Belt-pattern fusion cutter marks on the docking ring, the intercepted "Belt fleet chatter," the martyr video from a Collective cell that claimed credit — all of it was manufactured. I know because I helped manufacture part of it. I was told I was running a counterintelligence simulation. A war game. The scenario files were labeled EXERCISE CASSANDRA. By the time I understood what the exercise had actually produced, the station was already gone, and the men who had used my work were already giving speeches about retaliation.*

*The flight recorder from the Concord patrol vessel Meridian's Pride survived the engagement perimeter. It recorded the launch orders. It recorded the weapons discharge. It recorded the confirmation codes — Concord command authentication, third-tier clearance or above. I extracted it during the evacuation of the science annex. I have carried it ever since. It has cost me everything I have, including, I expect, my life, because if you are reading this, then I am dead, and the people who killed me will be looking for what I left behind.*

*Do not trust them. Do not negotiate with them. Do not believe anyone who tells you the truth can wait for a better moment. There is no better moment. There is only the relay at Ceres and the nineteen days I am giving you.*

Below that, the file tree opened like a flower made of knives: raw telemetry, spectral analysis of the weapon signatures, the authentication code logs, forty-one hours of bridge audio from the Meridian's Pride, and a signed affidavit from Varga herself, notarized under neutral station law, timestamped nine days before her official death date.

Xanthe realized she had stood up without deciding to. Her legs had simply removed her from the chair.

"Play the audio," Odile said. Her voice came out flat and careful, the voice of a woman handling ordnance. "The bridge audio. I want to hear it."

"Odile—"

"*Play it.*"

Xanthe played it.

* * *

Forty-one hours compressed into the moments that mattered. The hiss of a bridge atmosphere recycler. A captain's voice — young, uncertain, reading from something. *"Confirming launch authorization... authentication code Kestrel-Kestrel-Nine-Actual..."* A pause. Another voice, older, calmer, the kind of calm that comes from having done terrible arithmetic many times before: *"Weapons free. Cut the ring first. Make it look like boarding damage."*

Make it look like boarding damage.

Then the sound itself — not a sound really, more a pressure, a distortion in the recording medium as the fusion cutters bit into Cassini's docking ring, and beneath it, faintly, the open comm channel from the station: voices, ordinary voices, someone asking about a shift rotation, someone else laughing, and then the laughter becoming something else entirely, and then nothing, and then nothing, and then nothing.

Xanthe killed the playback. The silence afterward had texture. It pressed against her eardrums like deep water.

She turned to look at Odile and understood, in a single sickening drop of the stomach, why the pilot had gone so still. Why her good hand was white-knuckled on the console's edge. Why she was staring at the frozen waveform on the screen as if it were a face.

"Cassini," Xanthe said quietly. "You lost someone."

"My brother." Odile's voice was a ruin pretending to be steady. "Rafael. He was a hydroponics tech, second shift. He used to send me these stupid messages about his tomato plants. He named them. He *named individual tomato plants*, Xanthe, he had one called Admiral Hargreave because it grew crooked and demanded attention—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, smaller. "They said Belt saboteurs did it. I believed it. I *flew* for them for two years believing it. Every sortie, every convoy run, I was flying revenge for my brother against people who never touched him."

"The people who killed him were wearing your side's colors."

"The people who killed him were sitting behind desks." Odile laughed, and it was the worst sound Xanthe had ever heard, a laugh with no floor under it. "Two thousand three hundred and eleven. That's not a number. That's Rafael and two thousand three hundred and ten Rafaels."

Xanthe didn't touch her. She wanted to — some instinct in her reached toward the other woman the way a plant reaches toward light — but she understood instinctively that contact right now would shatter something that needed to stay whole. Instead she sat back down, and pulled up the final segment of Varga's message, and read it aloud, because some words needed air to become real.

*"The data package is keyed to self-erase. Nineteen days from activation of the beacon — from the day gravity stuttered, if my timing holds. After that, every copy in existence burns, including the originals I hid, and the truth dies with me. It must reach the neutral relay at Ceres and be broadcast on the open diplomatic band, unedited, witnessed by all parties. Ceres is the only place Concord cannot suppress it and the Belt cannot spin it. Nineteen days. Ceres. Unedited. If you are tempted to hand it to any government — either government — understand that both have had this opportunity and both chose the war instead."*

Xanthe lowered the screen. The beacon chamber hummed around them, patient as a held breath.

"Nineteen days started three days ago," she said. "Sixteen left."

"I can make Ceres in twelve, thirteen if the lanes are dirty." Odile wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, as though grief were a smudge that could be cleared away before the next preflight check. "Assuming my ship flies, which it currently doesn't, assuming we get fuel, which we don't have, and assuming Concord doesn't find us, which they will, because that patrol ship that came asking questions wasn't asking casually."

"They'll come back."

"They'll come back with a boarding party." Odile looked at her then, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear, and Xanthe thought: *this is what a person looks like when grief stops being a weight and becomes an engine.* "So here's the question, Keeper. That message says the truth dies with whoever carries it. Varga's dead. Whoever she sent fragments to besides us — I'd bet my ship they're dead too. This isn't a delivery job. It's a relay race where they shoot the runners." She tapped the console, gently, on the frozen image of the affidavit. "You've spent eleven years being invisible. You can walk away. Seal the vault, wipe the logs, tell yourself the seeds matter more than the dead. Nobody would blame you. *I'd* almost believe you."

"And if I don't walk away?"

"Then we fix my ship, and we fly to Ceres, and we spend whatever it costs." Odile shrugged, and the shrug contained multitudes: her brother, her squadron, her defection, the smoking crater her life had become since she'd fallen out of the sky onto Xanthe's glacier. "I'm asking because once we start, there's no version of this where we stop. You should know that going in. I've seen what happens to people who carry things Concord wants buried. It doesn't end with a tribunal and a reprimand."

Xanthe looked at the window. At the glacier. At the dark shape of the seed vault's entrance, half-buried in drift, the door she had sealed and unsealed ten thousand mornings without ceremony. She thought about the tribunal that had ended her own life eleven years ago — the navigation disaster she hadn't caused, the report she hadn't written, the verdict handed down by men who needed a name to hang the catastrophe on and chose hers because she was junior, and quiet, and had no one to object. She remembered standing in that cold hearing room thinking: *someone knows the truth. Someone has the data. Someone could say one sentence and save me.* And no one had. No one ever did.

Someone had the data now.

"I was scapegoated once," Xanthe said. "For something I didn't do. And the whole time, I kept thinking — somewhere there's a person who could fix this with the truth, and they're choosing to be comfortable instead." She turned back to the console, to Varga's affidavit, to the forty-one hours of dead voices. "I swore I would never be that person. I just never expected the universe to hold me to it."

Odile studied her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way pilots nodded — a whole contract signed in a centimeter of motion.

"Sixteen days," she said. "Let's go wake up your patient."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night, and it was only when the first grey light crept across the glacier that Xanthe understood, fully and bodily, what they had taken into their hands.

It happened in the galley, of all places. She'd gone to make tea — an absurd domestic reflex, her body insisting on rituals while her mind reeled — and she caught sight of her reflection in the darkened viewport: a thin woman in a worn thermal coat, holding a chipped mug, standing in a kitchen at the top of the world. Ordinary. Forgettable. The kind of person wars never bothered to notice.

And in the server room thirty meters away, humming on encrypted storage, sat the detonation of everything. Two fleets. Two governments. Billions of people who had organized their fear, their rationing, their mourning, their hatred around a single story — *they started it* — and the proof that the story was a lie built by their own side. Not peace in a file. A bomb in a file. The kind of bomb that didn't kill anyone but made every death already committed suddenly meaningless, and every future death suddenly unforgivable.

Her hands shook so badly the tea slopped over her knuckles, scalding, and she welcomed the pain because it was simple.

Vertigo. That was the word. She was standing on the edge of something enormous, and the ground was tilting, and there was no railing. Varga had known. *I am sorry for what I am about to give you.* Not sorry for the danger — sorry for the *weight*. Sorry for making one solitary keeper into the load-bearing wall of history.

Behind her, boots on the deck plating. Odile appeared in the galley doorway, hair tied back, sling adjusted, already halfway into her workday as if the world hadn't ended twice since dinner.

"Ship's worse than I thought," she said. "The landing burned out the port thruster gimbal. I can rebuild it, but I need parts, and the parts I need are—" she paused, and something in her expression shifted, "—Concord-standard. Military surplus. Which means our friend Captain What's-His-Name from the patrol ship will know exactly what a purchase order like that means, if it goes anywhere near a normal channel."

"So we don't use a normal channel."

"There is no normal channel out here. That's rather the point of you." Odile leaned against the doorframe. "Which brings us to the actual problem. Sixteen days. Broken ship. No fuel. One path to Ceres, and it runs straight through occupied space. And somewhere behind us, Concord intelligence is doing exactly what we're doing — reading the same timeline, counting the same days, and wondering how fast they can get a ship back to this rock." She smiled, and it wasn't a friendly smile; it was the smile of someone sharpening something. "Any ideas, Keeper?"

Xanthe set down the mug. Through the viewport, the sun was coming up over the ridge line, gilding the glacier, lighting the beacon array on its tower — her beacon, her silent, faithful, terrible beacon, which had woken after eleven years to hand her a dead woman's burden.

"One," she said. "We're going to need to lie to everyone, move fast, and probably fly through a blockade. So the first thing we do is figure out how much of your ship I can help you fix — because I've spent eleven years keeping machines alive alone, and I'm done waiting to die quietly."

Odile's eyebrows went up. Then, slowly, genuinely, she grinned.

"Attagirl," she said. "Welcome to the war."

Outside, far across the ice, a sensor pinged — brief, distant, easily mistaken for noise. Neither of them saw it. But it was a ship, and it was coming, and it was flying Concord colors.
