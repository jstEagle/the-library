## 9. The Long Dark Between

The Perihelion was not a beautiful ship. Odile Ferreira had flown beautiful ships — Belt-built corvettes with hulls like polished obsidian and engines that purred instead of coughed — and she had loved every one of them the way you love things that might get you killed. The Perihelion was different. The Perihelion was a freight hauler someone had crashed, patched with scavenged plating, crashed again, and then abandoned in a Svalbard-9 maintenance cradle for so long that lichen had colonized the port airlock seals. Xanthe had spent three days scraping it off with a thermal knife and calling the work "gardening."

"She's not a garden," Odile had told her. "She's a crime scene with an engine."

Now, four days out from the vault and eleven days from Ceres, Odile sat in the cockpit with her boots on the console and watched the fuel gauge lie to her. The gauge said sixty-two percent. The gauge was an optimist. She'd done the math twice on paper, the old way, because she didn't trust the Perihelion's flight computer any further than she could throw the Perihelion, and the math said fifty-four, maybe fifty-five if she nursed the burn. Which meant one unplanned maneuver would leave them drifting in the long dark between Jupiter and Ceres, a coffin with a nice view.

Behind her, Xanthe was inventorying their food stores out loud, because apparently that was something she did now. "Eleven ration bars, two of them the kind that taste like drywall. Fourteen liters of water if we recycle efficiently. One hundred and six seed packets, which I know aren't food, but I couldn't leave them."

"You brought seeds."

"I brought *seeds*," Xanthe confirmed, with the serene defiance of a woman who had lived alone for eleven years and had forgotten that other people's opinions existed.

Odile turned in the copilot's chair. Xanthe stood in the cockpit doorway holding a crate against her hip, her pale hair escaping its braid, her face still carrying that look it had worn since they'd burned away from Svalbard-9 — the look of someone who had left a grave and a nursery in the same breath and wasn't sure yet which one she missed more.

"Seeds don't fight gravity," Odile said. "Seeds don't dodge Concord patrols. Seeds are dead weight."

"Everything is dead weight until it isn't." Xanthe set the crate down with a soft click. "That's practically the definition of a seed."

Odile opened her mouth to argue, found she didn't have an argument, and hated it. This was the problem with Xanthe Winterbourne. Eleven years alone had not made her soft. It had made her *precise*. She said things like a scalpel says things, quietly, and then you bled out an hour later wondering where the cut came from.

"Fine," Odile said. "Keep your seeds. But if we're down to eating them, I get the ones that grow into something interesting."

* * *

The tracker was Xanthe's idea to find, which stung, because finding things that wanted to kill you was supposed to be Odile's department.

They were two days out when Xanthe came into the cockpit holding the ship's diagnostic slate like it had bitten her. "There's a power draw. Small. Point-zero-three percent. It's been there since launch."

"Background loss. Every hull leaks a little."

"Every hull doesn't leak *rhythmically*." She set the slate down and brought up the draw pattern. Odile looked at it and her stomach dropped through the deck plating. The curve pulsed. Regular intervals. Like a heartbeat. Like a beacon.

"Son of a—" She was out of the chair before the curse finished, pulling the access panel behind the cockpit bulkhead. And there it was, zip-tied to a coolant line where no sane inspector would ever look: a black wafer the size of her thumb, warm, alive, transmitting on a burst frequency toward the inner system.

Hale. That polite, patient bastard with his diplomatic credentials and his questions that weren't questions. He'd stood in the vault's entry hall with his hands folded and his voice soft as snowfall, and he'd planted this thing while Xanthe was showing him seed catalogs.

Odile ripped it free and held it up. "We need to kill it."

"If we kill it, he knows we found it," Xanthe said. "If we don't, he knows where we are."

"Those are the same problem."

"They're not." Xanthe took the wafer from her, turned it over in her fingers. Her hands, Odile had noticed, were always steady. It was unnerving. "He flagged us, didn't he? Terrorists. That's what the traffic chatter said this morning."

"Concord warrants list us as armed extremists in possession of stolen military intelligence. Which, technically—" Odile shrugged. "Half true. The intelligence is stolen. The arms are aspirational."

"Then he expects us to run. A tracker on a runner tells him everything." Xanthe's eyes had gone distant, the way they did when she was thinking in systems — weather patterns, germination cycles, the slow arithmetic of things that grow. "So we don't run. We make the tracker tell him a story."

They spent six hours on it, and it was the first time Odile thought of Xanthe as a pilot, even though the woman had never flown anything bigger than a surface skimmer. They couldn't spoof the wafer — it was Concord military hardware, hardened against exactly that — but they could *carry* it. Odile stripped a maintenance drone of its thruster pack, strapped the tracker to it, and programmed a flight plan: a long, panicked-looking burn toward the Jovian Trojans, the kind of trajectory a guilty fugitive would take if she were stupid. The drone lit its engines and limped away into the dark, faithfully reporting the flight of two terrorists who were no longer aboard.

Xanthe watched it go on the external feed until it was a spark among sparks. "Do you think he'll buy it?"

"Hale's smart. Smart people buy good lies faster than stupid ones, because they enjoy believing they saw through the obvious one." Odile settled back into the chair. "We've got maybe four days before the drone runs out of fuel and the story ends. Then we're ghosts."

"Is that better or worse than being terrorists?"

"Being a ghost is better." Odile pulled up the burn schedule. "Being a ghost means nobody's shooting at you. Being a *known* ghost, though—" she tapped the warrant flag still blinking on the traffic feed, their faces rendered in grainy Concord surveillance stills, hers from a cockpit camera five years old, Xanthe's from the vault's own door cam, looking startled and guilty in the way only an innocent person can — "that's the worst of both worlds. Everyone's looking, and nobody knows what they're looking at."

Xanthe studied her own image for a long moment. "I look like my mother in that photo."

"You look like a woman who's about to be shot."

"I look like my mother," Xanthe said again, quietly, and Odile let it go. Everyone had a face they didn't want to see in a warrant. Hers was just older.

* * *

The bickering started on day six, over water.

Specifically, over the recycling filter, which Odile had been running at maximum efficiency, which meant the water came out with a faint taste of coolant, which Xanthe declared "undrinkable," which Odile took as a personal insult to her engineering.

"It's *potable*," Odile said. "Potable is a chemical standard, not a flavor profile."

"I've drunk snowmelt for eleven years. I know what water is supposed to taste like. This tastes like a machine died in it."

"A machine *did* die in it. A machine that's keeping you alive. Show some respect."

"You want me to respect the water."

"I want you to respect the *machine that makes the water*, yes, because if you keep complaining about it I'm going to make you clean the filter, and the filter is full of things I will not describe while you're eating."

Xanthe set down her ration bar — the drywall one — and looked at Odile with an expression of profound, weary patience. "You argue like the tribunal did."

The cockpit went very quiet. The recyclers hummed. Somewhere in the hold, a seed crate settled with a soft wooden knock.

"I argue like someone who's right," Odile said finally, but her voice had lost its armor, and they both heard it.

"I didn't say it was a bad thing." Xanthe turned her ration bar over in her hands without eating it. "The tribunal argued like they'd already decided. You argue like you're still deciding. It's different. I've had eleven years to know the difference."

Odile didn't know what to do with that. She'd spent five years in the Belt fleet learning that silence was safety, that feelings were ballast you jettisoned before a burn, and here was this seed-keeper dismantling her in a cockpit over *water*. Cassio — her brother — used to argue like that. Like the argument was a gift, like the point was never winning but the two of you ending up somewhere new together. Cassio, who had died at Cassini Station because some Concord hardliner needed an atrocity, and whose face Odile still saw every time she closed her eyes longer than a blink.

"Your brother," Xanthe said, not quite a question.

"Cassio." Odile looked at the stars rather than at her. "He argued like you. Drove me insane."

"I would have liked him."

"You'd have hated him. Everyone did, for about a week. Then you'd have followed him anywhere." Her throat tightened around the words, and she let the silence do the rest.

Xanthe reached over and, without ceremony, took the water bulb out of Odile's hand, drank from it, and made a face. "That's terrible."

"It's *potable*."

"It's terrible," Xanthe said, "and I'm going to clean the filter tomorrow anyway," and something in Odile's chest — something that had been clenched since Cassini, since the crash, since the vault, since the war — loosened by a single degree.

* * *

On day eight, the traffic chatter caught up with them again, and the story got worse.

The drone had run out of fuel somewhere past the Trojans, and Hale — because it was Hale, it had to be Hale, the man thought in trajectories the way Odile did — had apparently done his own math. The warrants updated. The bounty went up. And a new flag appeared on the Concord fleet net, encrypted but leaking at the edges the way military secrets always leak, the way a hull leaks a little: *subjects believed to be attempting contact with Belt Collective assets. Intercept on sight. Lethal force authorized.*

"They're not even pretending we'll get a trial," Odile said. She'd pulled up the flag on the main display and was staring at it the way you stare at a storm front. "Eleven days to Ceres. Two checkpoints between here and there, both Concord-controlled. And every gun in the inner system is now allowed to shoot us."

"Then we don't go through the checkpoints."

"There's no *through*, Xanthe. There's the long way, which is longer than our fuel, and the short way, which is shorter than our lives."

Xanthe came to stand beside her at the display. Odile watched her study the system map — Jupiter's bloated bulk, the scattered checkpoints, the thin thread of their plotted course — and waited for the seed-keeper to say something about patience, about growth, about how everything is dead weight until it isn't.

What Xanthe said was: "Jupiter's radiation belts. Nobody checkpoints a radiation belt."

Odile laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, a short, sharp, disbelieving bark. "You want to slingshot through *Jupiter's* belts? In *this*? The shielding on this ship is a rumor. We'd be cooked."

"We'd be *untracked*. Hale's people won't follow us into that. And the slingshot gives us the velocity to make Ceres on our fuel." Xanthe traced the arc with one finger, and Odile saw it — really saw it — the way the trajectory would work, the physics of it, the beautiful stupid audacity of it. "You're the pilot. Can the ship do it?"

"The ship can do it. The question is whether the *people* can." Odile stared at the arc a moment longer. Her hands had gone cold. Every instinct she had, every scar, every lesson written in the blood of friends who'd pushed a burn too far — all of it said no. And underneath all of it, quieter, something new: the memory of a woman drinking terrible water and making a face, and saying *I'm going to clean the filter anyway.*

"Okay," Odile said. "Okay. We do it. But we do it my way. Pre-burn checklist, full suits, and if I say abort, we abort. No seed-keeper philosophy, no *everything is dead weight until it isn't*. We abort."

"Agreed."

"And Xanthe?" Odile turned to look at her, and found the woman already looking back, pale eyes steady in the cockpit's dim light, the warrant photo's startled ghost long gone from her face. "If this works, you're cleaning the filter forever."

Xanthe smiled — the first real one Odile had seen from her, crooked and unpracticed, like a mechanism that hadn't been used in eleven years and was still finding its tolerances. "Deal."

Odile turned back to the console and started plotting the burn that would either save them or kill them, and she caught herself humming — an old Belt shanty, Cassio's favorite, the one about the tug pilot who laughed at gravity — and she didn't stop.

* * *

They were nine hours out from the slingshot when the long-range array picked it up.

Not a Concord ship. Not a patrol, not a checkpoint, not the lethal-force flag made flesh. Something else — a transponder signature Odile knew in her bones, the way you know a voice in a dark room. Belt registry. Military. A Collective frigate running dark except for the one ping it wanted them to see.

Odile's blood went from cold to colder. The Belt had her service record. The Belt had her face, her call sign, her family name — a family name now famous for the worst reason in the system. If Concord wanted them dead and the Belt wanted them *found*, then somewhere out in the dark between, the message in Xanthe's vault — Varga's message, the Cassini lie, the truth that could end the war — had just stopped being a secret two governments wanted buried.

It had become a secret two governments wanted *owned*.

The comm board lit up. An incoming hail, tight-beam, official channel, the kind of hail you couldn't ignore without confirming you were exactly who they thought you were.

Odile looked at Xanthe. Xanthe looked at Odile.

"That's not Hale," Xanthe said.

"No," Odile said, and reached for the comm. "It's worse."
