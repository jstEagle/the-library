## 10. Port Meridian

The station announced itself the way a bad tooth announces itself: a dull, persistent wrongness on the edge of perception long before you could see it. Xanthe felt it first as a flicker in the Perihelion's nav display — a smear of reflected light where no light should be — and then, over four hours of deceleration, it resolved into Port Meridian: an asteroid the size of a fist-clenched city, hollowed out and girdled with docking spars that stuck out of its flanks like splinters in knuckle.

It had been a mining claim once. Now it was neither mine nor port so much as an argument between the two — shafts that had gone too deep to be profitable repurposed as habitation rings, ore cradles welded into berths, the whole structure lit by sodium floods that turned everything the color of old bruises. No Concord registry. No Belt charter. Just a hundred thousand people who had decided, collectively, that both governments could go to hell at their own expense.

"Charming," Odile said, from the pilot's seat. She said it the way other people said *catastrophic*.

"You've been here before."

"Twice. Once for fuel, once to have a bullet dug out of my shoulder." She flexed her left arm experimentally, as if checking that the memory was still theoretical. "The fuel was fine. The surgeon charged me extra for conversation."

Xanthe watched the station grow through the forward glass. Eleven years at Svalbard-9 had taught her to read ice, rock, and silence. Port Meridian read like none of them. It read like noise given mass. "We need how much?"

"Fuel, water reclamation filters, and a new coolant loop for the starboard drive. If we don't get the loop, we're running hot enough by Ceres that someone's thermal scope will find us before our transponder does." Odile killed the approach burn with a touch too gentle for the violence of the maneuver. "And we need to not be memorable. Which means I do the talking, you stand there looking like a botanist, and nobody mentions beacons, messages, or the end of the war."

"I *am* a botanist."

"You're a fugitive who alphabetizes seeds." Odile grinned, and Xanthe hated how much she'd come to rely on that grin — how many hours of dark transit it had carried them through. "There's a difference. It matters here."

* * *

Port Meridian smelled like every place humanity had ever abandoned and then repopulated against its better judgment. The docking bay air was machine oil and recycled sweat, cut with something sweet and chemical from a food stall two levels down. Gravity was a compromise — spun up to maybe sixty percent standard, enough that loose objects drifted with a kind of tired reluctance rather than floated free.

Odile negotiated. Xanthe stood where she was told and looked like a botanist, which turned out to be easy, because she was one, and because eleven years of talking mostly to herself had left her with a resting expression that discouraged conversation. She catalogued instead, because cataloguing was what her hands did when her mind was busy being afraid.

The fuel broker was a woman built like a cargo crane named Sefa, who quoted prices in a currency that seemed to be part scrip, part barter, and part reputation. Odile paid in all three. The coolant loop came from a salvage dealer who claimed it had come off a Concord cruiser, then a Belt frigate, then — when Odile raised an eyebrow — "a ship, lady, does it matter?" Water filters were easier. Everything on Meridian was easier if you didn't ask where it had been.

They were loading the last crates onto a cargo sled when Xanthe noticed the boy.

He was maybe fifteen, small for it, with the wiry build of someone who'd grown up in low-g and learned to eat whatever gravity offered him. He sat cross-legged on top of a stack of empty isotope drums near berth fourteen, three berths down from theirs, doing nothing with the total commitment of a professional. His eyes tracked them — tracked Odile's sidearm, tracked the crates, tracked the way Xanthe kept her body between the sled and the open bay — with an attention that wasn't idle. It was inventory.

"Don't look," Odile murmured, without moving her lips.

"I'm not looking."

"You're looking. You always look. It's your whole thing." Odile cinched a strap down. "Dock kid. Probably runs errands, probably picks pockets, probably knows everyone's business. Ignore him."

Xanthe ignored him for exactly as long as it took to finish loading. Then they sealed the Perihelion, paid their berth fees in advance for six hours, and went to find food that hadn't been freeze-dried in a Concord ration facility, because Odile had declared that morale was a consumable resource and hers was critically low.

* * *

The mess hall — Meridians called it the Long Table, a name that seemed to be half affection and half warning — occupied a converted ore hopper two rings in from the dock. It served something called stew, which was less a dish than a rotating consensus of whatever had arrived on the last freighter. Xanthe ate hers without complaint. After eleven years of her own cooking, food made by strangers tasted like a letter from a civilization she'd thought she'd lost.

They were halfway through when Odile went still.

Not dramatically. Her spoon just stopped, mid-air, and her eyes fixed on some middle distance where calculations were happening. Xanthe had learned to recognize that stillness. It meant Odile's instincts were screaming and her pride was deciding whether to listen.

"What?"

"Cargo hold." Odile set the spoon down. "When we loaded this morning — the mass balance was off. I wrote it off as sensor drift. Two hundred kilos, maybe. Maybe more." She was already standing. "I checked the seals myself before we burned out of the last station."

"Then drift—"

"Drift doesn't eat." Odile was moving now, threading through the tables. "Two hundred kilos eats."

* * *

The Perihelion's cargo hold smelled like cold metal and old fuel, and under that, faintly, like someone else's life. Odile came down the ladder first, sidearm drawn but pointed at the deck, which Xanthe understood was a specific kind of courtesy — a threat held politely in reserve. Xanthe followed with a hand light because someone had to see, and because she trusted herself slightly more than she trusted Odile's restraint.

Behind the water reclamation tanks, wedged into the gap between the tank housing and the hull ribbing — a space maybe a meter wide, insulated by a stolen thermal blanket — they found him.

The boy from the drums.

He was asleep, or pretending to be, curled around a battered handheld terminal like it was a stuffed animal. He had a second blanket, a coil of salvaged power line he'd clearly tapped into their grid, and an alarming quantity of food wrappers arranged with the fastidiousness of someone who knew that leaving evidence got you spaced. He'd been living there since Port Meridian, which was Port Meridian, which meant—

"We haven't even *left* yet," Xanthe said stupidly.

"He stowed away during the loading window." Odile's voice had gone flat and quiet, which Xanthe had also learned to fear. "While we were buying his fuel. While I was paying for his—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again with terrible calm. "Kid. I know you're awake. Your breathing changed."

The boy opened one eye. Assessed. Opened the other.

"Okay," he said. "In my defense, I checked your manifest first. You barely declared anything. Nobody declares nothing unless they're either broke or interesting, and your ship's patched with Concord naval sealant, which broke people can't afford." He uncurled with the unhurried confidence of someone who had calculated, correctly, that the meter-wide gap was the only thing keeping the situation civil. "So. Interesting."

"Interesting gets people thrown out of airlocks," Odile said.

"Not before they tell you why they're worth more aboard than outside." He held up the handheld terminal. On its screen, scrolling in a font Xanthe didn't recognize, was a partial copy of their own navigation log. Their route. Their heading. *Ceres.* "Because right now I know where you're going, and you don't know my name, and only one of those facts is dangerous for me."

Silence. Somewhere above them, the ship ticked as the station's temperature cycles worked at the hull.

"Tovi," the boy said finally, into the silence, because even professionals got tired of waiting. "Tovi Okafor. And before you decide — ask me about Ilse Varga."

The name landed in the hold like a dropped tool.

Xanthe heard her own pulse. She heard Odile's breath change beside her. Eleven days ago that name had been a dead woman's whisper in a beacon message; since then it had been coordinates, a decryption key, flight recorder data proving that the war's founding atrocity was a lie — and a countdown, nineteen days that were now fewer, ticking toward self-erasure.

"Where did you hear that name," Odile said. It wasn't a question. Questions gave ground.

"My aunt." For the first time, the boy's professionalism cracked, and underneath it was something younger and rawer. He hugged the terminal to his chest. "Adaeze Okafor. She was a signals analyst on Cassini Station. Before. She worked with Varga on the deep-array calibration — I've seen her correspondence, I keep her files, I keep *everything* — and Varga sent her a fragment once. Encrypted. Auntie Adaeze never cracked it. She used to work on it when she couldn't sleep, after the attack. She died in the evacuation of Pallas two years ago and I've been carrying her files ever since, and when I saw your ship's route and your patchwork hull and your *faces*—" he gestured at them both with a hand that wasn't quite steady, "—I took a chance. Sue me."

"You copied our nav log," Odile said.

"I *can* copy your nav log. That's the relevant fact."

Xanthe crouched slowly, bringing herself level with him. Up close she could see the details the dock lighting had hidden: chapped lips, the fine tremor in his hands that he was holding very still, the shadows under his eyes of someone who slept in meter-wide gaps and woke at every sound. A child wearing competence like armor over grief.

"Tovi," she said. "Show me the fragment."

His eyes narrowed — the same calculation, run again, faster. Then he thumbed the terminal and turned it around.

She recognized the format immediately. The same compression scheme, the same header architecture as Varga's beacon transmission — the same hand, reaching across years and dead vacuum. Not the full message. A piece of it. A piece that slotted, she realized with a vertigo that had nothing to do with spin gravity, into the gaps their key hadn't yet resolved.

"This is real," she said quietly.

"I know it's real."

"No — I mean—" She looked up at Odile, and saw her own conclusion reflected there. "This completes the checksum. The sections Varga corrupted to hide from casual interception — this is the correction data. With this, we can verify the whole recording. Prove it hasn't been altered. Make it *unimpeachable*."

Odile stared at the terminal for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge and gained something more dangerous: respect. "How long have you been living in ships' holds, Tovi?"

"Since Pallas. Three years." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something. "You learn the routes. You learn which captains check seals and which ones check feelings. You two check feelings." He said it like an accusation. It sounded, almost, like gratitude.

"The answer's no," Odile said. "You're not coming to Ceres. We drop you at the next—"

"Auntie Adaeze spent four years trying to crack that fragment because she believed Varga." Tovi's voice didn't rise; it sharpened. "Everyone on Cassini who believed her got called a conspiracy crank until the war started and then they got called dead. I'm not asking to join your crew. I'm telling you I'm the only person in this system who can authenticate your evidence, and I already know your route, and if you dump me here I'll sell the fragment to whoever pays, and *somebody* will pay, because a Concord patrol ship asked about beacon traffic at Svalbard-9 six days ago and the brokers here talk."

The silence that followed had weight. Xanthe felt it settle on her shoulders like the vault's old atmospheric pressure, familiar and heavy.

*Six days ago.* Hale. Or Hale's people. Already reaching this far, this fast.

"He's right about the patrol ship," she said. "I heard the same chatter at the fuel broker. A Concord corvette docked yesterday, asking about a patched civilian hauler on a Ceres trajectory."

"That's us," Odile said grimly.

"That's us. Which means the next station won't be safer, it'll be worse, and every hour we spend deciding what to do with him is an hour they spend closing distance." Xanthe looked at the boy in the gap between the tanks — at the terminal clutched to his chest, at the aunt-shaped hole he'd been carrying across three years and half the Belt. She knew that hole. She'd lived inside one for eleven years and called it a career.

She thought about Dr. Ilse Varga, who had died alone making sure the truth would outlive her, and who had addressed her final message not to a government, not to a fleet, but to *the Keeper* — betting everything on one stranger's decency. She thought about what Varga would say about a fifteen-year-old who'd smuggled himself aboard a doomed ship because he couldn't let go of a dead woman's unfinished work.

Varga would say: *of course.*

"Odile." Xanthe stood. "He authenticates the message, he rides to Ceres, and we put him on the first neutral transport out. That's the offer. Take it or leave it — but decide fast, because I want to be burning before that corvette finishes its inspection cycle."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Then at Tovi. Then she holstered her sidearm with a motion that managed to convey both surrender and threat.

"One rule," she said. "You touch my ship's systems, you ask first. You break that rule, I space you. I'll even fill out the paperwork afterward — I hear Concord loves paperwork."

"Deal." Tovi was already climbing out of the gap, unfolding into the hold like he owned it, and Xanthe caught the exact moment the fear left his posture and the professionalism came back — the armor going on again, plate by plate. "For the record, your coolant loop is counterfeit. The salvage dealer sold you a rebranded mining-grade unit. I can fix it tonight."

Odile closed her eyes. "I'm going to regret this."

"Probably," Tovi agreed cheerfully.

* * *

They burned out of Port Meridian nine hours later, ahead of schedule, while the Concord corvette was still tied up in a customs dispute with Sefa over docking fees that may or may not have been invented by Odile through three intermediaries. Xanthe stood at the observation port and watched the bruise-colored station shrink behind them, and tried to name what she was feeling.

It wasn't relief. It was closer to the sensation of the vault's seed drawers after a planting season — compartments that had held one thing, tightly, for years, suddenly rearranged to hold more. She had been one person maintaining one secret. Now she was three people maintaining one truth, and one of them was a child who ate their rations and fixed their equipment and flinched, sometimes, when the comm channel crackled — a flinch she recognized from her own mirror, years ago, in the tribunal's aftermath.

Family, she thought, was apparently something that happened to you while you were busy fleeing.

From the engineering alcove came the sound of Odile and Tovi arguing about torque tolerances, escalating, then collapsing into laughter — sudden, surprised, shared. Xanthe smiled at the dark glass, at her own reflection ghosted over the receding stars.

Then the terminal in her pocket buzzed, and the smile died.

An incoming transmission, tight-beam, no origin stamp. One line of text, displayed in clean Concord cipher:

**THE OTHER TWO KEEPERS SEND THEIR REGARDS. ASK YOUR STOWAWAY WHAT HAPPENED TO HIS AUNT'S CORRESPONDENTS.**

She read it twice. Behind her, the laughter went on, warm and oblivious, and Xanthe stood very still in the observation dark, holding a message that meant the countdown had never been only about the beacon's clock.
