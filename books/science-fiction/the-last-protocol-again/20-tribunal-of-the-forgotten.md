## 20. Tribunal of the Forgotten

The hearing chamber on Ceres had been a water reclamation hall once, before the war made neutrality profitable and the neutral powers needed somewhere to put their dignity. Xanthe could still see it in the bones of the place: the great cylindrical cisterns along the walls, now dry and repurposed as acoustic baffles; the catwalk where maintenance drones had crawled, now lined with delegates from three governments and a dozen smaller polities that had spent eleven years selling fuel to both sides of a war.

They had given her a chair. It was a small thing, but she noticed it. A real chair, at a real table, with a microphone that worked and a carafe of water that was actually clean. For eleven years her testimony had been a thing shouted into a tribunal that had already written its verdict — a navigation officer's voice, flattened by grief and politics, explaining how the *Meridian Dawn* had fallen out of the sky over Tycho with four hundred souls aboard while every instrument said she'd done nothing wrong.

She had been twenty-six then. She had believed the tribunal would listen.

Now she was thirty-eight, and she did not believe in listening anymore. She believed in evidence, in redundancy, in backups stored in cold silicon and colder seed vaults. But she sat down in the chair anyway, because Odile had told her on the walk over: *They don't get to decide whether you're worth hearing. You decide whether they're worth talking to.*

Tovi had adjusted her collar twice and then pretended he hadn't. He sat in the gallery between Odile's empty seat — she was still aboard the *Perihelion*, under medical hold after Ceres Rising, furious about it in fourteen languages — and a cluster of Cassini survivors' families who had come from the Belt on donated passage. Tovi wore his aunt's old jacket, too big for him, and held a data slate against his chest like a shield. Every few minutes his eyes flicked to the doors, cataloguing exits. Old habits. The boy had lived in cargo holds too long to sit anywhere without knowing where the airlocks were.

The presiding arbiter was a thin woman named Okonkwo-Vance, appointed by neither Concord nor the Collective but by the relay consortium itself, which meant she owed her position to the only institution that had stayed honest through the war: the plumbing of information. She had gray at her temples and the patient expression of someone who had spent months listening to admirals explain why atrocities were complicated.

"State your name for the record," she said.

"Xanthe Winterbourne. Formerly Lieutenant, Concord Navigation Corps, serial—" She paused. "Formerly."

A ripple went through the Concord delegation. Rear Admiral Sorensen, who had flown out from Earth with a face like carved soap, leaned toward his aide and whispered something. Xanthe didn't look at him. Looking at them gave them weight, and she had spent eleven years learning how little weight most powerful people actually carried.

"You are here," Okonkwo-Vance said, "as the primary custodian of the Varga archive, and as a witness to its provenance. Do you understand that anything you say today will be entered into the permanent record of this inquiry?"

"I understand it better than most," Xanthe said. "I've been on the other side of that sentence."

* * *

They let her tell it in order, which surprised her. She had expected fragmentation, objections, the slow surgical process by which tribunals usually dismantled inconvenient witnesses. Instead Okonkwo-Vance simply asked her to begin at the beginning, and the beginning was nine seconds of silence when gravity forgot itself above an ice field at the top of the world.

So she told them about the vault. About the seeds in their foil envelopes, the wheat and barley and ten thousand years of patience stacked in permafrost, and the beacon she had maintained for a decade believing it was a relic. She told them about the message addressed to *the Keeper* — a dead-man's switch left by Dr. Ilse Varga, who had died in what the official record called a depressurization accident and what the Varga archive now proved was something else entirely.

She described the decryption. She watched the room lean forward, all those polished delegates, hungry for the drama of it. But the drama wasn't the decryption. The drama was what came after, and she made them wait for it.

"The flight recorder data from Cassini Station," she said, "shows that the breach sequence originated from a Concord-patterned demolition charge detonated inside the station's own structural spine. Not Belt saboteurs. Not pirates. A Concord weapons signature, deployed by a Concord black-operations cell, on a station full of civilians." Her voice held level. She had rehearsed this in the *Perihelion*'s galley with only Tovi listening, and even rehearsal hadn't stopped her hands from shaking then. They were steady now. Eleven years of logging weather teaches you a kind of calm. "Four hundred and six people died so that the Concord would have its casus belli. My predecessor found the proof. She hid it in the one place no one looks — with the seeds. With the things we keep in case everything else fails."

"And you broadcast it," said Sorensen, finally, unable to help himself. "Without authorization. Without chain of command. You hijacked a neutral relay and transmitted classified materials system-wide."

"Yes," Xanthe said.

"You understand that under Concord law—"

"Admiral." Okonkwo-Vance's voice was quiet and it stopped him like a hand on the chest. "You'll have your turn. The witness is mine until I'm done with her."

Something in Xanthe's chest loosened. She thought, absurdly, of the vault's airlock cycling — that small hydraulic sigh when pressure equalized and two different worlds agreed, briefly, to be the same world.

* * *

It was in the second session, after the midday recess, that Okonkwo-Vance asked the question Xanthe had been waiting eleven years for someone to ask.

"Witness, the archive references a prior proceeding. A Concord tribunal, eleven years ago, regarding the loss of the vessel *Meridian Dawn*. Your name appears in the annexes. Would you describe your connection to that case?"

The chamber went very still. Tovi's head came up. In the gallery, one of the Cassini families — a broad-shouldered woman with a mourning band woven into her sleeve — turned to look at her.

Xanthe folded her hands on the table. The carafe of water sat there, clean and ordinary, and she looked at it instead of the hundred faces, because it was easier to speak to water.

"The *Meridian Dawn* was a colony transport making the Earth-to-Mars run," she said. "I was the junior navigation officer. Four hundred and twelve people aboard, including crew. We lost thrust guidance during a solar storm — a genuine storm, later confirmed by three independent observatories — and the ship came down in the Tycho approach corridors. There were survivors. Nineteen of them."

She paused. The number never got easier. Nineteen out of four hundred and twelve. She had carried each of their names for a decade, the way she carried the seeds: carefully, in the dark, against a future she couldn't predict.

"The storm damage was real. But the guidance failure had a contributing cause the tribunal chose not to examine: a maintenance directive, issued eight days earlier, deferring critical gyroscopic recalibration in order to meet a fleet deployment schedule. That directive came from the same command structure that later authored the Cassini operation. Some of the same officers. I know because Ilse Varga's archive contains their names, cross-referenced, and because I have spent eleven years reading everything I could find about the machinery that ruined my life."

"Objection," Sorensen said. "Relevance. This witness is attempting to relitigate a closed case."

"It is entirely relevant," Okonkwo-Vance said, "because the witness is describing a pattern, Admiral, and patterns are what this inquiry exists to establish. Continue, please."

So Xanthe continued. She told them about the tribunal — how the deferred-maintenance directive had vanished from the record, how her flight logs had been edited, how a lieutenant with no political family and no money had been handed the entire weight of four hundred and twelve deaths and told to carry it into exile. How the sentence had technically been a discharge, but everyone understood it as banishment. How she had taken the only job offered to a disgraced navigator: keeper of a seed vault at the end of the world, tending the past because the present wanted nothing from her.

"They didn't need me guilty," she said. "They needed me *gone*, and quiet, and useful as a warning to anyone else who noticed loose threads. And it worked. For eleven years I kept my head down and my logs clean and I told myself the past was past." She looked up, finally, and let herself see the room. "Then a dead woman sent me a message proving that the same machine that broke my ship started your war. And I learned that the past isn't past. It's just stored. Like seeds. Waiting for conditions to change."

Silence. Then, from the gallery, the woman with the mourning band began, softly, to clap. Others joined her — the Belt families first, then delegates, then, awkwardly, a handful of Concord staff who had not been told not to. Okonkwo-Vance let it go on for exactly five seconds and then restored order with a glance.

* * *

They cleared her record on the fourth day.

Not dramatically. There was no moment of thunder. Arbiter Okonkwo-Vance read a summary finding — the *Meridian Dawn* tribunal had relied on falsified evidence; Lieutenant Winterbourne's exoneration was retroactive and unconditional; reparations would be calculated — and moved on to the next docket item, because tribunals are machines and machines do not pause for the people they grind or the people they release.

But afterward, in the corridor outside the chamber, a clerk caught up to her with a slate and a stylus and a form that required her thumbprint. "Restoration of rank and standing," the clerk said. "Effective immediately. Congratulations, Lieutenant."

Xanthe looked at the form for a long time.

Eleven years ago she would have signed it with tears in her eyes. She would have signed it kneeling. Rank, standing, a uniform, a place back inside the warm body of the institution that had chewed her up — it had been the whole of her ambition once, the shape of every dream she'd had between the ages of nineteen and twenty-six. To be *in*. To belong.

She thought of the vault. Of the smell of frost and foil and the faint green ghost of living things in the dark. Of Odile swearing at a coolant coupling in three languages, laughing with her whole scarred body when the engine finally caught. Of Tovi asleep in the copilot's chair with his aunt's jacket pulled over him like a blanket, data slate still glowing against his chest, guarding other people's secrets even in his dreams.

"Actually," Xanthe said, "no."

The clerk blinked. "No?"

"No, thank you." She pushed the slate gently back. "I'm not a lieutenant. I haven't been for a long time. I'm the Keeper of Svalbard-9, and I'm going back to work."

The Concord delegation intercepted her an hour later, in a reception hall with actual gravity plating and wine that had crossed the system at ruinous expense. Rear Admiral Sorensen himself, all carved soap smoothed over into something almost warm. Behind him stood a civilian aide holding a second offer — this one from the Provisional Reconstruction Authority, joint Concord-Belt, newly chartered, desperate for credible faces.

"Lieutenant— Keeper," Sorensen said, correcting himself with visible effort. "The Corps would be honored to restore you with full seniority. Command track. Or, if you prefer, the Authority is establishing an Office of Archival Integrity, and frankly there is no one in the system whose name carries more—"

"Admiral." She picked up a glass of the expensive wine, considered it, and set it down untouched. "Do you know what I did for eleven years? I checked seeds. I logged wind speed over an ice sheet. I counted the seconds between lightning and thunder. And it was the most honest work I've ever done, because seeds don't lie, and weather doesn't care who you know, and nobody ever asked me to sign my name to a story I didn't believe."

Sorensen's jaw tightened. "You'd refuse a flag officer's recommendation. Twice."

"I'd refuse it a hundred times." She smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, but it was not a soft one either. "You built a machine that eats people and calls it justice. I spent a decade learning how to grow things instead. I know which job I want."

She left him standing among the diplomats, and found Tovi waiting by the corridor windows, watching the traffic of a free Ceres spin past — haulers and ferries and, for the first time in eleven years, ships flying both flags docked at adjacent berths without anyone shooting.

"They offered you stuff, huh," Tovi said. It wasn't a question. Word traveled fast in a station the size of a city.

"They did."

"You gonna take it?"

"I'm going home," Xanthe said. "To a vault full of seeds and a beacon that needs rewiring and a pilot who's going to complain about the coffee for the entire transit. Are you coming?"

Tovi tried to play it cool, the way sixteen-year-olds do, shoulders up, face arranged into indifference. It lasted about two seconds. "Yeah," he said. "Obviously. Someone has to keep your archives from being boring."

Through the window, a Belt freighter and a Concord supply ship maneuvered past each other, close enough to wave, and did.

Xanthe pressed her palm briefly against the cold glass and thought of the log entry she would write tonight, the first line already forming: *Day 4,043 since the nine seconds. Conditions changing.*
