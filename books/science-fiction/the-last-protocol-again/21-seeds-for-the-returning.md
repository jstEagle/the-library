## 21. Seeds for the Returning

The first thing Tovi noticed when the Perihelion slid into orbit around Svalbard-9 was that the ice had a color he'd never seen on any station screen. Not white. Not blue. A pale, patient gold, like old paper held up to a lamp, the kind of light that made you think of things being kept safe rather than things being hidden.

He stood at the viewport with his tablet clutched against his chest, watching the vault's shadow crawl across the glacier below, and tried to remember what it had felt like to be afraid of this place. Six months ago it had been coordinates in a dead woman's ledger, a pinprick of light at the top of the world where two keepers had died and a third had gone silent. Now it was going home. That was the word Odile used — *home* — said with the careless certainty of someone who'd grown up on stations where home was wherever your bunk was bolted down. Xanthe never used the word at all. She just touched the glass once, lightly, the way you'd touch a door you weren't sure would still open.

"Approach vector's clean," Odile said from the pilot's chair, feet up on the console until Xanthe cleared her throat without turning around, and then not up on the console. "Atmospheric interface in forty minutes. You gonna be sick again, Tovi?"

"I was *adjusting* my inner ear."

"You were decorating the starboard bulkhead."

"That was one time."

"One time is a tradition now." But she grinned at him in the reflection off the viewport, and the grin had none of the old razor in it. Six months of truce patrols and relay runs and three people learning how to share a ship smaller than their tempers had sanded her edges down to something almost soft. Almost. Tovi had learned not to trust the softness entirely. He'd also learned it was real more often than not.

Below them, the landing field Xanthe had cut into the ice eleven years ago — and abandoned six months ago with a decoy loop singing lies into the dark — waited under its skin of fresh snow. And around it, arranged in the careful geometry of people who didn't trust good fortune yet, sat four ships.

Tovi counted them twice. "That's more than the survey team."

"Refugees," Xanthe said quietly. "From Ceres. From the Belt stations that got gutted during the hardliner purge. The tribunal posted the reopening last month. Honest work, honest pay, honest records." Her mouth did something complicated. "I wrote the posting myself. I checked the grammar nine times."

"Nine times," Odile repeated, delighted. "Keeper."

"It's an official document."

"It's a job ad."

"It's an official document," Xanthe said, with the exact tone she used when she was losing an argument and refusing to acknowledge the terrain, and Tovi laughed and went to strap in for descent, because some traditions were worth keeping too.

* * *

The atmosphere hit the hull like a fist made of wind, and then they were through it, and Svalbard-9 opened beneath them the way it must have opened for Xanthe a hundred thousand times: white ridges, blue crevasse-shadows, the long scar of the access road, and the vault itself rising out of the ice like a buried ship's keel — dark metal, patient geometry, the beacon tower at its crown.

The beacon was dark. Of course it was. It had sung its last secret half a year ago, and the whole system had heard it, and the war had cracked along the fault line of that broadcast like river ice in spring. Tovi still couldn't watch replays of that night without his hands shaking. Every screen on every ship and station, Varga's voice and the flight recorder data laid bare, the Cassini lie detonating in a billion skulls at once. He'd been seventeen years old, crouched in a hijacked relay core with his aunt's name burning in his throat, and he had pressed the key that ended a war.

Some days he felt ten meters tall. Some days he felt like a kid who'd broken something enormous and wasn't sure anyone could ever put it back. Most days, lately, he felt both before breakfast.

They set down in a plume of snow-dust, and the airlock hadn't finished cycling before people were coming across the ice toward them — maybe thirty of them, bundled in mismatched cold-gear, and at their front a broad-shouldered woman with a Ceres accent thick enough to chew who stuck out a hand and said, "Nadia Okonkwo, agri-systems. We got your posting. Is it true there's really work here?"

Xanthe shook her hand and said, "There's really work," and something in her face came undone so gently that Tovi had to look away, the way you look away from someone crying at a wedding.

* * *

The vault smelled the same, Odile said later — cold stone and machine oil and that faint green ghost of the growing rooms — but to Tovi it smelled new, or rather it smelled like a place deciding what it wanted to be. For eleven years it had been a hiding place: one woman, a million seeds, a dead man's switch sleeping in the dark. Now there were voices in the corridors. There were children — actual children, three of them, belonging to a hydroponics tech named Yusuf who'd lost everything on Pallas — running races down the seed-stack galleries, their boots slapping the deck plates, their echoes bouncing off shelves that held the frozen future of every crop humanity had ever loved.

Yusuf's youngest, a girl of about six named Amara, appointed herself Tovi's shadow within the first day. She followed him everywhere with the absolute authority of the very young, asking questions in a relentless stream: why the lights were orange in the stacks (so the seeds thought it was always late afternoon, Xanthe explained, and Amara nodded as if this were obvious), why the doors were so thick (because the world outside was serious about its weather), whether Tovi was a pirate (no), whether he *used to be* a pirate (a pause; "I was a stowaway," he admitted, and she received this information with the reverence it deserved).

On the fourth day she found him in the beacon chamber, sitting cross-legged under the tower with his tablet balanced on his knees, staring at the code he'd been staring at for a week.

"You're sad-mad," Amara announced, from the doorway. "That's your thinking face but your eyebrows are doing the angry thing."

"I'm working."

"On what?"

Tovi hesitated. Then he turned the tablet so she could see. "This. The beacon. It used to send secrets. Now it doesn't have anything to send, and it just... sits here. Like a mouth with nothing to say."

Amara studied the schematic with the fierce concentration of someone sounding out her first words. "So give it something to say."

"Yeah." He rubbed his eyes. "That's the problem. What? The tribunal archives are public already. Anyone can pull them from Ceres. The beacon's just a repeater now. It feels like—" He stopped, embarrassed by how much it mattered to him. "It feels like we won the war and forgot to tell the lighthouse."

Amara considered this with six-year-old gravity. "My papa says seeds aren't secrets. Seeds are letters. To people you haven't met yet."

Tovi looked at her. Looked at the beacon. Looked at the catalog manifest glowing on his tablet — 1.2 million accessions, each one a small green argument against extinction, most of them undocumented beyond a barcode and a collection date, gathered by keepers across two centuries who had mostly worked alone and mostly died alone and had never once been able to tell anyone what they'd saved or why.

"Oh," he said.

* * *

It took him nineteen days, working nights, with Xanthe supplying archive protocols and Odile supplying commentary ("You're building a library in a *lighthouse*, kid, that's either genius or a cry for help") and the refugee crews supplying hands once word got around that the quiet hacker boy was building something. Nadia Okonkwo turned out to have a gift for metadata taxonomy. Yusuf knew legacy encoding formats from his days maintaining Pallas station records. Even Amara contributed, inscribing a drawing of the beacon onto Tovi's tablet with a greasy finger, which he screenshotted and, though he denied it forever after, made the project's icon.

What they built was simple, which was the hardest part. Every accession in the vault got a public record: what the seed was, where it came from, who collected it, what it was for. Not just barcodes — stories. Keeper's notes, some of them a century old, scanned and transcribed. Growing instructions. Failsafe cultivation data for worlds with thin soil and thinner hope. And at the top of it all, unencrypted, unowned, unkillable, the beacon itself, repurposed from a dead-man's switch into a living one: broadcasting the open archive on a loop, free to anyone with an antenna, from the top of the world, forever.

"The Last Protocol Again," Odile read off the header draft, and snorted. "You're naming it after the book?"

"Tribunal made the case file title public record. It's poetic."

"It's *vanity*."

"Says the woman who named her ship after her own ego."

"Perihelion is an astronomical term."

"Uh-huh."

But Xanthe, when they showed her the finished interface, was quiet for a long time. She stood in the beacon chamber with the tower humming softly above them — its first live transmission in its new life, a wheat accession collected in 2081 by a keeper named Marta Osei whose only surviving note read *for the ones who come after* — and Tovi watched her read that note, and watched her hand rise to her collarbone where Varga's key used to hang on its chain.

"She'd have liked this," Xanthe said finally. "Ilse. She always said a vault isn't a tomb, it's a promise. I never understood what she meant until—" She stopped. Started again, steadier. "It's good work, Tovi. It's the best work."

He shrugged, ears hot. "Amara helped."

"Amara," Xanthe said gravely, "is hereby credited as consulting architect," and Amara, summoned at a run and told this, glowed for three days.

* * *

On the evening of the beacon's relaunch, the whole settlement crowded into the observation deck — thirty-some souls pressed against the long window, mugs of reconstituted cider steaming, breath fogging the glass. Below, the ice fields lay blue-gray under the low sun. Above, the first stars were coming out.

Odile found Tovi at the edge of the crowd and handed him a mug. "Toaster duty's yours, kid. Xanthe says you built it, you launch it."

"That's not how ceremonies work."

"That's exactly how ceremonies work. I've been to one."

He laughed, and raised the mug, and looked out at the gathering — Okonkwo arguing cheerfully with Yusuf about irrigation schedules, Amara asleep against her father's shoulder, Odile standing close enough to Xanthe that their shoulders touched, which was as much as either of them ever said out loud, and which everyone understood perfectly anyway.

Six months ago he'd been a stowaway in a cargo hold with a stolen tablet and a dead aunt's name. Now he stood in a room full of people who knew his name and used it kindly, above a vault full of seeds that were letters to people nobody had met yet, under a beacon that no longer carried secrets — only gifts.

"Hit it," Odile said.

He hit it.

Out on the tower, the beacon lit — not the harsh strobe of alarm, not the pulse of a dead-man's switch, but a slow, steady, warm gold, the color of the ice at dawn, the color of paper held up to a lamp. The archive began its first circuit of the sky. Somewhere out there, on ships and stations and struggling colonies, antennas would catch it, and strangers would read the keeper's notes, and plant things.

"To the ones who come after," Xanthe said softly, and thirty voices repeated it, and the cider was terrible and everyone drank it anyway.

Later, when the deck had emptied and the others had drifted down to sleep, Tovi stayed at the window a while longer, watching the beacon turn. His tablet chimed — a routine sync ping from the relay network, the kind he barely noticed anymore.

Except this one wasn't routine. It was routed through a legacy protocol he recognized, because he'd spent a week inside Varga's fragments and knew her handshakes like handwriting. An old dead-drop address, dormant since before Cassini. Someone was using it again.

The message was four words long:

**KEEPER THREE IS ALIVE.**

Tovi stared at it, the beacon's gold light reflected in the black glass, his heart beginning to count.

Down the corridor, he could hear Xanthe and Odile laughing over dishes. He looked at the message a long moment. Then he got up, and went to find them.
