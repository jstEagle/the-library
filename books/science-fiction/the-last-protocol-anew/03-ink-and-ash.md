## 3. Ink and Ash

The packet sat in Casper's cabin for six hours before she touched it.

She told herself it was caution — that a courier who opened sealed freight before delivery was a courier who stopped getting contracts. That was true, and it had been her rule for twelve years, longer than some of the ports on her route had existed. But rules were also armor, and she knew the difference between discipline and fear better than anyone alive, because she'd built her whole life out of the gap between them.

The *Vesperine* rode at anchor in Threnody's high orbit, riding out a customs backlog that would take two days to clear. Two days of nothing but the hum of the recyclers and the slow rotation of the planet below, its storm bands scrolling like ink dropped in water. Plenty of time to think about a name written in someone else's hand.

*C. Iversen.*

Not hers. Never hers. Her name, legally speaking, was nothing at all — a null entry, a voided line in a registry nobody read. She paid her fees under a shell company called Quiet Harbor Freight, signed her manifests as M. Okafor, and answered to whatever the dockmasters felt like calling her. The original Iversen had been dead nine years. Whatever was left of him lived in her cells and nowhere else, and the law had made very sure those cells belonged to no one.

So who had addressed mail to a dead man?

She turned the packet over again. Heavy weave paper, the kind used for legal documents and last wills, sealed with plain wax instead of a tamper strip — deliberately old-fashioned, deliberately unscannable. No return address. No routing code. Just the name, in handwriting that looped and slanted with an impatience she recognized from the crew manifests she'd memorized years ago, back when memorizing the original's paperwork had felt like studying a stranger.

It didn't feel like studying a stranger anymore.

*You're being stupid,* she told herself. *Open it or burn it.*

She opened it.

* * *

Inside were two things: a strip of dense polymer film, matte black, etched with a sequence of characters too small to read without magnification — half a cipher key, if her instincts were right, cut cleanly as if snapped from a longer strip — and a note on the same heavy paper.

The handwriting hit her before the words did. She'd spent one entire year of her early life, back when she still thought of herself as an apprentice rather than a copy, transcribing the original's route logs by hand to learn his shorthand. The way he crossed his sevens. The little hook he put on the letter *d*, like a flag planted in conquered ground. She had hated how much she remembered. She remembered all of it.

The note said:

**If you're reading this, then the route held longer than I expected it to. Half is yours now. Trust the line, not the ports. And don't go looking for the other half until you've decided who you are — because whoever delivers this message has to be someone the fleets can't erase.**

Below it, a date.

Casper read the date four times. Then she set the note down on her desk, walked to the galley, drank a full glass of water standing up, and came back and read it again, as though the numbers might have rearranged themselves out of shame.

The date was seven months after the *Halcyon Drift* burned.

Seven months after the original C. Iversen died in the debris field off Calloway's third moon, along with eleven crew and whatever cargo the fleet's incident report had declined to itemize. Seven months after the registry closed his file with a neat black line and the courts voided his clones' personhood so thoroughly that even the word *clone* got redacted from the public record.

And yet here was his handwriting, dated well past his death, talking about halves and decisions and erasure like a man leaving instructions for his own ghost.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere underneath the steadiness, her pulse was going like a shuttle engine, but the surface held, the way it always held, the way it had held since the day she'd stood in a licensing office on Calloway and listened to a bored clerk explain that she could purchase the deceased's route contract but not his name, his estate, or his cause of death. *Cause of death: undetermined,* the file said. *Incident under review.* Nine years of *under review*. Fleets didn't leave things under review for nine years unless everyone involved preferred it that way.

She photographed the note, both sides, then the polymer strip under three angles of light. Then she sat down and did what couriers do, which is think about routes.

*Trust the line, not the ports.* The line — the long corridor between Calloway and Threnody, the old war road, dotted with relay stations and derelicts from the fighting. Not the ports, where everything was watched, bought, or forged. He was telling whoever held this key that the answer wasn't in civilization. It was out along the scar tissue of the war itself.

But half a key was a lockpick with no door. Useless alone. Worse than useless — dangerous, because someone had gone to considerable trouble to get it into her hands, and people who went to trouble wanted something, and Casper had survived twelve invisible years by never being what anyone wanted.

Except someone *had* wanted something. Someone had put her name — his name — on a packet and moved it through her own pickup point, through her own handler, onto her own ship. Which meant they knew the route. Knew it well enough to know that a packet marked *C. Iversen* would end up exactly here, in the hands of the woman running it.

They hadn't addressed it to her cover identity. They hadn't addressed it to the shell company. They'd addressed it to the dead man, knowing the dead man's shadow would pick it up.

Someone knew what she was.

Casper looked at the black strip of polymer for a long moment, then did something she almost never did: she locked it and the note in the floor safe beneath the navigation console, spun the dial, and went to find Tamsin.

* * *

Dockside informants were like weather on Threnody — unreliable, everywhere, and best consulted before you needed them. Casper's was a woman named Odile Voss who ran a chandlery on Ring Four of Meridian Station and knew everything that moved through the port's middle layers, which was to say everything that mattered.

Odile occupied a shop crammed between a hull-salvage broker and a noodle stand that had violated seventeen health codes across two jurisdictions. Inside, the shelves were organized according to a system comprehensible only to Odile, and possibly not even to her. Rope next to coolant filters. Salvaged airlock seals stacked beside crates of contraband tea. A taxidermied fish hanging from the ceiling for reasons Casper had never dared ask about.

"You look terrible," Odile said by way of greeting. She was sixty-ish and built like a cargo pallet, with forearms that could crush a fuel cell casing and eyes that missed nothing smaller than a moon. "Sit. Drink. You're pale even for you."

"I'm always pale."

"That's the tragedy of it." She poured tea from a dented thermos without asking, sliding the cup across a counter buried under invoices. "So. You came down from the ship during a customs hold to visit me, which means either you're lonely or you're scared. You don't get lonely. So."

Casper wrapped her hands around the cup. The tea was terrible and perfect. "How much do you hear from the fleet side these days?"

"Depends who's asking and what they're paying." Odile leaned back against her shelving, arms crossed. "Try me."

"A fleet auditor. Would you have heard about one?"

Something shifted in Odile's face — a small tightening around the mouth, gone in half a second. Casper had spent years learning to read micro-expressions in dockmasters and customs officers, the tiny flinches that preceded trouble, and she'd just watched one cross the face of the woman who was supposed to warn her about trouble.

"Odile."

"I'm thinking."

"Think faster."

Odile exhaled through her nose. She glanced toward the front of the shop — empty, the noodle stand's lunch rush audible through the wall — then lowered her voice anyway, which from Odile was the equivalent of a civilian screaming.

"There's been a woman making rounds. Fleet credentials, truce-inspection authority, the whole ceremonial kit. Rennick, she signs herself. Sefa Rennick." Odile picked up a rag and began wiping a section of counter that was already clean. "She's been at it three weeks. Started on Calloway-side stations, worked her way along the corridor. And here's the part you're going to hate."

"Tell me anyway."

"She's not asking about smuggling. Doesn't care about the tea, doesn't care about the arms parts, doesn't care about anything that would actually justify her travel budget. She's asking about couriers." The rag stopped moving. "Specifically, clone couriers. Who runs voided-personnel contracts. How many there are. Whether any of them inherited routes from the war years." Odile's eyes came up, flat and direct. "Whether anyone matching a certain description bought a certain dead man's route nine years ago."

The tea in Casper's cup had gone cold, or her hands had; she couldn't tell which. "Description."

"Your description, Casper. Or close enough that when she showed me the holo and asked if I'd seen this woman, I said no so fast I nearly bit my tongue." Odile set the rag down. "I've lied to fleet auditors before. I'm good at it. But she's not fishing. She knows the route exists. She might already know who runs it. What I can't tell you is whether she knows what you are, or just suspects, or—" she waved a hand, "—whether she's hunting you or hunting whoever killed him. Auditors hunt both ways sometimes."

"Did she say why? After nine years?"

"No. That's the ugly part. Nine years is a long time for a murder investigation, and a short time for a records audit, and she's doing neither thing properly. She's doing both badly, on purpose, like someone following orders she doesn't love." Odile studied her for a moment. "You know something. I can see it sitting on you like a wet coat."

"Maybe."

"Then here's free advice, worth every credit." Odile leaned forward, forearms on the counter, close enough that Casper could smell machine oil and the tea she chain-drank. "Whatever you found in that packet — and don't tell me, I don't want to know, knowing things gets my shop burned down — whatever it is, decide fast whether you're carrying it or burying it. Because a fleet auditor who asks about clone couriers isn't building a case. She's building a list. And lists get acted on."

"She could be legitimate," Casper said. "Truce inspections happen."

"Mm. And I'm a licensed florist." Odile straightened up. "One more thing, and then you buy something so this visit looks like commerce. The auditor booked onward transit yesterday. Destination manifest says Calloway Prime, standard rotation." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat on a table. "But her berth request listed a stopover at Meridian first. This station. Tomorrow."

Casper kept her face still. Twelve years of practice. "Buying passage?"

"Inspection tour, per her filing. Ring Four, commercial tier." Odile smiled without warmth. "My tier."

* * *

Casper took the long way back to her berth, which was habit, and checked her tail twice, which was also habit, and found nothing, which meant either she was clean or the person following her was better than she was. Both possibilities had stopped feeling mutually exclusive somewhere over the last forty-eight hours.

Back aboard the *Vesperine*, she sealed the airlock, ran a sweep for passive listeners out of thoroughness, and pulled the note from the safe again.

*Don't go looking for the other half until you've decided who you are.*

She read it standing in the middle of her cabin, in the lamplight, with Threnody's storms turning below the viewport like something slow and enormous waking up. The handwriting was unmistakable. She would have sworn it on anything — on the route, on the ship, on the twelve careful years of her life. It was his hand. His impatient loops, his flagged *d*s, the sevens crossed hard enough to tear paper.

Dated seven months after he died.

There were only so many explanations, and she made herself walk through them like a courier walking a dark corridor, checking each door. One: forgery. Possible, but why forge a dead man's hand to reach his clone, and why include half a cipher key that matched nothing she could verify yet? Forgeries wanted money or access; this wanted *delivery*. Two: misdating. A slip, a stale document, an old note mailed late. Except the note referenced the reader's survival, the route holding, the decision of identity — it was written *forward*, at her, across nine years of silence. Three: he hadn't died when the fleets said he died. And that door didn't open so much as swallow the corridor whole, because if the original Iversen had been alive seven months after the *Halcyon Drift* burned, then the incident report was a fabrication, the memorial was theater, and the man whose memories lived in her skull had spent his last months writing letters to a copy of himself while someone, somewhere, arranged to make sure neither of them ever mattered.

Or — colder thought, arriving last and staying longest — he *had* died, on schedule, and someone else had kept writing in his hand afterward. Keeping him alive on paper. Keeping the route alive. Using a dead courier's name as a dead drop that only one living person in two star systems could open.

Either way, the conclusion landed in the same place: the past wasn't past. It was a live circuit, and someone had just closed it through her.

Casper folded the note along its original creases and put it away. Then she sat at the navigation console and, on a screen not connected to anything, began pulling her oldest route logs — the ones she'd inherited, digitized from the original's own records, years of coordinates and timing data that had always looked like nothing more than the tedious skeleton of a mail run.

*Trust the line, not the ports.*

If the key fragment meant anything, it would mean something against the line. Against the old battle corridor, the relay stations, the wreckage fields where the war had done its loudest work and the truce had done its quietest forgetting.

She started the cross-reference running and watched the first matches crawl up the screen.

Outside, in the black beyond Threnody's storm-wrapped curve, a fleet cutter holding truce-inspection colors adjusted its burn and settled into a parking orbit two kilometers off the *Vesperine*'s stern — close enough to be courtesy, close enough to be a leash. Its transponder read clean and official:

**FSV PATIENT ZEPHYR — INSPECTION DIVISION — RENNICK, S., AUTHORIZING OFFICER.**

On Meridian Station, tomorrow, Ring Four. And now here, already, watching.

Casper stared at the cutter's signature glowing on her proximity display, and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only after the last comfortable illusion goes, that the quiet part of her life was over. The question was no longer whether the past had found her.

It was what she intended to say to it.
