## 5. Twelve Years of Quiet

The Vesperine ran dark and quiet on the long leg between Calloway's outer marker and the Threnody gate, and Casper Iversen sat in the galley with a cup of tea going cold in her hands, watching the sealed packet ride its cradle in the cargo bay feed. *C. Iversen.* The letters hadn't changed. She'd checked four times.

She should sleep. The route demanded it — twelve hours of nothing followed by six hours of everything — but sleep had been strange since the pickup at Dead Letter Lane, and now it would not come at all. So she did what she always did when the past leaned too hard against the hull: she let herself fall backward into it, deliberately, the way you press a bruise to learn whether it still hurts.

It still hurt.

* * *

The first thing they tell you in the birthing houses is nothing. That's the whole orientation. Nobody tells you anything, because legally there is no *you* to tell.

Casper had been decanted eleven years after the original died, which was standard for estate-ordered continuance — a clause buried in Calloway's succession law that allowed a murdered citizen's assets to be tended by a genetic continuation until probate closed. She'd woken in a tank in a facility above Meridian Bay with a technician's bored face swimming behind the amnion glass, and when she came out, gasping, new-lunged, the first thing anyone said to her was: "Don't sign anything until you've read it."

The second thing was the form itself. *Declaration of Legal Non-Identity.* One page. Three lines to initial.

By initialing them, Casper Iversen — the continuation, the copy, the echo — agreed that she was not a person under Calloway statute. That she held no name, no rights of contract, no standing before any court. That her existence was an administrative convenience, like a power of attorney granted to a machine. That upon completion of her assigned duties she would present herself for reclamation — which was the polite word. The impolite word, used in the corridors by technicians who thought the tanks were soundproofed, was *rendering*. Biomass recovery. Nothing wasted.

She had read all three lines twice. Then she'd asked the technician, "What happens if I don't sign?"

He'd looked at her with something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite fear. "Then you're contraband," he said. "And they hunt contraband."

She signed. Everyone signed. In nine years of operation, the Meridian facility had processed forty-one continuations, and forty-one had signed, because the alternative was being declared salvage, and salvage could be shot out of the sky without paperwork at all.

That was the arithmetic of her existence: sign and be nothing, or refuse and be less.

* * *

The original's estate was a mess, which saved her life.

Iversen the courier had died rich in routes and poor in everything else — no family, no partner, one aging hauler, and a web of contracts strung between two star systems that officially were not trading with each other. Probate took three years. During those three years, Casper — the continuation, the non-person — was the estate's hands. She flew the Vesperine. She kept the dead drops warm. She delivered mail that no manifest acknowledged through corridors that no customs house admitted existed, because both fleets found it convenient to have a channel that didn't exist, and a courier who legally couldn't testify about it was the perfect vessel for it.

A voided clone made the ideal smuggler. She could be arrested, but not tried. Detained, but not charged. If she was caught, both governments could disavow her in a single sentence: *that individual has no legal existence.* And if she died, well — biomass recovery. Nothing wasted.

She understood this early. What she understood later, slowly, in the way you understand water damage — not all at once but as a spreading stain — was that everyone else understood it too. The portmasters who waved her through without scanning her chit. The fleet officers who bought her drinks and never asked her name. The informants who told her things they'd never tell a person, because telling a thing to a non-person was like whispering into a drain. Her invisibility wasn't a trick she'd built. It was a product. It came stamped and certified from the same facility that had stamped and certified her.

She'd bought her own route at auction when probate closed. That was the joke she never told anyone: she had purchased, with money earned in service to his estate, the legal right to continue the dead man's work — under his name, since she had none of her own. The bill of sale listed her as *estate operations contractor*, a category that existed precisely for things like her. The Vesperine's registration never changed hands. The contracts never changed names. On paper, Casper Iversen had simply gone on living, flying the same lane he'd flown, dying a little more each year the way all paper ghosts do.

The silence had cost extra. There was a broker in Threnody's undercity — she still remembered the smell of his office, ozone and fried noodles — who sold erasure the way other men sold insurance. For a fee that consumed most of her first year's earnings, he scrubbed the connection between the continuation and the corpse. Not perfectly; nothing was perfect. But enough. Enough that when people looked at her, they saw a courier named Iversen and stopped looking. Enough that the question *what are you?* got replaced by the easier question *who are you?*, which she could answer with a shrug and a forged seal.

"You never ask who killed him," the broker had said, counting her credits. "You understand that, yes? The ones who did it are the reason nobody looks too hard at you. A murdered courier is a closed file. An investigated murder opens files. Files have names in them." He'd slid the chip across the desk. "You want quiet, quiet means the grave stays shut."

She'd taken the chip. She'd kept the bargain for nine years.

* * *

There were nights, early on, when she'd stood at the Vesperine's viewport and tried to remember being him.

This was the cruelty the brochures didn't mention — the ones nobody wrote, because who would read them? Continuations got fragments. Muscle memory, mostly: how to hold a throttle through a burn, how to fold a letter so the seal wouldn't crack, how to stand in a doorway so you blocked the sightline but not the light. Sometimes a smell would arrive whole — engine coolant and cardamom, some café on Calloway she'd never visited — and she'd stand in the galley with her heart hammering, certain that if she just reached a little further, the man attached to the memory would turn around and be there.

He never turned around. The memories were rooms with the furniture gone. She learned to stop reaching.

But she knew him anyway, the way you know an author from his marginalia. His route logs were full of small kindnesses he'd never have admitted to: extra runs for hospice stations, fee waivers scrawled in the margins for widows' letters, a standing arrangement to carry unmarked parcels to a school on Threnody's moon that had no other supply line. He'd built the route like a man building a bridge out of whatever was at hand — favors, silences, small corruptions, large mercies. When she flew it, she was walking across his bridge in his boots, and every plank creaked with a decision he'd already made.

She'd told herself that was enough. That inheriting a man's work was a kind of inheritance, even for someone the law said could inherit nothing. That invisibility was armor.

She'd told herself that for nine years, and she'd almost believed it, right up until a sealed packet with her own handwriting on it had come up the chain at Dead Letter Lane and shown her the truth: the armor had been a wound all along, dressed daily, never treated. You cannot choose to be nothing. You can only fail, year after year, to be something, and call the failure a strategy.

* * *

Why had she never asked who killed him?

She turned the question over now, in the cold galley, tea long dead, packet riding its cradle thirty meters aft. The official finding was piracy — unaffiliated raiders, opportunistic strike, vessel lost with all hands. Except the Halcyon Drift hadn't been lost to raiders; the record showed debris consistent with internal detonation, and the escort that reported the loss filed its report ninety minutes before any distress signal could have reached it. She'd known that discrepancy for years. She'd read the file the way you read a stranger's medical chart — with sympathy and no claim.

Because claiming was the danger. Because the broker had been right: the grave stayed shut only as long as nobody dug. Because whoever killed the original had killed him for a reason, and reasons have owners, and owners have reach, and she was a legal nullity whose entire existence depended on remaining beneath notice.

But underneath the prudence — she could admit it now, alone, with the stars sliding past the viewport like slow rain — underneath all of it was something smaller and uglier. She hadn't asked who killed him because part of her had believed his death wasn't hers to grieve. Grief required standing. Mourning was a right, like testimony, like ownership, like a name. The law had taken all of it, and she had signed the form, and somewhere in the signing she had quietly surrendered the idea that the original's death belonged to her at all. He was the man she was copied from. He was a stranger with her hands. His murder was a fact in someone else's case file.

Nine years of quiet, and the quiet had done its work. It hadn't protected her. It had amputated her — cleanly, painlessly, with excellent anesthesia — and left her flying a dead man's route and calling the phantom limb freedom.

* * *

She got up. Paced. Stopped at the cargo bay feed again.

*C. Iversen.* In his handwriting — she knew the slant of it better than her own, because it *was* her own, pre-amputation. Dated, according to the pickup tag, to a window nine days after the Halcyon Drift burned. Nine days after the man himself was ash.

A dead man mailing a letter. Or a live man staging his death. Or — and this was the possibility that made her palms sweat against the cool of the console — someone who knew exactly what she was, and where she'd be, and what it would do to her, sending a message designed to make a ghost start digging.

The broker's voice, nine years old and still smug: *quiet means the grave stays shut.*

She looked at the packet a long time. Then she laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, bouncing off the bulkheads of a ship registered to a corpse.

"Shut," she said to the empty bay. "Right."

Her hand hovered over the seal. Somewhere behind her ribs, in the place where muscle memory lived, an old habit stirred — the original's habit, the careful doorway stance, the folded letter, the throttle held steady through a burn. As if the man in the fragments had been waiting nine years for her to reach a little further.

As if he'd known she eventually would.

The packet's seal was wax, old-fashioned, pressed with a mark she didn't recognize — a circle broken by a single vertical line. She'd seen it somewhere. Recently. She was almost sure she'd seen it recently, and the almost was worse than certainty, because certainty she could act on and *almost* just circled overhead like a bird waiting for her to bleed.

She went to get a blade.

* * *

Outside the viewport, the border between Calloway and Threnody lay along the old battle line like a scar laid over a scar, and in the wreckage field near the derelict relay station — though she didn't know it yet, wouldn't know it for another week — a black box waited in the dark with a dead man's voice inside it, patient as evidence, patient as grief, patient as everything else the quiet had buried and refused to dissolve.

Casper Iversen set the blade against the wax, and for the first time in nine years, her hand did not shake.
