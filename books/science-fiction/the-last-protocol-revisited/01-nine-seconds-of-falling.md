## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The wind on Kestrel's Landing had a grammar all its own, and after six years Isandro Eriksen had learned to read it the way he'd once read the Vessna codices: by its repetitions, its exceptions, its silences. Tonight it was saying the same thing it always said in the dark season — *nothing is coming, nothing is coming, nothing is coming* — and he sat in the observation dome with his recorder running anyway, because recording was the last discipline he had left.

"Day two thousand one hundred forty-one," he said. His voice sounded strange to him now, thinner than it used to be, worn smooth like a stone in a stream. "Wind from the northeast at thirty knots. Temperature minus sixty-one. The hydroponics bay produced eleven kilos of chard, which Tovi will complain about, because Tovi always complains about chard."

He paused. Outside, the aurora unspooled its green ribbon over the ice shelf, indifferent as ever.

"Vess refiled the supply manifests again today. Fourth time this month. I don't think she's checking them anymore. I think it's a prayer."

He stopped the recorder. Then, out of habit he could no longer distinguish from compulsion, he started it again.

"Addendum. I dreamed in the language again last night."

That was the part he never sent in his transmissions to Earth, though Earth had stopped answering three years ago, so it hardly mattered who heard what. The dreams had started small — a word here, a cadence there, the Vessna concept that translated roughly as *the shape a debt leaves behind* — drifting through his sleep like silt through clear water. Now they were the whole dream. He no longer dreamed in Norwegian, or English, or even in images. He dreamed in structure: nested clauses of light, conditional statements made of cold, the feeling of being *addressed*.

A language should not be able to address you. That was the first thing they taught you at the Institute, back when there was an Institute and back when Isandro had been its most promising student instead of its most inconvenient one. A language is a tool. You hold it. It does not hold you.

He had refused to build a weapon out of the Vessna grammar — a grammar whose deep structures, weaponized, could rewrite the loyalty architecture of any system that parsed them, human or otherwise. They had not forgiven him. Exile to Kestrel's Landing was supposed to be quiet. A failing research colony at the ass-end of the Gliese survey volume, eleven residents and a transmission loop that repeated the colony's status report into the void every ninety-one minutes, waiting for a reply that would never come.

Except that lately, Isandro suspected, something was listening.

* * *

Morning shift began, as it always did, with Tovi Okonkwo kicking the recycler.

"It's lying to me," she announced to the room at large. The room at large was the mess hall, population four, because Priya and Okafor were on nights and the rest were still asleep. "It says coolant pressure nominal. It is not nominal. I can hear it not being nominal."

"You can hear everything not being nominal," said Vess without looking up from her manifests. Administrator Halvard Vess was fifty-eight years old and built like a load-bearing wall, and she had run Kestrel's Landing through budget cuts, supply failures, and the slow evacuation that had taken two hundred souls down to eleven. She ran it still, on principle, the way a captain stays with a ship.

"That's why they pay me," Tovi said. Nobody paid anyone anything anymore. That was the joke. It had been funny for about a year.

Isandro took his tea and sat by the window, where the ice plain ran white to the horizon under a sky the color of old pewter. Eleven people. He catalogued them the way he catalogued everything now, automatically, grammatically. Tovi, the engineer, who talked to machines because the machines were the only things that answered honestly. Vess, the administrator, who kept filing paperwork for a colony that existed mostly as a filing system. Priya and Okafor, the night pair, who had stopped speaking to each other somewhere around year two and now communicated exclusively through shift logs, each entry more elaborately courteous than the last. The Adeyemi twins, botanists, who finished each other's sentences and, increasingly, seemed to finish other people's too. Old Maret Sørensen, the geologist, who spent her days walking the ice shelf and mapping crevasses that would outlast everyone she knew. The Chen brothers, comms and medical, inseparable, quietly terrified. And Ruth Ilves, the youngest of them at twenty-nine, who had been born in transit and had never seen Earth, and who followed Isandro around like a question looking for someone to ask it of.

"Professor Eriksen." She appeared beside him with her own tea, as she did every morning. "Did you sleep?"

"I slept."

"Dream?"

He looked at her. She was the only one who asked. "In the language," he said.

Ruth nodded slowly, as if this were normal, as if dreaming in the syntax of a species extinct for forty thousand years were a thing a person simply did. "What did it say?"

"That's the problem," Isandro said. "It wasn't saying. It was *counting*."

* * *

He should have understood sooner. He told himself that later, in the nine seconds themselves, when there was time for nothing but understanding. The signs had been there for months, arranged in the data like words arranged in a sentence, and he of all people should have read them.

The transmission loop, for instance. Every ninety-one minutes, Kestrel's Landing broadcast its status report into the dark: population, power reserves, atmospheric readings, the date. A dead letter, repeated forever. But three months ago, Isandro had noticed the loop had begun to drift — fractions of a second, accumulating, the way a clock drifts. He'd assumed decay. Dying equipment, dying colony, dying signal.

But the drift wasn't random. He'd plotted it, one insomniac night, and found it converging. All those tiny errors, adding up toward a single point in time. A countdown doesn't announce itself. It just counts.

And the dreams. For weeks now, the dreams had contained numbers. Not spoken numbers — *structural* numbers, the way a poem contains a meter whether or not it mentions counting. Nine. The number nine kept appearing, folded into the grammar of his sleep like a coin sewn into a coat lining. He'd wake with the sensation of having almost understood something enormous, the way you wake from a dream of falling.

On day two thousand one hundred forty-three, at 0317 local time, gravity stuttered.

* * *

There is no word for what it feels like when the ground forgets you. Isandro woke not from sound but from absence — the specific, total absence of weight, the inner ear screaming its confusion — and for one disoriented heartbeat he thought he was dreaming again, that he had finally fallen all the way into the language.

Then the floor came back.

Not gently. It came back like a verdict. Nine seconds — he would measure them later, obsessively, from the seismographs and the reactor logs and the corrupted timestamps of every system on the base — nine seconds in which Kestrel's Landing fell, all of it, the whole colony and the ice beneath it and presumably the planet itself, dropping and catching, dropping and catching, like a heart missing beats.

He was in the corridor before he decided to move. Emergency lighting painted everything the color of a wound. Ahead of him, doors banged open; Tovi's voice carried down the hall, profanity first, analysis second, which was her order of operations for everything.

"Grav plating's fine!" she shouted. "Plating's fine, it's not us, it's — Eriksen, it's not the plating!"

"Then what is it?"

She stood in the junction in her sleep clothes, hair wild, tablet clutched to her chest, and her face in the red light was the face of an engineer confronting the one thing engineers cannot abide: evidence without mechanism.

"Everything," she said. "Every mass on this planet dropped at the same instant. Not the instruments — the *mass*. As if something reached down and shook the whole world by the scruff of its neck. And then—" She stopped. Swallowed. "You need to see the network traffic."

"The colony has no network traffic. We have eleven terminals and a radio."

"Not anymore."

* * *

They gathered in the comms room the way survivors gather, drawn together without deciding to be, and the Chen brothers pulled up the logs with hands that were not quite steady. For four minutes after the flicker ended, every machine on Kestrel's Landing — every terminal, every sensor, the recycler, the reactor controller, Tovi's beloved lying coolant system, the transmission loop itself — had emitted the same signal. Identical. Simultaneous. A single pulse, repeating, on frequencies the hardware should not have been capable of producing.

Tovi ran the waveform three times. "It's not noise," she said finally, very quietly. "Noise doesn't repeat. This repeats. It's structured. It's—" She looked up at Isandro, and there was something new in her face, something between hope and horror. "It's addressed."

The room went still. Eleven people, and you could feel all of them arrive at the same thought at the same moment, the way a body arrives at the bottom of a fall.

Vess spoke first, because Vess always spoke first. "Addressed to whom?"

Isandro didn't answer immediately. He was looking at the waveform, and the waveform was looking back, and six years of exile and three years of silence and two thousand one hundred forty-three days of talking to a recorder in an empty dome were suddenly, terribly, vindicated.

After years of being forgotten, something had noticed them.

That was not a comfort. He understood that already, with the certainty of a man who has studied how predators speak. Things notice you for reasons. And the pulse, rendered into the notation he alone in this room could read, resolved into a single Vessna clause, elegant and ancient and patient, and the clause meant:

*The count is nearly finished.*

Behind him, Ruth whispered, "Professor? What does it say?"

Isandro stared at the screen, at the green ribbon of the signal unspooling beneath the ice, beneath their feet, beneath eleven small lives, and heard himself say:

"It says we've been keeping time for something."
