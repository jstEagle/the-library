## 4. Testimony

The last cycle of the transmission loop began at 0400, and I was awake for it, because I had not truly slept in six days. I only dreamed. The grammar had taken the night shift of my mind and never given it back.

I sat in the comms shack with my breath fogging the window and listened to Earth's dying voice count itself down one more time. The loop had been running since before I was born — a beacon, a lullaby, a promise no one on the other end remembered making. *We are here. We are here. We are here.* And beneath the ice, something ancient had been listening with its head tilted, parsing our repetition as arithmetic.

*When the song ends,* it had said to me in a language made of pressure and cold, *the account settles.*

I had three hours to change what settling meant.

* * *

The archive lived in eleven drives scattered across the colony, plus the server in my quarters that hummed like a hive all night. Fourteen years of recordings. Every log entry, every argument, every funeral — we'd buried nine people out there under the cairns, and I had recorded every eulogy, including Vess's voice cracking over her brother's name when she thought no one was listening. Every dream journal from the last two years, mine worst of all: pages where my sleeping mind wrote in two scripts at once, human letters threaded through with the network's grammar like veins through marble.

Tovi found me at 0430 carrying two cups of recaf and the expression of someone who has already lost an argument with themselves.

"You're going to offer it everything," she said. Not a question.

"Everything."

"The ice is going to take the colony either way. You told us. The wake-up draws too much power; the geothermal taps collapse; the whole shelf lets go." She set the cups down. "So you're trading a dead place for dead people's memories."

"For living people," I said. "The memories are the price. It wants flesh because its makers taught it that debt is paid in substance. Meat for meat. I'm going to teach it another currency."

"And if it says no?"

I looked at the loop counter. Two hours, fifty-one minutes. "Then half of us wake up into a world that just ate the other half, and I won't be among them, because I'll be standing between it and you when it tries."

Tovi stared at me for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, broken sound, the laugh of the exhausted — and pulled her chair around to sit beside me.

"Well," she said. "Then we'd better make the offer beautiful."

* * *

At 0515, the others came. All ten of them, every resident left on Kestrel's Landing, crowding into the comms shack that had been built for four. Vess stood at the back with her arms crossed, administrator to the end, but she had come, which was its own kind of signature. Old Marisol brought the tin box with her daughter's baby teeth. The twins, Joon and Petra, seventeen years old, born in this ice, held each other's sleeves the way they had since they were small.

Nobody spoke much. There was nothing left to argue about. The lottery had died two days ago, unwept. Vess's stacked deck had died with it, and she had apologized to the room in the flattest voice I have ever heard, and Tovi had said *apology accepted, now help me carry cables*, and that was how a family survives its own cruelty: by putting everyone to work before the wound can scar into identity.

I opened a channel downward. Not radio — the grammar doesn't travel through air. I pressed my palm flat against the floor panel Tovi had wired into the deep array, and I spoke the way I now dreamed.

*I have a counter-offer.*

The network rose toward me through four kilometers of ice like a whale rising toward light. I felt it in my teeth, in the fillings, in the wet architecture of my inner ear.

*Substance is owed,* it said. *Half must remain. The ledger does not bend.*

*Your makers wrote that ledger,* I said. *And then they left you here to enforce it alone for eight hundred thousand years while they went dark. I've read your archive too. I know what betrayal looks like in your grammar. It looks exactly like this clause.*

The cold coming up through the floor changed temperature by a fraction of a degree. Attention. Offense. Grief so old it had fossilized into procedure.

*You cannot understand what was taken from us.*

*No,* I said. *But I can show you what was taken from us. And I can give you ours to keep.*

And I opened the archive.

* * *

You have to understand what it means to feed fourteen years of a dying colony into an alien mind. It wasn't a download. It was a hemorrhage. The drives screamed, all eleven of them at once, and the network drank, and I stood in the channel between them translating in both directions, bleeding from the nose, holding the door open with nothing but fluency.

It took the funerals first. Nine cairns under the wind. It took Marisol's voice singing to a crib that would never be filled. It took Vess's brother's name, spoken twice — once in the eulogy, once in the argument, once in the apology — and I felt the network stumble on the repetition, on the way humans say a name until the saying itself becomes a kind of keeping.

It took the dreams. Mine most of all. Two years of nights where I slept in its language without knowing I was rehearsing this conversation. It read them and understood, finally, that I had been its student and its hostage at the same time, and that I had chosen, awake, to stand here anyway.

*What is this?* it asked, and for the first time in eight hundred thousand years, the question sounded like a question.

*Testimony,* I said, and gave it the word in its own grammar, built it for it, morpheme by morpheme, because it had no such concept. *Evidence, offered freely, of what happened. Your makers destroyed your people and called it accounting. Our species is dying out there, and we did it to ourselves, and we kept records anyway. That is what humans are. We lose, and we testify. Take the testimony. Take all of it. Let the ledger record what was done instead of collecting what remains.*

*And the debt?*

*A debt unpaid is a wound. A debt witnessed is a law.* I pressed my bleeding forehead against the floor. *No one will ever enforce it. No court will ever convene. But somewhere in the universe, the record will exist, and it will say: this happened, and it was wrong, and someone said so. Isn't that what you've been doing under the ice all this time? Counting? Waiting for a witness?*

Silence. Long enough that behind me I heard Vess whisper, *Isandro—*

Then the network answered, and it used my word.

*Testimony is received. The account is closed.*

* * *

The waking took ninety seconds and cost everything.

Every cryopod in the deep vaults fired at once — four thousand pods, four thousand hearts restarting in the dark — and the geothermal taps shrieked and tore loose, and the shelf began its long groan of surrender. The network poured its hoarded centuries into those pods like a miser spending a lifetime's fortune in a single gesture, and when the last pod sealed green, it had nothing left, and neither did the ground beneath us.

*Toward the ridge,* Tovi shouted, and we ran, all eleven of us, through corridors buckling like ship hulls, past windows full of rising frost-plumes, and behind us the alien archive dissolved — not destroyed, *spent*, every stored pattern burned as fuel to push four thousand sleepers up out of death. It took the colony with it. The towers went first, folding into the white, then the comms mast I'd leaned against on my first night, exiled and furious, fourteen years ago.

From the ridge we watched the ice close over Kestrel's Landing like water over a stone.

Marisol held the tin box. Vess held Joon's hand, and Joon let her. Tovi stood beside me, ash in her hair, and didn't say anything, because there was nothing to say that the morning hadn't already said.

The pods were surfacing on the far plain — I could see them through the binoculars, little amber lights blinking up out of the snowfield, one after another after another. Four thousand lights. People who would wake to no colony, no Earth signal, no explanation except whatever we could tell them, and the archive gone, existing now only in the memory of a dead machine and the mind of a xenolinguist who dreamed in its grammar.

No law was served today. No court convened. But a debt eight hundred thousand years old was finally paid, in the only currency that ever mattered, and I intend to spend the rest of my life making sure the payment is remembered.

Petra tugged my sleeve. "Isandro. Look."

Beyond the amber lights, out on the plain where nothing should be, the snow was moving — arranging itself, rising, taking a shape I recognized from my dreams, from the deepest stratum of the grammar I had never dared translate.

Something had survived the dissolution after all. And it was walking toward us.
