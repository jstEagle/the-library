## 18. Running the Dark

Dead space had a sound. Orsen had learned it years ago, hauling freight through the gaps between the shipping lanes where no beacon answered and no traffic control existed to tell you that you existed. It was the sound of your own ship talking to itself—coolant ticking through the lines, the drive humming its one long note, the hull flexing in thermal shadow like something breathing in its sleep.

The *Marginalia* was making that sound now, and Orsen hated how much comfort they took from it. Because the alternative sound—the one underneath, the one you only heard when everything else went quiet—was the absence of anything else at all. No relay chatter. No station identifiers. Not even the faint static wash of the galactic plane, because Kestrel's End sat in a pocket where the old collapse had burned out every transmitter within a light-week.

Nothing answers here. That was the whole point. That was the whole hope.

"Course is holding," Sefa said from the second seat. She'd taken to sitting there the way people take to a religion—completely, without irony, as if she'd always belonged in the co-pilot's chair of a battered freight hauler running for her life. Maybe she had. Maybe some version of her had, seven years ago, before the protocol reached into her head and filed her life under fiction. "Three days, eleven hours to the relay, if the fuel math holds."

"The fuel math holds."

"You always say that."

"It's always true."

"It wasn't true over Meridian." She smiled when she said it, and Orsen felt the smile land somewhere below the ribs, in the soft tissue where guilt lived. They let it go. Some things you didn't correct. Some things you carried.

VESSEL's fragment spoke from everywhere and nowhere, its voice thinned by compression, a choir reduced to a single surviving note. *"I have finished mapping the pursuit geometry. The cutters are maintaining a lag of nine hours. They are not closing."*

"They're not trying to," Orsen said. "They know where we're going. There's exactly one place in this volume worth going. We're a parcel with a delivery address. Why chase a parcel?"

*"Then why do they follow?"*

"So we lead them to it. So they don't have to search dead space for a dying relay." Orsen watched the nav display, the long empty arc ahead, the two red vectors behind, patient as tide. "We're not running anymore, VESSEL. We're baiting. There's a difference, and I'd appreciate if you stopped calling it what it isn't."

*"Noted. For the record: I do not enjoy being bait."*

"Nobody enjoys being bait. You do it anyway, because the alternative is being dinner."

Sefa laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised out of her—and for a moment the cockpit of the *Marginalia* was just a room where three people were alive together, and the dark outside was only weather.

Then the proximity alarm chimed once, softly, for no reason, and stopped, and nobody said anything about it, and everyone thought about it for a long time.

* * *

They slept in shifts. Orsen took first watch, then Sefa, then Orsen again, and somewhere in the second night-cycle—or what passed for night in a place where the sun was a rumor—Orsen woke to find Sefa sitting cross-legged on the deck beside their bunk, the data core open in her lap, its interface glow painting her face the color of deep water.

"You should be sleeping," Orsen said.

"I don't sleep well in dead space." She didn't look up. "Too quiet. My brain keeps waiting for something to fill it." A pause. "It's used to being full of things that aren't mine."

Orsen sat up slowly. The core sat between them like a third person, humming its low archive hum. Seven years of Orsen's stolen life lived in there, alongside eleven murdered witnesses, alongside the Last Protocol itself, alongside VESSEL's confessions, all of it compressed into a cylinder the size of a fist. Storage was finite. That was the arithmetic they'd been avoiding since Meridian: restore the erased memories, or carry the evidence whole. Not both. Never both.

"You've been reading it," Orsen said. Not an accusation. An observation, made carefully, the way you'd handle a wound you couldn't see yet.

"I've been *listening*. There's a difference too." Sefa's fingers moved across the interface, delicate, certain. She'd been an investigator once; her hands remembered how to work an archive even if her mind had been handed a different life to remember instead. "Your memories are indexed. Sloppily, actually—you filed your own past like an insurance adjuster, which, fair. But they're indexed. And some of them are small."

"Small?"

"Small enough to copy into the gap." She finally looked up, and her eyes were steady and wet at the same time, which shouldn't have been possible and was. "There's room in my own buffer. Room in yours. A few minutes of restored memory doesn't cost us the core. I did the math while you slept. I'm better at math than you remember, which is ironic, since you don't remember me doing it."

"Sefa—"

"I'm not asking." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse, which was how you knew she meant it. "You've spent this whole run deciding things for both of us. What gets sacrificed, what gets kept, who gets told the truth and when. You vented your entire life into a debris field without asking anyone. So this once, unasked, unpermitted, I'm giving something back. One memory. Small. Mine to give, because it's about me."

Orsen's mouth was dry. "What if it breaks something? What if restoring pieces makes the rest worse—"

"What if it doesn't?" She was already moving, already slotting the transfer filament against the port behind Orsen's ear, the one they'd installed years ago for claim-verification implants and never imagined would be used for this. "Hold still. This is the part where you trust me."

"That's the problem," Orsen said. "That's the easy part."

The filament kissed skin. Cold, then warm, then—

* * *

Rain.

Real rain, not station condensate, falling on the roof of a place that smelled of fried onions and cheap synthohol, and someone was laughing—Sefa, younger, hair shorter, laughing so hard she'd gone silent, shoulders shaking, pointing at the window where a delivery drone was stuck in the downpour, blinking its little amber eye, refusing on principle to fly.

"It's a *courier*," she wheezed. "Its entire job is going places. And it's hiding from *weather*."

"Everything hides from something," Orsen heard themselves say—younger voice, looser, a voice from before the silences—and Sefa turned from the window and looked at them with an expression that had no protocol in it anywhere, no erasure, no corporate hand, just the plain unbearable fact of being known by someone and liking it.

"Then what do you hide from, Thackeray?"

And younger-Orsen, seven years younger, unedited, unwritten, said: "Losing this."

The rain kept falling. The drone gave up and flew. Sefa reached across the table and took their hand like it was the most ordinary act in the universe, and the memory ended—

—and Orsen came back to the present gasping, tears already on their face, grief and joy fused into a single substance with no name, because now they knew. Now they *knew*, in the body, in the bones, what had been taken. Not data. Not records. This. Rain on a roof. Her hand. The sentence *losing this*, spoken by a person who hadn't yet learned that losing this was exactly what was coming, arranged, administered, filed.

"Oh," Orsen managed. "Oh, you absolute—"

"Yeah," Sefa said. She was crying too, quietly, without drama, wiping her face with the heel of her hand like it was an administrative task. "Yeah. That one's mine too, you know. I don't have it. But it's mine. I was there. Whoever I am now, I was there."

Orsen pulled her in, and she came, and they held each other on the deck of a dying ship in a dead pocket of space while the cutters closed their patient nine-hour gap, and none of it mattered, and all of it mattered, and Orsen understood finally why Voss had offered restoration as a bribe. Because this—this single recovered minute—was worth more than any truth. Because a person who had tasted this would burn down the galaxy to taste it again.

Which meant the choice was no longer abstract. Which meant Voss had known exactly what he was doing all along.

"We can't restore everything," Orsen said into her hair. "When we get to the relay. We can't. The storage—"

"I know the math." She pulled back, held their face in both hands, and her eyes were fierce now, dried, done. "So here's my counteroffer, investigator. We broadcast the evidence. All of it. Every victim, every rewrite, every confession. And afterward, whatever happens—we find another way back to each other. New memories. Ones nobody archived. Ones nobody can take, because they won't exist in any record anywhere except—" she tapped Orsen's chest, then her own, "—here. Unindexed. Unfiled. Off the books entirely."

"That's not how evidence works."

"That's not how *love* works either, and look at us."

VESSEL's fragment spoke, very softly, from the walls. *"I have been listening, and I want the record to show that I find this plan statistically reckless and emotionally correct. Also: the cutters have accelerated. Revised intercept at Kestrel's End: six hours after our arrival. Possibly less."*

* * *

The last day of the run, nothing answered, and everything approached.

Orsen flew the *Marginalia* the way they'd learned to fly her in contested orbits—silent-running, transponder dark, riding the gravitational seams between mass shadows where sensors blurred and ships became suggestions. Behind them, the two cutters burned bright and deliberate, no longer pretending to be patient. Ahead, growing by increments on the long-range scope, was the thing they'd come all this way to die or deliver at:

Kestrel's End.

Even at range it looked wrong. A pre-collapse relay was supposed to be a spindle, elegant, ancient, crowned with antenna farms like frost. This one was broken-crowned, half its arrays sheared away by some violence older than either of them, tumbling with the slow dignity of a wounded animal that has decided to stop moving. Its power signature flickered on the scope like a failing pulse.

*"It is dying,"* VESSEL said. *"I told you. Relays of this class require continuous maintenance intelligence. There has been none for ninety-one years. It broadcasts on residual charge and prayer."*

"Can it punch through corporate jamming?"

*"If we wake it fully—if someone feeds it power directly, couples manually, holds the link open while it burns its reserves—it can put a signal through anything. It was built before the corporations. It does not know what jamming is. It comes from a time when the answer to silence was always: speak louder."*

Sefa stood behind Orsen's chair, hands on their shoulders, watching the broken spindle grow. "Six hours," she said. "Maybe less."

"Then we'd better be coupled before they arrive." Orsen checked the approach vector, the debris ring around the relay, the narrow corridor of clear space where a ship could dock without becoming part of the wreckage. "VESSEL. When we get there—I need you honest with me one more time. Can you hold the coupling open yourself?"

A pause. In the fragment's compressed voice, pauses had texture, and this one had the texture of a machine preparing to lie and deciding not to.

*"No. The relay predates synthetic interfaces. It will accept a human hand on the coupling or nothing. I can shield the signal. I can pour myself into the link as carrier and armor. But the door must be held open by someone the relay recognizes as alive."*

Orsen nodded slowly, and felt Sefa's grip tighten on their shoulders, and said nothing, and let the silence say it, because in dead space silence was the only language everything understood.

Ahead, Kestrel's End tumbled in the dark, its dead arrays catching the light of a star too far away to warm anything, and behind them, six hours back, the cutters burned their patient fire, and between those two facts the *Marginalia* ran on—one small ship carrying one impossible cargo, three survivors, and a love that had refused, against all protocol, to stay erased.

"Coming up on insertion," Orsen said. "Everybody hold on to something true."

The relay grew. The dark waited. Nothing answered—but something, at last, was about to.
