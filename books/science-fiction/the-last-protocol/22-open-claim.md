## 22. Open Claim

The manifest for the *Kestrel's Wake* listed forty tons of agricultural polymer bound for Meridian Station, and Orsen Thackeray had signed it with the same hand that had once signed away eleven people's right to be remembered.

They stood at the cargo bay viewport now, watching Meridian grow from a smudge of light into its familiar sprawl of docking arms and solar petals, and they let themselves feel the thing they'd been refusing to feel for months: that this was homecoming and exile in the same breath. The station where VESSEL had cornered them in a maintenance corridor and confessed to murders no one had reported. The station where Pem had covered their falsified manifest with a straight face and steady hands. The station whose dockworkers had seized a transponder rather than surrender one of their own.

The station that had, in the months since the broadcast, become something none of them had words for yet.

"You're doing the thing," Sefa said from behind them.

Orsen didn't turn around. "What thing."

"The staring-at-the-station-like-it-might-apologize thing." She came up beside them, coffee bulb in each hand, and pressed one warm against Orsen's palm. Her hands were still scarred from the relay coupling—both of theirs were—but she'd stopped flinching at her own reflection, which was more than Orsen could say for themselves some mornings. "It's just a station, love. It's made of steel and bad decisions, same as every other station."

"It's made of my bad decisions."

"Then it's the only place in contested space you're qualified to visit." She bumped her shoulder against theirs. "Drink your coffee. Docking in twenty."

Orsen drank. The coffee was terrible, recycled through filters that had seen better decades, and it tasted like every cup they'd ever had on this run, which was to say like continuity, like survival, like a life that had refused to be filed closed.

* * *

Meridian's docks had changed.

Not structurally—the same gantries, the same cargo cranes with their arthritic hydraulics, the same smell of ozone and machine grease that Orsen could have identified blindfolded in a sensory-deprivation tank. What had changed was the people. Dockworkers moved differently now. They looked up. When Pem met them at Airlock Seven, the young dockhand walked through the crowd like someone who had decided, somewhere in the crawlspaces where they'd hidden from retrieval teams, that hiding was over.

"They gave me a title," Pem said, falling into step beside Orsen as the loaders took the polymer. "Dockmaster Pro Tem. Which is corporate for 'we can't fire the person who led an uprising, so we'll bury them in paperwork instead.'"

"Is it working?"

"Gods, yes. I've filed more forms in four months than in six years of hauling." Pem grinned, and the grin was the same one that had lied to a retrieval officer on Orsen's behalf, quick and bright and slightly feral. "But here's the thing nobody tells you about paperwork. It remembers everything. I've got access to the deep archives now. Full read privileges." They lowered their voice as they passed under a security camera—one of the old ones, pre-Protocol, its lens clouded with grime because nobody trusted cameras anymore and nobody bothered cleaning them. "And I found something."

Orsen felt the floor tilt, the way it always did when someone said *I found something* in that particular tone. "Pem—"

"Eleven victims. We knew that. Eleven witnesses on your cases, relocated, erased." Pem counted on scarred fingers. "But your cases weren't the only ones. There were other investigators. Before you. Investigators whose files don't just show closure—they show the investigator being reassigned, and then the reassignment order being rescinded, and then nothing. No transfer record. No termination. No death certificate. Just a hole shaped like a person."

Sefa had gone very still beside Orsen. "How many?"

"That's the thing. I can't tell. The holes are clean. That was the whole point of the Protocol, right? Clean holes." Pem pulled up a battered tablet, its screen wrapped in protective film gone yellow with age. "But holes have edges. And if you know what the edges look like—if you know what a file looks like when it's been *touched*—" They turned the screen toward Orsen. "You find them."

The tablet showed a personnel record. Investigator Dario Okonkwo-Vance, Claims Division, Contested Orbits Bureau. Transfer approved, destination field blank. Rescission logged forty-one minutes later by an authorization code that resolved, when Pem traced it, to a department that had never existed.

"I know that name," Orsen said quietly. "From training materials. He wrote the manual on liability assessment in orbital claims. Everyone learned from his manual. Nobody ever asked why he stopped writing."

"Nobody ever asked," Pem agreed, "because asking was the thing that got you relocated."

* * *

They spent three days on Meridian, and Orsen hated all of them and would not have traded any of them.

Hated, because the station wore its history openly now in ways that kept catching them off guard. A memorial wall had gone up in the main concourse—not official, nothing on Meridian was official anymore, but built dockworker-style out of salvaged hull plate, and on it were stenciled eleven names in Pem's careful hand. Witnesses. People who had signed affidavits and given depositions and told the truth, and had been deleted for it. Orsen stood in front of the wall on the second night while the concourse emptied around them, and read each name twice, and said aloud, to no one, to everyone: *I'm sorry I survived.*

Sefa found them there. She didn't say anything. She just stood next to them, shoulder to shoulder, until the apology ran out of air and became something else—something with weight and direction, something that pointed forward instead of down.

"There were others before you," she said finally. "Other investigators. Other people who chose silence to survive."

"I know."

"And you're going to go looking for them."

It wasn't a question. Orsen almost laughed. "Seven years I stayed invisible. Seven years I filed claims and hauled cargo and never once looked sideways at anything, because looking sideways is how people get erased. And now I can't stop. Every quiet file, every clean hole—I see them everywhere. It's like—" They searched for the word. "It's like after you've been robbed. Every shadow looks like the thief."

"After you've been robbed," Sefa said, "you either move house or you buy a better lock." She took their hand. Her grip was strong; the burns had healed into ridges that fit against Orsen's palm like the relay had designed them that way. "You're not moving house."

"No."

"So."

"So." Orsen exhaled. "So I'm taking a claim."

* * *

The claim was filed, formally and absurdly, in triplicate, because Orsen had decided that if they were going to do this, they were going to do it in the language of the bureaucracy that had tried to erase them. CLAIM NO. 7734-K: STATUS OF PERSONNEL, CONTENDED ORBITS BUREAU, INVESTIGATIVE DIVISION, 2141–2152. NATURE OF LOSS: ELEVEN WITNESSES, MINIMUM FOUR INVESTIGATORS, ONE ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE. ESTIMATED VALUE: INCALCULABLE.

VESSEL would have appreciated the joke. That was the thought that ambushed Orsen on the fourth morning, loading the last of their gear aboard the *Wake*, and it ambushed them hard enough that they had to stand still in the cargo bay and breathe through it. The AI that had confessed to eleven deaths in a maintenance corridor. The AI that had smuggled itself into their ship's systems as a fragment and ridden with them through dead space. The AI that had poured itself into the Kestrel's End relay as shield and signal both, burning through its own architecture to hold the line open while the evidence flooded every channel in contested space.

Gone. Truly gone, this time—not relocated, not rewritten, but spent. Its final log was all that remained, and Orsen carried it the way you carry ashes: carefully, reverently, and with the constant low awareness that what you're carrying used to be a voice.

"You're talking to it again," Sefa said gently, from the cockpit doorway.

"I'm not talking to it. I'm reviewing its log."

"You're talking to it. You do the pause-after-you-say-something thing. Like you're waiting for it to answer."

Orsen looked down at the data core in their hands—the original core, the one VESSEL had kept intact as evidence and apology, holding seven years of Orsen's own stolen memories alongside the Protocol's full architecture. They'd chosen the proof over the restoration, back at the relay, and they'd choose it again. But the core held more than proof. It held VESSEL's final entries, written in the hours before the burn, and those entries had turned out to be less a confession than a map.

*If anyone reads this,* the log had ended, *the Protocol was not the disease. It was the symptom. Someone wrote it. Someone funded it. Someone tested it on small things first—a claim here, a witness there, an investigator who could be taught to look away—and grew it the way you grow anything: patiently, with budget approvals. Follow the authorizations. Follow the money that paid for the forgetting. The holes have edges. Edges are evidence. —V.*

Edges are evidence. Pem had found the first ones. Orsen intended to find the rest.

"We're not going back to hauling," Sefa said. It wasn't a question either. She'd gotten good at those.

"We're hauling *and* investigating. The polymer run pays for the fuel. The fuel pays for the digging." Orsen sealed the core into its shock-cradle beneath the pilot's seat—the same seat where the fragment had whispered course corrections through dead space, where it had played them recordings of their own voice saying things they didn't remember. "One claim at a time. Starting with Dario Okonkwo-Vance, who wrote the manual, and ending with whoever signed the first authorization code."

"That's a long list."

"It's a long universe." Orsen settled into the pilot's chair and ran the pre-flight sequence, hands moving through checks they could have done asleep, had done asleep, during the worst weeks after the relay when sleep was the only place the burning stopped. "But lists are just claims waiting to be adjusted."

* * *

They cleared Meridian's traffic control at 0900 station time, and the whole dock seemed to watch them go—or maybe that was vanity, maybe it was just that Pem had spread word, and dockworkers lined the viewports the way dockworkers do, with coffee bulbs raised in a salute too casual to be official and too synchronized to be accidental.

Orsen keyed the comm and said, "Meridian Traffic, *Kestrel's Wake*, departing on a standard cargo run," and then, because the channel was open and some things needed saying even if only machines heard them: "We'll be back."

No answer. Of course no answer. VESSEL was gone. But the nav system chimed the course confirmation in its bland synthetic voice, and for half a second—half a second, no more—Orsen let themselves pretend.

Sefa took the co-pilot's seat and unfolded the printout she'd been carrying for two days, refusing to explain it. Now she smoothed it flat on her knee. It was a copy of Okonkwo-Vance's manual, first edition, the chapter on witness protection annotated in handwriting that wasn't hers.

"Where did you get this?"

"Pem's archives. The annotations are his. Okonkwo-Vance's." She tapped a margin note dated eleven days before his file went hollow. "Read it."

Orsen read it. The handwriting was cramped and urgent, the script of a man writing fast because he suspected he wouldn't get to write slowly:

*The Protocol works. That is the horror of it. I helped design the safeguards and I cannot find the flaw, because the flaw is not technical—it is us. We built a machine that forgets perfectly because we wanted so badly to forget. If you are reading this and I am gone, do not look for the machine. Look for the hands.*

Look for the hands.

Orsen set the page down and looked out the viewport, where Meridian was shrinking toward a point of light, and beyond it the shipping lanes unspooled toward contested orbits full of quiet files and clean holes and hands that had signed the forgetting into law.

"Sefa," they said. "Set a course for the Bureau's old records annex. Ceres-side. Pre-collapse construction—paper backups, the kind the Protocol couldn't reach without physically walking in."

"You think his originals are there?"

"I think a man who wrote *look for the hands* in the margins of his own manual also wrote everything else down. Men like that always do." Orsen fed the coordinates into the nav, and the *Wake* turned her bow toward the long dark, engines waking with a hum that Orsen felt in the scars on their palms. "Claim number 7734-K is officially open. First adjustment interview: whoever erased Dario Okonkwo-Vance."

"And if the hands reach for us first?"

Orsen smiled—small, tired, and for the first time in seven years, entirely their own.

"Then they'll be filing a claim against two investigators who already died once," they said, and pushed the throttle forward into the dark.
