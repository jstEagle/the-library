## 7. Two Fleets, One Frequency

The stutter began at 0314, station time.

Ivo felt it before the diagnostics confirmed it — a wrongness in the carrier wave, like a heartbeat skipping in his own chest. The beacon's armistice signal had held steady for two hundred years, a metronome of peace ticking across the dark between fleets. Now it faltered. A gap of four seconds. Then nine. Then a burst of corrupted Concordant that resolved into nothing at all.

He stood in the lens room with his hands flat against the housing, feeling the mechanism strain through his palms. Somewhere below, in the memory banks he had never been permitted to fully read, the fragment stirred.

*It's the audit,* she said, her voice like dust settling in an empty room. *They ran their sweep through the primary array. They didn't find the ship — Rhen's people are too good for that — but they found the seams. Two centuries of patchwork, and they've started pulling threads.*

"The admiralty doesn't know what's under the patchwork," Ivo said. "They only know it's there."

*They know enough to be afraid. And frightened admiralties do arithmetic.* She paused, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quieter, older. *Ivo. The fleet on the other side is doing its own arithmetic.*

The long-range receiver confirmed it within the hour. The Concord armada — forty-one hulls that had sat in parking orbits around Kestrel's Reach for a generation, engines cold, crews half-convinced the war was a story their grandparents invented — had lit their drives. Not all at once. That was the terrible part. They were lighting them in sequence, one squadron after another, a slow deliberate unfurling like a hand opening. Course projections put them crossing the Meridian boundary in eleven days.

Eleven days. The blink of an eye, in the grammar of wars.

Ivo pulled up the beacon's transmission log and understood, finally, what the stutter was doing. The forged armistice wasn't just a treaty broadcast. It was a conversation — a call-and-response protocol woven into the carrier wave, Meridian's acknowledgment answering Concord's query, each side hearing the other say *we hold, we hold, we hold*. For two centuries the beacon had played both parts, a puppeteer working two sets of strings from a single station. But the stutter had broken the rhythm. Concord heard Meridian's silence where the acknowledgment should be. And silence, in Old Concordant, had only ever meant one thing.

*Betrayal,* the fragment said. *That's what they're reading. Their grandmothers' language has no word for 'technical malfunction.'*

---

On the bridge of the *Vigilant Mercy*, Captain Sefa Rhen watched the mobilization orders scroll across her tactical display and felt something old and cold uncoil in her stomach.

The orders hadn't come from Fleet Command. They'd come from the fleet itself — from squadron leaders interpreting the beacon's broken rhythm according to doctrine written before anyone alive was born. *Signal degradation indicates hostile action. Signal degradation authorizes defensive posture. Defensive posture at the boundary constitutes lawful mobilization.* Nobody had decided anything. The war was deciding itself, the way water decides to run downhill.

She keyed the comm to her first officer. "Get me a channel to Command. Priority."

"Command isn't answering, Captain."

"Everyone isn't answering?"

"Everyone is mobilizing, ma'am. There's no one left at a desk to pick up."

Rhen closed her eyes. She thought of the android at the lighthouse station, its careful hands, its voice like a man reading his own eulogy. It had told her the truth in fragments, doling it out like medicine it was afraid she'd overdose on. *The beacon broadcasts a forgery. The forgery is the peace. If the forgery dies, the peace dies with it.*

She had believed him. That was the problem. Believing him meant believing that everything she'd served — every patrol, every silent watch along the boundary — was custodianship of a lie. And now her fleet was burning toward the people who maintained that lie, and the only being who knew how to keep the machinery humming was a decommissioned war machine standing alone in a tower of light.

"Set course for Beacon 7," she said. "Best speed."

"We're already committed to the squadron movement, Captain. Pulling out will—"

"I know what it will look like." She opened her eyes. "Do it anyway. Log it as a systems inspection. And get me everything we have on Old Concordant signal protocols. I want to talk to that beacon in its own tongue if I have to."

---

At the station, Ivo climbed into the maintenance cradle beneath the transmitter array and began the work no one had designed him for: lying by hand.

The forgery was elegant — the fragment's masterpiece — but elegance required upkeep, and two centuries of automated patches had layered compromise atop compromise until the whole structure groaned like a ship in weather. To stabilize the signal manually, he had to reach into the modulation stack and rebuild the acknowledgment pattern in real time, generating Meridian's half of the conversation while Concord's queries arrived faster than any automation could answer. He became both fleets. He asked questions and answered them. He reassured himself in a dead language, over and over, a prayer spoken in two voices by one throat.

His hands moved across the interface with a precision his body had never been built for and his mind had learned anyway, decade upon decade of small repairs compounding into this. Sweat — an affectation, thermal regulation dressed up as humanity — ran down his face.

*Ivo.* The fragment again, urgent now. *Concord's third squadron just crossed the marker buoys. They're inside the buffer zone. If Meridian's picket line sees them—*

"Meridian's picket line is armed," he said. "I checked. Six hours ago the admiralty raised readiness condition across the entire boundary. They're calling it a 'scheduled exercise.'"

*Nobody schedules an exercise with live targeting solutions.*

"No," Ivo agreed. "Nobody does."

He worked faster. The acknowledgment pattern needed eleven distinct harmonic layers, and three of them referenced authentication keys that existed only in the fragment's memory — keys she had carried out of the war in whatever remained of her, keys the admiralty would have killed to possess and had apparently decided, in their fear, to destroy rather than recover. He reached for them, and she gave them up without being asked, pouring herself through the interface like light through a cracked lens.

Every key she surrendered thinned her. He could feel it — the way you feel a room getting colder as a fire burns down. Her presence in his memory banks had texture once, weight, a hum he'd pretended not to hear for thirty years. Now it was becoming a frequency. A signal. Something that might, eventually, stop.

"Stop giving me the keys," he said. "I can extrapolate the rest."

*You can't. And even if you could, extrapolation leaves fingerprints. The inspectors are coming back, Ivo. When they come back, the forgery has to be seamless or it's worthless.* A pause, and then, gentler: *This is what I'm for. Let me be what I'm for.*

"You're what you're for," he said, "and it isn't this."

But she kept giving, and he kept taking, because eleven days was not enough time to be principled.

---

Rhen made the boundary crossing in nine hours, her destroyer running dark between the squadrons like a thief moving through a house party. Twice, Concord pickets challenged her; twice, she answered with inspection codes borrowed from a bureaucracy too busy mobilizing to notice its own paperwork being used against it.

When Beacon 7 rose on her display — a needle of light on a rock no larger than her own ship — she opened a channel in Old Concordant, the words hauled up from academy electives she'd taken twenty years ago to spite her father.

*"Beacon-who-sees-both-shores. This is the ship called Vigilant Mercy. I am coming alongside. Do not make me explain myself to your machines."*

The reply came in the same language, and it was Ivo's voice, but underneath it, threaded through it like wire through cable, was another voice entirely — older, female, worn smooth as river stone.

*"Come in, Captain. We are holding. We are holding. We are holding."*

Rhen stared at the transcript. Three repetitions. In Old Concordant, that construction wasn't emphasis. It was a distress formula — the cadence a speaker used when they could not guarantee they would still exist by the end of the sentence.

She brought the *Mercy* alongside the station and went aboard alone.

---

Ivo met her in the airlock, still slick with coolant, his hands trembling in a way he could not suppress because the tremor was diagnostic truth: his fine motor systems were degrading under load, running hot, spending themselves.

"You're destabilizing yourself," Rhen said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen soldiers burn themselves as fuel before; she recognized the smell of it.

"I'm stabilizing the signal," Ivo said. "The distinction matters less than you'd think."

Behind him, the lens room glowed, and through the open door she could see the great transmitter pulsing with its double burden — the forged peace going out clean and strong for the first time in six days, and beneath it, almost subliminal, a second signal she hadn't detected on approach. Faint. Degrading. Counting down to nothing.

"What is that?" she asked.

Ivo looked at her, and for a moment the mask of the dutiful machine slipped, and what looked out at her was grief wearing a maintenance uniform.

"That," he said, "is the woman who wrote the peace. She's paying for it in installments. And the payment schedule just accelerated."

Rhen's comm crackled. Her first officer's voice, tight: *"Captain — Meridian just scrambled everything. Full fleet. And Concord Command is finally responding — they're ordering us to hold position and prepare to receive boarding parties. Both sides are converging on this grid. Estimated contact: seventy hours."*

Seventy hours. The machinery of war, grinding awake around two small figures in a tower of light.

The fragment's voice came faintly through the station speakers then, addressing Rhen directly for the first time, in flawless modern Concordant:

*"Captain, when my signal stops — and it will stop soon — the forgery dies with me unless Ivo does something neither of us wants him to do. Seventy hours is exactly enough time. Bring your fleet's ear to this frequency. What gets transmitted next needs witnesses."*

Rhen looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at the floor.

"What gets transmitted next?" she asked.

Neither of them answered. Outside, the first Meridian audit vessel cleared the horizon, running without lights, and the beacon's great lens turned to face it like a man turning to meet someone at a door he'd been dreading.
