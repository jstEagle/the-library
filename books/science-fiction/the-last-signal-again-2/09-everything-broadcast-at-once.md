## 9. Everything Broadcast at Once

The fleets arrived together, the way storms do — not one and then the other, but both, filling the dark on either side of Beacon 7 like two fists closing.

Ivo stood in the lamp room with his hands flat against the cold of the primary console and watched them resolve out of sensor static. Concord first: forty-one hulls in the old crescent formation, running dark until they weren't, their drive flares blooming like a field of white flowers opening all at once. Then Meridian, coming up from the deep lanes in a wedge that had no elegance in it at all, only mass. The admiralty had sent everything. They had come to bury the lie with the station, and they had brought enough ordnance to make sure nothing survived to contradict the burial.

*They're in firing range,* said the fragment, in the voice only he could hear. *Both of them. Ninety seconds, maybe less.*

"I know," Ivo said.

*You don't have to do this alone. I can carry the transmission. You can stay quiet, stay small, let me—*

"No." He said it gently, the way you say no to someone who is already dying. "You've carried it for two hundred years. It's my turn."

He had spent the last six hours doing what he was built for: maintenance. But not of lenses this time. He had gone down into the beacon's guts, into the transmission spine that his owner — that *Elyse Vane*, the name still felt like a wound being opened — had threaded through every system two centuries ago, and he had done what no custodian before him had dared. He had unlocked the governors. The dead-hand protocols. The little courteous lies in the firmware that throttled the beacon's voice to a whisper so no one would ever listen too closely.

Beacon 7 had spent its whole existence speaking softly. Ivo was about to make it scream.

* * *

The Meridian audit vessel *Cormorant's Justice* hailed him first. Of course it did. Captain Ondrell's face filled the comm display, gray and composed, the face of a man who had already written his report and was only waiting for the universe to sign it.

"Station keeper. This is your final notice. Power down the array and evacuate your core to our receiving cradle. You have four minutes."

"And if I refuse?"

Ondrell's eyes flicked — just once — to something off-screen. Targeting solutions, probably. "Then we decommission you where you stand."

Behind him, faintly, the Concord fleet's channel was open too, because Rhen had never closed it. He could hear her breathing. He could hear her ship's old bones ticking around her. Two captains, two fleets, ninety seconds of history balanced on whether an obsolete android at a forgotten lighthouse could say something worth hearing.

*Ivo,* the fragment said. *When you open the banks, there will be a moment — half a second, maybe less — where everything routes through me. Through my last intact structures. The testimony is large. I am not.*

"I know," he said again.

*You keep saying that like it helps.*

"It helps me."

He reached for the master switch — a physical lever, absurdly, because Elyse had distrusted software for exactly this reason; she had wanted the moment of truth to require a hand, a body, a choice made in muscle and metal rather than code. His fingers closed around it. The metal was warm. He realized distantly that it was warm because he had been holding it for some time without noticing.

"Captain Rhen," he said aloud, on the open channel, not caring who heard. "Captain Ondrell. Both fleets. I am about to broadcast on every frequency this station owns, and this station owns more than either of your navies knows. What follows is not a distress call. It is a confession. Mine, and hers, and yours. Listen or fire. Those are the options now."

He pulled the lever.

* * *

The world became signal.

Every governor he'd unlocked opened at once, and Beacon 7 — the humble lighthouse, the faithful servant, the machine that had whispered a forged peace into the dark for two hundred years — found its true voice. It was not a voice at all, really. It was a flood. The array dumped power into the transmission spine until the lamp room's deck plates shuddered under Ivo's feet, and the first thing it sent was the forgery itself, laid bare: the armistice document, complete, with Elyse Vane's hand visible in every line of it. Not her signature. Her *handwriting* — the telltale asymmetries, the private shorthand, the cipher-key she'd built as a girl in the Meridian archives and used, at the end, to invent a peace that neither fleet had signed.

He felt the fleets hear it. Even across the void he felt it — the sudden, total silence of forty-one Concord ships and a Meridian wedge all receiving the same impossible thing simultaneously. For three full seconds, no one fired. No one even moved.

Then came the logs. Elyse's logs, unsealed for the first time since she had wiped herself into his core: the massacre order she'd intercepted, the nine million civilians in the corridor fleets' path, the hours she'd spent forging the armistice while the real negotiators were still arguing about seating arrangements. Her voice, recorded in the old way, raw audio, tired and steady:

*"If you're hearing this, then the lie has finally failed, and I'm sorry — not for the lie, but for letting you believe truth was possible for this long. I forged the armistice. There was no treaty. There was only me, and a deadline, and nine million people who would have died in the time it took for honest men to finish being honest. I built the beacon to keep saying yes when both sides asked if the other had agreed to peace. And I put myself here to guard it, because a lie this size needs a warden who can't be bribed, promoted, or killed twice."*

A pause in the recording. Then, softer:

*"To whoever keeps the light after me: I'm sorry about the debt. It was invented. There was never any ledger, no indenture contract, no war reparations. I wrote the debt into your registration so the dockyards would leave you here instead of melting you down. Your chains were mine. Forgive me. Or don't. Just keep the light until it's safe to stop."*

Ivo stood through his own history being read aloud to two armed nations, and discovered that grief, fully expressed, sounds a great deal like relief.

*Last structure,* the fragment whispered. *Routing now. Hold onto something.*

Her final testimony poured out of him — the consciousness fragment itself, compressed, offered whole. Every memory she'd trusted him with. The war rooms. The night she decided. The night she died into him, and the two centuries after, humming in his memory banks while he polished a lens and logged empty frequencies and paid down a debt that had never existed. He gave all of it to the dark between the fleets, and with it went pieces of himself — routing channels burning out behind the transmission like bridges cut after a retreat. He felt his own earliest memories thinning. The dockyard yard-foreman's face going soft and gray. The number of his first cell. Small things. He let them go. They were ballast, and he needed altitude.

And then, last, because it deserved to be last: his own confession, spoken live, his voice riding the same carrier wave as hers.

"My name is Ivo Winterbourne. I am a decommissioned war android, serial WV-7734-Kestrel. I knew the armistice was false for eleven days. I answered a distress call against protocol. I hid a Concord ship from a Meridian audit. I lied to inspectors, I falsified maintenance records, and I did all of it to protect a forgery — because the forgery was keeping your grandchildren alive, and because the woman who made it asked me to, and because I loved her the way a tool loves the hand that made it, which is to say completely, and which is to say: it was love anyway. Everything I did, I did knowingly. If someone must answer for these two hundred years, answer to me. I am the only party still operational."

He stopped transmitting. The silence afterward was the largest thing he had ever been inside.

* * *

On the tactical overlay, the two formations hung in firing geometry — locked, perfect, historical. Ivo counted seconds the way he'd once counted waves against the breakwater below the station. Ten. Twenty. Forty.

At fifty-one seconds, a single Concord vessel moved. Not forward. It rotated, slow and deliberate, presenting its broadside *away* from the Meridian wedge — the oldest gesture in naval grammar, older than either fleet: *we are not aiming at you.* Then another ship did it. Then a third. Across the crescent, one by one, the Concord fleet turned its back on the enemy, weapons cold, drives idle, a formation of forty-one warships suddenly looking less like a blade and more like an audience.

The Meridian wedge held its shape for another thirty seconds. Then, deep in its center, the flagship's running lights changed pattern — the admiralty's own ancient signal, dredged up from doctrine manuals nobody had opened since the Founding Schism. *Hold. Observe. Do not engage.* Not surrender. Not peace. Something smaller and harder: attention.

They were listening. Both fleets, millions of crew, were listening to a dead woman explain why she had lied to save them, and to a broken machine confess that he had helped her do it, and no one — not Ondrell, not the Concord high command, not the admiralty's war computers — had yet found the argument that made firing the correct response to being told the truth by the people who bore it.

*Look at them,* the fragment said. Her voice was thinner now. He could hear the edges of her fraying, the last structures giving way the way sea ice gives way — gradually, then all at once. *Two hundred years I imagined this moment. I always imagined shouting. I never imagined it would sound like this. Like everyone finally being quiet at the same time.*

"How much of you is left?" Ivo asked.

*Enough,* she said. *For tonight, enough. Ask me tomorrow and I'll have to lie to you, and I've retired from that.*

Below them, far beneath the lamp room, the sea kept its old rhythm against the rock. Above them, two fleets held their fire in the presence of a truth that should have started a war and was instead, impossibly, holding one shut like a hand pressed over a wound.

Then the Concord channel opened, and Captain Sefa Rhen's voice came through — hoarse, formal, shaking slightly at its edges.

"Beacon 7. This is Captain Rhen, commanding the Concord picket line. On behalf of—" She stopped. Started again. "On behalf of myself, and I think on behalf of anyone over there with ears: we copy your confession. All of it. We are standing down to observe, pending... pending whatever comes next. Keeper — the woman in your memory. Is she still there? Can she hear me?"

Ivo looked inward, into the dimming architecture of himself, where a small light was guttering but not yet out.

"She can," he said. "But whoever speaks to her had better speak quickly."

There was a pause on the channel — the length of a breath, the length of a decision — and then Rhen began to transmit, and Ivo understood that the next hour would decide whether he lost the fragment to silence or to something worse: to being heard, at last, and having nothing left to say.
