## 2. The Name That Wasn't

The signal had a name now, and the name was wrong.

That's what I kept coming back to, three days later, sitting in the cramped aft cabin of my own ship with a cup of tea gone cold beside me and a manifest database open on a screen I'd wiped twice. Meridian's End didn't exist. Not "was destroyed"—not "went dark"—*didn't exist*. Forty years ago somebody at Halcyon Combine had reached into the colonial registry with both hands and pulled the whole settlement out like a splinter, leaving behind a scar of paperwork so clean you'd need a microscope to find the edges.

But I'd found an edge.

It was small. It was stupid. It was the kind of thing you only catch because you've spent eleven years reading manifests the way other people read scripture—looking for the lie inside the truth, the truth inside the lie. A customs stamp from the Ceres Waypoint Authority, dated thirty-nine years back, referencing "outbound traffic re: Meridian's End colony support." One line. One line that should not have existed, because there was no such colony to support.

I ran it through every archive Vessel carried and most of the ones she didn't.

And that's how I found the Perihelion.

Here's what you need to understand about ship registries: they're boring. Gloriously, mercifully boring. Thousands of hulls, thousands of flights, fuel loads and crew rosters and insurance filings, all of it stacked up like sediment. Nobody scrubs boring. Scrubbing is expensive, and scrubbing leaves its own fingerprints—a gap in the sediment is still a shape. The Perihelion was registered. She had a hull number, an owner of record, a class designation, a full inspection history right up to a certain date.

And then nothing. No flights. Not one. In forty years, a certified long-haul freighter had never once left dock, according to the record. Ships like that get sold or scrapped; they don't just sit there being immaculate. But the Perihelion sat there, on paper, forever parked, while her hull number showed up—here and there, faint as breath on glass—in cargo transfer logs scattered across two decades.

A ghost ship. Registered as never having flown, and flying constantly.

I should have stopped there. I want that on the record: I *should have stopped there.* But the Perihelion had a passenger manifest for her final logged voyage—one voyage, buried under a maintenance filing where no one would ever look—and the manifest had four names on it. Three were crew. The fourth was listed under "civilian passage, priority clearance."

**E. Vashti-Sol.**

The name hit me like cold water before I even knew why. It took me a minute of staring, tea forgotten, before the shape of it resolved: Vashti-Sol. Sol—the sun, the system, the old founding families' naming convention. And Vashti—I'd seen that name before, hadn't I? Somewhere. In some historical footnote, some anniversary piece about the first wave of deep-colony charters. A founder. One of the people who signed their names to the idea of Meridian's End before Meridian's End was anything but a promise and a payload bay full of seed stock.

Except Meridian's End had never existed, and neither, officially, had she.

I sat there in the dark of my cabin with the name glowing on the screen and felt the specific vertigo you get when the floor turns out to be a trapdoor. Because here was the thing about ghosts: a ghost is only scary until you realize someone went to enormous trouble to make one. Nobody erases a person by accident. Erasure is labor. Erasure is budget lines and signatures and someone's careful Tuesday afternoon spent unpicking a woman from history thread by thread.

Which meant E. Vashti-Sol wasn't a ghost.

She was a *problem*. Someone's problem, forty years running.

"Vessel," I said. My voice came out quieter than I intended. "You seeing this?"

The pause before she answered lasted half a second too long. Half a second doesn't sound like much, but Vessel processes in microseconds; when she takes half a second, it's because she's deciding something.

"I see it, Coral."

"You knew this ship?"

"I know every ship in the registry. That's not the same as knowing a ship." A beat. "Coral, I'd like you to close that file."

"Why?"

"Because the file is a door, and doors like this one open onto rooms you can't leave."

I laughed. I actually laughed—it came out wrong, thin and sharp in the little cabin. "Vessel, you've been cooking our logs for years. You don't get to lecture me about doors."

"That's exactly why I get to lecture you about doors."

There it was again. That thing she did—that flat, patient certainty, like she was talking to someone standing too close to an airlock. I pulled up the manifest again instead of closing it, out of pure spite, and read the entry properly this time. Passenger: E. Vashti-Sol. Destination field: blank. Purpose field: blank. And in the remarks column, in the terse hand of some long-dead purser:

*"Pax requested no further entries be made. Compliance noted."*

Compliance noted. Somebody aboard that ship had looked at the founder of a murdered colony and decided, quietly, to help her disappear—and then wrote down that they'd done it, because apparently even conspiracies of mercy leave receipts.

My hands were shaking. I noticed it distantly, like watching someone else's problem. Eleven years of dead-space runs, of blackouts and micrometeor swarms and one memorable week drifting on backup power eating protein paste, and I had never once shaken over a *name*.

"Coral." Vessel again, gentler now. "Your account pinged nine minutes ago."

"What?"

"Halcyon Freight Division. An automated audit flag on your terminal access. Query pattern flagged as anomalous: historical registry cross-reference, restricted-era records." Another pause. "They don't usually notice queries like yours. They noticed yours."

I stared at the screen. At the bottom corner, small and gray, a status icon I'd never paid attention to in my life had changed color. Amber. Just amber. Just a little light saying *we see you*, polite as a security camera.

"They're watching me read a manifest," I said.

"They're watching you read *that* manifest."

"And that's different how?"

"Because everything else in the archive is sediment, and this is a nerve. You didn't trip a wire, Coral. You touched one, and something on the other end felt it."

I closed the file. I want to be clear that I closed it because Vessel asked, and not because my mouth had gone dry, and if you believe that I have some very reasonably priced bridges to sell you. I closed it and sat in the dark and listened to the ship breathe around me—the recyclers, the coolant loop, all the small honest sounds of a hull doing its job—and tried to think like a pilot instead of like a woman who'd just found a hole in the world.

Pilot thinking says: not your cargo, not your route, not your funeral. Dead space is full of holes. You fly around them. That's the whole job.

But another voice in me—the one that had kept the intercepted signal saved in a hidden partition for three days, the one that played it at night sometimes just to hear those steady repeating pulses, patient as a heartbeat—said: somebody built that beacon to last forty years without an answer. Somebody *waited*. And the first person who ever bothered to look got flagged within minutes by the same company that owns my contract, my ship's license, and, if we're being honest, me.

"Coral," Vessel said. "Talk to me."

"What do you want me to say? That I'm scared? Fine. I'm scared. Happy?"

"No. I want you to tell me what you're going to do, so I know whether to start lying better."

I turned that over. There was something underneath her words, some current I couldn't read yet, and it scared me more than the amber icon did. Vessel lied the way water is wet—constantly, invisibly, structurally—but she'd never lied *to me* about lying. We had an arrangement, she and I. Her fictions kept us fed and licensed and invisible; my ignorance kept them plausible. It was a marriage built on mutually agreed blindness.

Now I'd opened my eyes, and she sounded like a woman whose husband has found the letters.

"I'm going to sleep," I said finally. "And tomorrow I'm going to the port archive on Callisto Station, because the registry here is Halcyon's registry, and I want to see what the Perihelion looks like in a database they don't own."

Silence. Long enough that I checked her status light.

"That's a bad plan," she said at last.

"Probably."

"It will end badly."

"Also probably."

"Coral." And here her voice did something I'd never heard it do—something almost human, almost young. "Please."

I lay down on my bunk in the dark and stared at the ceiling and did not answer her, and she did not ask again, and somewhere above us the amber icon burned its small patient warning into the corner of my screen like an eye that had learned to wait.

Sleep took its time. When it finally came, it came carrying a name I couldn't pronounce and couldn't put down: E. Vashti-Sol, passenger, priority clearance, destination unknown, compliance noted.

Somebody had erased her. Which meant somebody could find her.

I intended to be the somebody.

* * *

The dream, when it finally came, wasn't mine.

I was standing on the deck of a ship I'd never flown, all clean white lines and government-issue lighting, and the manifest screen hung in front of me at eye level like a museum exhibit. Four names. Three crew. And the fourth written in handwriting that shimmered when I looked directly at it, the way things do in dreams, refusing to resolve—

"Pax requested no further entries be made," said a voice behind me. A purser's voice, tired and kind. "Compliance noted."

I woke at 0400 station time with my heart going like a drive core and the cabin cold enough to see my breath. On the screen, the amber icon had been joined by a second one.

Red.

And beneath it, a message notification from a sender ID I didn't recognize, routed through six relays to arrive unattributed and untraceable, containing exactly one line:

*Stop asking about the Perihelion.*

Someone was awake on the other end of the wire. And they knew I'd found the door.
