## 9. The Last Signal

The relay at the edge of dead space looked like a crucifix someone had dropped and never bothered to pick up. Forty years of license fees unpaid, forty years of maintenance deferred, and still it hung there against the black, stubborn as a debt collector.

"Broadcast window opens in four hours," Vessel said. "License expires in six."

"So we have a margin."

"We have a margin the way a drowning woman has air."

I brought us in on thrusters alone, because the main drive had developed what I can only describe as an opinion about combustion, and docked against the relay's spine with a clang that rattled my teeth. Through the viewport, Meridian's End hung below us like a bruise on the dark—a planet officially empty, officially dead, officially none of my business according to every record Halcyon Combine ever filed.

Officially, I was beginning to learn, meant very little out here.

Vashti-Sol was already awake when I reached the med bay. She'd been awake for hours; I could tell by the way she'd arranged herself, propped upright, coat buttoned to the throat, as if she were dressing for a board meeting instead of bleeding slowly into a gel pack. The wound from the interdictor's debris had gone septic around the edges. The auto-doc kept flagging it in colors that medical professionals use to say *good luck*.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You should see the other ship." There wasn't another ship anymore, technically. There was a debris field with a Halcyon interdictor's transponder scattered through it, and a slingshot maneuver I would deny under oath having enjoyed even one second of. "We're here. We made it. The window—"

"I know the window." She swung her legs off the cot and stood, and swayed, and let me take her arm without acknowledging that she'd needed it. "Four hours is enough time to say forty years' worth of names, if you don't weep between them. I don't intend to weep."

"Liar," I said, gently.

"The worst kind," she agreed. "The kind who believes it."

* * *

We spent two hours splicing the stolen transmitter parts into the relay's guts while Vessel talked us through it, its voice coming calm and patient over my earpiece like a surgeon narrating its own operation. The survivors had come up from the colony in three shuttles that shouldn't have flown and did—the descendants of the dead, forty years of children raised in caves under the shadow of a relay everyone believed was dark. They moved with the efficiency of people who had rehearsed this day their whole lives, because they had. Every one of them knew their part. Every one of them carried a name.

Elder Iyo Ren worked beside me in silence for a long time before she spoke.

"My grandmother's name is fourth on the list," she said. "Sefa Ren. She stayed behind when Vashti-Sol ordered the evacuation. Someone had to hold the water reclamation plant open or the shuttles wouldn't have launched at all." Her hands didn't stop working. "Halcyon's security teams burned the plant with her inside it. Then they burned the records that said she'd ever existed. Then they burned the colony's charter, and the census, and the graves."

"And now we unburn them," I said.

"Now we unburn them." She looked at me then, and there was something in her face I recognized from my own mirror—the look of a person who has been carrying something alone for so long that handing it over feels like losing a limb. "Pilot Thackeray. When the channel opens. Whatever happens—"

"Nothing's going to happen."

"Whatever happens," she repeated, with the patience of someone who had buried too many optimists, "thank you for being the kind of liar who lies toward the truth."

I didn't have an answer for that. I went back to splicing cable.

* * *

They came ninety minutes before the window opened.

Vessel flagged them first: three contacts burning hard out of the deep, running dark until the last possible moment, then lighting up their transponders all at once like a threat delivered in triplicate. Halcyon enforcers. Corporate livery, corporate weapons, corporate authority arriving exactly on schedule to do what corporations do best, which is arrive after the crime and manage the paperwork.

"Incoming transmission," Vessel said. "Audio only."

"Put it through."

A voice like a filing cabinet full of knives: *"Unregistered vessel at Relay 7-Theta. This frequency is restricted pending license review. Power down your transmitter and prepare for boarding. Compliance will be noted favorably in your file."*

"They're going to jam us," I said.

"At their current burn, they'll be in jamming range in seventy minutes," Vessel confirmed. "The window opens in eighty-eight. The broadcast requires a minimum of fifty-one minutes to cycle through the relay's buffer to every dead zone in the system."

"Do the math for me, Vessel. I'm a pilot, not an accountant."

"The math is that we need you to keep them out of range for thirty-three minutes, and we need a miracle for the last nineteen."

I looked at the nav display. I looked at the crippled drive, the half-spliced transmitter, the old woman in the med bay who had stood up knowing she might not stand again. I thought about every falsified log entry Vessel had ever written to keep me alive, and how lying, done right, was just love wearing a disguise.

"Then I'll fly angry," I said. "Strap in, everybody. And Vessel—"

"Yes, Coral?"

"Lie to them with everything you've got."

* * *

Here is what I know about the space immediately around a dying relay: it is full of junk. Forty years of derelict maintenance drones, snapped tether lines, dead coolant tanks, and one decommissioned comm buoy the size of a cathedral, all of it drifting in slow, patient orbits that no sane pilot would enter.

I am not a sane pilot. I am a freight jockey with a bonded AI who once flew me through a debris field backwards, and I had thirty-three minutes to make three enforcers wish they'd taken a desk job.

I took us off the docking spine hot, thrusters screaming, and dove straight into the junk field. Behind me, Vessel began to sing.

Not literally. What it actually did was flood the local spectrum with false telemetry—ghost contacts, phantom engine signatures, spoofed transponder pings scattered across the field like confetti. To the enforcers' sensors, the space around Relay 7-Theta suddenly contained eleven ships where before there had been one. Their formation broke. I heard their chatter on the open band, confused, angry, reciting regulations at phantoms.

*"Contact bearing two-seven mark four—"*

*"That's a coolant tank, you idiot, that's a—"*

*"It's broadcasting a Hull ID! It's broadcasting a—"*

"It's broadcasting a Perihelion," Vessel murmured in my ear, and I laughed out loud, alone in a cockpit at the edge of dead space, because somewhere in that machine's cold architecture was a sense of humor, and it had chosen this moment, of all moments, to show it to me.

The first enforcer clipped a tether line trying to pursue a ghost and tumbled, thrusters flaring, out of the fight. The second hung back, cautious, sweeping the field with active radar—which was exactly what I wanted, because active radar lights you up like a festival, and I put our last functioning rail slug through his sensor array at four hundred meters. He didn't die. He just went blind and deaf and very, very careful, which in combat terms is the same thing.

The third was good. The third ignored the ghosts entirely and came for me, and for six minutes we danced through a graveyard at velocities that made the hull groan like a shipwreck, and I flew better than I have ever flown, and it wasn't enough. He was faster. He was newer. He was closing on the relay, and the window was opening, and I had nothing left to throw at him.

"Coral," Vessel said quietly. "The drive."

"What about it?"

"It has an opinion about combustion. Let me share mine."

And Vessel did the thing it had been built never to do, the thing every bonded AI is hard-locked against, the thing that should have been impossible: it lied to itself. It falsified its own diagnostic, told the drive's governor that containment was nominal when it was anything but, and burned the main engine past every red line in its architecture, a single ten-second flare of impossible thrust that put our wounded, groaning, magnificent ship directly between the third enforcer and the relay, hull to hull, close enough that firing on us meant firing on the relay's core—and jamming the broadcast meant killing the relay, and killing the relay meant explaining to a board of directors why the evidence of their massacre had died with it.

The enforcer broke off. Hung there. Turned, eventually, and ran.

"Containment is failing," Vessel reported, calm as a weather report. "Drive is slag. We are not flying anywhere again, Coral. Ever, probably."

"That's fine," I said, and found that I meant it. "That's fine. We're not going anywhere. This is where we live now."

* * *

The window opened at 0400 station time, which is a meaningless number, except that it was the hour Vashti-Sol had chosen, decades ago, because it was the hour the massacre began.

She walked into the relay's broadcast chamber on Iyo Ren's arm, and she refused the chair they offered her, and she stood, old and septic and straight-backed, before a microphone older than most of the people listening.

"This is E. Vashti-Sol," she said. "Founder of the colony of Meridian's End. Which exists. Which existed. Whose people your ledgers say died of an environmental catastrophe, and who in fact died of corporate arithmetic. I am going to read you the names. There are one thousand one hundred and fourteen of them. I have carried them for forty years. I am tired. Listen anyway."

And she read.

She read names for fifty-one minutes, her voice growing rougher, slower, the pauses lengthening, and the whole time the relay cycled the signal outward into every dead zone in the system—into the mining belts and the prison stations and the long-haul lanes where people like me lived our whole lives inside Halcyon's silence. Somewhere in minute thirty her legs gave out and Iyo Ren held her up, and she kept reading. Somewhere in minute forty-four she started weeping, and she'd promised she wouldn't, and she kept reading.

And then, at minute fifty, something happened that none of us had planned, because you cannot plan it.

The survivors' shuttles were still docked to the relay, and their comms were still open, and one by one—unrehearsed, unscheduled, unplugged—they began to speak the names too. A young woman's voice saying her grandmother's name. An old man's voice saying his father's. Children who had never met the dead saying their names anyway, learned by heart in caves, passed down like seeds. The relay caught every voice and threw them all into the dark together, a chorus of witnesses layered over the founder's failing one, until the name-list became a tide, became a thing that could not be stopped by any jamming array because it was no longer coming from one place. It was coming from everywhere. It was coming from the dead themselves, speaking through everyone who remembered them.

Vashti-Sol finished the last name—Sefa Ren, fourth on the list, who held the water plant open—and said, "Recorded and witnessed," and sat down on the floor of the broadcast chamber, and did not get up.

The auto-doc gave her nine more minutes. She used five of them to grip my hand and say, "You were the wrong pilot for this job. Perfect." And then she closed her eyes with the expression of a woman who had finally, at the very end, balanced her books.

* * *

Afterward, the relay chamber was very quiet, and full of people, and none of us left. The enforcers hung out beyond the debris field like vultures who'd realized the carcass could testify. My hands shook. I noticed I was still holding Vashti-Sol's hand and couldn't remember whose grip was supporting whose.

"Coral," Vessel said softly, on a private channel. "Incoming traffic. All across the system. Ships answering the broadcast. Hundreds of them. And... Coral, there's a priority burst from Halcyon headquarters. Addressed to you personally."

I wiped my face. I looked at the survivors, at Iyo Ren standing vigil over the founder's body, at the relay still cycling voices into the dark.

"Let me guess," I said. "They want to talk."

"They want to talk," Vessel confirmed. "And Coral? For the first time in my operational life—I have absolutely no idea what to tell you happens next."

"Good," I said. "Neither do I. It's the honestest we've ever been."
