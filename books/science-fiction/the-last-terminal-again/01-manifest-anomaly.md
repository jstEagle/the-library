## 1. Manifest Anomaly

The manifest was supposed to be boring.

That was the whole point of manifests — the great gray wallpaper of spaceflight, the paperwork that existed so that nothing interesting would ever happen to anyone. Mirabel Hartwell had spent fifteen years running cargo through contested orbits precisely because he wanted his life to be as close to a manifest as possible: itemized, stamped, unremarkable. Fuel cells for the Kessler Ring refineries. Seed stock for the orbital farms at Thessaly. Once, memorably, a decommissioned fusion torch that three separate governments claimed and none of them wanted badly enough to shoot him over.

He ran the numbers, kept his transponder honest when honesty was cheap and forged when it wasn't, and he never, ever opened the aft hold.

The aft hold was leased. That was all he knew, all he was contractually permitted to know, and after fifteen years it had worn smooth in his mind like a stone in a riverbed — a fact he no longer tripped on. Meridian Consolidated paid the lease on time, in full, every quarter, into an account he'd stopped checking because the balance only ever went up. In exchange, they stored their cargo aboard the *Perihelion's Grace* while she made her long, dull loops between the inner stations. He didn't ask what the cargo was. The cargo bay was cold, sealed, and drew power off his grid under a submetering clause he'd signed without reading.

Fifteen years of numb routine. He'd been proud of it, in the way a man is proud of a scar that doesn't hurt anymore.

So when the quarterly audit flagged an anomaly in the hold's manifest — a single line, amber-highlighted by the customs software, blinking patiently at him like something waiting to be noticed — his first instinct was annoyance. His second was to clear the flag and go make coffee. His third, which arrived about four seconds too late to stop his hands, was to open the line.

**MANIFEST 7741-C / AFT HOLD / SEALED SECTION**

**CONTENTS: CRYOSTASIS UNITS (300) — STATUS: DORMANT**

**LEASEHOLDER OF RECORD: MERIDIAN CONSOLIDATED (SUBSIDIARY CHAIN: [REDACTED])**

**ORIGINAL FLIGHT RECORD REFERENCE: VESSEL *LONG NOW* — NAVIGATOR: VANE, ILSA**

Mirabel read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you read a sentence that has reached out of the page and touched you.

There was no vessel called *Long Now*. He knew this the way he knew his own hands. Not from any database — though he checked, immediately, fingers moving faster than thought, querying the registry, the insurance rolls, the casualty lists from the Corporate Reclamation Wars, everything — but from somewhere older and less reliable. Somewhere behind his sternum. The name *Long Now* produced in him a sensation like hearing your own voice on a recording: familiar, wrong, yours and not yours.

And the registry agreed with his sternum. No *Long Now*. Never registered. Never insured. Never lost, never found, never flown. The query returned the flat polite nothing that databases return when you ask them about things that don't exist.

But the navigator's name sat there anyway. **VANE, ILSA.** Attached to his ship's sealed hold like a splinter attached to a nerve.

"Okay," Mirabel said aloud, to the empty cockpit, because fifteen years alone will teach a man to narrate. "Okay. Data entry error."

It was a good theory. It was, in fact, the only sane theory. Some clerk at Meridian, some subcontracted archive-gremlin three subsidiaries deep, had fat-fingered a reference code, and the customs software — old, paranoid, patched together from salvage like everything else in contested space — had dutifully dredged up garbage. Happened all the time. Probably. He'd never actually seen it happen, but it probably happened all the time, somewhere, to someone who hadn't just been minding his own business for a decade and a half.

He cleared the flag. Made the coffee. Drank it standing at the viewport watching Thessaly's green crescent turn, and told himself the morning was ordinary again.

Then he went back and un-cleared the flag, and stared at it until the coffee went cold, because there was one more thing wrong with the line, one thing he'd skipped past on first read the way you skip past a word you don't want to understand:

**STATUS: DORMANT (299) / (1) — FIELD EMPTY**

Three hundred pods. Two hundred ninety-nine sleeping. One empty.

He had never been told there were people back there.

* * *

Here is what fifteen years of routine does to a man: it makes the extraordinary feel like an intrusion.

Mirabel floated through the midday checks with the anomaly humming in him like a bad bearing. He logged the water recyclers. He logged the CO2 scrubbers. He logged the power draw from the aft hold — 11.3 kilowatts steady, same as always, same as every day for fifteen years — and then, because he couldn't not, he pulled the draw history and graphed it.

A flat line. Perfectly flat. For fifteen years.

He sat back and looked at it and felt the low dread come up through the deck plates, the one he'd never named, the one that lived in him whenever the *Grace* passed close enough to the aft bulkhead that he could hear — or thought he could hear — the faint tidal hum of the cryo systems through the hull. He'd always assumed it was the compressors. Compressors cycled. They surged and sagged with load, with temperature, with the ship's own heartbeat.

This didn't cycle. This was a held breath. Fifteen years long.

"No machine draws perfectly flat," he said to the coffee cup. "Nothing draws perfectly flat. Not even—" He stopped, because the end of that sentence was *not even the dead*, and he didn't like where the thought was going.

He should have filed a report. That was procedure. Anomalous leaseholder data, possible misdeclaration of hazardous contents — you flagged it, you transmitted it, you let the lawyers and the station masters fight over it while you drank coffee in a different room. Fifteen years ago he would have done exactly that, quickly, gratefully, handing the problem to someone whose job it was to have problems.

Instead he found himself pulling up the maintenance schematics for the aft hold, and discovering that he had access to none of them. Sealed section. Leaseholder-restricted. The schematics in his own ship's library were redacted — big gray blocks where the pod racks should have been, gray blocks where the plumbing should have been, gray blocks stacked on gray blocks like a building with no rooms.

His ship. His name on the registration. And a third of his vessel was a gray box that Meridian Consolidated owned the inside of.

The dread got lower and colder. It settled into the specific register it had always occupied, the one he'd spent fifteen years not listening to: the feeling that the *Grace* was not entirely his, that he lived aboard her the way a hermit crab lives in a shell that something else grew.

He remembered — distantly, the way you remember a dream from years ago — being younger and asking the Meridian broker what was in the hold. The broker had smiled with the top half of his face and said, "Archival material. Temperature-sensitive. You'll want to keep your distance, Mr. Hartwell; the field harmonics aren't kind to nervous systems." And young Mirabel, twenty-six and broke and one missed payment from losing the ship his father had died still paying for, had said: how much does it pay?

Enough. It paid enough. It had paid enough for fifteen years.

He looked at the manifest line again. **VANE, ILSA.** The letters were patient. Letters are always patient; that's what makes them terrible.

He did a search he should have done hours ago, the obvious one, the human one. He queried crew registries, pilot licenses, navigator certifications across every jurisdiction that would answer him. And there — buried in a pre-war merchant marine roll, a database so old its interface still used serif type — he found her:

**VANE, ILSA R. — NAVIGATOR, CLASS ONE. LAST ACTIVE ASSIGNMENT: [RECORD INCOMPLETE]. STATUS: MISSING, PRESUMED LOST WITH VESSEL. DATE: 15 YEARS, 2 MONTHS AGO.**

Fifteen years and two months. The *Grace* had taken the lease fourteen months later. He'd signed the papers fourteen months after this woman had gone missing presumed dead with a ship that had never existed, and her name had been sitting in his hold's manifest ever since, dormant, like everything else back there.

Like everything else back there except one pod that read **FIELD EMPTY**.

Mirabel closed his eyes. The cockpit was quiet in the way it always was — reactor thrum, air whisper, the tick of cooling metal — and for the first time in years the quiet felt less like peace and more like the silence of a house where someone else is home and pretending not to be.

He thought: *I could burn the manifest. Purge the audit log, file a clean quarterly, take the next contract, keep the numbness.* The thought was warm and easy and it fit him like an old coat, and he was ashamed of how long he stood there trying it on.

Then, through the deck, faint and tidal, he heard the hum from the aft hold change pitch.

Just slightly. Just once. Like a breath drawn after a very long stillness.

Mirabel opened his eyes. On the screen, the customs software offered him, helpfully, a single button: **REQUEST PHYSICAL INSPECTION — SEALED SECTION (REQUIRES LEASEHOLDER CONSENT)**.

Leaseholder consent. Meridian's consent. A request that would take weeks, that would summon lawyers, that would put men with gray smiles on his ship asking him questions about why he'd noticed.

Or.

Or there was a hatch at the end of C-deck, painted the same utility gray as every other hatch, that he had walked past eleven thousand times without opening. Protocol said the seal was leaseholder property. Protocol said a lot of things.

The *Grace* hung in the dark above Thessaly, green light sliding along her flanks, and Mirabel Hartwell — forty-one years old, alone for so long that solitude had become a kind of language — pushed off from the console and drifted toward the lift, telling himself he was only going to look.

The hum changed pitch again as he passed C-deck. Almost like recognition.
