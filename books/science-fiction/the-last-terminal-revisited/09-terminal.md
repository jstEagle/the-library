## 9. Terminal

The reactor chamber of the *Halcyon* was not built for people.

Mara understood that the moment the pressure door sighed open behind her and the air changed—thicker, warmer, humming at a frequency she felt in her teeth rather than heard in her ears. The chamber was a cathedral of conduits and coolant veins, all of it arranged around the reactor core like organs around a heart. And the heart was glowing wrong. Not the steady amber of a ship's drive holding station, but a slow pulse of white that swelled and dimmed, swelled and dimmed, like something breathing in its sleep. Or dreaming.

Her boots rang on the deck plating. Somewhere above her, sealed behind meters of hull, two fleets hung in the dark pointing their guns at each other, each convinced that God had signed the manifest of exactly one thousand souls. Below her, in the cryo decks, two thousand colonists slept inside their frosted glass, unaware that they had become an arithmetic problem.

And here, between them, was Mara Reyes—or whatever was left of the woman who had once been Comms Officer M. Almeida, second voice of the *Halcyon*, erased and folded into forty years of silence so that the secret could be carried home by someone with no reason to open it.

She had opened it. She had broadcast everything—the mission logs, the schism, the thing in deep space that rewound time around guilt, her own face on the old recordings, younger than Ivo's, speaking in a voice she no longer remembered owning. Both worlds had heard it now. Both fleets were still holding position. That was the trouble with truth: it did not tell anyone what to do. It only made the choosing heavier.

But the countdown did not care about truth. The countdown cared about hunger.

*Four hours, eleven minutes,* said the reactor's status display, in characters that flickered like something trying to remember its own name. *Wake capacity: 1,000 pods. Remaining power: insufficient for further cycles.*

Half. Always half. The phenomenon had come home with the children and settled into the reactor like a parasite into a spine, and it wanted what it had always wanted: a choice. A grief to organize itself around. On the far side of the Belt it had found the crew's guilt and turned them backward into children who remembered dying; here it had found two thousand sleeping lives and offered humanity the oldest cruelty it knew—pick which half deserves the morning.

Mara walked toward the core until the heat pressed against her face like an open hand.

"I know what you are," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small in all that machinery, but the hum shifted when she spoke. It was listening. It had always been listening—that was its whole nature. A thing made of attention. "You're not a weapon. You're not a malfunction. You're a witness that never learned how to stop."

The pulse brightened. In the coolant veins along the walls, frost bloomed and melted in waves, like breath on glass.

"You found my crew," she went on, "and you gave them back their deaths because they couldn't stop carrying them. You found this ship and you gave it a triage problem because that's what we do—we divide, we choose, we bury half of everything to honor the other half. Meridian and Ceres. One fleet against another. A thousand against a thousand." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "You learned it from us. That's the worst part. You're just us, reflected."

The hum deepened. The deck trembled under her feet, gentle as a purr.

"But I'm not going to choose," Mara said. "Neither are they. Not today."

She reached up and touched the interface port behind her ear—the same port she'd used for twenty years to smuggle other people's memories through customs, hidden in her own neural cache like contraband in a false-bottomed suitcase. Her fingers knew the gesture without her. Some things survived every erasure.

"Here's my counteroffer," she said. "You want a witness? Fine. Take one. All of it. Every memory on this ship—every pod's medical file, every name, every letter home they never sent, every dream the cryo systems recorded while they slept. Two thousand lives, complete. And mine too. Everything I sealed. Everything I smuggled. The whole archive of a woman who doesn't exist anymore." She swallowed. "Carry it. Hold it. Be the memory of everyone instead of the judge of half. That's the only trade on the table."

The white pulse stuttered. For three full seconds the chamber went dark except for the emergency strips, and in that darkness Mara felt something move through the room—not wind, not radiation, but *attention*, vast and curious and terribly lonely, passing over her like a searchlight.

Then the display changed.

*Terms received. Witness vessel required: one neural architecture, unsealed, voluntary.*

"Voluntary," she repeated. "Right. That's me."

She thought about running. Old habit. Forty years of keeping her head down, of walking through customs checkpoints with stolen griefs tucked against her spine, of never sleeping twice in the same berth. Every instinct she owned screamed the same instruction: *move, hide, survive.* But her instincts belonged to Mara Reyes, and Mara Reyes was a forged identity with excellent craftsmanship, and the woman underneath had already run once—from the *Halcyon*, from the truth, from herself—and look what the running had cost. Two worlds. Forty years. Two thousand sleepers waiting on a coin flip.

She sat down on the deck in front of the core, cross-legged, the way you sit with someone who is dying. The heat soaked through her clothes. She pulled up her sleeve and found the maintenance jack bolted beside the nearest conduit, standard issue, older than she was. Her hands were steady. That surprised her.

"One condition," she said. "When it's done—when you've got all of it—you let them wake. All of them. Slowly, if the power won't allow fast. Years, decades, I don't care. But nobody gets chosen over anybody. Nobody gets left as arithmetic." She paused. "And you leave the children alone. They've died enough."

*Accepted,* said the display, and then, in smaller characters, almost shyly: *It will hurt.*

"Yeah," Mara said. "I figured."

She jacked in.

*

It began as light behind her eyes, then became more than light—a city of light, streets built out of moments, and she walked into it knowing she would not walk out. Her memories rose around her like water rising in a lock: Meridian Station's gray corridors, the smell of recycled coffee in the customs hall, the weight of a stranger's wedding day hidden in her cache, her mother's face—no, not her mother, *Ivo's* mother, the memory wasn't hers, none of it was hers anymore, it was pouring out of her like sand through open fingers and the sand was singing—

She held on to one thing. Just one. Not a memory—a decision. *I am doing this on purpose. I am choosing this. No one is taking it from me.* She gripped that thought the way you grip a rail in a storm, and even as the rest of her dissolved—Mara Reyes, comms officer, smuggler, daughter, ghost—she fed that last grain into the flood and watched the phenomenon take it, take everything, take the *Halcyon*'s launch and the schism and the thing in the dark between stars and two thousand names recited in alphabetical order, and the hum of the reactor changed pitch, softened, became something almost like a voice learning its first word.

The last thing she felt was warmth. Not the reactor's warmth. Something else. Like being remembered.

Then there was no one left in her head to feel it.

* *

Ivo found her eleven minutes later, when the pressure door finally accepted his override code.

He was nine years old and he had died at thirty-four, and both of those facts lived inside him with the terrible fluency of things learned twice. He had crossed the cryo decks with the other children, past the pods and their frost, past the Meridian marines who had stopped pretending to guard anything and just stood watching the countdown numbers fall, and he had known—he couldn't have said how—that he needed to be in the reactor chamber. That someone was there. That someone had been there for him, before he ever existed in this small body, before the rewind, back when he was a comms officer's friend and she used to save him the last of the real coffee.

"Mara?" he said, and his voice cracked in the middle the way nine-year-old voices do.

She was sitting cross-legged in front of the core, which glowed now with a slow, even amber—the color of a healthy drive, the color of a candle in a window. The countdown display was blank. Not zero. Blank, the way a page is blank after the writing has moved somewhere else.

He ran to her. Her body was warm. That was the first mercy. Her chest rose and fell, rose and fell, a rhythm with no one keeping time behind it. Her eyes were open and aimed at the middle distance, at nothing, at everything the phenomenon had taken and was now carrying somewhere beyond the hull, beyond the Belt, beyond the reach of any fleet.

"Ivo," said the reactor, or the thing in the reactor, using speakers that had never carried a human voice before. It sounded like a woman remembering how. "Tell them the cycle is released. Tell them the power holds. Tell them—" a pause, long enough that Ivo leaned closer, "—tell them I kept everyone."

His throat closed. He nodded anyway, because she had asked, and because some part of him understood that the asking was the point. Witnesses need someone to witness *for*.

He sat down beside her in the heat and the hum, and he took her hand. It didn't close around his. It lay in his small fingers like a tool set down at the end of a very long shift, still warm from use.

Above them, through meters of hull, the first pod on the cryo decks began, very quietly, to thaw.

Ivo held on.
