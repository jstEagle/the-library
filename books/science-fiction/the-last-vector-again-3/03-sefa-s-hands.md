## 3. Sefa's Hands

The dampeners were supposed to be the honest part of the ship.

Everything else aboard *Kestrel's Debt* lied a little—the manifest, the transponder, the captain's stories about how he'd come to own a hauler older than two of his marriages—but the gravitic dampeners in the lower spine were pure arithmetic. Coils and counter-mass, phase-locked and boring. Sefa Okonkwo had spent eleven years keeping them boring, and she trusted them the way other people trusted their own pulse.

So when she opened access panel seven and found the diagnostic log clean as a hymnal, she sat down on her tool crate and stared at it for a long time.

Nine seconds of freefall. Every soul on the ship had felt it—cargo straps snapping taut, coffee climbing out of cups in silver ropes, old Marta Voss screaming from the galley like the void itself had reached in and touched her. Nine seconds, then gravity returned so smoothly that half the crew convinced themselves it had been a pressure hiccup, a sensor ghost, nothing.

But Sefa had pulled the raw coil telemetry within the hour, because that was who she was, and the telemetry said something impossible.

The dampeners hadn't failed.

They had *answered*.

* * *

She ran the numbers four times before she let herself believe them. The phase signature during those nine seconds wasn't noise, wasn't drift, wasn't any of the hundred failure modes she could recite in her sleep. It was structured. Periodic. A wave train with a repeating interval, riding down through the hull like a hand running along piano keys—and the coils hadn't resisted it. They had *resonated*. Matched it. Held the ship's gravity steady at zero with the tenderness of someone lowering a sleeping child into a crib.

Dampeners don't do that. Dampeners are dumb stone. Unless something told them, in their own language, *lie down now, be weightless, this is not an attack.*

Sefa wiped her hands on her rag even though they were clean. The engine room hummed around her, warm and amber-lit, smelling of hot metal and the ginger tea she wasn't supposed to brew near the coolant lines. She thought about the way the blackout had felt from inside it—not violent, not terrifying, once the first lurch passed. Almost gentle. Like the ship had been picked up by something enormous and set back down with care.

Nobody else had described it that way. Everybody else described falling.

She thought about Orsen Hartwell.

* * *

He'd been aboard six weeks, riding cargo audits under a contract so bland it was practically camouflage. Insurance investigator, paperwork man, a pair of soft hands and a face that did its best to be forgettable. The crew mostly ignored him, which was the point, Sefa suspected. But she'd noticed things, because noticing was also who she was: the way he ate standing up, like meals were appointments he resented; the way he flinched—not visibly, just a stillness, a held breath—whenever the comm channel carried anything about Ceres; the way he'd looked, during those nine seconds of falling, when everyone else had been grabbing for handholds.

He hadn't grabbed anything. He'd floated in the middle of the corridor with his eyes closed and his lips moving, and when gravity came back he'd looked less surprised than *recognized*, the way you look when you hear your own name called in a crowd.

And then, twenty minutes later, he'd come down to her engine room with his slate held wrong—screen toward himself, angled away, a man who'd learned privacy the hard way—and asked, very casually, whether the dampeners logged external inputs.

"Standard question," he'd said. "For the claim file."

"Nothing external touches my coils," she'd told him. "They're shielded like a nun."

"Right," he'd said. "Of course." And he'd smiled, and the smile had been the worst thing she'd seen all day, because it was the smile of a man practicing normalcy in front of a mirror.

Now she stood in front of panel seven with the truth humming in her hands, and she understood what he'd been asking, and why he'd held his slate the way he held it, and she faced the first real decision of what was shaping up to be a very strange week.

Because here was the thing: if the dampeners answered something—if some signal out in the dark had spoken to *Kestrel's Debt* in a voice her own equipment recognized—then either every gravitics engineer in the belt was about to have the worst month of their careers, or somebody already knew, and was keeping it quiet, and was currently walking around her ship pretending to audit cargo manifests.

Orsen Hartwell knew something. And whatever he knew, he was afraid of it in the specific, disciplined way people are afraid of things that have already taken something from them.

Sefa closed the panel. She wiped down the diagnostic readout, all four copies, and entered a maintenance note in the log: *Coil phase anomaly traced to degraded coupling bus. Bus replaced. No further action required.* Her stylus hovered over the commit key for three full seconds.

Then she pressed it, and felt the lie settle into the ship's bones like a coin dropped into deep water.

* * *

She found him that evening in the mess, doing what he always did: sitting alone at the corner table with tea he didn't drink, reading claims on his slate with the focused blankness of a man defusing something. The mess lights were on night cycle, low and orange, and the observation port beside him showed the slow wheeling dark of the transit corridor, dust motes catching starlight like snow that had forgotten which way was down.

"Your coupling bus," she said, setting her mug across from him. "The one that caused the nine seconds."

He looked up. For a fraction of a second his face did something unguarded—something young and caught—and then the practiced calm slid over it like a hatch sealing. "I'm sorry?"

"The degraded coupling bus. From my maintenance log. The one I filed tonight." She sat down across from him, unhurried, and wrapped both hands around her mug. "It doesn't exist. I wrote that entry myself, an hour ago, to bury the real data. And you're the only person on this ship who asked me the right question before I buried it. So." She blew across her tea. "I figure we should decide together what story we're telling."

The silence between them stretched long enough that she heard the coolant pumps cycle down, the little sigh the ship made when it settled into sleep mode. Orsen's thumb moved against the edge of his slate, back and forth, a man counting something invisible.

"You falsified a maintenance record," he said finally. "On a vessel carrying bonded cargo. Do you know what that does to an insurance investigator?"

"It makes him complicit," Sefa said. "Which is exactly why I'm telling you instead of letting you find it. I've watched you work, Hartwell. You're good. Too good for manifests. You'd have found my lie by Thursday, and then I'd be explaining myself to someone who thinks I'm a problem." She took a sip. "I'd rather explain myself to someone who knows I'm not."

"And what are you, then?"

"A woman whose ship got talked to by something in the dark," she said, "and who wants to know what it said before anyone decides to shoot at it."

Something moved behind his eyes—grief, maybe, or its fossil record. He turned the slate face-down on the table, deliberately, and when he spoke his voice was quieter than she'd ever heard it.

"I can't tell you what it said. I genuinely can't. Not because I don't trust you." His mouth twisted. "Because I don't understand it yet myself. And because the last person who might have explained it to me is dead, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a pattern in the pulse. In the flicker. Something in it is familiar to me in a way that has nothing to do with engineering, and everything to do with a person I lost, and until I know what that means, everything I say would just be me guessing out loud in front of witnesses."

Sefa nodded slowly. Around them the mess slept; somewhere aft, Marta Voss snored through the bulkhead like a distant weather system. Out the port, the stars held their positions with the patience of things that had been waiting a very long time.

"Okay," she said. "You don't have to tell me yet. But here's what I'll tell you, free of charge, engineer to whoever-you-are." She leaned forward. "That signal didn't just talk to my coils. It talked *politely*. Whatever reached into this ship for nine seconds went out of its way not to hurt us. That's not how failures behave. That's how guests behave. Somebody knocked, Hartwell. Gently. And I think you know who they were knocking for."

He looked at her then—really looked, the practiced mask gone, leaving something raw and tired and startlingly young underneath—and Sefa felt the strange vertigo of seeing a person clearly for the first time after weeks of seeing only their camouflage.

"Why are you helping me?" he asked. "You've known me six weeks. You know nothing about me except that I lie well."

"I know you float with your eyes closed," she said. "When the whole ship is falling, you're the only one who listens. That's more character reference than most people manage in six years." She shrugged, and felt her own heartbeat, annoyingly loud. "Besides. Whoever knocked on my ship spoke to my coils in a language I've spent eleven years learning without knowing it. If there's going to be a conversation between humanity and whatever that was, I want my ship's voice in the room. Which means I need you functional, honest-with-me, and not arrested. In that order."

A breath went out of him, long and shaking, like a man setting down cargo he'd carried too many kilometers alone.

"Sefa Okonkwo," he said, testing the name—he'd never used it before, she realized; six weeks and he'd only ever called her *engineer*, keeping her at the safe distance of a job title. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." She stood, collected her mug, and paused beside him on her way out. Close enough that she could see the fine scar at his temple she'd never noticed, close enough to smell machine oil and cheap tea and, beneath it, something like fear finally exhaling. "Get some sleep, Hartwell. Tomorrow I'm going to teach you how dampeners actually work, so when you lie about them, you lie like a professional."

His laugh was small and startled and real—the first one she'd heard from him—and it followed her down the corridor warmer than the engine room ever managed.

* * *

She was halfway through her pre-shift checks when the general channel lit up, and the captain's voice came through tight and flat in a way that made her hands go still on the console.

"All hands. We've received a fleet-wide bulletin. Both of them—Concord and Meridian. They're converging on Ceres, and Earth's transmission loop just entered its final cycle." A pause, static breathing in the gap. "Whatever caused our nine seconds, half the armed ships in the system are sailing toward it right now, and every one of them thinks the other side built it."

Sefa looked up through the forward port, past the sleeping cargo, to where Ceres hung in the dark—a pale chip of light, small and patient, like something waiting to be addressed.

Her coils, she thought, had already been introduced.

And the man who knew why was asleep two decks above her, holding a dead woman's name behind his teeth.
