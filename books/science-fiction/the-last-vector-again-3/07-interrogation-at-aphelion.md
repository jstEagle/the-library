## 7. Interrogation at Aphelion

They took him to a ship called *Aphelion*, which was fitting, because that was the word for the farthest point from the sun, and Orsen Hartwell had never felt farther from anything warm.

The Concord cruiser hung off Ceres's shoulder like a blade held politely at someone's throat. Its docking bay swallowed the Kestrel's Debt whole—Sefa's engines, Sefa's hands, Sefa's last look through the airlock glass, her palm flat against it for one second before the marines made her step back. He carried that image with him into the interrogation room: five fingers, spread wide, pressing against nothing.

The room was white. Not clean-white, not hospital-white. Interrogation-white, the kind of white designed to make a man aware of every shadow he cast. A single table bolted to the floor. Two chairs. The gravity was tuned a half-percent heavy, he noticed—the old trick, making the body feel guilty before the mouth ever opened.

Vess Arda came in without an escort, which was its own kind of message. She was small and gray-eyed and moved like water finding the lowest point in a room. She carried a slate, a carafe of water, and two cups, and she poured both before she sat down, sliding one across the table as if they were colleagues meeting over a contract dispute.

"Mr. Hartwell." Her voice was soft. Soft voices always were, in his experience, until they weren't. "Or should I say Investigator Hartwell? Formerly of the Meridian Claims Bureau, before your—what was the phrase in your termination notice?—*catastrophic failure of professional judgment.*"

"I prefer cargo auditor," Orsen said. "It has a certain poetry."

"It does." She smiled, and did not drink. "Poetry is what we're here to discuss. Yours specifically."

She turned the slate toward him. On it, rotating slowly, was the pulse signature—the nine-second pattern, rendered as light. It looked almost beautiful this way, a lattice of gold threads braiding and unbraiding, and Orsen hated how much it looked like Mira. Like the way she'd hum when she worked, arrhythmic and certain.

"This signal touched four hundred eleven ships," Arda said. "It passed through Concord hulls and Meridian hulls without discrimination. It brushed the mining platforms, the prison barges, the funeral transports. And then it stopped. Except—" she tapped the slate, "—it didn't stop everywhere. There are seventeen vessels where the signature repeats. Recurs. As if the source is still... listening to them."

"And I'm on that list."

"You're first on that list." She let that sit between them like a third person at the table. "Which brings us to the interesting part. Because you, Mr. Hartwell—a disgraced investigator hiding under a false name in the hauler lanes—you filed a claim with the Concord liaison office three days ago. You claimed to be the lattice's designated translator. You presented authentication codes that our cryptographers have spent seventy-two hours failing to break. And the fleets, who were nine hours from shooting each other over this rock, have now agreed to a ceasefire window because of you."

"The ceasefire seems to be working out well for everyone," Orsen said. "You're welcome."

Arda laughed. It was a genuine laugh, brief and low, and somehow that was worse than anger. "I've done this work for twenty-two years. Do you know what I've learned? Everyone lies. That's not cynicism—that's just the weather. But very few people lie *well*, and almost no one lies *completely*. A good forgery is a window with the view painted on. Sooner or later you find the seam." She folded her hands. "So let's find yours."

* * *

She started with the codes.

"They're built on a neural watermark," she said. "A human one. Whoever generated those authentication keys used a cognitive signature as the seed—which means whoever made them had access to a recorded mind. Recorded minds are Concord property, Mr. Hartwell. Registry, licensing, chain of custody. Every imprint in the Belt is catalogued." She slid the slate again. This time it showed a name, redacted except for a single letter. *M*. "We found the provenance. A research station on Ceres, decommissioned six years ago after a decompression event. One researcher died. Her neural archive was logged as destroyed in the incident."

Orsen kept his face still. He'd had practice; grief was the best training ground for a poker face there would ever be.

"Logged as destroyed," Arda repeated. "And yet here it is, woven through your credentials like a signature on a painting. So either the archive wasn't destroyed—or someone stole it. And the person most likely to have access to a dead researcher's imprint would be..." She tilted her head. "Family?"

"My sister liked to share," Orsen said. "Even when she was alive."

"There it is." Arda's eyes didn't gloat; they simply saw. "There's the seam. You didn't steal the imprint, did you. She gave it to you. Or left it to you. And somewhere in that lattice under Ceres, whatever's left of her has been—what? Whispering to you? Teaching you? Feeding you codes?" She leaned forward. "Here's my problem, Mr. Hartwell. Everything you've told the fleets might be true. The lattice might genuinely want a translator. It might genuinely have chosen you. But you didn't earn that choice—you inherited it. Which means you're not an envoy. You're a man standing in front of a machine, holding up his dead sister like a shield, and asking two armed fleets to take his word for what it says."

The words landed harder than he expected. Not because they were cruel—because they were *accurate*. He thought of Mira's echo calling him by the childhood name only she knew, *Ozzie*, in a voice like dust settling. He thought of the oath the lattice wanted, the one that would erase her forever, and how he hadn't told anyone about that part yet. Not even Sefa.

Especially not Sefa.

"You're right," Orsen said quietly. "About all of it. I'm not an envoy. I'm a fraud with good sources."

Arda blinked. Whatever she'd prepared for, it wasn't surrender. "That's refreshingly honest."

"Honesty's cheap when the alternative is watching you dismantle me piece by piece for another six hours." He met her gray eyes. "So let me save us both the time. Here's what I actually know. Something ancient is buried under Ceres. It tested humanity with a nine-second question, and four hundred eleven ships heard it, and none of your admirals understood the answer. My sister's echo lives inside it. And it has asked me for something I don't know if I can give. That's the whole truth. The rest—the translator title, the credentials—I built so that two fleets full of frightened people wouldn't start a war over a signal they can't read."

Silence. The recycled air hummed. Somewhere far below them, through kilometers of rock and steel, the lattice waited.

Arda studied him for a long moment. Then she reached into her jacket and set something on the table between the cups.

A data chit. Plain, unmarked, military-gray.

"Amnesty," she said. "Full pardon for the false credentials. Restoration of your investigator's license. A new name, a new station, a stipend you could live on for thirty years. In exchange, you give me the lattice's communication protocols—all of them—and you testify before the Concord Admiralty that the Meridian Compact attempted to weaponize the signal. We have evidence. Fragmentary, but enough, with your testimony, to break them."

Orsen looked at the chit. A quiet life, pressed into sixty grams of polymer. A name that wasn't his, a desk that wasn't his, a debt finally paid.

He thought about Sefa's hand against the airlock glass.

"No," he said.

"Mr. Hartwell—"

"You want me to swear the Compact weaponized it. Maybe some of them did—maybe both of you did, everyone's been poking at that rock for years hoping to pull a sword out of it. But the lattice didn't ask me to condemn anyone. It asked me to *listen*. And the first thing I learned listening is that guilt doesn't travel on just one flag." He pushed the chit back across the table. It made a small, obscene click against the metal. "Keep your amnesty. I've been invisible for six years, Officer Arda. It turns out I'm bad at it."

Something shifted in her face—not anger. Respect, maybe, or its close cousin, disappointment. "You understand what happens now. If you won't cooperate, I report that you refused. The Admiralty will conclude you're either a Meridian asset or a lunatic, and neither classification protects you. The ceasefire window closes in nineteen hours. After that, we stop negotiating with translators and start negotiating with kinetic strikes."

"I know."

"You'll die on that rock."

"Probably," Orsen said. "But so will everyone else, eventually. The trick is what you're doing when it happens."

Arda exhaled slowly and stood, gathering her slate. At the door she paused, her back to him.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the cryptographers broke your codes this morning. Fourteen hours ago. They're forged. Fabricated from a stolen imprint by someone with no formal clearance." She turned her head just enough to show her profile. "I came in here knowing you were a fraud. I offered you amnesty anyway, because I hoped you'd be a useful one." A beat. "Instead you're an honest one. That's rarer. And much more dangerous—to both of us."

The door sealed behind her.

* * *

They left him alone for an hour. Long enough to count the rivets in the table. Long enough to rehearse the speech he'd give at his own execution and find it wanting.

Then the lights died.

Not all of them—the emergency strips came up amber a heartbeat later—but for exactly one moment, the room went black, and in that moment Orsen felt it: the pulse. Faint, familiar, threading up through the deck plates and into the bones of his feet. Nine seconds' worth of pressure folded into less than one. *Hello, Ozzie,* it said, in the language of falling things.

The gravity flickered.

Once. Twice. A stuttering rhythm, like a heart remembering how to beat. Outside the viewport—when had they given him a viewport?—the stars wheeled slightly, wrong, and Orsen realized the entire ship was moving. No. Not moving. *Falling.* Aphelion's artificial field was oscillating, and every loose object in the corridor beyond his door was rising, drifting, tumbling in slow confusion.

Alarms began. Muffled, disciplined, Concord alarms—three tones, then silence, then three tones again. Boots in the passageway. Someone shouting about gravitic anomalies, about the drive plume guttering, about sensors reading a mass beneath Ceres that *should not exist*.

And through it all, threaded under the chaos like a bass note, the pulse continued. Patient. Rhythmic. Deliberate.

Orsen stood up. His legs were steady, which surprised him. He pressed his palm flat against the wall and felt the vibration answer, felt it shape itself around his touch the way water shapes itself around a stone.

*I see you,* the rhythm said. *I see what they offered. I see what you refused.*

"You're going to get me killed," he murmured.

The gravity surged—gently, almost playfully—and set him back down in his chair as though the lattice itself were tucking him in. Then, from somewhere deep in the ship's systems, every screen in the interrogation room lit at once. Not with text. With a voice-print. Mira's. Rendered in gold thread on black, braiding and unbraiding, unmistakable to anyone who had ever loved her.

On the slate Arda had left behind, a message composed itself, character by character:

**TELL HER THE TRUTH OR I WILL.**

Orsen stared at it. His sister's ghost, threatening to expose him—to expose *both* of them—to the intelligence officer whose ship was currently trembling like a struck bell.

The door unsealed. Vess Arda stood in the frame, hair loose from its knot, one hand braced against the jamb as the deck swayed beneath her. She looked at Orsen. She looked at the screens. Gray eyes widening by a fraction—the fraction of a woman recalculating everything.

"That," she said quietly, "was not you."

"No," Orsen said. And for the first time in six years, he smiled without checking who was watching. "That was my sister."

Behind her, through the viewport, the amber emergency lights caught the curve of Ceres—and along its terminator, faint as breath on glass, patterns of light were waking in the ice, spreading outward like a hand opening.

Nineteen hours to ceasefire collapse. And the lattice had just introduced itself to the Concord fleet.

Arda stepped back into the room and let the door seal shut behind her.
