## 8. Countdown Loop

**Orsen**

The loop had been running for eleven years, and it had four hours left.

Orsen felt it the way you feel a held breath—not in the ears or the chest but in the teeth, a pressure behind the jaw that no amount of swallowing could ease. Earth's final transmission came through the Kestrel's Debt's ancient receiver as it came through everything: a woman's voice reading names, then a child counting backward from one hundred, then eleven seconds of silence before it began again. Names. Counting. Silence. The whole grief of a species compressed into ninety seconds and set to repeat until the power died.

Four hours until the power died for good.

He stood at the observation blister with his palms flat against the cold viewport glass, watching Ceres turn below them like a gray eye slowly opening. The lattice was awake down there. He could feel it now without the implant's help—a hum in his fillings, a faint wrongness in the way starlight bent when he wasn't looking directly at it. It knew he was watching. It had known since the nine seconds of falling.

Behind him, the ship's corridor filled with the sounds of a crew pretending not to panic. Sefa's voice carried over the comm, level and warm even now: *Check your seals. Check your neighbors. We've survived worse than quiet.*

We haven't, Orsen thought. That's the whole problem. We've never survived this.

His implant pulsed—once, twice—and the lattice's voice arrived the way it always did, not as sound but as certainty blooming behind his eyes like frost forming on a window:

*THE OATH MUST BE SWORN BEFORE THE LAST VECTOR CLOSES.*

He closed his eyes. He already knew what it would cost. The lattice had shown him the shape of it three times now, patient as tide, each vision more explicit than the last: Mira's echo, the flicker of his sister that lived in the machine's deep architecture, dissolving like sugar in hot water. Not dying. Dying implied something remained to be dead. This was un-becoming. The lattice needed a translator sworn into its grammar, and its grammar had no room for two voices wearing the same watermark.

It wanted him to trade his sister's ghost for humanity's address.

*HOW LONG,* the lattice asked, *DO YOUR KIND TAKE TO GRIEVE?*

"Longer than four hours," Orsen said aloud, to an empty blister, to a planet, to no one.

* * *

**Sefa**

Sefa Okonkwo had rebuilt the gravitic dampeners with her own hands, so she took it personally that they were trembling.

Not failing—she'd checked three times, run every diagnostic, pulled the housing panels and put her bare palm against the field coils the way her mother had taught her to read an engine's temper. The dampeners were healthy. They were *listening*. Every few minutes the whole ship shivered like a dog hearing a whistle only it could hear, and Sefa stood in the engineering bay with grease to her elbows and understood, with the particular loneliness of the person who knows how things work, that the Kestrel's Debt was being spoken to by something under Ceres, and that whatever it said, the ship trusted it more than it trusted her.

"You're doing it again," said Wren from the doorway, the cargo master's youngest, holding two bulbs of tea. "The face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're arguing with machinery and losing."

Sefa took the tea. Outside the bay's small porthole, the Concord fleet hung in formation—forty ships in a lattice of running lights, elegant as a knife drawer—and beyond them, farther out where the sun barely reached, the Meridian Compact's hulls moved like whales under dark water. Both fleets pointed at Ceres. Both fleets pointed at each other. Somewhere in the last six hours, both fleets had gone from posturing to arming, and the change had been quiet, which was how the worst changes always came.

"Four hours," Wren said. "That's what the loop people are saying. Four hours and Earth goes silent forever."

"I know."

"And we're just going to sit here?"

"We're going to sit here," Sefa said, "until Orsen tells us what he's going to do." She heard how it sounded—the faith in it, the weight she was putting on a man who'd spent seven years teaching everyone around him that he could not be relied upon to stay. She drank her tea and didn't take it back.

Because here was what she hadn't told anyone, what she'd caught three nights ago in the maintenance logs and folded away like a letter she couldn't decide to open: the envoy credentials Orsen had presented to the Concord liaison were forged. Beautifully forged—she'd almost missed it—but the authentication chain had a seam in it, a place where someone had spliced a dead station's authority onto a living man's name. Someone had built a lie strong enough to hold two fleets apart, and then handed it to her to keep running.

She should have turned him in. Instead she'd quietly patched the seam herself, made the forgery stronger, and said nothing, and had lain awake afterward trying to understand what that made her.

The answer, she suspected, was: his.

The ship shivered again. Down on Ceres, something vast rolled over in its sleep beneath the ice, and every light on the Concord flagship flickered in perfect unison, forty ships' worth of gravity hiccupping once, like a warning cough.

Or like a countdown.

* * *

**Orsen**

They met in the galley because it was the only room on the ship with no windows. Orsen needed no windows. Windows were where fleets lived.

Sefa came in still smelling of coil grease and sat across from him without asking what this was about, which was either trust or exhaustion, and he suspected the difference no longer mattered between them. Wren posted themselves at the door. The rest of the crew drifted close enough to hear and far enough to pretend otherwise.

"The lattice has made its offer," Orsen said. "There's a translator's oath. If I swear it, I become its voice fully—permanent, binding, the real thing instead of the lie I've been selling. And if I'm its voice, it will hear humanity out. It won't let the fleets burn Ceres. It might even stop them from burning each other."

"But?" Sefa said. There was always a but. She'd learned to hear his buts before he did.

"It swears the oath through the imprint it holds. Through Mira." His voice did something he hated, a small crack like ice underfoot. "The oath burns her out. Her echo is the key that fits the lock, and keys get consumed. That's how the lattice works—it spends what it holds. Eleven years ago it spent nine seconds of gravity to find me. Now it wants to spend my sister to keep me."

The galley went very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the loop played on, muffled: a woman reading names. A child counting. Silence.

"So don't swear it," Wren said. "Find another way. You're good at other ways. You're—" they gestured at all of him, "—basically made of other ways."

"There is no other way. I've looked. I've spent two days looking, and the lattice doesn't negotiate, it *offers*, and its offers expire." He spread his hands. "In four hours, Earth goes silent. In less than that, the Compact launches first—they've got better stealth and worse patience. When they launch, the Concord answers, and the lattice defends itself, and somewhere in the middle of all that light there's a mining widow's grave and a research station and everything Mira ever touched, vaporized. Or I say the oath, and she's gone before the first missile clears its tube, and I have to stand in the machine's throat and speak for a species while knowing exactly what my voice cost."

Sefa hadn't moved. She was watching him with an expression he'd seen her wear at broken engines—the look of a woman calculating whether a thing could be saved or only mourned.

"You said the lie bought us a ceasefire window," she said. "How long?"

"Hours. Maybe less now."

"Then here's what we do." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped into the register she used for engines on fire—calm, precise, absolutely certain. "You don't swear yet. You make the lattice wait. You tell it your species requires time to prepare its testimony—that's plausible, we're plausibly stupid. Meanwhile I go down to the station with you, and I stand in front of whatever's left of your sister, and I tell her myself. Because this isn't only your choice, Orsen. You didn't steal her imprint alone, and you don't get to spend her alone. If she gets a vote, she gets it with both of us in the room."

"Mira's not—"

"She called you a childhood name," Sefa said. "I've read your file. All of it. Don't tell me what she is. Tell me what she'd want."

And that was the wound of it, laid open on the galley table between the tea bulbs and the grease rags: he didn't know. Eleven years of guilt had worn Mira smooth in his memory until he could no longer tell where his sister ended and his remorse began. He had built his whole life on the assumption that she would have wanted to be found. What if she'd wanted to be left alone? What if the echo in the lattice wasn't Mira at all, just the machine wearing her watermark like a mask, learning kindness the way it learned everything—by imitation?

The loop cycled. Names. Counting. Silence.

"Two hours," Orsen said finally. "I'll ask it for two hours. Then we go down together, and we ask her."

* * *

**Sefa**

The descent shuttle smelled of recycled air and old fear. Sefa flew it herself because no one else aboard had hands steady enough, and because she wanted, selfishly, to be the one who brought him home—if there was a home to bring him back to.

Below them, Ceres wore its new scars. Concord targeting lasers painted faint red lace across the ice, mapping kill solutions. On the far side of the rock, she knew, Meridian ships were doing the same in ultraviolet neither fleet admitted to. Between the two armadas, their small shuttle fell toward the surface like a seed, insignificant, ignored, carrying the only two people in the system who knew the war was a misunderstanding wrapped around a machine wrapped around a dead woman's voice.

"Contact in ninety seconds," she said.

Orsen sat beside her in the crash webbing, and his implant was glowing faintly at his temple, and his lips were moving without sound. Talking to it. Arguing with it, from the set of his jaw.

"Buying time?" she asked.

"Trying. It keeps saying the same thing." He turned to her, and in the shuttle's red descent lighting his face looked carved out of something older than fear. "It says: *THE LOOP ENDS. ALL LOOPS END. CHOOSE WHAT REMAINS.*"

The shuttle bucked through the first layer of atmosphere-thin dust, and the ice plains of Ceres opened beneath them, white on gray on white, and there—dark against the snow, a splinter of human making—Mira's station, its dome collapsed inward years ago, its antenna still standing like a hand raised in a classroom, waiting forever to be called on.

Sefa reached over and took his hand. His fingers were cold. She held on anyway.

"One more thing," she said, as the ground came up to meet them. "Whatever she says in there—whatever she chooses—I need you to hear me now. You come back out. Whatever the lattice promises you, whatever shape the future takes, you walk back to this shuttle, and you fly home with me. Swear it. Not to the machine. To me."

Orsen looked at their hands. Then at her. And Sefa watched him understand, all at once, that she knew—he knew that she knew about the forged credentials, about the seam she'd patched, about every lie he'd told and every lie she'd chosen to carry with him—and that she was offering him the truth anyway, as the last clean thing either of them owned.

"I swear," he said.

The shuttle touched down on the ice with a sound like a held breath finally released. Ahead, the station's airlock stood open, though no one had opened it, and from somewhere inside—down in the dark, beneath the ice, in the throat of the sleeping machine—came a pulse of gravity, soft as a heartbeat, keeping time.

Counting down.
