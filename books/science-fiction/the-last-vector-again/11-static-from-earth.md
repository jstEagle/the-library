## 11. Static from Earth

The tomato plants were sulking again.

I stood in Greenhouse Zero at 0600 ship time, which was a fiction I maintained the way I maintained everything else aboard the *Halcyon* — out of stubbornness and a vague sense that if I stopped pretending, the whole arrangement would collapse like a bad soufflé. The tomatoes had been producing fruit of a color best described as "apologetic," and I'd spent three days adjusting their light spectrum in increments so small that a physicist would have wept.

"Come on," I told them. "You're going to feed children someday. Act like it."

The children in question were currently frozen at minus one hundred ninety-six degrees Celsius in two bays, only one of which I'd known about until recently, and all of whom existed to me as names on manifests and, increasingly, as faces I'd reconstructed from medical files. E. Vashti — Elam Vashti, age fifty-one, former soil chemist, listed deceased for six years while his heartbeat ticked away behind a hydroponics wall — had become something of a fixation. I caught myself talking to the tomatoes about him. This was either grief or gardening technique. With me it had always been hard to tell the difference.

I was logging the morning's readings when the comm panel chimed.

Not the soft internal chime that meant *Idris has left you another passive-aggressive note about power draw anomalies*. Not the maintenance alert tone. This was the deep, resonant gong reserved for external transmissions — a sound I had heard exactly four times in eleven years, all of them routine pings from relay buoys that had long since gone deaf.

The panel read: **INBOUND — DEEP RELAY — PRIORITY: COMMAND — DECRYPTION IN PROGRESS.**

My hands went still over the seed tray. Outside the greenhouse glass, the stars did what they always did, which was nothing, magnificently.

Earth didn't call us. That was the first thing. Earth hadn't called us in six years, not since the last budget review concluded that keeping a terraformer on retainer for a colony ship drifting through the dark was, quote, "an emotional expenditure rather than a logistical one." We got supply schedules. We got automated acknowledgments. We did not get phone calls from home, because home had stopped considering itself our home somewhere around year nine.

The decryption bar crawled. I wiped my hands on my coveralls, thought better of it, wiped them again anyway, and walked to the panel as though approaching a sleeping animal.

**DECRYPTION COMPLETE. ROUTING TO COMMAND DECK.**

Of course. Command deck. Sefa Adeyemi's territory, sealed since the lockdown protocols tightened after my little greenhouse rebellion started making noise in the power logs. Idris had been covering for me with falsified reports that made my stomach hurt every time I thought about them — good, honest Idris Okonkwo, committing fraud with the same careful precision he used to alphabetize spare fuses.

But routing wasn't encryption. And command decks had speakers. And I, as the sole agricultural officer, had standing authorization to monitor any transmission affecting life-support resource planning, because whoever wrote that regulation had clearly never imagined a day when the two things would collide.

I pulled up the audio feed. My finger hovered over the accept key for a full ten seconds.

"Whatever this is," I said to the tomatoes, "it's going to change something."

They offered no comment. Tomatoes are terrible confidants.

I pressed the key.

* * *

What came through was static wearing the costume of a voice.

"—*Halcyon*, this is Sol Relay Command, priority dispatch, do you copy—" A burst of white noise, long enough that I checked the connection twice. Then, fragmenting through it: "—by authority of the Colonial Continuity Council... effective as of cycle 4,411... vessel *Halcyon* is hereby formally decommissioned—"

I sat down. There was no decision involved; my legs simply resigned.

"—all rescue funding rescinded... all recovery missions cancelled... asset recovery protocol 7 remains authorized... crew and cargo are reclassified as... salvage rights transferred to..."

Static swallowed the middle. I leaned close to the speaker, absurdly, as if proximity could repair a signal that had crossed forty years of vacuum to reach me. Fragments surfaced:

"...regret..."

"...the Council thanks the crew for their service..."

"...resource run is now the sole sanctioned operational plan..."

And then, cutting through the noise with terrible clarity, a single sentence delivered in the flat cadence of someone reading a legal document they'd read a hundred times:

"*The Halcyon and all persons aboard are declared lost with all hands.*"

Then silence. Real silence, the kind that follows a sound rather than preceding it.

I stayed crouched by the speaker for a long time. Long enough that the greenhouse lights cycled from morning spectrum to midday spectrum without my noticing. Somewhere behind me, an irrigation line hissed its scheduled hiss. The tomatoes continued existing. The universe declined to acknowledge that anything had happened.

Lost with all hands.

All hands. All two thousand and fourteen of them, including the half nobody was supposed to know about. Including Elam Vashti, who had been dead on paper for six years and was now dead on more paper. Including me. I put my hand flat against my own sternum, half expecting to find a hole there, some official seal stamped through the center of me.

Here is the strange thing, the thing I've turned over so many times since that it's worn smooth as river stone: I didn't cry.

I'd expected to cry. I'd built up a whole interior architecture around the day Earth finally said it out loud — the abandonment, the grief, the image of some committee room on a planet I barely remembered, people with coffee cups voting my existence into a footnote. I'd rehearsed the heartbreak the way you rehearse a speech you hope you'll never give.

Instead, what I felt — and I'm ashamed of how long it took me to name it — was relief.

Not the good kind. Not the kind with warmth in it. It was the relief of a diagnosis finally confirmed, the horrible gift of knowing. For eleven years I had tended crops for colonists who might wake when rescue came, and every seed I planted had been planted inside a question mark. Would the funding hold? Would the Council reverse course? Would someone, somewhere, remember us? Every log entry I recorded was addressed to a future that might arrive or might not, and I had bent my whole life around that maybe.

The maybe was dead now. Officially. Stamped. *Lost with all hands.*

And underneath the grief, quiet as a root finding water, a thought I didn't want to have but had anyway:

*Dead ships aren't watched.*

* * *

I found Idris in the maintenance spine, level seven, doing something unnecessary to a coolant junction because Idris processes emotion by tightening bolts. He heard my boots on the grating and turned, and whatever was in my face made him set down the wrench before I said a word.

"You heard," he said.

"The whole ship heard. It routed to command and the command deck speakers are still set to corridor broadcast because somebody never fixed the audio matrix after the year-three retrofit." I paused. "That was you, wasn't it? The retrofit."

"That was me." He rubbed his eyes under his glasses. "Eleven years ago. I keep meaning to file the work order."

"So everyone heard Earth declare us dead between breakfast and second shift."

"Everyone heard it badly." He almost smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful thing, that almost-smile, because it meant he was where I was — past the shock, into the strange clear air on the other side. "Half the crew thinks we're being decommissioned into scrap. Ravi Osei came to me asking whether 'salvage rights' means we're legally allowed to take the good chairs."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him to stop worrying about chairs." He looked at the coolant junction as if it might offer him a script. "Mara. The resource run. It's sanctioned now. Solely sanctioned. Which means Sefa doesn't need to wait for approval anymore. She has it. In writing. From a Council that just declared us ghosts."

"I know."

"And the selection list—"

"I know, Idris."

We stood there in the hum and the pipe-rattle, two people holding the same grenade and neither willing to be the one to drop it. Eleven years I'd been alone on this ship in every way that mattered. Now there was him, and the aloneness had changed shape rather than ended, and I found I preferred the new shape even though it scared me more.

"There's a version of this," I said slowly, "where we're free."

He frowned. "Free."

"Think about it. We're officially dead. Dead ships don't get audited. Dead ships don't get monitored. Nobody on Earth is listening to our telemetry anymore — why would they? You don't surveil a graveyard." I was pacing now, the way I paced the greenhouse rows, hands moving. "For eleven years everything we did was watched, logged, evaluated against the possibility of rescue. Every kilowatt accounted for. Every calorie. And now—" I turned to face him. "Now the accountants have closed the book. As far as Earth is concerned, this story ended today."

"Mara." His voice was gentle and careful, the voice he used when he thought I was spiraling. "Sefa is still here. The wake sequence is still scheduled. Being dead on paper doesn't stop the selection list from executing."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't stop it. But it means no one is coming to check the math."

He stared at me. I watched the idea land in him the way seeds land in trays — silently, invisibly, with all the real work happening underground. Idris Okonkwo was a man who had spent his entire life believing that rules were load-bearing, that if you removed one the whole structure came down on everyone's heads. I could see him testing the opposite hypothesis for the first time: that the rules had been removed *for* us, by fiat, from forty light-years away, and the structure was still standing, and the only thing holding it up now was us.

"A dead ship," he said quietly, "has no chain of command that Earth recognizes."

"It has whatever chain of command it builds for itself."

"That's mutiny."

"That's *gardening*," I said. "You plant something in ground everyone else gave up on, and you see what grows."

He laughed — actually laughed, short and startled, the sound bouncing off the pipes — and then pressed his hand over his mouth like the laugh had escaped without authorization. Which, for Idris, it had.

"This is insane," he said.

"Yes."

"We'd be committing crimes against a government that just declared us dead. Crimes against a ghost. Legally speaking, we'd be haunting ourselves."

"When you say it like that it sounds almost poetic."

"It sounds like a tribunal." But he wasn't saying no. I'd learned to hear the shape of Idris's refusals, and this one had no spine in it. He looked down the length of the maintenance spine, toward the bow, toward the command deck where Sefa Adeyemi was presumably receiving the same transmission and reading it very differently — not as liberation but as permission. "She'll move fast now. The sanction removes her last hesitation. Wake sequence could start within days."

"Then we move faster."

"Mara—"

"You said it yourself. No one's checking the math anymore." I stepped closer, close enough to see the exhaustion and the fear and, underneath both, the thing that had been growing between us since the corridor, patient as anything I'd ever planted. "Idris. They wrote us off. Everyone we were supposed to answer to just told the universe we don't exist. So the question isn't whether we're allowed to choose anymore." I held his gaze. "The question is whether we will."

For a moment he said nothing, and the ship breathed around us — air scrubbers and coolant pumps and the distant heartbeat of two cryo-bays, one legal and one secret, holding two thousand and fourteen people who had just been pronounced dead without waking up.

"My sister's on the discard list," he said finally. His voice was steady in the way that cost him something. "Amara. Bay One, berth 1-114. If the resource run executes as planned, she dies, and Earth files it under salvage." He took off his glasses, cleaned them on his sleeve, put them back on — the ritual he performed when he was rebuilding himself into a new shape. "I've been trying to save my integrity and my sister at the same time, and I finally understand I can't. There isn't enough of me for both."

"Integrity grows back," I said. "People don't."

"That's not how integrity works."

"It's exactly how integrity works. Ask any garden."

He exhaled, and something in his shoulders let go — not surrender, more like a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying, and discovering his arms still worked. "The wake sequence needs a systems officer's authorization to run at full draw. Mine. Which means I'm the last lock on the door." He met my eyes. "And you're apparently the person who picks locks."

"Hydroponics walls, technically. Same principle."

"God help us both." He picked up his wrench, then set it down again, deliberately, like a small ceremony. "There's a briefing tomorrow. Sefa's addressing the woken crew — framing the run, selling the triage. After that, the timeline accelerates. Whatever we're going to do, we do it before she finishes the sermon."

"Then we'd better decide what we're doing."

"We?" He raised an eyebrow, and beneath the dry tone ran something warmer. "Since when is it *we*?"

"Since a dead ship stopped having rules," I said. "Welcome to the afterlife, Officer Okonkko. Population: us."

"Okonkwo."

"I know how your name is spelled. I've spelled it in three falsified reports this month."

He laughed again, quieter this time, and shook his head, and for one unguarded moment he looked less like a man carrying a classified atrocity and more like what he'd probably been at twenty-two, before the *Halcyon* and its arithmetic got hold of him: someone who'd signed on to build worlds.

* * *

That night I made my log entry. I'd been recording them daily for eleven years, addressed always to the sleepers — *good morning, whoever you are, the tomatoes are struggling but the beans are heroic* — a one-sided correspondence with the frozen.

Tonight I sat in the greenhouse dark, mid-spectrum lights dimmed to embers, and spoke differently.

"Log entry, day 4,017," I said. "This one isn't for you. This one's for whoever comes next — next crew, next ship, next fool who inherits this garden. Today Earth declared us lost with all hands. Two thousand and fourteen people, written off in a four-minute transmission that arrived sounding like the ocean." I paused, watching the tomatoes in the ember-light, stubborn and green and utterly indifferent to their own obsolescence. "Here's what the transmission didn't include: we're still here. We woke up this morning. We're going to wake up tomorrow. And starting tonight, nobody — not a Council, not a commander, not a spreadsheet — gets to decide which of us counts as hands."

I touched the record-off key, then hesitated.

"One amendment," I added. "Elam Vashti, soil chemist, berth 2-007: hang on. Help is coming. It's just coming from inside the house."

I killed the recording. In the dark, the greenhouse hummed its low green hum, and behind the hydroponics wall, faint as a pulse under a wrist, Bay Two kept its secret rhythm — two thousand hearts beating in a ship that officially contained none.

Tomorrow, Sefa would preach. Tonight, I started pulling power schematics.

Dead ships, it turns out, can do anything.
