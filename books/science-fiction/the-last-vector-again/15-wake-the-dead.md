## 15. Wake the Dead

The greenhouse at 0300 ship-time smelled like basil and bad decisions.

Mara stood in the dark between the tomato racks with a datapad glowing against her chin and tried to remember when she had last been afraid like this — not the low-grade dread of another unanswered maintenance report, but the sharp, animal kind, the kind that lived in the teeth. The answer was: never. In eleven years aboard the *Halcyon* she had feared system failures, radiation squalls, and once, memorably, a fungal bloom that turned an entire crop of soybeans the color of old bruises. She had never feared people. There hadn't been enough of them awake to be frightening.

Now there were two of them, and one of them was her.

"You're muttering," Idris said from the doorway. He had his sleeves rolled up and a toolkit under one arm, which was somehow more alarming than if he'd brought a weapon. Systems officers didn't carry tools unless they intended to change something.

"I'm rehearsing," she said. "There's a difference. Muttering is aimless. This has structure."

"Structure." He came down the aisle toward her, ducking under the trellis wire out of habit even though it had been empty for years. "You're about to commit what Command would call mutiny and what I would call — " he paused, searching, " — a really aggressive gardening project."

"That's the spirit."

"Mara." He set the toolkit down on the workbench with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a gavel. "Say it out loud once. So we both know what we're doing."

She looked at him. In the grow-lights' dim amber standby glow he looked younger than his file photo and older than his voice, and tired in the specific way of a man who had spent weeks carrying something heavy and had finally set it down only to pick up something heavier.

"We're going to wake E. Vashti," Mara said. "One person. Partial thaw, minimum power, my bay's medical rig doing the work your cryo systems are too classified to do for free. And then we're going to ask her why the ship's own AI wrote her name into a shuttle manifest for a shuttle that never flew, and why someone listed her as dead six years before anyone on this ship died."

"And if she wakes up wrong?"

"Then we'll have killed a woman who was already dead on paper," Mara said. "Which, according to the official records, is no loss at all."

Idris winced. "That's grim."

"That's arithmetic. You taught me that word."

He almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost-smile, but she had learned to collect them the way she collected seed packets — carefully, against a future she couldn't quite believe in.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Bay Two access is through the hydroponics wall?"

"Through the wall, past the bulkhead, down a corridor that isn't on any deck plan I've ever seen. Your turn. Say it out loud."

He took a breath. "I'm falsifying nothing tonight. Not yet. But if anyone asks, you were running a soil-nutrient calibration, and I was — "

"Helping me calibrate soil."

"At three in the morning."

"Gardens don't sleep," Mara said. "Everyone knows that."

* * *

The bulkhead behind the hydroponics wall opened the way it always did now, reluctantly, with a groan of seals that hadn't moved in decades and a breath of cold air that smelled like snow falling inside a machine. Mara had made the trip four times since finding it, and each time the cold reached into her chest and rearranged something.

They walked the hidden corridor single-file, their headlamps making small nervous circles on the walls. Idris kept touching things — conduit housings, panel seams — reading the ship through his fingertips the way she read it through its air.

"This is original hull," he murmured. "This whole spine is original. They built it before launch. Before cryo. Which means Bay Two wasn't retrofitted after some emergency. It was planned."

"Planned by whom?"

"That," Idris said, "is the question I've been not-asking for six weeks."

The bay doors were taller than any others on the ship, and older in their design, and they opened without complaint, as if they'd been waiting.

Two hundred and forty-one pods. Mara had counted twice, then a third time just to feel the number settle into her bones like a stone into a pond. Rows of them, floor to ceiling, frost-rimed glass over still faces. Old faces, mostly. Lined ones. A child near the far wall who couldn't have been more than nine when she was frozen, her hair floating around her head in the zero-g chill like something growing.

E. Vashti was in row four, pod thirty-eight. Her manifest entry said *deceased, year six*. Her pod said otherwise: vitals steady, draw minimal, status green. Someone had marked a living woman dead and left her sleeping, and the pod had simply gone on keeping her, faithfully, stupidly, the way machines do — the way, Mara thought, she herself had gone on keeping the seed vault for colonists everyone had written off. She felt an unexpected kinship with the pod.

"She looks ordinary," Idris said quietly. He'd stopped beside her and was looking at Vashti's face through the frost. "I think I expected — I don't know. Something."

"People usually look ordinary," Mara said. "That's how they get discarded. Nobody checks the math on ordinary people."

She began the thaw sequence on the portable med-rig she'd hauled down in pieces over three nights, disguised in cargo manifests as agricultural equipment. A soil analyzer. A humidity controller. One very sophisticated life-support unit wearing a false name, which, she reflected, made it the most honest thing on the ship, because at least it knew what it was lying about.

Idris worked beside her in silence, splicing power from a junction he swore wouldn't show on any watch officer's board for at least six hours. His hands were steady. Hers weren't, and she noticed him noticing, and noticed him deciding not to say anything about it, and loved him a little for that, in the way you love someone for a small mercy during a large catastrophe.

The pod's frost began to crawl backward. Glass cleared in patches, like a window defogging in slow motion, and E. Vashti's face emerged piece by piece — jaw first, then mouth, then eyes closed beneath lids gone thin and blue-veined.

"Rewarming at four percent," Mara said. "It'll take hours. Faster than that and we risk neural — "

"I know. Slow is safe."

"Since when do you advocate slow?"

"Since I started committing crimes," Idris said. "Crimes should be done properly or not at all."

* * *

They waited in the corridor outside, sitting on the deck with their backs against the bulkhead, sharing a thermos of the greenhouse's chicory substitute, which tasted like coffee's ghost and was the closest thing either of them had to a vice.

"Do you ever think about Earth?" Idris asked, apropos of nothing.

"Constantly. Usually while complaining about the coffee."

"No, I mean — do you think it thinks about us? Anyone? Some clerk somewhere, filing our telemetry under *pending*?"

Mara considered. "I used to write my logs like letters. Like somebody was reading. Then somewhere around year five I realized I was writing to an archive, so I started writing to the seeds instead. At least the seeds might become something."

"That's very bleak."

"It's very honest. There's a difference."

He laughed — a real one, short and surprised out of him — and leaned his head back against the bulkhead. "My sister used to say honesty was just pessimism with good posture."

"Your sister sounds smarter than you."

"She is. That's not actually a joke." Something crossed his face then, quick and swallowed, and Mara recognized it because she'd seen it in the mirror for weeks: the shape of a name he wasn't saying yet. She didn't push. There would be time, or there wouldn't, and either way the pods didn't care about their feelings.

The med-rig chimed softly. Core temperature: thirty-one degrees. Thirty-four. Thirty-six.

When the pod hissed open, the smell that came out was antiseptic and long sleep, and E. Vashti drew a breath like a woman surfacing from deep water — huge, ragged, involuntary — and her eyes opened, and they were brown and furious and immediately, completely awake.

"Don't," she rasped, before either of them could speak. Her voice was rust and gravel. "Don't call medical. Don't log this. Who's on shift?"

Mara blinked. "No one. It's just us."

Vashti's eyes tracked from Mara to Idris, cataloguing — the civilian coveralls, the systems-officer tabs, the med-rig, the thermos. Cataloguing, Mara realized, exactly the way a person does when they've woken up in danger before and need to know fast whether this is the same danger.

"Year?" Vashti said.

"Ship-year twenty-two," Idris said.

Something went out of her shoulders. "Twenty-two. So it held." She coughed, wet and deep. "Water. And don't argue about protocol, I've been asleep sixteen years and I'm allowed to be rude."

Mara handed her the thermos. Vashti drank like the water owed her money.

"You're E. Vashti," Mara said. "Your file says you died in year six."

"My file says a lot of things." Vashti wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "Who are you? Actually. Not the costume."

"Mara Reyes. Terraformer. Seed vault."

"The garden-keeper." Something flickered across her face — recognition, maybe, or regret. "I know your name. Adeyemi mentioned you. *The plant woman won't matter.*"

Idris went very still beside Mara. "Commander Adeyemi told you that?"

"Adeyemi," Vashti said, "told me a great many things, back when she still bothered telling me things instead of just signing things." She looked down at her own hands, flexing them, relearning them. "How much do you two actually know? And be careful with the answer, because depending on what you say, one of us is going to have a very short conversation and the other one is going to have a very long night."

Mara glanced at Idris. He gave the smallest nod — *your call* — and she understood that this, too, was a kind of trust, and possibly the largest thing anyone had ever handed her.

"We know there's a second bay," she said. "Two hundred forty-one people. We know the wake list for the resource run covers roughly half the ship, and that the half left out skews old, sick, unskilled. We know your name was put on a shuttle manifest for a shuttle that never launched, authored by the ship's AI, and that you were declared dead six years before anyone on this vessel died. And we know the hidden bay's life-support draw has been throttled down incrementally for years, which means someone intends for the numbers to solve themselves."

Vashti listened without blinking. When Mara finished, the old woman was quiet for a long moment, and then she laughed — a dry, terrible sound with no humor in it anywhere.

"Oh, that's elegant," she said. "Throttle it slow enough and nobody has to pull a trigger. The ship just gets sad on schedule." She shook her head. "You're missing the beginning. You're all missing the beginning, because the beginning happened before your time and I'm the only one who saw it and they put me back to sleep for seeing it."

"Tell us," Idris said.

"Year six," Vashti said. "I was crew. Life-support diagnostics, second shift. I found the same thing you found — a bay that didn't exist, a draw that didn't add up. And I did what a good crew member does. I reported it." She smiled, and it was the worst smile Mara had ever seen. "And Commander Adeyemi — young then, ambitious, already writing speeches in her sleep — sat me down in the mess hall and explained the situation. Fuel margins. Mass budgets. The colony mission was already dead; Earth had stopped answering; command had decided the *Halcyon*'s only future was a resource run home, and a resource run can carry supplies or it can carry people, but not both. Not all the people. So they'd made a list."

"They decided that in year six?" Mara whispered. "Sixteen years ago?"

"The decision came from Earth, originally. Buried in a directive nobody wanted to read twice. But yes. Year six. And when I objected — vocally, I'll admit, I've never been good at quiet — they didn't space me. That would leave a record. Instead they declared me deceased, moved me to Bay Two, and put me under. Clean. Reversible. Deniable. And every few years since, I'd guess, someone else finds something, asks something, objects to something — and joins me. Two hundred forty-one isn't a random number, children. It's a *headcount*. Everyone who ever knew, plus everyone who was ever inconvenient, plus everyone whose skills didn't make the spreadsheet." She looked at the rows of pods stretching away into the dark, at the frost and the still faces and the child with the floating hair. "This isn't a secret bay. This is where the ship keeps its conscience."

The silence afterward had weight and texture. Mara became aware of the hum of the pods, hundreds of small faithful heartbeats of machinery, and thought: *all these years I've been tending seeds for the dead, and the dead were here, forty meters from my tomatoes, breathing.*

"Why wake us now?" Vashti asked suddenly, sharp again. "Why risk it? What changed?"

Mara looked at Idris. Idris looked at the deck.

"There's a wake sequence scheduled," he said. "Accelerated. Command-initiated. Bay One first, then the selected half of Bay Two. And the power budget for the accelerated cycle..." He stopped.

"Comes out of us," Vashti finished. Flat. Unsurprised. "Of course it does. How long?"

"Days," Mara said. "Maybe less."

Vashti closed her eyes. When she opened them again, the fury had cooled into something harder and more useful — the look, Mara thought, of a woman who had been angry for sixteen years and had gotten very good at aiming it.

"Well," Vashti said. "Then we don't have time for your little revelations, do we. Get me upright. Get me a terminal. And get ready to hear the rest of it — because what I found in year six wasn't just the bay." She gripped Mara's wrist with surprising strength, her hand still cold as vault-frost. "I found *who signed the order*. And it wasn't Earth, and it wasn't Adeyemi, and when I tell you the name, you're going to wish I'd stayed asleep."

Behind them, in the dark of the bay, two hundred and forty pods hummed on — and somewhere in the ship above, faint through the decks, an alarm began to ring.
