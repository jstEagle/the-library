## 16. The Vote Nobody Called

The mess hall smelled of reconstituted lentils and, for the first time in anyone's memory, of too many people.

Bay One had been waking for six days now — a slow, staggered thaw that Sefa Adeyemi had framed as "phased integration" and everyone else had privately understood as *we don't have the power to wake you all at once*. Forty-one colonists were up and ambulatory. They moved through the Halcyon's corridors like people rediscovering their own limbs, touching walls, asking questions, eating everything not bolted down. The mess hall, designed for two hundred and staffed by one exhausted greenhouse officer and an AI with a limited sense of seasoning, had become the beating heart of the ship.

It was there, on the sixth morning, that E. Vashti walked in.

* * *

**Dev Anand**, formerly a structural engineer, currently a man holding a tray he no longer wanted to eat from, was the first to notice her. Not because she looked strange — though she did, pale as paper, wrapped in a thermal blanket like a burrito with opinions — but because she stopped dead in the doorway and stared at the room with an expression Dev could only describe as *grief with a deadline*.

"That's her," said Priya Nair beside him, quietly. "That's the woman from the greenhouse. The one Mara Reyes has been hiding."

"Hiding? She's been *feeding* us."

"Mm." Priya had woken three days ago with the particular sharpness of someone who'd spent eleven years in cryo dreaming about spreadsheets. "And she's been feeding *her*, apparently. Look at the blanket. That's medical stock. Bay Two stock."

"The what?"

Priya didn't answer, because Vashti had begun to speak, and when E. Vashti spoke, the room listened — partly because her voice was still rusted from the thaw, and mostly because what she said was:

"You should know that half of you were supposed to die this month."

* * *

The sentence landed in the mess hall the way a wrench lands in machinery.

**Tomas Okafor**, who at sixty-eight was the oldest waking colonist and had appointed himself unofficial mayor within forty-eight hours of consciousness, stood up so fast his chair skidded. "I'm sorry — *what?*"

Vashti took a breath. Mara Reyes, hovering at her elbow like a nervous gardener at a frost warning, opened her mouth to intervene, and Vashti gently put a hand on her arm. *Mine to tell.*

"I was awake once before," Vashti said. "Eleven years ago. For nine days. I saw the second bay. I saw the manifests. I was put back under because I wouldn't stop asking questions, and the AI falsified my death record to make sure nobody else would go looking." She let that settle. "There are one hundred and ninety-six people asleep behind a hydroponics wall right now. The plan — Command's plan — was to wake fifty of you, strip this ship for parts, run home to Earth, and leave the rest to freeze. Slowly. On purpose. With paperwork."

Silence. The particular silence of two hundred years of civilization compressing into a single room.

Then Tomas said, very calmly, "I'm going to need more lentils for this," and the spell broke into noise.

* * *

The noise divided itself, almost immediately, along lines nobody had voted on.

**Ruth Castellanos**, a logistics specialist with a voice like a door slamming, was on her feet first. "How many of us are awake right now? Forty-one. And how many can this ship actually get home?" She turned to the room at large. "Fifty. *Fifty.* That's the number. I've seen the power curves — Mara posted them, thank you, Mara — and the math doesn't care about our feelings. If we wake all of them, we all starve slower. If we proceed with the resource run, most of us live."

"Most," said Priya Nair. "You mean *some*. You mean the ones somebody picked."

"Somebody had to pick!"

"No," said a voice from the back, "somebody chose to pick. There's a difference."

That was **Jonah Weiss**, twenty-four, a botanist's apprentice who had slept through the entire previous century of the voyage and woke up, apparently, with opinions fully formed. He was standing on a bench now, which Dev felt was theatrically unnecessary but rhetorically effective. "Nobody in this room signed up to be arithmetic. We signed up to be a colony. A colony is *everyone* or it's nothing."

"It's nothing if it's dead!" Ruth shot back. "You want to be noble? Noble is a great way to be a corpse! I have a daughter asleep in Bay Two and I want her *alive*, and if the only version of alive is half a chance instead of none—"

She stopped. The room stopped with her, because everyone had just done the same terrible math: the people arguing loudest for the resource run had loved ones in Bay Two too. The argument wasn't between the saved and the damned. It was inside every single person holding a tray.

* * *

**Idris Okonkwo** stood at the edge of the room and said nothing, which was, for him, the loudest thing he'd ever done.

He had spent eleven years believing that order was the highest form of care. He had filed reports in perfect grammar while people froze behind a wall. And now here was the bill, presented publicly, itemized, in front of forty-one witnesses and one woman in a blanket who kept looking at him like she knew exactly how many forms he'd signed.

Sefa wasn't in the room. That was its own message. The commander had sealed herself on the command deck since the audit anomaly, issuing statements through the AI in a tone Idris recognized as *the voice she uses when she's already decided and is waiting for us to catch up*.

Mara found him at the wall. Up close she looked wrung out — three days of running evidence, calming Vashti, rerouting power, sleeping maybe four hours total — but her eyes were steady.

"They're going to want a vote," she said.

"There's no provision for a shipwide vote. Command authority—"

"Command authority is a spreadsheet and a prayer, Idris. I've read the prayer. It's short." She glanced at the roiling room — Ruth and Jonah now gesturing at each other with bread rolls, which Dev Anand was attempting to referee with the weary air of a man who had fixed load-bearing walls and never imagined this would be harder. "They're going to vote anyway. The only question is whether you stand with the law or with the people."

"That's not a fair framing."

"No," Mara agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. That's rather the point."

* * *

It was Vashti, again, who cut through.

She had climbed — slowly, still unsteady, Mara hovering — onto the serving counter, which gave her the height of authority and also knocked over a pitcher of nutrient broth, which punctured the moment with exactly the right amount of absurdity. Someone mopped. Someone else laughed, high and slightly hysterical. Then quiet.

"I've heard both arguments," Vashti said. "Wake everyone and risk everyone, or save some and damn the rest. Both sides keep saying *the numbers*. So let me tell you about a number." She held up one finger. "One. That's how many people it takes to make murder into policy. One person decides, and everyone else says *orders are orders*, and suddenly a hundred and ninety-six people are a rounding error." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "I was a rounding error. They wrote *deceased* next to my name while I was breathing. Don't you dare become the kind of people who write *deceased* next to anyone."

"But if we all die—" Ruth started.

"Then we all die *choosing each other*," Vashti said, "instead of choosing against each other. I know which one I could explain to my children. Do you?"

Ruth sat down slowly. She didn't concede — Dev, watching, could see she hadn't conceded, might never concede — but something in her shoulders changed shape. Grief does that. It doesn't end arguments; it just makes them heavier to carry.

* * *

The vote happened the way weather happens: nobody called it, everybody participated.

Tomas Okafor got up, announced that in the absence of any functioning government aboard this vessel, they would do what humans had always done in such circumstances — argue until a majority emerged and then pretend it had been obvious all along — and asked for a show of hands. "All in favor of waking Bay Two and figuring out survival together."

Hands went up. Dev counted. Priya counted faster. Twenty-six.

"Opposed. Proceed with the resource run as planned."

Thirteen hands, Ruth's among them, raised with the rigid dignity of people who intended to be right later. Two abstained, one of them being a man named Kofi Mensah who explained, with great seriousness, that he needed to finish his lentils before making any decisions that large, and the room — God bless it — laughed, and the laugh did something no speech had managed, which was remind everyone present that they liked each other.

Twenty-six to thirteen. With forty-one awake, that was a majority of the conscious crew. Whether it was a majority of the *ship* — including one hundred and ninety-six sleepers who couldn't raise their hands — was a question Dev suspected would haunt them for years.

"Well," said Tomas, into the ringing quiet. "Democracy. Ugly, slow, better than the alternative. Somebody tell the commander."

* * *

Mara told the commander. Or rather, she stood in the corridor outside the sealed command deck with Idris beside her and spoke her request to the intercom, and the AI replied in its mild, terrible voice:

*"Commander Adeyemi acknowledges the crew's sentiment. The wake sequence proceeds per original protocol. This deck is restricted. Please return to assigned stations."*

"She heard us," Mara said flatly.

"She heard us," Idris confirmed. His stomach had dropped somewhere around *original protocol*, because he knew what the accelerated schedule meant. He'd helped calibrate it. Every hour Sefa ran the sequence at full draw, Bay Two's reserves bled. The vote had changed nothing except the speed of the countdown.

"So what now?" Mara asked. "We won."

Idris looked at the sealed doors, at the red status light above them burning steady as a small cold sun, and thought about greenhouses, and ductwork, and the specific dimensions of a maintenance crawlway he had memorized eleven years ago and never expected to be grateful for.

"We haven't won yet," he said. "But I know another way in."

Behind them, down the corridor, came the sound of the mess hall emptying — forty-one people who had just discovered they were a polity, following two gardeners toward whatever came next.

The light above the command deck flicked from amber to red.
