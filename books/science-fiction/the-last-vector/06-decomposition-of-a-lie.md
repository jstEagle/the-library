## 6. Decomposition of a Lie

The archive room on Meridian Station had once been a chapel.

I don't know which faith. The founders were eclectic in their piety, the way people are when they're building something they intend to be forgiven for later. There are recesses in the walls where votive lights used to burn, and now they hold data spools — forty years of station records, agricultural yields, air-scrubber maintenance logs, the slow bureaucratic heartbeat of a colony that believed, for most of its life, that someone in the Core was still reading its reports.

No one was reading them. I know because I am the one who reads them now, and I am one person, and I read everything, and I can tell you that the silence from Earthside has been total for thirty-one years. My predecessor — my original — kept filing anyway. Faith of a different kind. Or maybe just habit so deep it had grown a skeleton and learned to walk without him.

Tonight I pulled the murder file again.

* * *

The file is thin. That's the first thing you notice if you've ever handled a real investigation, and the second thing you notice is that it's thin *deliberately*. Documents have weight the way bodies do; a case with no body has no weight at all, and this one floats. Eleven years old and it still drifts through the station's records like something drowned, surfacing only when I go looking.

Incident report: Ansel Underhill, Records Keeper, found deceased in Reactor Annex Two, cause of death listed as thermal trauma secondary to coolant line failure. Time of death: 0347 station time, the ninth of Thawmonth. Investigating officer: Odile Voss, acting under emergency authority. Witness statements: two. One from Doctor Ilya Reznik, who signed off on the medical findings. One from Sefa Okonkwo's predecessor, an engineer named Brandt who took the next transport out and never sent so much as a postcard back to the world he'd helped bury.

Two witnesses. Both of whom are still here. Both of whom I have spoken to within the last three days about other things entirely, and both of whom went quiet in the particular way people go quiet when a door they thought was sealed turns out to have been left unlocked.

I sat in the chapel-archive with the file spread across three screens and the votive recesses glowing their soft amber standby-light, and I did what I do. What I was made to do, if you believe the technicians; what I choose to do, if you believe me. I catalogued.

The incident report says coolant line failure. The annex maintenance log for that night says the coolant lines were serviced eleven days prior and passed inspection with margin to spare. The medical certificate lists burns consistent with superheated vapor exposure across sixty percent of the body. But Ilya's own treatment notes — the ones he filed dutifully into the general medical archive, perhaps thinking no one would ever cross-reference them, perhaps hoping someone would — describe *cauterized* wound channels. Vapor doesn't cauterize. Vapor scalds, blisters, sloughs skin in sheets. Cauterization is what happens when energy passes *through* tissue, when something with intent behind it burns a hole clean and seals it as it goes.

Coolant lines don't have intent.

And there was a third document, buried in a subdirectory labeled REACTOR — ROUTINE, filed by my dead original himself, timestamped 0211 the same night: a work order requesting access to Annex Two's auxiliary console, reason given as "signal correlation study." He signed it with his full ceremonial signature, the one he used on documents he considered important. I know this because I use the same hand. When I first read my own name in that old script — the slight downward pressure on the terminal stroke, the way he crossed his t's like he was closing gates — I had to put the tablet down and breathe for a while.

He went into that annex at 0211 to study signals. He died at 0347. Ninety-six minutes.

I have spent four nights now trying to fill those ninety-six minutes, and tonight I finally had the piece I needed, because three nights ago a dead language knocked on our door and asked to be let in.

* * *

The signal arrived on the deep-array at 0312 station time. I've listened to it maybe two hundred times since. Structured, repeating, patient as a heartbeat — Kessari, or the ghost of Kessari, a language that has been extinct for two centuries except in the places extinction doesn't reach: court records, confiscated artifacts, and, apparently, the private files of a murdered records keeper.

Here is what I did tonight, alone in the chapel with the dead around me in their spools: I took the signal's arrival timestamp and I laid it against the reactor logs for the same window, eleven years ago.

Reactor Annex Two keeps a power-draw record. Everything on Meridian does; we are a poor colony and we meter every watt the way misers count coins. And on the night my original died, between 0211 — when he requested access — and 0347 — when his body cooled enough for the alarms to matter — the annex drew power. A lot of it. A sustained, anomalous draw on the auxiliary console circuit, peaking at 0339, eight minutes before the official time of death.

Eight minutes before they say he burned, he was running a decryption workload.

You don't decrypt anything on reactor auxiliary hardware unless you're hiding the fact that you're decrypting it. The mainframes are logged, monitored, visible. The annex console was a blind spot — powerful, isolated, forgotten. He chose it the way I would have chosen it. The way I *did* choose it, three nights ago, when I ran the first spectral analysis of the Kessari signal on that same machine rather than risk the network noticing what I was feeding it.

I sat very still when that realization finished arriving. The chapel hummed. Somewhere below my feet, past deck after deck of empty habitat, the reactor turned its slow wheel, and beneath the reactor —

— but I'm getting ahead of myself. Beneath is not yet proven. Let me stay with what is.

What is: my original decoded something in that annex, on that night, using hardware chosen for its invisibility. What is: whatever he decoded, he was killed for it within minutes of finishing. What is: the cover story — coolant failure — was signed by the two people still alive who could have contradicted it, and neither has contradicted it in eleven years, and one of them ordered me to destroy a recording three days ago with her hands shaking.

Odile Voss investigated her own cover story. She wrote the report that closed the case she opened. I keep turning that over like a stone, looking for what lives underneath.

* * *

There's one more thing. I almost didn't include it, because including it means admitting how far down the rabbit hole goes, and there is a part of me — the part that inherited his caution along with his face — that wants very much to stop digging and go back to counting scrubber filters and pretending the universe is boring.

But I am the record now. If I flinch, the truth flinches with me.

In the annex power log, the decryption draw wasn't continuous. It pulsed. Regular intervals, roughly every forty seconds, for the entire ninety-six minutes. I've seen that pattern exactly once before.

Three nights ago. In the signal.

The Kessari transmission repeats on a forty-second cycle. Not approximately. Exactly. Down to the tenth of a second, across two centuries of supposed extinction and however many light-years of distance, the rhythm matches the rhythm my original fed into a hidden console the night he died.

Which means one of two things, and both of them take the floor out from under me. Either he was decoding a signal then that is the same signal arriving now — which would make it not new, not sudden, not a knock from the forgotten past but a *return*, something that has been circling us for over a decade, patient as debt — or the pattern isn't coming from outside at all. Either way, the word "distress" starts to feel wrong in my mouth. Distress is random. Distress is desperate. This is scheduled. This is a lighthouse keeping its hours.

He heard it first. He understood enough of it to need ninety-six minutes of hidden computation. And then someone made sure he never said what he found.

I closed the file. All three screens went dark, and in the black glass I caught my reflection — his face, my face, the face from the incident photographs, younger in those, unburned, already tired. Eleven years ago someone looked at that face and decided it knew too much. Tonight the same face looked back at me from the dark, and I understood that the decision had never really been about him.

It was about the signal. It was always about the signal. Murder, on Meridian, is not passion and it is not madness. It is *maintenance*. You patch the hull, you service the coolant lines, and when a man decodes something dangerous in the dark of the reactor annex, you decommission him and file him under routine and the station goes on breathing, because the station must go on breathing, because forty-one souls depend on the lie the way they depend on air.

Except the lie is leaking. The signal came back. The dead language found its way home. And somewhere in the sealed sections of this station — I am certain of it now, certain the way you're certain of a load-bearing wall — there is a thing that speaks Kessari, and my original found the edge of it, and they killed him to keep the rest of us from hearing it sing.

Tomorrow I will ask Sefa to help me calibrate the deep-array. Routine maintenance. Nothing worth logging.

Tonight I sit in the chapel of a dead god, surrounded by the records no one reads, and I begin — carefully, quietly, in my own hand, the downward pressure on the final stroke — to write the true history of Meridian Station.

It starts with a murder that wasn't an accident.

It will end, one way or another, with what's buried underneath us.
