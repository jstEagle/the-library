## 29. The Battle of the Airlocks

Sefa had always believed the station would die the way old things die — slowly, in the dark, of a thousand small failures no one bothered to fix. She had spent thirty years patching those failures with her hands, and she knew Meridian's body better than her own: every groan in the coolant lines, every shudder in the spin bearings, every place the hull had thinned to something like paper. She had never once imagined the station would die loudly.

The first explosion came at 0347, and it came through her boots before it came through her ears.

She was in the lower machine shop, wedged under a coolant manifold with a welding torch in her teeth, when the deck jumped. A rain of rust and insulation sifted down through the overhead grating, catching the torch-flame like orange snow. She spat the torch into her hand and lay still, listening the way she listened to sick engines — not for the sound itself but for what the sound was doing to everything around it.

The station groaned. Somewhere above, a pressure door slammed. Then another. Then the alarms woke, all of them at once, a chorus she had silenced a hundred times for drills and never once heard for real.

*Airlock Two,* said the station. *Airlock Two. Breach. Breach.*

"Damn it, Reyes," Sefa said to the manifold, to the dark, to no one. "Damn it, damn it, damn it."

She rolled out from under the pipe, grabbed her kit, and ran.

* * *

The corridor outside the machine shop was full of people who should not have been awake, and none of them were screaming, which frightened her more than screaming would have. Forty-one souls, and it seemed like all of them were in the passageway at once — Ilya in his sleep shirt with a med kit already strapped across his chest like a bandolier, old Marta Okafor holding a fire extinguisher as if she'd been born with it, the Ferro twins, seventeen and terrified and trying not to show it, each gripping a length of hydraulic pipe.

They looked at her. That was the thing Sefa felt in her chest like a fist — they looked at *her*, because Ansel was down in the vault, because Odile was at the door, and because for thirty years when something broke, you found Sefa.

"Talk to us," Ilya said.

"Compact marines breached Two," she said. "Reyes moved early. Ansel said she might." She was already moving, and they fell in behind her the way water follows a crack. "They want the vault corridor. That's the only way down. So that's where we stop them."

"With what?" Marta asked, and it wasn't a challenge. It was an engineering question, and Sefa loved her for it.

"With the station," Sefa said. "Meridian's been trying to kill us for thirty years. Tonight it works for us."

* * *

The vault corridor ran from the junction at Hub C down a gentle spiral toward the buried vault — two hundred meters of deck plate and conduit and old, tired doors, descending through the station's bones like a throat. It had been built for cargo, wide enough for haulers, and that width was a gift and a curse: room to build barricades, and room for marines in combat armor to advance four abreast.

Sefa stood in the junction and saw it all in her head the way she saw a failed system — every fault line, every place the pressure would come. Behind her, colonists were dragging what she'd asked for: cargo pallets, the severed arm of a loading crane, plates of hull steel pried from the derelict shuttle bay, welding packs, and — her particular favorite — the contents of the water reclamation tanks' emergency drains.

"Here's the truth," she told them, turning, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "We can't beat them. They're soldiers with powered armor and we're — " she gestured at the pipe wrench in Marta's fist, at the twins, at all of it — "we're this. But they don't know this station. They know corridors that are lit and dry and honest. This station lies. Every deck of it lies. We're going to make them fight a building that's fighting back."

She pointed. "Marta — barricade at the C-three door, but leave it looking flimsy. I want them to think one push gets through. Twins — you're on the flood valves with me. Ilya — casualty station in the hydroponics alcove, back behind the bulkhead. Anyone bleeds, they bleed *there*, not in the open."

"And you?" Marta asked.

"I'm going to be everywhere," Sefa said, "and nowhere. That's the advantage of knowing every crawl space in this station. Nobody's ever shot an engineer in a duct."

Someone laughed — a short, cracked sound, but a laugh. It was enough.

* * *

The marines came up from Airlock Two at 0410, and the first thing they met was the dark.

Sefa had spent the previous hour killing her own station section by section — pulling breaker links she'd spent years keeping alive, dropping whole decks into blackout, leaving only the emergency strips glowing their sullen red. It felt like murder. It felt, she realized with a strange, cold clarity, like the opposite of everything she'd ever done, and she did it anyway, because the woman who kept Meridian alive knew better than anyone exactly how to let it die.

They came four abreast through the C-three junction, lamps sweeping, and the barricade held for eleven seconds. It was supposed to hold for eleven seconds. Then the crane arm went over with a shriek and the marines advanced into the corridor beyond, boots ringing on deck plate, and Sefa, flat on her belly in the maintenance crawlspace above the ceiling grid, pulled the valve.

The lower decks of Meridian had flooded decades ago — condensate, coolant, the slow weeping of a station no one drained. The water had nowhere to go, so it waited, and for thirty years Sefa had kept it waiting, pumping it from pocket to pocket like a secret. Now she opened the pocket above the spiral.

It came down the corridor in a brown, freezing wall, knee-deep, then waist-deep, carrying twenty years of silt and rust and the smell of dead pipes. The marines' lamps swung wildly. Powered armor was strong, but it was not buoyant, and it was not quiet, and in water that cold the servos screamed.

"Go dark," Sefa murmured into the comm Ilya had scrounged from the med-bay — a doctor's intercom, crackling, barely a whisper of a network. "Go dark and let them walk."

The colonists in the alcoves went still. And the marines, blind and deaf in the flood, kept coming, because soldiers advance, that is what they are for, and Sefa lay in the dark above them listening to their boots slow down in the water and thought: *you're in my station now. You're in my station, and it's drowning.*

The first shot went wild and hit a conduit, and the corridor filled with scalding steam.

* * *

It was not a battle. Sefa would spend the rest of her life refusing to call it a battle. It was a machine breaking, and she was the one who knew where it would break.

The steam drove the marines back to the junction. They regrouped — she could hear their commander's voice, clipped and furious, through the water's hiss — and came again along the ceiling grid, which was clever, which was the one route Sefa hadn't rigged, and so the fight finally came to hands.

She saw it from the crawlspace, through a grating, in flashes of red emergency light: Ilya's people dragging a wounded marine out of the water with a cargo strap, not to capture him but because he was drowning and they couldn't watch. Marta Okafor swinging her extinguisher into the faceplate of a marine who'd gotten a hand on the barricade, the white cloud billowing, the marine staggering back into the flood. One of the Ferro twins — she never learned which — braining a soldier with a pipe and then standing over him, shaking, saying *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry* in a voice that carried even through the steam.

And she saw the moment it stopped being theirs to hold.

The marines brought up a breaching charge. She heard the whine of it charging and understood, with the flat certainty of an engineer reading a gauge, that the next door in the spiral was the last soft one. Beyond it the corridor narrowed. Beyond that, the vault. Beyond that, Ansel, and Ashkav's people holding the lower approach, and Odile —

Odile. The comm crackled. *Sefa.* Ansel's voice, thin through the bad network. *Sefa, how long can you hold them?*

Sefa looked down through the grating at the flooded corridor, at the steam, at her neighbors bleeding into the water. She thought about the answer the way she thought about load tolerances. Honestly.

"Ten minutes," she said. "Maybe twelve. Then I'm out of station."

"Ten minutes," Ansel repeated, and there was something in the voice that she'd heard once before, years ago, from the original Underhill, in the one conversation they'd ever shared: the sound of a person deciding. "That's enough. Keep them in the water, Sefa. Whatever happens, keep them in the water."

The line died. She lay in the dark and did the only thing left in her inventory: she opened every remaining pocket at once.

* * *

The lower spiral became a river.

It came down the stairs in cataracts, black and howling, and it took the barricade with it, and it took the breaching charge's aim with it, and it took two of the marines off their feet entirely — she saw their lamps go under and come up again, saw hands — colonist hands, Ilya's people, always Ilya's people — haul them gasping into the alcove because Meridian had forty-one souls and not one of them could be taught to let a man drown, even tonight, even now.

The colonists paid. She kept a count the way she kept a parts inventory, because someone had to, because the record mattered, because *no one else would*:

Marta Okafor took a round through the shoulder dragging the second Ferro twin out of the open. Ilya had her in the alcove before the echo died, and when Sefa passed the bulkhead she saw the doctor's hands red to the wrist and his face perfectly calm, the calm of a man who had decided that shaking could wait.

Old Yusef, who had never missed a census day in forty years, caught a stun-baton across the temple and went into the water, and it was the marines' own wounded — the one they'd pulled out — who hauled him up, and stood there in the flood holding an old man he'd been sent to subdue, looking at his own hands like they belonged to someone else.

The twins held the valve station when Sefa had to run the length of the deck to re-seat a breaker, holding it the way she'd shown them, both hands, all their weight, two children keeping a river in its bed.

And through it all the station sang — that was the only word she had for it, the groaning, keening song of a hull taking stresses it was never rated for, and Sefa realized she was humming along, matching it, the way she did when she worked, because this *was* work, this was the last job Meridian would ever ask of her, and she was not going to fail it.

At 0451 — she would check the log later, because the record mattered — the marines stopped.

Their commander's voice came through the water and the steam, amplified, official, absurd in its calm: *This station is declared salvage under Compact statute. Resist further and forfeit protection under —*

The rest was lost under the sound of the gunship.

It came through the hull like a word in a language Sefa didn't speak but understood completely. The Remnant gunship — Ashkav's scarred, half-dead ship that should not have been spaceworthy — had come around the station's blind side, and it put its point-defense cannons into the Compact boarding torpedoes still docked at Airlock Two, and Airlock Two, along with the marines' ride home and their heavy equipment and their certainty, ceased to exist in a bloom of white that Sefa felt in her teeth.

The corridor went quiet, except for the water.

The marines stood in it, lamps down, weapons down, six of them left of the twenty who'd boarded, and their commander looked — Sefa saw it through the grating, and never forgot it — looked at the flooded corridor, at the colonists in the alcoves with their pipes and extinguishers and bleeding hands, at old Yusef dripping and alive, and made the arithmetic soldiers make when the numbers stop working.

"Fall back to the junction," the commander said. "Hold position."

Not retreat. Not yet. But hold — in a flooded corridor, on a station that fought, with no way home, while somewhere below them a clone and a Kessari captain and a dying old woman were doing something with a vault that had made two fleets stop shooting at each other and start shooting at the dark.

Sefa lowered herself out of the crawlspace, dropped the last meter into waist-deep water, and stood up in front of them, wrench in hand, an old woman in a burned coverall with rust to her elbows.

"This is as far as the station goes," she told the commander, and her voice didn't shake, and she would be proud of that for the rest of her life. "Everything past this door belongs to somebody else. And they're about to answer."

The commander's lamp came up, blinding her.

"Answer what?" the commander said.

And from below, from under the deck, from under two hundred years of silence, the vault began to sing.
