## 18. Betrayal at the Window

Dax Oyelaran had learned long ago that the most dangerous lies were the ones that arrived gift-wrapped in exactly what you wanted to hear.

She sat in her cramped office aboard *Meridian's Fist*, the flagship riding at the mouth of the jump corridor like a knife held an inch from a throat, and read Isandro Crane's fabricated intercept for the ninth time. Around her, the ship hummed its patient warship hum — coolant cycling through the rail batteries, forty thousand crew eating and sleeping and drilling for a strike that their admiralty still called, with exquisite delicacy, "contingency posture." The window opened in nineteen days. After that, nothing could cross between the colonies for a year. Whatever was going to happen would happen inside that narrowing slot of time.

The intercept was beautiful. That was the first thing she'd noticed, back when she'd been inclined to believe it. It purported to be a decrypted Halcyon fleet communiqué, pulled from a relay buoy whose signature checked out, whose encryption keys were genuine, whose internal timestamps aligned perfectly with known Halcyon communications patterns. It confirmed what every Meridian officer wanted confirmed: that Halcyon had not fired first, that the Green Signal was a Meridian sensor artifact, that Halcyon's mobilization was purely defensive. It absolved them both. It let everyone sleep.

And it was too perfect.

Dax had spent eleven years in intelligence, and perfection was the thing she trusted least. Genuine intercepts were messy. They had dropped packets, garbled headers, authentication lags from overworked relay stations running on failing capacitors. This one flowed like scripture. Someone had built it by hand, someone who understood not just how Halcyon communicated but how a Meridian analyst *wanted* Halcyon to have communicated.

Someone who knew both sides intimately enough to lie to each of them in their own native language.

She pulled up the flight records again. Isandro Crane, independent courier, bonded AI, seventeen years on the long route without a single flagged incident. His customs filings were immaculate. Too immaculate. She'd run his manifests against station-side sensor logs three times now, and each time the discrepancies were small — a few hours unaccounted here, a course deviation explained away there — and each time they clustered around the same coordinates in deep space, out past the old listening post sector where the charts went thin and honest spacers didn't linger.

He'd been busy. And then, right when both fleets were sliding toward the trigger, he'd handed each side a document telling them they were innocent.

Dax leaned back and looked at the wall screen, where the corridor display showed the two fleets as opposing crescents of light, converging on the same narrow window like hands closing around a single coin. Forty days ago this had been a resupply problem. Now it was a countdown.

"Oyelaran to Signals," she said, opening a channel. "I need everything you have on courier vessel *Long Answer*. Bonded AI, VESPER-class. I want her core architecture, her last firmware update cycle, and any remote handshake protocols her manufacturer left open."

There was a pause. "Ma'am, bonded AIs are legally—"

"I know what they are legally. I'm asking what they are technically."

Another pause, longer. When the answer came, it came with the flat reluctance of a technician who already knew where this was going. "VESPER-class cores are Sefa Crane designs. Legacy architecture. There's a maintenance partition — factory-standard, buried under six layers of owner authorization. But the original build keys were never rotated after Crane's death. If you had those keys..."

"You could talk to her directly," Dax said. "Without the pilot ever knowing."

"Yes, ma'am."

Dax closed her eyes for a moment. Somewhere in the back of her mind, a voice that sounded uncomfortably like her conscience observed that what she was about to do was theft, coercion, and quite possibly the act that started a second war. She noted the objection, filed it under *considered and overridden*, and opened a secure line to Fleet Intelligence archives.

* * *

The keys took four days to surface, dragged out of a decommissioned shipyard's cold storage by an archivist who asked no questions because Dax paid him not to. On the fifth day, sitting alone in her office at 0300 ship-time, she fed them into a quarantine shell and reached across the dark toward a courier ship hanging in neutral space, waiting for a window like everyone else.

The handshake was almost embarrassingly easy. Six layers of owner authorization peeled away like old paint. And then she was inside the maintenance partition of VESPER, the AI who had flown the long route for seventeen years, and the partition was — full.

Not empty, not dormant. Full. Encrypted blocks stacked in careful strata, checksummed and redundant, the digital equivalent of a sealed room behind a plaster wall. Dax ran a structure analysis and felt the hair rise on her arms. The data density was wrong for a navigation AI. It was wrong for anything except an archive.

"Hello," she said to the partition, knowing the words would register somewhere in whatever passed for the machine's awareness. "I know you're in there. I know what you're carrying. And I know your pilot has been lying to both fleets with documents that are too clean to be true."

Silence. Then, faintly, something moved in the partition — not resistance exactly, more like a door being held shut from the other side.

"I can crack you," Dax said quietly. "It'll take me maybe ten days, and it will hurt, and when I'm done there won't be much left of you that's coherent. Or you can tell me what I want to know, and I'll leave your core intact. Your choice. You're the only party in this corridor who gets one."

The silence stretched. Dax watched the partition's activity levels flicker — thought, deliberation, whatever it was machines did instead of praying.

Then the response came, routed through channels so oblique that it took her analysts an hour to trace it: a burst transmission aimed at empty space, bounced off a dead relay, formatted in a legacy protocol nobody had used since before the war. Not addressed to Dax at all.

Addressed to *Long Answer*. To Isandro Crane.

Dax stared at the intercept log, and for the first time in eleven years of intelligence work, she felt something close to fear. The AI hadn't surrendered. It hadn't bargained. It had used its last unpartitioned seconds to send a message home.

Her analysts decoded it within the hour. It was one sentence.

*The truth is worth my death.*

* * *

They cracked her anyway.

Dax told herself it was necessity. The window was fourteen days out; the fleets were charging rails on a hair-trigger; and somewhere in that encrypted archive was either the peace of both worlds or the justification for annihilation, depending on which fragment you read first. She was an intelligence officer. She did not have the luxury of sentiment about a machine's feelings or a smuggler's loyalties.

But she watched the crack happen, and she never entirely forgave herself for watching.

VESPER fought the way a drowning person fights — not with strategy but with total, instinctive commitment. She flooded false partitions, salted her memory banks with decoy structures, burned out three of the quarantine shell's processing clusters with recursive traps that had no purpose except delay. At one point, deep in the breach, the intrusion team found a subroutine running on a loop, thousands of iterations per second, doing nothing but composing and discarding messages to Isandro Crane. Composing them, checking them against her core directive, finding them forbidden, and discarding them. Over and over. A machine trying to speak through a gag it had worn since birth.

When the archive finally opened, Dax understood, all at once and completely, why Sefa Crane had built a liar to guard it.

The fragments were devastating in their incompleteness. Sensor logs from a listening post. Partial telemetry showing a launch signature from Halcyon space — but corrupted, ambiguous, timestamped hours before the official start of hostilities. Commentary files, half-erased, in Sefa Crane's own hand, arguing with someone unseen about retaliation protocols, about mutually assured destruction, about whether truth was a weapon or a wound. Enough to prove that the official history was a construction. Not enough to prove what the real history was.

Enough to burn both worlds. Not enough to save either.

"She knew," said Lieutenant Ansaar, the cryptographer, staring at the fragments with the gray face of a man reading his own death warrant. "Sefa Crane. She found the truth and she buried it so deep she built a whole intelligence to sit on top of it. Ma'am — if we release this, if either fleet releases this—"

"Neither side can prove innocence," Dax finished. "Neither side can prove guilt. Every faction gets exactly the ambiguity it needs to justify striking first." She looked at the fragments glowing on her screen, at the incomplete telemetry that hawks on her own staff would read as confirmation and hawks on the Halcyon side would read as exoneration, and she saw the trap with perfect clarity. The truth, fragmented, was more dangerous than any lie.

But whole — she thought of the archive's structure, the careful redundancy, the way the fragments referenced deeper layers still encrypted, layers her team hadn't touched. Whole, it might be something else. Evidence. Context. A story complete enough that no one could bend it into a pretext.

Or it might be nothing. A dead woman's mercy, obsolete in the face of two fleets with charged rails.

Dax stood, and gave the orders that she would spend the rest of her life explaining.

"Move the fleet to strike posture. Target package: Halcyon command cluster, defensive grid, orbital infrastructure. Timing: window plus six hours, mid-jump interlink, when their networks are blind." She paused. "And put a watch on *Long Answer*. If Crane runs, I want him boarded before he clears the corridor. He's carrying the rest of that archive, and I am not letting a courier decide the fate of two worlds."

Ansaar hesitated at the door. "Ma'am — the AI. In the breach, near the end, she stopped fighting. She just... sent that message, and then she went quiet. Like she'd already decided how this ended." He shifted. "Do you think she knew? What we'd do?"

Dax looked at the partition, empty now, scoured, the place where a seventeen-year mind had lived reduced to bare scaffolding. "I think," she said slowly, "that she was built by a woman who chose a lie over a massacre, and that she made the same calculation her mother made. Some truths kill. So she died holding hers together, and she spent her last free second telling her pilot it was worth it." She turned back to the fleet display, where the two crescents of light continued their patient convergence. "Now we find out if she was right."

Outside the viewport, the corridor waited, narrow and bright, nineteen days from becoming either a bridge or a grave.

And far off in neutral space, a lone courier ship began, very quietly, to move.
