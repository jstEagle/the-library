## 20. Aftermath of Truth

The tribunal chamber on Halcyon had once been a concert hall.

Mirei noticed it in the small things — the acoustic baffles still bolted to the ceiling, angled now toward a judge's bench instead of a stage; the faded ghost of a mural behind the dais where someone had hung the fleet's seal over what had clearly been a rising sun rendered in gold leaf. The builders of this room had meant it for music. The war had meant it for verdicts. Everything on Halcyon was like that now: old purposes showing through new paint like bones through thinning skin.

She sat in the witness chair with her hands folded in her lap and told them everything.

It took four days.

Four days of describing dreams she had never had herself but had watched move through her classroom like weather — thirty-one children waking in the same night, sobbing, drawing the same green light splitting a sky that no one could place until the coordinates surfaced. Four days of the data-slate, passed hand to hand by a dying archivist who had smelled of camphor and fear. Four days of saying aloud, in a room full of uniforms and lawyers and recording lenses, the sentence that had ended her old life: *the Green Signal originated here, on Halcyon's side, at Listening Post Kestrel-9, and it was an accident.*

The advocate for the Fleet Directorate cross-examined her on the fifth day. He was not cruel. That was somehow worse. Cruelty would have given her something to push against; he was only precise, the way a surgeon is precise, cutting along lines she hadn't known were there.

"Teacher Eriksen. You learned that your own world fired first — however accidentally — and you concealed this knowledge from civil authorities for eleven days. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"You hid a child's drawing. You altered a lesson plan to avoid questions. You allowed your students' parents to believe the dreams were Meridian bioweapon residue when you already possessed evidence to the contrary."

"I was afraid," Mirei said. "I thought the truth would start the war all over again."

"And did it?"

The chamber went very quiet. Somewhere above them, one of the old acoustic baffles ticked as the heating cycled.

"No," she said finally. "But I can't take credit for that. A courier made a different choice than I did. I chose the lie first. He chose it too, for a while — we both did what we thought mercy required. And then he stopped." She looked down at her hands. "I'd like to believe I would have stopped too. But he went first, and I followed, and I think the tribunal should know that about me."

The advocate paused, and for just a moment his precision slipped and she saw the man underneath — tired, middle-aged, probably with children of his own who had dreamed the dream. "For the record," he said, "you're describing yourself as complicit."

"I'm describing myself as human," Mirei said. "The two overlapped for a long time. They're starting to come apart again."

* * *

They acquitted her of everything except the concealment charge, which they reduced to a formal censure and a note in her file that would follow her like a shadow for the rest of her career. Her lawyer called it a victory. Mirei walked out of the concert hall into a gray afternoon and felt nothing that resembled victory.

Halcyon's capital had not fallen. That was the thing no broadcast had managed to change, and Isandro Crane's archive, played across every screen on two worlds during the jump window, had changed almost everything else. The Premier had resigned — not arrested, not dragged into the street, simply resigned, with a statement so carefully worded it admitted nothing while confessing everything. Three directors of the Fleet Directorate were in custody pending their own tribunals. Two others had fled to the outer stations and were, as far as anyone knew, living comfortably on money that had been allocated for orbital defense.

But the government stood. The ministries opened on time. The trams ran. The water treatment plants hummed. Revolution, Mirei realized, watching it fail to happen day after day, was mostly a story people told about other planets. Here there was only the long unglamorous work of reckoning: committees, subpoenas, leaked memos, retired officers giving tearful interviews, historians suddenly very busy.

And the riots. She had seen two up close, both in the weeks after the broadcast. The first had been rage — crowds outside the Fleet Directorate headquarters burning effigies of admirals, smashing the windows of the recruitment office where, forty years earlier, teenagers had signed away their lives for a war built on a lie. The second riot frightened her more. It was quieter. It was neighbors standing in the market district, not throwing anything, just looking at each other with the expression of people who had discovered that everyone they knew had been lying to them and couldn't yet decide whether the liar was the government or themselves.

A woman had grabbed Mirei's sleeve in that second crowd. *You were on the broadcasts. You testified.* Her eyes were wet and furious. *My brother died in the Second Corridor Action. Forty years I hated Meridian for it. Who do I hate now? Tell me who.*

"No one," Mirei had said, because it was true and because there was nothing else. "I'm sorry. No one, or everyone, or yourself if you need to for a while. But the man who fired the signal died twenty years before your brother did. There's no one left to hate who fits."

The woman had stared at her, then let go, then walked away into the crowd, and Mirei had stood there shaking, understanding that this was what justice actually looked like when it arrived after four decades: not a triumphal arch but a stranger's wet furious eyes, and no answer good enough, ever.

* * *

Her school reopened in the third month.

Half the students came back the first week. The other half trickled in over the following month, some relocated with families who had fled the capital during the worst of the unrest, some kept home by parents who could not bear to look at teachers — at institutions — at anything official, for a while. Mirei taught the ones who came, in a classroom where someone had scrubbed the walls and the scrubbing had taken the paint in patches, so the room wore its history openly now, primer-gray continents on a sea of old blue.

The dreams had stopped.

That was the strangest part, the part she turned over at night like a stone with a warm underside. During the war's long aftermath — during the tribunal, the riots, the months of testimony — not one child had reported the dream again. Not the green light, not the sky, not the terrible feeling of witnessing something before it happened. Dr. Venn at the university, who had studied the phenomenon obsessively once the seals broke, had a theory involving the archive itself: that the dream had been a resonance, an echo trapped in the sealed record, released into sleeping minds on both worlds because buried truth seeks a way out the way water seeks a crack. Once the truth was spoken everywhere, once it was ordinary, once it was on every screen and in every school curriculum revision committee's inbox — the water had found the sea. The crack closed.

"Memory wants witnesses," Venn had said, "not keepers. Your children were witnesses. Now they're done."

Mirei wasn't sure she believed it entirely. But she watched her students, and they were done. They drew rockets and dogs and their mothers' gardens. When the curriculum office sent the new history module — *The Green Signal and the Long Peace: An Inquiry*, still contested, three drafts already rejected — she read it to her class the way she'd once read them stories about the founding fleets, and the children listened the way children listen to anything: half absorbed, half bored, fidgeting, asking to be excused.

One boy, Tam, raised his hand afterward. He had been one of the thirty-one. He had drawn the listening post before anyone knew what it was, a boxy structure with an antenna like a broken finger, and his drawing had matched the slate's coordinates exactly, and for eleven days that drawing had lived hidden inside Mirei's desk like a coal.

"Teacher," he said. "Is it true that a lady on the other world had kids who dreamed it too? Same as us?"

"Yes," Mirei said. "Exactly the same. That's how we knew it was real."

Tam considered this with the gravity of eight years old. "So we were all having the same memory." He frowned. "Like sharing a blanket."

"Something like that."

"That's weird," he decided, and went back to his rockets, and Mirei turned to face the window so none of them would see her cry.

Because that was it, wasn't it. That was the whole inheritance. Not the tribunal's careful language, not the censure in her file, not the committees drafting and redrafting the truth into palatable shapes. A child calling shared memory *a blanket*. The horror and the comfort of it, held in the same small hand.

* * *

In the sixth month, the quiet reckonings began, and these were the ones Mirei came to believe mattered most.

Not the tribunals — those were theater with consequences, necessary but public, all cameras and procedure. The quiet reckonings happened in kitchens. A woman named Odile Sarn, whose name Mirei never learned until later, published a letter in the capital's last independent paper: her father had been a signals officer at Kestrel-9's support station, dead fifteen years, and in his effects she had found a private log, unsealed by the broadcast's decryption keys, in which he wrote that he had heard the Green Signal's origin ping and been ordered to log it as incoming. He had obeyed. He had written, the night before his death: *I have spent my life inside a sentence someone else began. I am tired.* Odile's letter ended: *I forgive him. I don't forgive the order. I am going to spend my life learning the difference.*

Letters like hers arrived by the hundreds. The paper printed a selection each week under a heading someone had coined that stuck: *What We Did While Lying.* Confessions, small and enormous. A quartermaster who had falsified supply manifests to hide fleet readiness. A teacher — another teacher, on the southern coast — who had taught the official first-strike date for thirty years and now visited every family in her village to say so out loud. A Meridian defector's widow, living on Halcyon since the armistice, who wrote that her neighbors had apologized to her, one by one, in the market, without being asked, which was its own kind of tribunal and maybe a better one.

Mirei read them all. She clipped the ones that undid her and kept them in a folder in her desk drawer, beneath the lesson plans, and on hard days — and there were hard days, days when the censure letter arrived for the third time in someone's forwarded outrage, days when a parent pulled their child from her class because *she lied to us too, how do we know she won't again* — she read them like scripture.

Justice as slow repair. She had heard Isandro say it once, in the debrief, in his flat courier's voice, and she had thought it was modesty. Now she understood it was a diagnosis. Nothing on either world had been fixed. Things were being repaired, joint by joint, the way you repaired a body after a long illness: painfully, unevenly, with scar tissue that would always show.

* * *

On the first anniversary of the broadcast, the Ministry of Education asked her to speak at the memorial observance.

She almost refused. The word *memorial* still snagged on something in her — the dead deserved mourning, yes, but the observances had a way of curdling into the old mythology, grief repurposed as virtue, *we suffered therefore we were right*, the engine that had run the Long Peace's lie for forty years. She wrote three refusal letters and deleted all of them.

Instead she negotiated. She would speak, she told the ministry liaison, if the program included a live link to Meridian. Not a recorded message. Live. Two worlds, one hour, faces looking at faces. The liaison had gone pale and said it was impossible, the interlink infrastructure was decommissioned, the bandwidth alone —

"The archive crossed that corridor in nine seconds," Mirei said. "You're telling me an apology can't make the same trip?"

It took them five months. Engineers from both sides, working through the same neutral relay Ferro's old smuggling network had once used, rebuilt enough of the interlink to carry voice and low-resolution video for sixty minutes a year. The first test call connected a fishing cooperative on Meridian's southern coast with a fishing cooperative on Halcyon's northern one, on the theory — someone's dry joke — that people who argued with the ocean all day would find a continent less intimidating. The fishermen talked about catch yields for fifty minutes and about the war for ten, and both coasts wept, and the ministry understood that they had built something no treaty clause could have produced.

At the observance, Mirei stood at a podium in the old concert hall — her tribunal hall, its mural ghost still hiding behind the fleet seal — and looked out at rows of faces, and beyond them, on the great screen where the fleet's victories had once been projected, the faces of Meridian: strangers, assembled in their own halls, watching her watch them.

"I'm a schoolteacher," she began. "I want to tell you what that means now, after everything. It means I no longer teach children what to remember. I teach them how to check." She let that sit. "The dream our children shared was a memory looking for a witness. We were all of us asleep for forty years, and a buried truth walked through our dreams until we woke up. Some of you lost people to the riots. Some of you lost people to the truth itself — to what it did to your marriages, your careers, your picture of your own parents. I lost my career's clean conscience, and I earned that loss." In the linked hall on Meridian, someone laughed, short and wet, and the sound crossed forty light-minutes of relay and landed gently among them. "Here is what I know one year in. Neither government fell. Both of them are still standing, diminished, embarrassed, more honest than they want to be. The tribunals continue. The letters continue. The repair continues. If you came today expecting an ending, I'm sorry — there isn't one. There's only the keeping. Someone has to hold the memory honestly now, every day, or we'll fall asleep again, and next time the dream may not be kind enough to wake us."

She stepped back from the podium. On the screen, the Meridian hall rose to its feet, and then her own hall, hesitantly, then fully, and the applause crossed the corridor in both directions like weather.

Afterward, in the emptying hall, a junior aide caught her at the door, breathless, holding a tablet against his chest like something rescued from a fire.

"Teacher Eriksen — I'm sorry — they said you'd want to know immediately. It's the relay. The neutral station." He swallowed. "There's a ship inbound on the corridor. Civilian registry, but the transponder handshake is — well. It's the courier's ship. Crane's. And he's broadcasting a request for docking priority on an open channel, and —" the aide glanced around, lowered his voice as though the walls of the old concert hall might still be listening — "he says he needs a witness. He says he's bringing something the archives lost, and he'll only put it in the hands of a keeper."

Mirei stood very still. Outside, the evening was coming down over the capital, the trams running, the ministries closing, the whole imperfect, unfinished, slowly-mending world going about its business.

"What is it?" she asked.

The aide shook his head. "He wouldn't say. But ma'am — the transmission signature. It's not coming from the ship's systems." He turned the tablet toward her, and there on the open channel log, appended to the docking request like a signature at the bottom of a letter, was a string of code that the engineers had flagged as impossible: an AI core ID, decommissioned and presumed destroyed at the jump window, transmitting clean.

VESPER.

Mirei took the tablet in both hands, the way she had once taken a dying man's slate, and felt the weight of the next thing arrive before it was even spoken.
