## 9. First Silence

Jun Okafor had a theory about silence that she'd never told anyone, mostly because it sounded like the kind of thing you said at parties to make people drift toward the snack table. Her theory was this: space didn't have a sound. Space had a texture. And after eleven years of running the Long Route, she could feel the difference between the good silences and the bad ones the way a sailor feels weather in an old scar.

The good silence lived between engine cycles, when the Kestrel's hull ticked and settled and Sefa's machinery hummed its low brown note through the deck plates. The good silence was the one she shared with Brannoc on the night watch, both of them awake and saying nothing, two people comfortable enough in their own skulls to keep each other company without words. The good silence was Ilse humming in the med-bay, off-key on purpose because she claimed perfect pitch attracted microbes.

The bad silence was different. The bad silence was what you heard when a ship stopped answering.

She'd heard it twice before this jump. Once when a trawler out of Meridian Verge went dark with nine souls aboard, and once—worse, stranger—when Ada had gone quiet for three days back in their second year together, after whatever it was that Ada never talked about happened to them, and the whole ship had walked on eggshells until the silence broke like a fever.

This was the third kind of silence. This one had a shape.

* * *

It started on the fourth day of the corridor, which by Brannoc's reckoning put them deeper into the jump lane than either fleet would bother chasing, assuming they'd even lost the Concordance pursuit cleanly back at the debris field. Jun was on comms rotation, which meant she was sprawled in the chair in the comm bay with her boots up, half-watching the traffic feed scroll past in amber characters, half-reading a novel about a detective who solved crimes using only her sense of smell.

The traffic feed was supposed to be busy. That was the thing nobody told you about jump corridors: they weren't empty. They were full of ghosts. Every hauler who'd ever flown the route left echoes—automated position pings, handshake protocols, the cheerful machine chatter of buoys gossiping to each other across light-hours. A healthy corridor sounded like a market square heard from three streets away. You didn't listen to it so much as lean against it.

On day four, the market closed.

Not all at once. That was the part Jun kept turning over later, trying to decide if she'd actually noticed or if her memory had dressed it up afterward. But she remembered the exact moment: she'd been reading about the detective identifying a murderer by the smell of his laundry starch, and she realized the amber feed hadn't scrolled in a while. She looked up. The last entry was forty minutes old—a buoy handshake, routine, forgettable—and nothing since.

"Brannoc," she said over the intercom. "You doing something weird to the array?"

"Weird is my whole personality," came the answer from the cockpit, unhurried. "Specifically?"

"Traffic feed's dead. Forty minutes of nothing."

A pause. She pictured him checking his board, long fingers moving over the nav console with that pianist's economy he pretended wasn't showing off. "Corridor's thin here," he said finally. "We're outside the main shipping lanes. Could just be sparse."

"It's not sparse. It's *quiet*. There's a difference."

Another pause, longer. "I'll run a sweep."

He ran a sweep. The sweep found nothing, which was fine, which was normal, which was exactly what a corridor should look like four days out from anywhere. Jun told herself that. She was very good at telling herself things; it was practically her job description aboard the Kestrel, along with cargo manifests, galley duty, and being the person who noticed when someone else needed to eat.

But that night, lying in her bunk with the ship breathing around her, she found herself listening for the ghosts. And there were none. Just the hull ticking, and Sefa's brown note, and somewhere beneath it all—so faint she might have invented it—a pattern she couldn't name, like a word repeated too many times until it stops meaning anything.

* * *

Day six was when she caught it properly.

She'd come down to the comm bay at 0300, ship's time, because sleep had gone strange on her—the deep-lane blues, Brannoc called it, the way human rhythms frayed when there was no sun to argue with. She made tea in the galley first, and passing the open door of the crew cabin, she heard Ada talking in their sleep.

Everyone did it sometimes. Brannoc narrated flight maneuvers in a flat murmur, hands twitching on invisible yokes. Ilse recited drug dosages. Sefa once delivered an entire argumentative lecture about heat exchangers to no one, complete with rhetorical questions. It was one of the small intimacies of shipboard life, hearing your family's sleeping minds run their errands.

Ada's sleep-talk was different, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, because that was the rule—the rule older than any of them could remember making it. Ada spoke in their sleep in a language that wasn't anything. Not English, not the trade-tongue pidgin every hauler picked up, not Mandarin or Arabic or Reyes-dialect or any of the forty languages Jun could half-recognize by ear. It was structured—that was the unsettling part. It had grammar. It rose and fell in deliberate cadences, stressed syllables landing where stress belonged, pauses falling in meaningful places. It sounded like language the way a drawing of a door looks like a door. Your hand wanted to reach for the handle.

Jun stood in the corridor with her cooling tea and listened, and for the first time in years she let herself actually hear it instead of folding it away into the category of *things about Ada we don't ask*.

And underneath the sleep-talk—no, woven through it, threaded into it like a second voice singing the same song a half-beat off—she heard the comm bay.

She set the tea down very carefully on the deck, because her hands had decided to be unreliable.

The Kestrel's automated systems routed incoming signals to the bay's monitor even when nobody was on watch. And the monitor was playing the only signal the corridor had given them in two days: a repeating transmission from somewhere ahead, faint as breath on glass, cycling through its pattern with machine patience. The Meridian's beacon. They'd logged it on day two—Brannoc had flagged it as unremarkable, an old colony ship's automated pulse, probably been repeating for decades, probably nobody home.

It was the same language. Or the same non-language. The same structure, the same cadence, the same impossible grammar—and Ada, asleep ten meters away, was speaking along with it. Not echoing. *Answering.* The beacon said a phrase; Ada's sleeping mouth shaped a response; the beacon continued; Ada followed. Call and response. Antiphon. Two voices in a conversation that had been going on, as far as Jun could tell, for longer than anyone aboard had been listening.

Her skin did something cold and complicated.

She didn't wake them. She wanted to—her whole body wanted to, wanted to shake Ada's shoulder and say *you're answering it, do you know you're answering it?*—but the rule was the rule, and more than the rule, there was the look she imagined on Ada's face if she did. Eleven years of carefully not-knowing things about this person, and she understood suddenly that the not-knowing had been load-bearing. Pull it out and she didn't know what fell.

She picked up her tea, went to the comm bay, and sat down in front of the monitor instead.

The signal was beautiful. That was the horror of it, she thought later—it wasn't static, it wasn't eerie noise, it was *composed*. Whoever or whatever had built the Meridian's beacon had built it to speak, and it spoke with the patience of something that had been saying the same thing for a hundred years and intended to say it for a hundred more. Jun pulled the waveform up on the display and watched it move: phrases with clean edges, internal repetition, a rising motif that recurred every ninety seconds like a refrain. Language-shaped. Meaning-shaped. And utterly, absolutely alien—not in the dramatic way of the entertainment feeds, tentacles and chrome, but in the quiet way of realizing that a sound you'd been hearing your whole life had a source.

She recorded everything. Then she sat with her cold tea and watched Ada's sleep-cycle biometrics on the shared crew display—steady, peaceful, heart rate barely elevated—and felt like a burglar in her own home.

* * *

"You're doing the face," Sefa said.

They were in the galley, day seven, and Jun was doing the face. She knew she was doing the face because Sefa had named it, and Sefa naming a thing was like a court clerk reading it into the record: it existed now, officially.

"I don't have a face."

"You have seventeen faces and I've catalogued all of them. This one is 'Jun knows something and is deciding whether it's dangerous.' You wore it when Brannoc's fuel math didn't add up outside Torvane, and you wore it when Ilse's mother sent that letter." Sefa turned a wrench with the absent precision of someone who could rebuild an engine during a conversation. "So. Which flavor?"

Jun looked at the doorway. Looked at the bulkhead. Considered, briefly, the structural integrity of her own discretion, and found it compromised.

"The beacon," she said quietly. "The Meridian beacon. I've been listening to it."

Sefa's wrench stopped. "And?"

"And I think—" Jun exhaled. "Sefa, I need you to promise me you'll hear the whole thing before you react."

"That request has never once worked in human history."

"Promise anyway."

Sefa set the wrench down, which from Sefa was a gesture of enormous ceremony. "Promised."

So Jun told her. She played the recording—beacon phrase, Ada's sleeping response, call and response, the antiphon laid bare in the little speaker—and she watched Sefa's face go through the same sequence hers had gone through: confusion, then recognition of the structure, then the specific pale stillness of a person whose understanding of their own household has shifted underfoot.

"That's not coincidence," Sefa said. Her voice had dropped into the register she used for engineering problems, which meant she was frightened and dealing with it by being precise. "Coincidence doesn't do antiphon. Coincidence doesn't hold tempo across two sources. Either that beacon is broadcasting something Ada learned once—"

"—or Ada learned it from the beacon," Jun finished. "Or learned it from wherever the beacon learned it. Or—" She rubbed her eyes. "Or Ada *is* something the beacon was built to talk to. I don't know. I've been running scenarios since 0300 yesterday and they all end in places I don't want to live."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. In the background, the ship hummed, and somewhere ahead—days ahead still, but coming closer every hour—the beacon said its patient sentence to no one.

"We tell them?" Sefa asked finally.

"Do we have a choice? We're flying toward that thing. Brannoc's got us burning hard to beat both fleets to the window, and the entire plan rests on Ada walking onto that ship and doing—whatever it is Ada does. And now we know the ship talks in Ada's sleep." Jun laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and sharp. "Eleven years of not asking. Eleven years, and the universe just asked for us."

"Maybe we still don't ask." Sefa picked the wrench back up, turned it over in her fingers. "Maybe we ask *something else*. Not 'what are you.' Maybe 'are you okay.' Maybe 'do you know what that signal is.' There's a version of this where we find out everything and lose them, and there's a version where we find out nothing and keep them, and I don't love either one."

"That's not how information works, Sefa."

"No," Sefa agreed. "But it's how families work."

* * *

They told Brannoc first, because Brannoc was flying the ship and had a right to know what was ahead of them, and because of the three of them he was most likely to receive terrible news as a navigation problem. He listened to the recording twice with his eyes closed, hands resting flat on his thighs, and when it finished he said, "Huh," which from Brannoc was the equivalent of another man screaming.

"Options," he said. "One: we turn around. Two: we keep going and say nothing. Three: we keep going and say something."

"Turn around isn't real," Jun said. "Both fleets are behind us and the window closes without us. Turning around means going home to interrogations."

"Then it's two or three." He opened his eyes. "My vote's three, but slowly. Not an ambush. Not 'we recorded you sleeping and here's the evidence.' Just—" he made a loose gesture, "—open a door. See if they walk through it."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we fly into a derelict colony ship with a hundred frozen strangers and an arbiter designation with our friend's name on it, and we're all keeping secrets together, which, honestly, is the traditional Kestrel way." He almost smiled. It didn't fully arrive. "Ilse next. She'll want medical context. And then Ada, all four of us, in the mess, doors shut. Family meeting."

"Since when do we have family meetings?"

"Since the universe started eavesdropping on our sleep," Brannoc said, and turned back to his console, and Jun saw him pull up the beacon's waveform and just—look at it. Listening with his eyes. Learning its shape the way you learn the shape of a wave you'll have to sail through.

* * *

Ilse took it differently. Ilse took everything differently; it was her function, Jun suspected, the way Sefa's function was engines and Brannoc's was flight and Jun's was noticing. Ilse listened to the recording once, then asked to hear Ada's sleep-talk alone, without the beacon, and played the two side by side on the med-bay's diagnostic display, overlaying the waveforms until they nearly matched.

"Look at the respiratory coupling," she said softly. "When the beacon phrases, Ada's breathing syncs to it. Even asleep. Even unconscious. That's not memory, Jun. Memory doesn't entrain autonomic rhythm. That's—" she searched, "—that's training. Deep training. The kind they do to pilots and surgeons and, apparently, arbiters."

"You believe the arbiter thing, then. Really believe it."

Ilse was quiet. She had Ada's old medical file open on her display—the file Ilse had built herself over eleven years, patchwork and incomplete, full of gaps that had always looked like privacy and now looked like surgery. Scars consistent with neural interface work. Implant beds healed over with a technique no civilian clinic used. The missing year before Ada had shown up on Torvane Station with forged papers and a smile that didn't quite fit.

"I believe that whoever Ada was before us, they were made for something," Ilse said. "And I believe that thing is sitting out there in the dark, saying their name in a language they speak in their sleep." She closed the file. "And I believe that when we tell them, we tell them sitting down, with tea, and we don't lead with the recordings. We lead with 'you're safe.'"

"They might not be."

"Nobody is. That's why we say it anyway."

* * *

The family meeting lasted forty minutes and changed less than Jun hoped and more than she feared, which was, she supposed, the definition of a functional family.

They didn't play the recording. Brannoc held to his door-not-ambush strategy, and it was Ilse who found the words: she told Ada, plainly, that the beacon ahead was transmitting in a structured pattern, and that she'd noticed—professionally, medically, gently—that Ada's sleep patterns seemed to respond to rhythmic signals, and did Ada want to talk about that, or did Ada want tea, and both options were equally available forever.

Ada had gone very still. Jun watched it happen—the stillness of a person hearing a floorboard creak in a house they thought was empty—and then Ada had said, "Tea," and then, after a moment, "Thank you for asking before playing anything."

Which meant Ada knew. Or suspected. Or had known all along and had spent eleven years waiting for the rest of them to catch up. Jun couldn't tell, and the not-being-able-to-tell sat in her chest like swallowed glass.

That night, Jun took the comm watch again, because she couldn't sleep and wouldn't have trusted herself to. The corridor stayed silent around them—no traffic, no buoys, no ghosts, just the Kestrel's own small sounds and the beacon ahead, closer now, stronger, its patient sentence filling more of the band with every watch.

At 0340, Ada appeared in the comm bay doorway. Awake. Dressed. Holding two cups of tea.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Jun asked.

"No." Ada handed her a cup and settled into the second chair, and for a while they just sat there, two people and a signal, the oldest arrangement in the world. The beacon pulsed. The waveform crawled across the display between them like something alive.

"Jun," Ada said eventually, not looking at her. "If I asked you to turn the ship around, would you?"

"You know I can't answer that."

"I know." Ada sipped their tea. Their reflection floated faintly in the dark viewport, ghosted over the stars, and Jun thought: *there are two of everyone on this ship right now, the people we know and the people we don't, and we're all flying toward the place where the two versions meet.*

"But you'd want to," Ada said. "If I asked."

"Yeah," Jun said honestly. "I'd want to."

Ada nodded slowly, as if filing the answer somewhere important. Then they leaned forward, rested their chin on their hand, and did something that made the hair rise on Jun's arms: they began, quietly, consciously, deliberately—for the first time in eleven waking years, as far as Jun knew—to speak the other language. Not sleep-mumbling. Speaking. Shaping the syllables with care, testing them like a man testing ice.

And the beacon, far ahead in the dark, changed.

Just slightly. Just enough. Its rhythm shifted, its refrain bent a half-tone, its patient century-old sentence paused—and answered.

Ada closed their eyes. "It knows we're coming," they said, in English, in a voice Jun had never heard them use. "It's been waiting for me."
