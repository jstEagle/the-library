## 22. The Child Who Left

Captain Amara Reyes had been awake for eleven days, and she had learned that the century did not forgive you all at once. It came in installments.

The first installment was her body: the tremor in her hands that Ilse swore would pass, the taste of cryo-gel at the back of her throat that no amount of water washed away, the way her own reflection startled her each morning—a woman of thirty-four wearing the face she'd had at thirty-three, with a hundred years of frost in between that no one could see.

The second installment was the ship. She walked the Meridian's corridors every day now, because if she stopped walking them they would become someone else's ship, and she refused—she *refused*—to let that happen while ninety-six of her people still slept. The frost had been cleared from the main gallery. Sefa had rigged work lights along the ceiling, and their yellow glow made the old colony banners look almost warm again, the woven patterns of the Founding Cohorts hanging where they'd hung since before launch. She touched one each morning. A small ritual. Her grandmother's cohort had woven it.

The third installment came at night, when the others slept and she sat alone in what the Kestrel's crew had started calling the wardroom, reading the logs.

That was where she was tonight, third watch by the Meridian's ancient clock, a mug of reconstituted tea going cold beside her elbow, when she found the girl.

* * *

It should have been simple. That was the bitter joke of it. Ada Almeida had asked her—politely, carefully, with that watchful stillness Reyes had come to distrust—for access to the Meridian's administrative records. Crew manifests, wake protocols, the chain of custody of the arbiter designation. Reyes had granted it, because refusing would have meant admitting she didn't trust them, and because some stubborn part of her wanted to be proven wrong about the soft-spoken xenolinguist who wore authority like an ill-fitting coat.

But Reyes had grown up on stories about ships that lied to their crews. Her mother had served on a Verity survey vessel and come home with a settlement payout and a silence she never broke. So Reyes didn't just grant the request. She granted it—and then she went digging herself, through channels Ada hadn't asked about, because trust was a thing you extended and verified in the same motion or you were a fool.

The cryopod telemetry was the obvious place to start. Ninety-eight pods, two empty. Everyone knew that. Everyone had walked past those two open cradles in the dark of Deck Four and felt the same cold draft of wrongness. What nobody had done—not Ada, not Ilse, not even Sefa with her gift for coaxing secrets out of dead machines—was pull the full diagnostic history of the pods themselves. Not the crew records. The machine's own memory. Pods kept their own logs, separate from the ship's mainframe, sealed against tampering by a design standard a century out of date. The Meridian had been built paranoid, back when people still believed a colony ship might need to survive its own government.

Reyes cracked the seal at 0300 ship time, using credentials that had been hers since she was twenty-two and would apparently still be hers until she died, whenever that was now.

Pod 31: occupied. Pod 32: occupied. She scrolled down the list like a woman counting her congregation, and every green status light in the data was a name she knew, a face she'd shaken hands with on the launch gantry at Meridian Station while the bands played and the politicians smiled their lacquered smiles. Okonkwo, pod 44. Lindqvist, pod 45. The Adeyemi twins, pods 46 and 47, who had insisted on being frozen side by side so they'd wake at the same instant and neither would have to be first.

Then pod 63. Status: OPENED. Date stamp: Year 3, Day 211 of transit.

Three years out. Three years into a hundred-year crossing, someone had opened a pod.

She expected a malfunction record. An emergency thaw, a medical crisis, a death. The log would show a body, a burial protocol, a grief she could add to the long ledger of what had been taken from her people.

There was no death. There was no malfunction.

Wake authorization: Arbiter E. Almeida. Subject: minor, female, age six. Reason code: CUSTODIAL TRANSFER.

Reyes read it four times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into anything saner. *Custodial transfer.* Someone had opened a cryopod three years into the deep dark and taken a six-year-old girl out of it, and the authorization signature was the arbiter's—the arbiter whose quarters down on Deck Two stood preserved like a museum exhibit, whose handwriting was on the protocol documents, whose name—

Whose name was Almeida.

She sat very still for a long moment. Somewhere below her feet, a coolant pump cycled, the heartbeat of a ship that had been dreaming without them.

She pulled the secondary file attached to the reason code. It was short. Most of it was redacted—blacked-out blocks in the kind of dense military-grade encryption that civilian colonial infrastructure had no business containing—but the unredacted fragments read like the edges of a wound showing through bandages:

*...transfer of custody per Concordance Fleet Directive 9-Theta... subject to be raised shipboard, identity sealed... arbiter to retain visitation rights pending review...*

Concordance. The word sat on the screen like a splinter.

A Concordance directive, executed aboard a supposedly independent colony ship, three years into a crossing both fleets now claimed never to have sanctioned. And a child—six years old, female—taken from her frozen sleep and handed over to a war fleet to be raised.

Reyes closed the file. Opened it again. Closed it. Her tea had gone completely cold and she drank it anyway, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

* * *

She didn't tell anyone. Not yet. This was the discipline her mother had taught her without ever meaning to: when you find something that changes the shape of everything, you sit with it alone first, until you know its weight, because the moment you speak it, it stops being yours to understand and becomes everyone's to react to.

For two days she dug. The pod logs led her to the transfer manifest, which led her to a shuttle bay record—Bay 2, Year 3, Day 212, one shuttle launched, one life sign aboard besides the pilot. Which led her, through a chain of relay buoys the Meridian had pinged automatically over the following decades, to a final handshake: the shuttle had docked with a vessel whose transponder ID resolved, in the Concordance naval registry the Kestrel's databases still carried, to the *CSV Halcyon Reach*, a long-patrol command cruiser, decommissioned forty-one years ago.

A child raised on a Concordance cruiser. Sealed identity. Custodial transfer authorized under a fleet directive that predated the colony's launch—which meant the fleets hadn't stumbled onto the Meridian after she went missing. They had been inside her from the beginning. They had helped build the lie.

And the arbiter—the man whose quarters held a child's drawing pinned to the wall, a crayon sun over two stick figures holding hands, labeled in a six-year-old's wobbling letters: *PAPA AND ME*—had signed the order himself.

Reyes stood in those quarters the next morning, alone, looking at the drawing. She'd seen it before, in the first terrible days after waking, when Sefa had found the room and called them all down to look. Then it had seemed like evidence of the arbiter's humanity, a father's keepsake. Now it looked like something else. An apology, maybe. Or a wound he'd kept where he could see it.

*Visitation rights pending review,* the file said. He'd given up his daughter and kept a picture of her. She tried to hate him for it and couldn't quite manage the full weight; there was too much she didn't know, and hating on partial information was a luxury the last eleven days had taught her she couldn't afford.

What she could do was find out who the girl became.

* * *

The answer was already aboard. That was the thing that made her laugh—actually laugh, alone in the archive room, a short ugly sound—at 0200 on the third night, when she finally cross-referenced the right databases.

Because the Kestrel's crew, bless them, were hoarders. Brannoc kept ship registries the way other pilots kept lucky charms: every hull number, every commissioning record, every officer's service history that had ever crossed their path, filed away against the day it might matter. "Information's the only cargo that doesn't cost fuel," Brannoc liked to say. And buried in that hoard, indexed under Concordance personnel files pulled during some long-ago customs dispute, was the service record of a long-patrol officer born Year 3 of the Meridian's transit—no birthplace listed, birth date matching the custodial transfer within a standard week, childhood residence: *CSV Halcyon Reach.*

Name at enlistment: Vey, Nade.

Current rank: Commander, Concordance Fleet Security. Current assignment: the task force converging on the Meridian.

Reyes stared at the portrait in the file—a hard-faced woman in dress grays, jaw set like a bulkhead, eyes that looked at the camera the way you'd look at an obstacle—and overlaid it, in her mind, with the crayon drawing pinned to the wall two decks below. *Papa and me.* The stick figures' joined hands.

The officer who had boarded the Kestrel's berth during the Concordance inspection at departure—who had looked at Ada Almeida's face with that flicker of recognition, that impossible half-second of *I know you from somewhere*—was the child from pod 63. Was the arbiter's daughter. Had been raised by the very fleet that had helped strand this ship, fed on its stories, commissioned into its service, and was now sailing home at the head of its guns.

The past didn't just have a face. The past had a rank, a command, and a weapons battery.

* * *

She told them the next morning, all of them, around the wardroom table: Ada, Jun, Sefa, Brannoc, Ilse. She laid the documents out on the display and watched each face as the pieces landed—the pod log, the transfer order, the shuttle record, the service file. Jun said nothing at all, which from Jun was louder than shouting. Brannoc whistled low and long. Sefa immediately started talking about transponder spoofing and whether the *Halcyon Reach*'s decommission records could be forged retroactively, which was Sefa's way of saying she was frightened.

Ada sat very still through all of it. Stillness was Ada's native language, Reyes thought—the fluency of someone who had learned early that movement gave you away. But when the service file came up, when Nade Vey's hard gray face filled the screen, Reyes saw something move behind Ada's eyes. Not surprise. Something older and more tired than surprise. Recognition, maybe, of a shape Ada had been dreading without knowing its name.

"You knew," Reyes said quietly. Not an accusation. A measurement.

"I suspected," Ada said. "Officer Vey looked at me like—" A pause. The careful machinery of Ada's speech engaging, gear by gear. "Like she was checking a memory against a face. I've been trying to work out why ever since."

"And now?"

"Now I think she wasn't checking it against my face." Ada's voice dropped. "I think she was checking it against someone else's."

The table went silent. Ilse reached over and put her hand flat on the display, over Vey's portrait, as if she could warm the frozen child underneath the uniform. "Six years old," Ilse said softly. "They took a six-year-old out of cold sleep and gave her to a war fleet. Whatever she is now, they made it."

"Whatever she is now," Reyes said, "is coming here. With a battle group." She stood, and the chair scraped, and everyone looked up at her, and she felt the strange doubled weight of her own authority—a captain a century late, leading a crew that wasn't hers, defending a ship that barely remembered her. "So here's what happens next. We finish waking Captain Reyes's people—" she caught herself, huffed, "—my people, God, I'm going to be mixing pronouns for a year—we secure the colonists' claim, and we get ahead of whatever story the Concordance tells. Because when Vey arrives, she won't be arriving as a lost daughter. She'll be arriving as a fleet officer with orders, and orders don't care whose picture is pinned to whose wall."

"She might care," Jun said. Jun had been quiet the whole meeting, arms folded, watching Ada with an expression Reyes couldn't fully parse—hurt braided through loyalty. "People aren't their orders. I've spent my whole life proving that."

"People with her training are exactly their orders," Brannoc countered. "Until it costs them something. Then we'll see what's left."

Reyes let them argue. It was good, the arguing—it meant they were still a crew and not yet a garrison. She watched Ada listen, watched the linguist's gaze move from face to face like someone translating a language spoken entirely in loyalties.

Later, alone again in the archive room, Reyes pulled up the drawing one more time. *Papa and me.* She thought about the arbiter's final logged entry, the one Ada had decrypted days ago—the entrusting of his work to a trained child, wiped clean and sent out to grow up human. She had assumed the child in that entry was Ada.

Now she wondered if she'd been reading it wrong. If there had been two children all along: one sent out into the dark to become a stranger, and one left behind to become a soldier. Two answers to the same unbearable question, asked by a father who couldn't keep either of them.

Two children, and both of them were coming to this room eventually. Both of them were standing on opposite sides of a closing door.

The comm panel chimed. Sefa, from the bridge, her voice tight and fast: "Captain? Long-range just lit up. Single contact, running dark until now, squawking Concordance security escort. They're requesting docking clearance." A pause, and Reyes could hear Sefa swallow. "It's Vey. She's coming alone. She says—" another pause, longer, "—she says she wants to talk to the arbiter. She says it's about pod sixty-three."

Reyes looked at the crayon sun. At the two stick figures holding hands across a hundred years of frost.

"Tell her," she said, "that the arbiter will receive her."

Outside the viewport, the stars wheeled slow and indifferent, and somewhere among them a ship was decelerating toward them carrying a woman who had been stolen from this one before she was old enough to remember leaving.
