## 23. Officer Vey

The truce ping arrived at 0400 ship-time, and Nade Vey had been awake for all of the hours that preceded it.

She stood in the shuttle bay of the *Concordance Vigilant*, watching the Meridian turn against the stars—a hundred-year-old hull, frost-silvered along its spine, rotating with the slow patience of something that had never been in a hurry. Behind it, visible even from here, the Verity Compact ships hung in their precise formation like a hand of cards held close to a chest. And between them, small and stubborn as a burr caught in cloth, the independent hauler called *Kestrel*.

"Truce protocols confirmed," said Ensign Oduya beside her. "Both fleets have acknowledged. Weapons cold for the duration of boarding."

Weapons cold. Vey almost smiled. As if the weapons were ever the dangerous part.

"Prep my shuttle," she said. "I'll take a skeleton crew. Four."

Oduya blinked. "Officer, standard truce boarding is—"

"I know what standard is." She kept her voice level, unhurried, the voice of a woman who had spent thirty years making other people's panic look like her own calm. "This is not a standard boarding. This is a courtesy call. I want to be polite."

What she wanted was to see him again. What she wanted was to see the child.

She had wanted both for thirty years, and she had learned to want quietly, the way you learn to carry a wound through inspection after inspection without letting anyone see the bandage.

* * *

The Meridian's docking ring accepted the shuttle with a groan of old machinery, magnetic clamps engaging one by one, each impact shivering up through the deck plates into Vey's boots. Through the viewport, the corridor beyond was dark except for emergency strips glowing a patient amber—the color of things waiting.

Her four crew fell in behind her: Oduya, two marines for show, and Lieutenant Casimir, whose job was to record everything and understand none of it. Vey had chosen them carefully over the years, the way a surgeon chooses instruments. None of them had served on the *Halcyon*. None of them would recognize what she was about to see.

The airlock cycled. Cold air rolled out, smelling of frost and machine oil and—underneath it, faint as a memory—recycled atmosphere of a particular vintage, the scrubber chemistry of an older era. Vey had breathed that chemistry once, as a girl of nine, aboard a colony ship that was supposed to be a legend.

"Boarding party, Concordance Vigilant," she announced to the empty corridor, because protocol demanded it, because the ship's AI was listening, because somewhere on this vessel there were people who deserved to know exactly who was walking their halls. "We come under truce. We carry no arms set to fire."

The lights ahead brightened by degrees, leading them inward, and Vey followed the light with her hands clasped behind her back and her heart beating like a trapped bird she refused to acknowledge.

They passed the cryo bays first. The AI—Meridian-Prime, its designation read out in a voice like warm gravel—escorted them past rows of pods, ninety-eight of them occupied, faces floating pale and dreaming beneath frosted glass. Her people paused to stare. Vey did not. She had seen these sleepers before, in records sealed so deep inside the Concordance archive that retrieving them had required three forged authorizations and a favor she still owed a woman she despised.

A hundred souls launched toward a world that two governments had decided they could live without.

Vey had been one of the people who signed the paper saying so. She had been nine years old, and the paper had been a crayon drawing, and she hadn't known what she was signing. That was the joke she kept locked in the vault where she kept everything: Nade Vey, rising officer of the Concordance Fleet, architect of the Meridian recovery operation, had been the colony's youngest passenger before she was its betrayer.

They reached the negotiation chamber at 0530. The others were already assembling—the Verity delegation arriving through the opposite lock, their envoy a silver-haired woman named Sorensen whom Vey had crossed words with across three systems; the independents trailing in last, out of uniform and unapologetic about it.

And there, standing at the head of the table with a data slate in one hand and the terrible composure of someone performing a role too large for them—

There she was.

* * *

Time did strange things in vacuum. It stretched. It folded. For a moment, standing in the doorway of the arbiter's chamber, Vey was nine again, sitting cross-legged on the deck of the *Halcyon*'s observation blister while a man with tired eyes and ink-stained fingers taught her and a smaller child to fold paper birds.

*You have to be patient with the crease,* he'd said. *Everything important is decided at the crease.*

The smaller child had laughed—a sound Vey had carried for thirty years without knowing its name—and pressed her thumb flat along the fold, and the bird had come out perfect, and the man had looked at his daughter with an expression Vey had spent her whole career trying to find again in mirrors.

Ada Almeida looked up from the data slate. Their eyes met across the chamber.

Vey watched recognition fail. That was the thing about a memory wipe done properly—it didn't leave absence, it left smoothness. The child who had folded paper birds looked at her now with polite, neutral attention, the attention of a stranger cataloguing a stranger, and Vey felt the smoothness of that gaze pass over her like water over glass.

"Officer Vey," Ada said. "Concordance Vigilant. Welcome aboard the Meridian."

"Arbiter Almeida." Her voice came out steady. Thirty years of practice. "Thank you for receiving us under truce."

They shook hands. The child's hand was larger than she remembered; the grip was firm, trained, entirely wrong. Somewhere behind Vey's sternum, the bird with the perfect crease turned over in its long sleep.

She took her seat. She opened her portfolio. She began, in the flat cadence of diplomatic procedure, to lay out the Concordance's claim to the colony vessel—salvage rights, navigational precedent, the security interests of the fleet—and every word of it was true in the way that ledgers are true, and every word of it was built on a lie she had helped construct when she was young enough to believe that institutions were just families that had learned to scale.

Across the table, Ada Almeida arbitrated.

That was the word for it. Not performed—arbitrated. When the Verity envoy objected to a clause, Ada restated it in language that gave Sorensen's objection dignity while dismantling its substance. When one of the independents muttered something about being summoned like witnesses to their own funeral, Ada turned to them with a half-smile and said, "Funerals are for things that are finished. Nothing here is finished," and the room's temperature dropped five degrees in the good direction.

Vey watched, and her blood ran cold in a way that had nothing to do with the ship's chill.

Because she knew that technique. She knew the specific rhythm of it—the way the arbiter let silence do half the work, the way he'd reframe a threat as a question until the person who'd made the threat heard how small it sounded. Elias Almeida had invented that rhythm in the observation blister of the *Halcyon*, practicing on his daughter with paper birds and negotiation games, and here it was, thirty years later, living in the daughter's hands like a ghost that had found its house.

He trained her. He wiped her. He sent her out to grow up human, and she grew up human, and then she walked back into the exact shape of the life he'd carved for her.

Vey had spent three decades telling herself a story: that Elias Almeida had abandoned the mission, abandoned the fleet, abandoned *her*—the girl he'd woken early, the girl the Concordance had taken in and raised into an instrument—because grief had broken him. That the great arbiter had simply failed. It was a comfortable story. It made her the abandoned one rather than the accomplice.

But the woman sitting across the table, steering two fleets with nothing but grammar and nerve, was not the work of a broken man. She was the work of a desperate one. A man who had looked at the conspiracy closing around his colony and understood that the only weapon left was a person who could hold the line without knowing what line they were holding.

And Vey had come here to destroy that person. Or to own her. She hadn't decided which. She had thirty years of practice not deciding things.

* * *

The session broke at midday for the meal the Meridian's galley provided—real food, grown in hydroponics the colonists had stocked a century ago, and the taste of it made several of the fleet officers go quiet in a way Vey noted and filed. People fought harder over things they'd eaten.

She found herself, by accident or by gravity, standing near Ada Almeida at the galley's narrow counter. The independents hovered nearby—a broad-shouldered pilot, a medic with watchful eyes, a young engineer who hadn't stopped glaring at Vey since the airlock. Found family, written all over them. Vey recognized the architecture. She'd never been permitted to build any herself.

"You've done your homework," Ada said, pouring tea. "Your claim documents cite precedents I haven't seen outside the restricted archive."

"The Concordance has a long memory."

"So I'm learning." Ada handed her the cup. Their fingers did not touch, but it was close. "May I ask you something off the record, Officer?"

"You may ask."

"When the *Kestrel* departed Ceres Station, twenty-six days ago, a Concordance inspection team came aboard. One of the officers looked at me—" Ada paused, choosing the word with the same care they chose everything, "—like they were trying to place a face. Your file photo is in the briefing packet. Was that you?"

Smoothness. Vey admired it even as it terrified her. The wipe had taken the memories but left the instincts, and the instincts were asking questions now, circling the truth the way a tongue circles a missing tooth.

"No," Vey said. "I was in transit to the corridor rendezvous. But I've reviewed the station footage." She let the pause stretch—one beat, two, the arbiter's own technique turned back on its maker. "Faces echo, Arbiter. Certain structures repeat across unrelated people. It unsettles us, when it happens. We look twice. We wonder if we're seeing a ghost."

Something moved behind Ada's eyes—not recognition, never that, but the animal awareness of a trap's geometry. "Ghosts," Ada repeated.

"This ship is full of them." Vey sipped her tea. It was excellent. Of course it was; the man had planned everything else. "Ninety-eight sleepers. Two empty pods. A founder who walked out of history. If I believed in ghosts, Arbiter Almeida, I'd say this whole vessel is haunted by unfinished business."

"And do you? Believe?"

Vey set down the cup. Around them the galley hummed, the fleets' officers talking in careful clusters, the independents watching her with open hostility, and none of them—not Sorensen, not the hauler crew, not the sleepers in their frozen dreams—knew that the most dangerous conversation happening on this ship was the one composed entirely of subtext between a woman who remembered everything and a person who remembered nothing.

"I believe," Vey said, "that the past has a way of presenting itself for adjudication. Usually at the worst possible moment." She smiled, and made it warm, and hated how easily the warmth came. "When it does, I hope whoever holds the gavel is prepared for what gets entered into evidence."

Ada held her gaze for a long moment. Then, politely, professionally, they inclined their head and turned to answer a question from the Verity delegation, and the mask closed over their face like water over a stone, and Vey stood alone at the counter with her heart hammering and her secret intact.

Not yet. Not yet. There was still a negotiation to win, a claim to secure, a career thirty years in the building that would collapse the instant the truth collapsed. She told herself that was why she waited. She told herself it was strategy.

But when the afternoon session resumed and Ada Almeida rose to speak—when the arbiter's cadence filled the chamber, Elias's rhythm wearing his daughter's voice—Nade Vey looked down at her notes and saw that she had written, in the margin, in handwriting she didn't remember using:

*She folds the paper perfectly.*

She crossed it out. She would decide soon. She had to.

Outside the viewport, the jump window's remaining hours ticked away, and both fleets' clocks began counting down to a deadline that only one person in the room fully understood—and she was pretending, beautifully, terribly, to be someone else.
