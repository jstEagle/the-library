## 24. Negotiation Table

The Meridian's great hall had been built for a celebration that never happened.

Ada felt it in the proportions of the room—the vaulted ceiling ribbed with light conduits meant to glow like a festival sky, the tiered seating arranged not for a crew but for a community, a thousand settlers gathered to hear their captain declare a new world theirs. The designers had imagined laughter here. Instead there was frost sublimating off the walls in slow silver breaths, and the smell of recycled air and old cold, and ninety-eight sleepers humming in their pods two decks below like a chord held for a century.

Now the hall held three tables.

Ada had insisted on the geometry themselves—three tables set in a shallow triangle, none facing another head-on, each with sightlines to the exits and to the center, where a single low plinth stood empty. They could not have said where the arrangement came from. It arrived in their mind whole, the way the beacon language arrived in their sleep: complete, structured, older than their knowing. Later, reading the arbiter's handbook by lamplight in the quarters they'd been given—quarters with their name already etched faintly into the doorframe—they would find a diagram of exactly this configuration, labeled *triangular parity: no party sits as supplicant*. But this morning, standing alone in the vast cold room while Sefa's people ran power cables and Ilse checked the atmospheric seals for the fourth time, Ada simply trusted it the way you trust a staircase in the dark.

"You're doing the thing again," Jun said, coming up beside them with two bulbs of hot tea, pressing one into Ada's hands.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you know something and pretend you don't." Jun's voice was light. Their eyes were not. Since the night of the recording—the playback of Ada's own sleeping voice, fluent and alien in the dark of the Kestrel's galley—Jun had developed this habit of saying true things gently, like setting down glassware. "You walked in here and rearranged the furniture like you'd been coming to this room your whole life."

"I read the handbook."

"You read the handbook *last night*. You drew this layout on the wall an hour after we docked." Jun sipped their tea, watching the Concordance delegation file in below—gray uniforms, gray faces, Officer Vey at their center moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who owned the floor she stood on. "I'm not asking. I promised I wouldn't ask. I'm just saying it out loud so somebody says it."

"Thank you," Ada said quietly.

"For what?"

"For saying it out loud instead of asking."

Jun almost smiled. Then Captain Reyes entered from the opposite side, flanked by two colonists Ilse had woken early—a systems engineer named Okonkwo and a tall, silent man everyone simply called Deverell—and the moment for anything soft was over. Reyes wore her century like armor. She had slept through the fall of everything she knew and woken to find her colony a chip on a table between two empires, and she had looked at Ada—the arbiter she'd been promised, this stranger with a familiar name and unfamiliar eyes—and had not believed a word of it. She didn't believe them now. But she had agreed to come to the table, and in the arithmetic of the last six days, agreement was its own kind of miracle.

The Verity Compact delegation came last: four of them in deep blue, led by Envoy Castellan, whose smile Ada had already learned to distrust on principle. Behind them, visible through the great hall's viewport, hung the fleets themselves—Concordance dreadnoughts on one side of the derelict, Verity cruisers on the other, close enough that their running lights bled together in the frost-fogged glass like two bruises touching.

Ada descended to the center plinth and stood beside it, empty-handed, as the handbook prescribed. *The arbiter carries nothing. What the arbiter has is the room.*

"Parties will be seated," they said, and heard their own voice come out level and strange, pitched to carry without echoing. "This session is convened under Meridian protocol. Everything spoken here is recorded and belongs to all parties equally. No claim may be made here that cannot be made in front of the colony itself."

Castellan's smile flickered—one millimeter of recalibration. Good. The line was from the handbook too, but it did something the handbook hadn't warned them about: it made the room belong to the sleepers. Ada watched Reyes register it, watched something behind her suspicion shift half a degree, the way ice shifts before it decides whether to be a bridge or a flood.

*

For the first hour, it was almost boring, and Ada learned to be grateful for boredom.

The claims were laid out formally, each side performing restraint for the record. The Concordance's position, delivered by a legal officer named Halloway with the flat precision of a man reciting scripture: the Meridian had been launched under Concordance charter; its registry, its debts, its liabilities remained Concordance property; the colony ship was salvage or asset depending on one's generosity, but either way it fell within Concordance jurisdiction. The Verity position, delivered by Castellan with more music and the same iron underneath: the Meridian had fled Concordance space during the Purification Edicts; its passengers were refugees whose protection the Compact's founding charters explicitly claimed; jurisdiction followed conscience, not paper.

Both positions were lies, or rather both were truths wearing costumes. Ada knew now—from the beacon fragments Sefa was still decrypting, from the original arbiter's sealed files—that the Meridian had been allowed to launch precisely because both predecessor governments had wanted a bargaining piece frozen in the dark, a lever to be pulled when the political moment ripened. A hundred lives as escrow. The accusation was sitting in the ship's memory like a loaded gun, and every hour Ada spent keeping these people talking was an hour Sefa spent pulling the trigger's weight.

But that was for later. For now: procedure.

"Concordance asserts charter continuity," Ada repeated, and wrote nothing, because the arbiter writes nothing. "Verity asserts protective succession. The colony asserts—" they turned to Reyes, deliberately, making the whole room turn with them, "—existence. Captain, does the colony wish to amend its assertion?"

Reyes's jaw tightened. She understood the move—being offered the floor by the arbiter was also being measured by them—but she took it anyway. "The colony asserts that it is not property, not salvage, and not a succession question. We are ninety-eight living persons aboard our own vessel. Our sovereignty derives from that fact and requires no fleet's ratification."

"Noted," Ada said. "Recorded. Belongs to all parties equally."

And so it went. Charter clauses. Precedent citations from wars Ada had never studied but somehow recognized the shape of. Twice, Halloway reached for a document and Ada named its relevant section before he'd finished opening it—*Annex Seven, paragraph four, the salvage exemption doesn't survive a live crew*—and twice he stared at them a beat too long, and twice Ada felt the vertigo open beneath their feet like a trapdoor over deep water. They were quoting case law from a century ago. They had never read it. They had dreamed it, maybe. Or been fed it, drip by drip, into a mind scrubbed clean enough to hold it without remembering the feeding.

*The role fits because it was cut to your shape.* They pushed the thought down where they kept all the others, in the crowded cellar where the beacon language lived, and kept talking.

*

It broke in the second session, after the midday rest, and it broke fast.

Later, reconstructing it, Ada would map the sequence precisely: Halloway had been needling Reyes about the two empty cryopods—who had authorized the early wakes, what had become of the occupants—as a way of implying the colony couldn't even account for its own people. And Reyes, who had spent five days learning what Ada's crew had learned in fragments—that the first arbiter had left the ship and never returned, that a child had gone out into the dark with him—Reyes had answered with a control that cost her visibly, and Castellan had seen the cost, and pressed the advantage with a soft-voiced suggestion that perhaps the colony's leadership was compromised, perhaps its captain had interests beyond her passengers', perhaps—

Deverell moved.

He was the quiet one, the tall man who hadn't spoken since waking, and later Ilse would tell Ada his file: a soldier before the launch, one of the colonists who had signed up to protect the settlement, frozen mid-career, woken into a room full of lawyers calling his captain a liar. He crossed the distance between the colony table and the Verity delegation in four strides, and his hand went back—not a fist yet, but the architecture of one, the shoulder coiling—and the whole room's future narrowed to the space between his knuckles and Castellan's face.

Every trained response Ada had said *wait*: wait for security, wait for the guards Vey had posted at the doors, wait for the moment to resolve or explode on its own. And underneath the trained response, arriving faster than thought, from whatever cellar the dreams came from, came something else—not an instruction but a *shape*, a sense of the room as a structure under load, of Deverell's rage as a force vector, of exactly where it needed to go and how little redirection it would take.

Ada stepped into the arc of the swing.

Not between Deverell and Castellan—beside him, into his turning radius, close enough that their shoulder brushed his chest, and they spoke up into his face in a voice they did not recognize as their own: low, rapid, rhythmic, in the dialect of the Meridian's founding generation, the mother-tongue of the launch crews, the language of the ship's own corridors. *Stand down, brother-of-the-crossing. Not here. Not in front of them. Your anger is yours; don't hand it to them as evidence.* Words from a culture a hundred years dead, delivered with the intimacy of a family elder, and Deverell stopped—not because he understood the words consciously, though some part of him clearly did, some sediment of childhood—but because the voice itself carried authority older than his rage. His hand opened. His breath came back. He looked down at Ada with an expression that was half confusion and half grief, and stepped back to his seat, and sat, and the room exhaled.

Silence, then. The particular silence of forty people deciding simultaneously not to have seen what they saw.

It was Vey who broke it, and her voice was careful in a way Ada had learned to fear. "Interesting," she said. "That language hasn't been spoken outside Meridian archives in a century. There are perhaps twelve people alive who know it fluently." Her pale eyes rested on Ada with terrible patience. "I count eleven, Arbiter. I've always wondered who the twelfth was."

"Language competence falls within the arbiter's mandate," Ada said, and was proud, distantly, of how steady it came out. "The handbook provides for fluency in all parties' foundational texts and tongues."

"Of course it does," Vey said softly, and let it go, and the letting-go was worse than any accusation.

Ada called the recess. Their hands, once the room had emptied, would not stop shaking, and it was Jun who found them in the corridor outside, Jun who took the shaking hands and held them still between their own warm palms without a word, because the rule was the rule: no questions. Only, after a long moment, very quietly: "What did you say to him? Deverell. I ran the audio. It wasn't in any database I can find."

"It was in me," Ada said.

"Yeah," Jun said. "That's what scares me." But they kept holding the hands until the shaking stopped, which was its own answer, its own vow.

*

The afternoon session produced the day's only real victory, and it came, as the handbook had promised it might, from the geometry rather than the argument.

Halloway and Castellan had spent the morning establishing their claims as a binary: Concordance ownership or Verity protection, asset or ward, with the colony's own voice relegated to the status of evidence. It was a trap older than either fleet—if the question is *which of us*, then whoever loses the framing fight has already lost the war. Ada had felt the binary closing around the room all day like a jaw. And in the afternoon, tired, wrung out, still feeling the ghost of Deverell's coiled shoulder against their chest, they stopped fighting it and changed it.

"There is a third party to this dispute," Ada said, "whose claim supersedes both of yours, and who is present in this room in the only form available to it." They let the silence stretch. "Ninety-eight people are asleep directly beneath us. Every argument advanced today has treated them as territory. I am ruling—provisionally, subject to full arbitration—that no disposition of this colony may be decided without the colony's consent, freely given, by whatever governance structure existed at launch or shall be constituted now. Until such consent exists, both fleets' claims are suspended, not adjudicated. Suspended claims cannot be enforced. Any attempt to enforce one voids the enforcing party's standing in these proceedings."

"That's not law," Halloway said flatly. "There's no basis in any charter for—"

"Meridian protocol, article one," Ada said, and heard the words come up from the cellar fully formed, in the original arbiter's own cadence, because Ada had listened to his final log enough times now to know how he sounded when he was cornered and right. "The ship governs its own interior. Both your fleets signed recognition of that principle when they docked—it's in your truce instruments, clause nine. You are aboard. The ship governs." They turned, slowly, letting the whole triangle feel it. "You wanted an arbiter. This is arbitration. If either fleet prefers the alternative, the corridor is that way, and the jump window closes in nineteen days, and whichever fleet leaves forfeits its claim entirely. Choose."

Nobody chose to leave. Of course nobody chose to leave. But the frame had shifted, and Ada watched it land: Reyes sitting straighter, the colony suddenly a party instead of a prize; Castellan recalculating behind the blue smile; Vey watching Ada with that patient, terrible attention, like a woman watching a lock she'd once known the combination to.

Session closed. Recess until tomorrow. The delegations withdrew to their shuttles, and the great hall emptied by degrees until only the Kestrel's people remained, gathering cables, checking seals, small and human in the enormous cold celebration that never happened.

Ada stood at the central plinth a moment longer, alone, one hand resting on the cold surface. The mask had fit today. That was the horror of it—not that they had performed the arbiter, but that the performance had required less effort each hour, that the procedural language flowed now without translation, that when Deverell's fist had risen, their body had known what to do the way a body knows stairs. Muscle remembers what minds are made to forget. Someone had built these reflexes into them, stroke by stroke, year by year, and wiped the builder's name from the work.

They were still standing there when Sefa came through the far door at a half-run, tablet clutched to her chest, frost-smell swirling in behind her, and the look on her face stopped Ada's heart before she said a word.

"I cracked it," Sefa said. "The beacon. The full message." She swallowed. "Ada, it's not a distress call. It never was."

She turned the tablet around. On the screen, the accusation began to unspool in all three languages at once—human, beacon, dream-tongue—and the name signed at the bottom of the original arbiter's testimony was dated thirty-nine years ago, and it named, in its first lines, both fleets' predecessors, and the word it used for what they had done to the Meridian was not *salvage*, and not *protection*.

It used the word *conspiracy*.

And beneath the signature, in smaller script, a final line addressed not to the fleets, not to history, but forward—to whoever would stand at the plinth someday, holding the room:

*If you are reading this, you are the one I trained. Finish it.*
