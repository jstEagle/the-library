## 28. The Original Arbiter's Log

The corridor to the arbiter's quarters had not been opened in forty years.

Ada knew this because Meridian-Prime told them, in that voice like warm water over stone: *Sealed at the request of Arbiter Elias Almeida. Last accessed: forty-one years, two hundred six days ago.* The AI had hesitated—Ada had not known an AI could hesitate—before adding, *He said the next person to open it would be the one who needed it. I believe that is you.*

The seal was a physical lock, absurdly old-fashioned, a lever that required a human hand and a human weight. Ada put their palm against the cold of it and thought: *I was a child when he closed this door. I might have been standing exactly here.*

The thought did not feel like speculation. It felt like memory with the picture scratched off.

Behind them, the corridor lights of the Meridian hummed at emergency minimum, frost blooming and sublimating in slow cycles along the seams of the walls. Somewhere aft, Ilse was monitoring the second wave of thaw cases. Somewhere forward, Jun was not speaking to them, which had its own acoustic signature, its own weather. Ada had learned, over the past days, to navigate by the silences of their crew the way other people navigated by stars.

They pulled the lever. The door exhaled forty years of held breath—cold, mineral, the smell of a room that had been waiting.

* * *

It was small. That was the first wrongness, or the first rightness—Ada couldn't decide. They had expected, from the shrine-like reverence of the manifest designation, something grander. Instead: a bunk made with military precision, a desk, a shelf of paper books—*paper*, the extravagance of it, trees that had died a century ago so a man could underline his sentences—and a single chair angled toward a viewport that looked out on nothing, on the long dark between stars, on the exact view a man would have chosen if he intended to spend years looking at it and thinking.

The walls were the archive.

Photographs, printouts, hand-drawn star charts with annotations in three scripts. A child's drawing, pinned at eye level—the same drawing Sefa had found and photographed and shown Ada with hands that shook. Ada looked at it again now. A stick figure with dark hair standing between two large angular shapes, one labeled in a child's uneven hand, the other labeled in a script Ada's eyes read fluently and their mouth could not yet pronounce. Between the two large shapes, the small figure held up both hands, palms out. The universal grammar of *stop*. Or of *peace*. Or of a child who had already learned that her job in the universe was to stand between things.

Below the drawing, a strip of medical printout. Growth charts. Neural plasticity indices. A schedule, handwritten, in a cramped and urgent script:

*Week 1–4: passive immersion, beacon recordings during sleep cycles. Week 5: first production drills. Do not correct her pronunciation; let the phonemes settle on their own. Week 8: begin code-switching exercises—she should feel the two languages as different rooms in the same house, not different houses.*

Ada's hand went to their own throat without their permission.

They had been eight years old, or thereabouts, the age the growth chart indicated. They had no memory of this room. No memory of the man whose handwriting this was, whose surname they had carried through eleven years of cargo runs and silence like a coin they assumed was counterfeit. And yet the schedule on the wall described, with the specificity of lived routine, the exact texture of Ada's earliest fragmentary memories: the feeling of falling asleep to a sound like wind through a canyon that was not wind, that was *speech*; the feeling of being gently, endlessly corrected without correction; the feeling of two rooms in one house.

The dreams. The dreamless years. The language no one recognized, that Ada had spoken into their pillow since before Jun had known them, since before the *Kestrel*, since before everything—had been *curriculum*. Had been homework, assigned by a dead man, graded by no one, submitted nightly for forty years into the dark.

Ada sat down in the chair. It fit them. That was the worst part. The chair had been worn to the shape of a larger body, a man's body, and it fit them anyway, the way a key fits a lock cut from the same blank.

The desk's terminal was analog-fallback, a crank-charged archive unit, the kind of thing you built when you wanted your last words to survive the death of the power grid. Ada turned the crank. It resisted, then gave, with a sound like a joint popping. The screen bloomed green.

One file. Labeled in the clear, no encryption, because he had wanted it found: *FOR THE ONE WHO WAKES. IF YOU ARE READING THIS, I AM SORRY, AND IT WORKED.*

Ada pressed play.

* * *

The recording was audio only. A man's voice, middle-aged, tired in the way that tiredness becomes a country you live in rather than a state you pass through. He spoke English with the flat, careful clarity of someone dictating for a transcript.

*"Log final. Arbiter Elias Almeida, Meridian, sole conscious crew. If the protocols worked, you already know who I was. If they worked better than I dared hope, you know who you are, and I won't insult you by explaining it. But I was trained never to assume the protocol survives contact with reality, so I'll say it plainly, once.*

*"You are the child in the drawing. You are the girl I pulled out of the second pod when you were six years old, because the Compact's advance scouts had boarded us and I could not—"* the voice stopped. A long breath. When it resumed, it was lower, rougher. *"I could not let them find you. A child in cryo, on a ship both fleets had already decided was a bargaining chip. You were the only thing on this ship that was not a chip. I intend to keep it that way.*

*"So I woke you. And I did the unforgivable thing, which is that I did it in the only way I knew how. I made you into the mission. Not the cargo. The mission. The arbiter protocol requires a neutral party—someone with no flag, no fleet, no debt to either power, someone whose loyalty is to the negotiation itself. I spent twenty years training myself and I was never neutral. I had a daughter on this ship. I had a rage. I was the wrong instrument and I knew it, and then the universe handed me a six-year-old and I understood, with a clarity I would pay for the rest of my life, what the protocol had actually been waiting for.*

*"Not a trained arbiter. A *grown* one. Someone raised inside the work so deeply that neutrality wouldn't be a discipline for her. It would be her native language. Like the beacon tongue. Like the dream-talk."*

Ada's breath fogged in the cold room. They did not wipe their eyes. There seemed little point; more were coming.

*"I taught you the beacon language the only way it can be taught, which is the way it teaches itself—by immersion, by sleep, by years. You spoke it before you spoke English. I have the recordings. Sometimes at night you would talk in your bunk and I would sit outside your door and listen and think: there it is. The Meridian's own voice, coming out of a child. The colonists' century of silence, given a mouth. And I thought, whoever she becomes, whatever name she carries, she will be able to speak to all three parties of this disaster as a native. No translation. No flag. No debt.*

*"Then the Concordance found us. Not the ship—they never found the ship. They found *me*. Forty years ago I made a supply run in the tender, masked as debris, and their long-range picket caught my drive flare. They didn't know what I was. But they knew what I knew, because I had spent two years trying to negotiate with their predecessors before the conspiracy went final, and they had files, and files have faces.*

*"I could have run back here and sealed the ship and let the sleepers sleep another century until someone honest found them. Maybe that was the right call. I've had forty years to decide and I still don't know. But they had my face on file and a description, and descriptions travel, and one day a patrol was going to stop a hauler and see my daughter grown and recognize the jaw I gave her. I couldn't let you be found. Found, you were a lead. Lost, you were free.*

*"So I gave you back to the human world. Wiped the ship, wiped me, wiped the rooms, wiped the language's name if not its sound—I couldn't erase what was in your blood, only the index. I set you down at a station with papers and a name—mine, I'm not sorry for that, you were mine in every way that matters and names are the only inheritance I had—and I let you grow up human. Grow up small. Grow up invisible. Because invisible is what the protocol needs. The arbiter cannot arrive from anywhere. She has to be already there, in the corridor, when the moment comes.*

*"I went after the people who did this. Not for justice. Justice is a luxury word. For *time*. Every year I spent leading their intelligence services in circles was a year the Meridian stayed dark and the sleepers stayed frozen and you stayed free. I was good at it. I had been trained for misdirection my whole career; it turned out that was the transferable skill. I kept them chasing a ghost ship through three sectors for—*" a dry, cracked sound that might have been a laugh. *"Longer than I should have. I'm old. The tender's older. I am recording this in the tender, in orbit of a rock that isn't on any chart, and I am not going back, and we both know why.*

*"Which brings me to the last thing, and it is the hardest, so I'm going to say it the way I taught you to say hard things: plainly, once, and then let it stand.*

*"I chose the mission over you. Twice. I chose it when I woke you and made you into an instrument, and I chose it again when I wiped your mind and sent you away, and I will choose it a third time now, by dying out here instead of coming back to be your father in whatever years you had left for one. You deserve to hate me for it. I want to tell you that you shouldn't, but that would just be one more thing I decided for you, and I have decided enough.*

*"So: hate me, or don't. But take the work. Not because I ask—never because I ask. Take it because you'll find, when the moment comes, that you're the only one in the room who can hold it, and because the ninety-eight people in those pods never agreed to be chips, and because the beacon has been screaming the truth into the dark for a hundred years and someone finally needs to answer it in its own tongue.*

*"The full accusation is in the beacon. I finished it before I left. Both fleets' predecessors built this stranding together—the Compact's founders sold the route data, the Concordance's founders sold the silence, and the colony was the price of their peace with each other. It's all in there, timestamped, sourced, signed. I made sure it would survive. Truth is the only cargo that ships itself, if you pack it right.*

*"The dreams were homework. I'm sorry. I hope they were also—"* the voice stopped again, and this time the silence went on so long Ada thought the recording had ended. When it came back, it was barely above a whisper, and the flat dictation clarity had cracked clean through. *"I hope they were also mine. Some nights, when you talked in your sleep, I talked back. You never knew. I'd like to think some part of you did. I'd like to think that somewhere in the forty years, in the stations and the haulers and the long routes, some part of you heard me answering, and knew you hadn't been left alone in the dark with a language no one else spoke.*

*"You weren't. I was always on the other end of it. Until now.*

*"Log final. Arbiter Almeida, signing off. Wake them gently. Tell them the truth. Hold the line.*

*"Goodnight, little star. You were the best of me."*

* * *

The recording ended. The green screen held its empty light.

Ada sat in the dead man's chair in the dead man's room and did the arithmetic of a destroyed life. Forty-one years. A tender, a rock, an old man running out of fuel and time, choosing a cold orbit over a reunion because even his ghost would have been a danger to her. The dreams were homework. The sleep-talk was correspondence. Every night for forty years, Ada had been speaking fluent beacon-tongue into their pillow, and for the first eleven of those years—or however many there had been before the wipe's edges blurred—a man had lain on the other side of a thin bulkhead and answered.

*I talked back. You never knew.*

Ada's hands were shaking. They watched them shake with a strange detachment, the detachment of a linguist observing an unfamiliar phoneme: *this is what grief looks like in a body that has just learned it was loved by a stranger.* They had grieved, over the years, without knowing for what—a nameless homesickness, a sense of a door closed somewhere behind them, the feeling that had driven them to the Long Route in the first place, to the silence and the cargo and the small unremarkable life. They had thought they were running from nothing.

They had been running *toward* the protocol. Invisible. Flagless. Already there, in the corridor, when the moment came.

And the moment had come. Both fleets in high orbit, weapons warm. Ninety-eight sleepers thawing in waves below decks. Reyes watching them with a suspicion that was starting to look, horribly, like recognition. Vey aboard under truce, carrying a secret in her mouth like a loaded weapon. Jun not speaking to them. The jump window closing.

*The dreams were homework.* Ada said it aloud, in English, and the room took the words and gave back nothing but cold.

Then, because there was nothing else to do with a thing that size, they said it again in the beacon tongue, and the ship's dead air seemed to lean in and listen, and somewhere below them, Ada would have sworn, ninety-eight sleepers shifted in their frost.

* * *

They were still in the chair when the knock came—two raps, Brannoc's rhythm, the pilot's knock that meant *time is a thing we are running out of.*

"Ada." A pause. "You need to see this. Both of you. Bridge, now."

Ada rose. The chair released them with a creak of old polymer, and they stood a moment in the doorway, looking back at the room: the made bunk, the paper books, the child's drawing with its small figure holding up both hands between two great shapes.

*Stand between things.* It had never been a role. It had been a childhood.

They closed the door behind them and followed Brannoc's quick footsteps up the dark corridor, toward the bridge, toward whatever new fracture the universe had prepared—though later, much later, Ada would remember that Brannoc had not said *you need to see this* with anger, or even urgency, really.

He had said it with something very close to fear.
