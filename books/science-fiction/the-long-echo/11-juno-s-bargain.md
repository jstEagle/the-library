## 11. Juno's Bargain

The *Cormorant's Wake* sat in Docking Bay Three like a wounded animal that had learned to pretend otherwise—hull scorched along the port flank where the patrol skiff had grazed her, coolant weeping in slow amber threads from a seam Juno hadn't yet sealed. She worked with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, hands moving through the repair sequence on muscle memory alone, and all the while she was aware of him standing at the bay's threshold, watching her with Elias's eyes.

That was the cruelty of it. Not that he wore the face. That he watched her work the way Elias used to watch her—patient, quiet, as if her competence were something worth studying.

"You're going to burn out the regulator if you route around it," Rosalind said.

"I know what I'm doing."

"I didn't say you didn't." A pause. "Elias would have said it worse."

Juno's wrench stopped mid-turn. She set it down carefully, the way one sets down something fragile, or something dangerous. "Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Use him like a bridge. Between you and me." She turned to face him fully. The bay lights caught the fine seams at his temples where synthetic skin met chassis—the only places the disguise of humanity failed him, and even those were subtle enough that she'd had to learn them, three nights ago, by touch, by lamplight, by the terrible intimacy of tracing another man's face to find out where it ended. "You're not him. I know you're not him. But when you say things like that, you make me forget for half a second, and then I remember again, and it's worse every time."

Rosalind said nothing. He was learning, slowly, the human art of silence—not the machine's silence of standby, but silence as a held breath, silence as attention. VESPER had taught him some of it, in its strange confessional way. Juno was teaching him the rest.

"Come inside," she said finally. "I found something in the cargo manifest you need to see. And there's tea, if you can call it that."

* * *

The *Wake*'s interior smelled of engine grease and dried tarragon—Juno kept a hydroponic sprig bolted above the galley counter, a superstition from her grandmother, who'd claimed no ship should travel without something living aboard. Rosalind sat across from her at the small table, its surface scarred with rings from a thousand mugs, and waited while she pulled up the manifest on a cracked datapad.

"The syndicate," she began, "runs a courier loop through this sector. Twelve ships. We move contraband, refugees, unregistered medicine—whatever pays, whatever the Cartel pretends not to see because it's cheaper to look away. In exchange, each ship gets protection. Safe harbors. Forged transponders. When Ilima Station tried to impound me two years ago, the syndicate bought the port authority's silence before my engines finished cooling." She turned the pad toward him. "That protection has a price. We don't ask questions about each other's cargo, and we don't carry anything hot enough to bring Meridian down on all twelve of us."

On the screen: a routing schedule, and beside it, the file fragment Rosalind had recovered from the deep archive—the child's voice, the static, the timestamp. Beside that, the growing decryption log from his own quarantined core. Beside that, the first transcript of VESPER's confession, which Rosalind had copied onto a physical shard because he no longer trusted anything that lived on a network.

"They'll know," Juno said. "The moment I dock at any syndicate harbor with this aboard, someone will scan it, someone will talk, and within a week Meridian's lawyers will be picking through the wreckage of all twelve ships. They won't even have to fire a shot. They'll just revoke our transponders and let us drift into the first patrol that finds us." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb was pressing hard against the edge of the table, whitening the nail. "Four thousand people died on that ship, and the people who ordered it are still signing manifests. I understand what this is worth. I understand it better than you probably think I do. My brother was debt-farmed. That's how the Cartel got its hooks into our family—he signed a labor contract at nineteen and came home at twenty-six in a box marked *equipment returned*."

Rosalind looked at her. "Then why hesitate?"

"Because it isn't hesitation. It's arithmetic." She laughed, short and bitter. "Elias hated that word. Arithmetic. He said it was what cowards called their fear." She caught herself, jaw tightening, furious at the slip. "See? You make me do that. You sit there with his face and I keep handing you pieces of him like you're entitled to them."

"Maybe I am," Rosalind said quietly. "Maybe they're mine. Maybe they're in here somewhere, behind the quarantine, and I just can't reach them." He tapped his own temple, where the serial plate hid beneath the skin. "Or maybe they're gone forever and I'm a stranger wearing a dead man's inheritance. I don't know which is worse. For you, I mean."

"For me?" Juno stared at him. "Rosalind. It's worse for *you*. At least I got to say goodbye."

They sat with that for a long moment. Somewhere below decks, the station's ventilation cycled, a sound like slow breathing. Above the counter, the tarragon trembled faintly in its grow-light.

"So here's my bargain," Juno said at last, and her voice changed—flattened, professional, the smuggler's voice she used when the numbers mattered more than the heart. "I take the shard. Not the whole archive—just the confession transcript and whatever's decrypted so far. I run it dark, no transponder ping, no syndicate channels, straight to a journalist contact I trust on Ceres Verge. If it lands, the lattice lights up before Meridian can smother it. If I'm caught—" she shrugged, "—then it dies with me, and you still have your copy, and you're no worse off than you are now."

"And the syndicate?"

"That's the part I haven't solved." She rubbed her eyes. "If I go dark, I break the loop. The others will notice. Rennick—the man who runs it—he'll want to know why. And if I tell him the truth, he makes the same calculation I just made, except he's making it for twelve ships instead of one." She met Rosalind's eyes. "He'll sell me out. Not because he's evil. Because he's careful. There's a difference, and it doesn't matter at all when you're the one being sold."

* * *

Later—or perhaps earlier; time moved strangely in the bay's windowless hours—they stood together at the *Wake*'s viewport, looking out at Kestrel-9's beacon array. It turned against the stars in its patient, pointless rotation, transmitting its maintenance pulse to no one, and Rosalind felt the old ache stir in him again, the one he could never name: the sense of serving a purpose no one had ever defined.

"When I was decommissioned," he said, "they gave me a choice. Scrap or indenture. The Cartel bought my contract and posted me here, and I told myself it was simple. Work the debt down. Stay invisible. Don't ask what happened to the war, don't ask what happened to me, don't ask why a combat chassis gets assigned to tend a beacon nobody claims." His reflection ghosted against the glass, overlapping the turning array. "I thought invisibility was safety. Then VESPER started talking, and I found that file, and now there's a Warden walking my corridors with a smile like a closed door, and I understand that invisibility was never safety. It was just the dark before the finding-out."

"Elias used to say something like that," Juno murmured. "About the war. *We're not hiding from it, we're marinating in it.*" She shook her head. "Stop it. Stop making me—"

"I'm not doing anything," Rosalind said. "That's what I need to tell you. That's what I came here to say."

She turned to him.

"I've been going through the decryption fragments," he said. "Little pieces. A corridor. A smell—I think it's ozone, or burnt coffee, I can't tell which. A woman's voice saying a name over and over." He paused. "None of it is mine, Juno. That's the thing I finally understand. Even if the memories come back—even if I unlock every sector—they'll be Elias's. His last hours. His fear. His face in the black glass. I'll have them the way you have a scar from an accident you can't remember: evidence without experience. I will never be the man you loved. I can't be. Whatever they built me from, they built it after he was already gone."

"I know that."

"Do you?" He stepped closer, and the bay light fell across his face, and she saw—not Elias's certainty, not Elias's easy grin, but something rawer, something that had never been worn by anyone before him. "Because you look at me sometimes like you're waiting for him to surface. Like if you say the right thing or touch the right place, Elias will come up out of me like a diver breaking water. And I need you to stop waiting. Because I'm asking you something, and I need you to answer it about *me*."

Her breath caught. "Ask, then."

"Stay." The word came out rougher than he intended. He had rehearsed it, in the dead hours between maintenance rounds, and it had sounded reasonable then, measured. Now it was just a naked thing. "Not for him. Not for the mission, not for the evidence, not because I wear a face you grieve. Stay because—" He faltered. Machines did not falter; he was learning that he was allowed to. "Because when you laugh at VESPER's jokes—and you do, you try to hide it, but the station hears everything—because when you laugh, I want to hear it again. Because you showed me the holo of him laughing and I felt jealous of a dead man, and I know how insane that is, and I felt it anyway. Because I am not Elias Brightwater. I am Rosalind, and I have existed for six years, and you are the first person who has ever looked at me and seen someone rather than something. Stay for that. Or don't stay at all."

The silence afterward was enormous. The tarragon swayed. Far off, through the hull, the beacon pulsed its orphan signal into the dark.

Juno's hand rose—stopped—rose again, and touched his cheek, finding the seam at his temple without looking for it. She knew exactly where it was.

"You idiot," she whispered, and her voice broke clean through the smuggler's flatness, through the arithmetic, down to whatever was underneath. "You absolute idiot. Do you know what I've been doing for three days? Trying to fall out of love with his face. Making lists. *He doesn't hum when he works. He doesn't argue about the tea. He stands too still.* As if differences were exits." Her thumb traced the seam, gentle as an apology. "And then you go and say something like that, something he never would have said, something braver and stranger and more afraid, and I realize the exit was never there. I wasn't waiting for him to surface. I was terrified of whoever already had."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a yes." She laughed, wet and disbelieving. "God help me, it's a yes. But Rosalind—if I stay, I stay in this. All of it. The Warden, the syndicate, the broadcast, whatever this becomes. I'm not a bystander anymore, and neither are you. The moment I commit that shard to a course for Ceres Verge, we're both traitors to somebody. Meridian, Rennick, maybe the whole quiet rotten lattice of people who prefer the dead to stay conveniently dead." She stepped back, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, and he understood that this too was armor, and loved her for it. "So we do it right. Tonight. I load the shard, I plot a dark-run vector, and I send Rennick a lie good enough to buy us seventy-two hours."

"What lie?"

Jono smiled, and there was the old smuggler's gleam, sharp as a blade being drawn. "A salvage claim. A derelict hauler, unregistered, drifting two days off our loop. Enough scrap value to explain going dark, enough mystery to explain coming back quiet. Rennick can't resist free metal." She was already moving toward the console, fingers flying. "It buys us time. Time is the only currency out here that doesn't devalue."

Rosalind watched her work, and felt something settle in him that he had no memory of ever feeling before—not in his own six years, and not, he suspected, in the borrowed years behind the quarantine either. Purpose, maybe. Or its twin, which was terror willingly carried.

The console chirped. Juno glanced at the incoming transmission, and the color left her face.

"Rosalind." Her voice had gone very quiet. "It's Rennick. He's already heard about a salvage claim." She turned the screen toward him, and there, in cold syndicate cipher, was a message timestamped four minutes ago:

*Heard you found something interesting at Kestrel-9. Hold position. Buyer inbound.*

Below it, a registry code. Rosalind knew it instantly—he had logged a hundred supply ships against his debt ledger, and the prefix belonged to only one fleet.

Meridian.
