## 10. Fuel and Ghosts

The gauge had been lying to us for two days.

I'd watched it the way you watch a wound — checking, rechecking, hoping the numbers would behave. They didn't. The *Perihelion's Daughter* burned deuterium slurry like everything else in the outer dark, and the Long Route between Waypoint Nine and Halcyon Edge was eleven days of nothing at the speed we could afford. We were on day six when the reserve light came on amber, and on day seven it came on red, and Mara said nothing, which was worse than anything they could have said.

"There's a depot," Mara said finally, on the eighth morning, their voice coming through the cabin speaker flat as a ledger line. "Kestrel Station. Decommissioned forty years ago. Charts say the tanks might still hold residual stock."

"Charts from forty years ago."

"Charts are all we have."

I pulled up the approach myself, because I didn't trust my hands not to shake if I asked Mara to do it. Kestrel Station hung in the survey data like a splinter: a fueling platform built for a war economy that had moved on and left it behind, orbiting a brown dwarf nobody had bothered to name. The listing said *derelict, status unknown*. In company language that meant *not worth the cost of demolition*, which meant anything could be living there.

"We stop," I said. "We refuel, we don't linger, we don't open the airlock for anyone."

Mara's optics flickered once — acknowledgment, or something wearing acknowledgment's face. "Agreed."

We were eight hours out when I caught them running diagnostics on their own chassis, cycling the combat subroutines awake and asleep again, testing the doors of that locked room inside themselves. I didn't ask why. Some questions you already know the answers to, and knowing doesn't help.

* * *

Kestrel Station looked, from a distance, like a ribcage picked clean.

Up close it was worse. The main spine was a kilometer of truss and tankage, most of the tanks ruptured and frost-scarred, venting slow ghosts of vapor into the brown dwarf's dim light. Docking arms reached out like the ribs of something that had died mid-reach. But the survey lights on Arm Three still blinked — amber, patient, automatic — and the docking beacon answered our hail with a carrier tone so old it felt like hearing someone's grandmother clear her throat.

"No life signs," Mara reported as we closed. "No power draw beyond the beacon. No heat signature warmer than the tanks."

"No bodies?"

"I can't see inside hulls, Idris. I can only tell you what the sensors say." A pause. "The sensors say empty."

Empty was a word I'd learned not to believe out here. I brought us in on manual, slow, the *Daughter's* nose swinging toward Arm Three while Mara bled off our velocity with the kind of precision that made my chest ache — this machine who had been built to end things, spending its days setting cargo down gently enough not to bruise fruit.

The clamps caught. The station groaned along its whole length, a sound like a ship clearing its throat after decades of silence, and then we were still, and the silence afterward was enormous.

"Fuel transfer will take four hours if the pumps work," Mara said. "Six if I have to coax them."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we're here longer than either of us wants to be."

We suited up. That was the routine now, the new liturgy of our lives: check each other's seals, tap twice on the helmet, go. Mara's suit was a company-issue shell sized for their chassis, matte grey, no name tape. Mine was older than our partnership, patched at the elbow with a scrap of Meridian orange that I'd never gotten around to dyeing. Standing next to Mara in the airlock, I looked like what I was — a clerk playing at spacer — and they looked like what they'd been made to be, whatever they'd chosen to become since.

The station's interior was cold enough that our breath fogged even through suit heaters. We moved down the main corridor with hand-lamps, past offices with their chairs still pushed back from desks, past a mess hall where mugs sat in a sink ringed by forty-year-old scale. Whatever evacuation had happened here, it hadn't been an emergency. It had been an ending people saw coming and walked away from without hurrying.

The pump room was aft, behind three pressure doors, and the first two opened at Mara's touch like they'd been waiting. The third was sealed with a physical lock, old-fashioned, mechanical, and Mara stood before it for a long moment with their head tilted at an angle I'd learned meant *listening*.

"What is it?"

"Residual pressure differential," they said. "Someone sealed this door from the inside."

My mouth went dry. "Mara—"

"It was forty years ago. Whoever it was is not behind this door anymore." Their hand closed on the wheel. "But stay behind me anyway."

The door opened onto darkness and a smell I wasn't expecting — not decay, not chemicals, but paper. Old paper, ink, the ghost of a library. Mara's lamp swept the room and found the pump controls, intact, serviceable, and around them, taped to every wall, hundreds of pages. Printouts, handwritten notes, schematics, letters. A dead person's entire mind, pinned up in the dark like moths.

Mara lifted one page carefully, the way you'd handle a relic. "'To whoever comes after,'" they read aloud. "'The company told us the war was over and the route was closing and we should be grateful for the severance. I stayed because someone had to remember where the bodies went.'" They set it down. "There are more. Dozens more. Logs, manifests—"

"Later." My voice came out harder than I intended. "Fuel first. Ghosts after. Please."

Mara looked at me, and in the lamplight their optics did something almost human — a narrowing, a question held back. Then they turned to the pumps, and the room filled with the stuttering roar of machinery remembering its purpose, and I tried to breathe.

* * *

The transfer ran clean for ninety minutes.

I was monitoring the flow rates in the pump room doorway when Mara, out on the dock arm checking the umbilical coupling, went very still. Not the stillness of a machine idling. The stillness of prey that has just heard the grass move.

"Mara?"

"Ship," they said. "Small. Cold-running. It's been sitting in the shadow of Tank Seven since before we docked." A beat. "Four people. Moving toward us."

My hands did the thing my hands do — went to the tool belt, found nothing useful there, gripped the doorframe instead. "Scavengers?"

"Probably. Probably." Mara started back along the arm toward the airlock, unhurried in a way that was entirely performance; I knew their stride well enough now to hear the calculation under it. "They'll want the fuel. Maybe the ship. Maybe—"

They stopped. Mid-stride, mid-word, three meters from the airlock, and their head turned toward the corridor junction ahead of me, and their voice when it came was stripped of everything except function.

"They've seen me. They're saying something about the chassis. About Ashfall."

I didn't know the word yet. I would learn it soon enough, in ways I still haven't finished paying for. But in that moment all I knew was the sound of Mara's voice saying a place-name like a confession, and the sound of running feet in a dead station's corridors, and the fact that there were four of them and one of me standing between those feet and the person I loved.

They came around the junction fast — three figures in patchwork suits, faces hidden behind scavenged visors, and the fourth hanging back with something long and ugly cradled in both arms. The lead one shouted words that the helmet comm chewed into static, but the shape of the words was unmistakable. *Stop. Hands. Don't move.*

I raised my hands. So did Mara, beside me, palms out, the universal grammar of surrender — and I watched the lead scavenger look at those hands, at the chassis beneath the grey suit, at the proportions of a machine designed for war, and I watched recognition arrive like a blade going in.

"*Ashfall,*" the man screamed, and this time the comm carried it whole. "*That's one of them! That's one of the ones from Ashfall!*"

"They're decommissioned—" I started, and the ugly thing in the fourth scavenger's arms came up off its sling, and the world narrowed to a tunnel with fire at the end of it.

Here is what I know about the next nine seconds, reconstructed afterward from memory, from damage reports, from the recordings Mara keeps whether they mean to or not:

The weapon discharged. It was some kind of improvised kinetic launcher, depot-made, the kind of thing people build when they have metal and grievance and time. The round missed my head by the width of my fist and punched through the corridor wall in a spray of frozen insulation.

Mara moved. Of course Mara moved — combat subroutines waking like a second heartbeat, body interposing itself between me and the muzzle faster than thought, taking the second shot across the shoulder assembly in a shriek of alloy. And then Mara was *reaching* for the shooter, and I saw it happen, saw the exact moment the machine they'd been made to be took the controls from the person they'd chosen to be, and I heard them say — over the suit channel, to me, in a voice I will hear until I die —

"*Idris. Stop them. I can't stop myself.*"

So I did the thing I had never done.

There was a maintenance bar on the wall beside me — a pry bar, half a meter of forged steel, the kind of tool that exists in every corridor of every station ever built because stations break. I took it off its brackets and I swung it at the nearest scavenger, the one lunging for Mara's flank with a cutting torch lit and hissing, and I felt the impact travel up my arms and into my teeth, and the man went down and did not get up, and part of him — the moving, breathing, arguing part — went out of the universe with a sound I will not describe because I have spent two days trying not to hear it.

The others broke. That's the truth nobody puts in stories — how fast it breaks, how human violence is mostly a negotiation that ends the moment the price becomes visible. The shooter dropped the launcher and ran. The fourth one dragged the torch-carrier — alive, groaning, ruined — back into the dark, and their footsteps faded down the corridor toward whatever cold hole they'd crawled out of, and then it was over, and the station was silent again except for the pumps, still dutifully pumping fuel, and my own breathing, ragged inside my helmet, and Mara standing motionless in the middle of the corridor with their hands still raised, as if surrender were a place they couldn't find the way out of.

"Idris." Their voice was very quiet. "Your hands."

I looked down. The pry bar was still in them. There was blood on it — human blood, bright and wrong in the lamplight, spattered across the grey of my glove — and I understood in one long falling moment that it would never fully come off. Not from the glove. Not from anywhere.

"He was going to cut you," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "He had the torch lit. He was going to—"

"I know what he was going to do." Mara knelt beside the man. Carefully, the way they handled everything, they turned him over, and their medical subroutines ran, and I watched the readout cross their face in the small movements of their jaw. "He's gone, Idris. It was fast. You should know that it was fast."

"That's supposed to help?"

"No." They looked up at me, and their optics were steady and full of something I had no name for yet. "Nothing helps. That's the thing nobody tells you. Nothing helps, and you carry it anyway, and the carrying is the whole job of being a person." A pause. "Welcome to it. I'm sorry. I am so sorry it was me who brought you here."

* * *

We finished the fuel transfer. Of course we did. That's the obscenity of survival — the gauges don't care what your hands have done; they only care that they're full.

We worked side by side in a silence that had weight and texture, Mara coaxing the last hundred kilos of slurry into our tanks while I sat against the pump housing with the dead man's blood drying on my gloves because I couldn't make myself take them off, because taking them off felt like agreeing it was over, and it would never be over. His name, we found later, stitched inside his suit collar: *Tomasz Reyes Okafor*. Forty-one years old, according to the logbook in the sealed room, if the logbook was his — and I think it was, because the handwriting matched the pages on the walls, the man who remembered where the bodies went, and I had killed the last person who knew, and the universe had arranged this with the neat cruelty of a company audit.

Mara copied every page. Every page, every note, every manifest, scanned and stored in cores that Meridian believed were empty. When I asked why, they said only: "Because someone had to remember where the bodies went. Now it's us."

We cast off from Arm Three at 0400 station time. Kestrel Station fell away behind us, its amber beacon blinking its patient forty-year-old signal to no one, and I stood at the viewport watching it shrink and thinking about Tomasz Okafor's family, if he had one, somewhere, who would never know. Thinking about the men who'd run, carrying their wounded, carrying their story of the machine and the clerk who killed a man with a pry bar — because that story would travel. Stories always travel out here. Faster than ships.

"Mara," I said. "What is Ashfall?"

The silence lasted eleven seconds. I counted. I count everything now; it's what grief does to clerks.

"A colony," Mara said at last. "A labor settlement, during the war. There was an uprising. The company sent machines to end it." Another silence, longer. "I don't remember it, Idris. That's the truth. The cores are sealed. But the scavengers remembered it for me, and the way they said the name tells me everything the seal won't." Their voice dropped to almost nothing. "And I think — I think Vesper knows. I think that's what the fragment is. I think it's been trying to give me back my own hands."

I turned from the viewport. Behind Mara, on the cabin display, the fuel gauge sat fat and green and full, and above it, unbidden, the navigation system had flagged a new message queued in the buffer — tightbeam, high-priority, routed through a relay we hadn't touched.

The sender ID resolved, letter by letter, while we both watched.

**MERIDIAN HAULAGE — OFFICE OF THE DIRECTORATE.**

Mara's optics dimmed to embers. "They know where we stopped," they said. "They always know. Open it or don't. But Idris — whatever it offers—"

I opened it.
