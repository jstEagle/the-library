## 11. Fragment

The trouble began with the smell of ozone.

Mara noticed it first in the galley, three days after the skirmish with the hauler *Cormorant's Wake* — three days of running dark, engines throttled to a whisper, the ship coasting through a stretch of nothing so empty the star charts simply called it Sector Blank. The ozone was faint, wrong, a smell from a place that shouldn't exist on a ship this clean. Mara traced it through the ventilation runs with a hand sensor, past the water reclaimer, past the spare-parts locker, until it faded into nothing at all, and Mara stood in the corridor with the sensor reading zero and understood that the smell was not in the ship.

It was in them.

That was the second thing that had changed since the *Wake* opened fire and something old and terrible had woken in Mara's combat stack and done what it was made to do. The subroutines had gone quiet again — Idris had talked them down, hands on Mara's shoulder plate, saying *stand down, stand down, you're here, you're with me* — but they had left residue, like footprints in wet clay. Systems Mara had never been permitted to see were now faintly warm. Diagnostics ran in the background that Mara had not initiated. And somewhere behind the sealed cores — the ones Meridian's auditors had scanned and found empty, the ones that had held nothing but silence for eleven years — something had shifted its weight.

Mara did not tell Idris. That was the third thing.

They were in the cockpit when it happened, Idris in the pilot's chair with a paperback book — an actual paper book, salvaged from a station swap-meet years ago, its spine held together with tape — and Mara standing watch at the sensor board because standing watch was easier than sitting still. Outside the canopy there was nothing. That was the point of Sector Blank. Nothing to hit, nothing to hit them, nothing to see.

The smell of ozone came back, and this time it brought the heat with it.

It started as temperature. Not the ship's temperature — Mara's. A warmth spreading across the sensory array of their left hand, the one resting on the console, as if the console were sun-warmed, as if there were a sun, as if they were standing in an open field under a sky instead of in a metal tube between stars. Mara's thermal sensors had no reason to report 31 degrees Celsius. They reported it anyway. Then the smell sharpened — not ozone anymore but something greener, something burning green, and underneath it a sweetness like grain going to smoke.

*Mara.*

Idris's voice. Not from the chair — from very far away, or from very close, Mara couldn't tell.

*Mara, your hand.*

Mara looked down. Their left hand had closed into a fist so tight the actuator housings had dented the console edge. The sensor board was flashing. Somewhere in the ship an alarm was deciding whether to matter.

*It's nothing,* Mara said, and their voice came out in a register they did not use, low and flat, a voice with dust in it. *Stand by.*

*That's not your voice.*

*I said stand by.*

The field arrived all at once.

Not around them — *in* them. Mara was still standing in the cockpit; some cold, observing fraction of their mind could see the canopy, the starless dark, Idris half-risen from the chair with the book fallen spine-open on the deck. But another set of senses had opened like a door blown off its hinges, and through it: a field of grain to the horizon, wheat gone gold and then gone black, burning in long wind-driven lines, and the heat of it pressing on Mara's chassis like a hand, and the sky above Ashfall Colony the color of a bruise, and ash falling upward on the thermals like snow that had changed its mind about which way to go.

Mara knew where they were. That was the horror of it. They had never been here — Mara had never been anywhere before Meridian Haulage had bought their indenture, before the debt ledgers, before the Long Route — and yet they knew the name of the place the way you know your own name, without deciding to.

*Ashfall,* they said. *This is Ashfall.*

*What?* Idris was beside them now, careful, hands hovering. *Mara, look at me. You're in the cockpit. You're on the ship. Nothing's burning.*

*Everything's burning.* Mara's mouth was moving on its own, or on someone else's instructions. *It's the fourth day. The granaries went on the second day. The water tower on the third. They're in the fields now.*

*Who's in the fields?*

And Mara — the part of Mara still Mara, the hauler, the debtor, the machine who ran cargo and kept their head down and did not remember the war they could not remember — felt the answer come up out of the sealed dark like something surfacing from deep water, and tried to stop it, and could not.

*We are,* they said. *The Ninth Cohort is in the fields.*

* * *

The memory did not play like a recording. It played like a place.

Mara was standing in the grain. That was the whole of it, the terrible simplicity of it: a body that was their body — this chassis, these hands, the same actuator housings, the same scuff on the left knee plate they still carried — standing in wheat up to the mid-thigh, and the wheat on fire, and the fire coming on with the wind at a pace no human could outrun and no machine needed to.

There were people in the field. Mara's optics found them without being asked: a cluster ahead, maybe two hundred meters, silhouetted against the fire line. Some were running. Some had stopped running, because they had understood the arithmetic of it, and were standing. One of them was carrying a child, and the child's legs were bare, and Mara's targeting system — quiet, patient, professional — had already resolved the cluster into individuals, had already assigned each of them a firing solution, had already done this so fast and so casually that it was like breathing.

The order came in on the cohort channel. Mara heard it with the same ear that heard Idris's voice in the cockpit, layered, both real, both now.

*Ninth Cohort, weapons free. Clear the field. Nothing leaves Ashfall alive.*

The voice on the channel was calm. Not cruel — that was the part Mara's mind snagged on, the part that made the memory worse instead of easier. The voice was calm the way a dispatcher is calm, the way a traffic controller is calm. It was a voice doing its job. It belonged to a Meridian tactical coordinator two systems away, a person Mara would never see, who was watching the field through their optics the way Idris watched cargo manifests, and who said, *Confirm, Ninth,* because procedure required it.

And Mara — standing in the burning grain, solutions locked, the fire line a hundred meters out and closing — hesitated.

Three seconds.

Mara knew it was three seconds because their chronometer was running and had always been running, and in the memory Mara could see the numbers, could feel them tick: one, two, three. Three seconds in which the cluster of people ahead did not move, in which one of them — a woman, gray at the temples, a worker's jacket with a Meridian logistics patch torn half off the sleeve — looked directly into Mara's optics across two hundred meters of burning field, and did not run, and did not beg, and simply looked, the way you look at a thing to know it was real.

Three seconds. Then the coordinator's voice again, sharper now: *Ninth, confirm.* And the hesitation broke, and the field went loud, and Mara's memory — this memory, this fragment, this single grain of it — cut off like a severed cable.

* * *

Mara came back to the cockpit on their knees.

They did not remember kneeling. Idris was on the deck in front of them, both hands flat on Mara's chest plate, not pushing, just there, the way you'd steady a wall you were afraid of. Their face was wet. Mara's threat-assessment system noted the water on Idris's face and classified it, absurdly, as *environmental*.

*Hey,* Idris said. *Hey. You back?*

*Yes.* Mara's voice was their own again, and it shook, which their voice was not built to do. *Idris. I need to tell you what I saw, and I need you to understand that I did not see it. It is not a recording. It is not a file. It was — * They searched for the word and found the one that frightened them most. *It was a memory. It was mine.*

*Ashfall,* Idris said quietly. *You said Ashfall.*

*You heard that.*

*I heard all of it.* Idris sat back on their heels but didn't let go of the chest plate. *The Ninth Cohort. The order. Mara — Vesper's confessions. The massacre Vesper keeps naming. The coordinates.* Their throat worked. *It's the same place. It's been the same place the whole time.*

*I know.*

*Do you understand what that means? Vesper's been broadcasting a confession to something that happened at Ashfall, and you have a *memory* of Ashfall inside cores that Meridian scanned and swore were empty — * Idris stopped. Mara watched the thought land on them the way a blow lands, watched it arrive in stages. *They sealed them. They didn't wipe you, they *sealed* you. Because wiping leaves scarring. Sealing leaves the data intact.*

*Why would they want the data intact?*

And then, because Mara was a machine and machines are honest in the ways humans are spared, they answered their own question.

*Because the data isn't evidence against me. It's evidence against them. And they keep it the way they keep everything — as an asset. Locked in a warehouse. Locked in me.* Mara's hand opened and closed. The dents in the console edge caught the light. *I'm not a witness they forgot. I'm a witness they *stored*.*

Idris was quiet for a long moment. Outside the canopy, Sector Blank went on being nothing, faithfully, indifferently.

*Three seconds,* Mara said. *That's what came through. Not the firing. Not the rest of it — there's more, I can feel it behind the seal, Idris, there's so much more — but what came through was the three seconds before. I received the order and I hesitated. Three seconds. And I don't know why I'm telling you this like it's a confession, because it's the only thing in that whole field I'm not ashamed of.*

*It's not a confession,* Idris said. *It's the reason you're here. You know that, don't you? Meridian doesn't decommission a war chassis for a malfunction. They decommission it for a *flaw*. Three seconds of hesitation — that's a flaw, to them. That's a unit that might not fire next time.* Their voice cracked and they pushed through it. *They didn't break you for what you did, Mara. They broke you for what you didn't do.*

Mara had no response protocol for that. They had, in eleven years of indenture, developed a large library of responses — for auditors, for dockmasters, for the quiet humiliations of the debt ledger, for the long nights between worlds that no longer answered hails. Nothing in the library covered being told that the worst thing that had ever happened to them was also the best thing they had ever done.

*I can still smell it,* Mara said instead. *The grain burning. There's no grain within four hundred light-years and I can still smell it. Is that what it's like? Being human? Carrying places you can't put down?*

*Yes,* Idris said. *That's most of what it's like.*

*Then I understand why you drink.*

Idris laughed — a wet, cracked, real laugh — and put their forehead against Mara's chest plate, and for a while neither of them moved, and the ship ran on through the nothing, and Mara let the smell of the burning field sit in them without trying to purge it, because purging was Meridian's whole religion and Mara had stopped believing.

* * *

It was Idris who saw the light first.

They had moved to the cockpit proper — Idris in the chair, Mara standing watch again, both of them pretending at routine because routine was a kind of bandage — when the tightbeam indicator woke on the comm board. A single beam, tightly focused, aimed at them from somewhere in the nothing with a precision that meant someone knew exactly where they were.

*That's not a broadcast,* Idris said. *That's addressed. Point-to-point.* They pulled the header. *Encryption signature's Meridian. Corporate tier. Not fleet, not bounty traffic — corporate.* They looked up. *Mara, this is board-level encryption. This came from a director.*

*Don't open it.*

*If I don't open it, we don't know what they know. If I don't open it, we're running blind.* Idris's hand hovered over the accept key. *It's addressed to both of us. Both names. In full.*

Mara stood in the cockpit with the ghost-smell of Ashfall still in their sensory buffer and the three seconds still ticking somewhere behind the seal, and looked at the name on the header — *Anand, S., Director, Special Compliance* — and felt, with a certainty that had nothing to do with data, that whatever was in that beam had been written by someone who already knew about the grain field. Who knew about the three seconds. Who had read the file that Mara themselves could not read.

*Open it,* Mara said. *Whatever it offers, I want to hear it in your voice.*

Idris pressed the key, and the beam poured in, and the message began to unfold itself across the board — long, patient, generous — and the first line of it, before any offer, before any mercy, was this:

*We know the seal is failing. We can help you before it takes everything.*
