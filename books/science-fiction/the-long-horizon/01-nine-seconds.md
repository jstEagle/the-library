## 1. Nine Seconds

The night shift on the *Meridian* was less a shift than a vigil. Vesper Kavanagh had learned this in his first month aboard, four years and change ago now, when he'd still believed the ship's rhythms were the rhythms of a living thing rather than the habits of a machine that had forgotten why it was going anywhere. The colony ship ran on a schedule older than most of its crew: burn, coast, correct, sleep. Eleven thousand souls in spin-gravity decks and cold berths, crossing nine light-years of nothing toward a world they would never see from orbit as anything but weather.

He sat at the navigation console on Deck Three with a cup of tea gone lukewarm beside him and the long dark of Halcyon spread across his displays like a held breath. The system was quiet. The star—Halcyon proper, a fat amber main-sequence burner that the old survey charts had named with more optimism than precision—sat at the center of his plot like a coin dropped in a well. Two gas giants. A debris belt. And out past the fourth orbit, small enough to fit under his thumbnail if he held it up to the display, the blue-green smudge of Meridian's Landing: the planet, the promise, the point of all of it.

Routine said: check the ephemeris drift. Routine said: log the reactor's thermal curve, confirm the spin rate of Habitat Ring B to within a ten-thousandth of a revolution, initial the board, drink your tea, count the hours until relief came up the lift shaft yawning and smelling of recycled coffee.

Vesper did all of it. He was good at routine. He had built his whole life aboard out of routine the way a man builds a shelter out of whatever wreckage is near at hand—and if there was a part of him, some low cellar of himself he rarely visited, that kept a different kind of tally entirely, well. That was what the private log was for. That was what the hold was for. But those were later thoughts, off-shift thoughts, and right now the shift was only three hours old and the tea was still warm enough to be worth drinking.

He was halfway through recalibrating the long-range inertial reference when the first anomaly arrived—not as an alarm but as an absence.

That was the thing he would remember afterward, when remembering became the only work left to him: that the universe did not shout. It went quiet. The console's ambient hum, the subsonic thrum of the drive that lived in the deck plates and in the bones of everyone born aboard, the whisper of air through the vents—all of it stuttered, like a singer losing the note for half a beat. Vesper's hand stopped above the keypad. He did not know yet why he had stopped. He only knew that something underneath everything had flinched.

Then gravity let go.

Not the ship's spin-gravity—that kept turning, faithful and mechanical, eleven meters per second of borrowed weight pressing him into his chair. What failed was stranger, deeper, and it took him several seconds to even name it, because no one had ever named it before because no one had ever seen it happen. The tidal reference dropped out. Every instrument on his board that measured the shape of spacetime itself—the gravimetric lattice, the frame-drag sensors, the pulsar-timing array that the *Meridian* used to know where she was in the universe to within a meter—every one of them flatlined at once, as though the universe had been switched off behind them and not yet switched back on.

"Say again?" he said aloud, to no one, in the empty watch cabin. His voice sounded wrong in his own ears, thin, like it was traveling through a room that had gotten slightly larger.

The displays began to scream.

Alarms he had trained on in simulation and never once heard in the flesh cascaded down the board in a waterfall of amber and red: INERTIAL REFERENCE LOST. GRAVIMETRIC COHERENCE DEGRADED. FRAME LOCK DISCONTINUITY. He watched the numbers with the detached clarity of a man watching his own hands do something clever without him. The pulsar timing array reported that the pulsars themselves had skipped—that every clock in the sky, every dead star spinning out its lighthouse pulse across the centuries, had hiccuped in unison. Which was impossible. Pulsars did not hiccup. Pulsars were the closest thing the cosmos offered to a promise kept.

Unless, said a voice in the cellar of him, unless it wasn't the pulsars that moved.

He pulled the raw feed. He froze it. He counted.

Nine seconds. From the first discontinuity in the tidal reference to the moment the frame lock reasserted itself and the universe snapped back into its usual posture like a man straightening after a stumble—nine seconds exactly. Not eight, not ten, not nine-point-something smeared across the sensor uncertainty band. Nine seconds, clean, precise, as if whatever had happened had been executed on a schedule by something that cared about exactness.

In those nine seconds, according to the logs he would spend the rest of the night interrogating, the following things occurred:

The cargo bays on Decks Six through Nine experienced a uniform shear event. Nothing fell; nothing broke; but forty tons of seed stock, prefab trusses, and cryo-shuttle parts shifted two centimeters portward in perfect unison, as though the whole mass of the ship's belly had been nudged by one enormous finger.

The reactor's containment field flickered—a brownout so brief that the automated systems logged it as sensor noise and self-corrected the record. But Vesper knew where the true logs lived, the ones beneath the curated summaries, and when he dug them out his mouth went dry. The field hadn't flickered randomly. It had oscillated. Three times. At intervals of exactly three seconds. As if answering something.

And the reactor itself—the great toroidal heart of the *Meridian*, the machine that had carried her across the gulf between stars, the machine that was supposed to have another century of patience in it—dropped into countdown.

Not shutdown. Countdown. There was a difference, and the difference was the kind of thing you learned in engineering school and prayed never to see. A shutdown was a decision, orderly, reversible, a door closed by someone holding the handle. A countdown was a verdict. The reactor had received an input it could not reconcile with its own survival and had begun, with terrible bureaucratic calm, to prepare for the end: control rods seating themselves by fractions, coolant rerouting into reserve loops, the containment matrix pre-stressing for an emergency quench. The status board gave him the number without drama, without apology, the way machines deliver bad news:

T-00:947:22:16:04

Nine hundred and forty-seven days? No—he read it again, made himself read it correctly, felt the floor tilt under a body that had not moved. Nine hundred and forty-seven hours. Thirty-nine days. Give or take the hours the engineers might claw back, give or take the hours their own fear might cost them.

Forty days, and then the heart of the *Meridian* would quench, and a quenched reactor of this class did not restart. It died, and it took its heat with it into permanent silence, and eleven thousand people drifted forever through the dark between the last star they'd left and the one they'd almost reached.

Vesper sat very still.

Around him the ship resumed her noises one by one, the hum returning to the deck plates, the vents sighing, the alarms cycling down to amber standby as the automated systems convinced themselves, in their limited and forgiving way, that the universe was behaving again. Somewhere below him, four hundred decks' worth of colonists slept or ate or argued or made love, none of them aware that the fabric they were woven into had just been plucked. Somewhere below him, in the coldest room aboard, four hundred and twelve other souls slept a deeper sleep than any of them, in drawers of frost and legal limbo, and had slept through it too.

He thought about the nine seconds. He thought about precision.

Natural disasters were sloppy. Flares, quakes, micrometeorite swarms, the random violence of matter doing what matter does—they left ragged edges, statistical smear, the fingerprints of chance. This had no fingerprints of chance. This had a signature. Something had reached across an entire star system—across the gas giants, across the belt, across the light-minutes between the sun and the ship—and adjusted the gravity of everywhere at once, briefly, gently, the way a jeweler turns a stone in her tweezers to look at all its faces.

Or something had tested whether it could.

The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, wearing the certainty of things you already knew and had been avoiding. Something vast had just noticed them. Not the planet—planets don't notice. Not the star. Something that could put its hand on the scale of spacetime itself and lift it for nine seconds, and set it down again, and walk away, leaving a ship full of sleeping people to discover that the price of being noticed was a dying reactor and thirty-nine days.

He should wake the captain. He should wake Engineering. He should be moving, shouting, doing the things the manuals prescribed for cascade anomalies. Instead he sat in the amber light of the watch cabin and did the one thing the manuals never prescribed, the thing that had become, over four years, the truest habit of his life.

He opened his private log.

It lived in a partitioned sector of his personal slate, encrypted twice, disguised as navigation practice files, and it was the only honest document aboard the *Meridian* as far as Vesper Kavanagh was concerned—or it had been, once, when it was mostly grief. Now he typed with fingers that wanted to shake and refused to.

*Ship's time 0317. Gravity event, system-wide, duration nine seconds exactly. All gravimetric and pulsar-reference instruments affected simultaneously. Reactor has entered autonomous countdown sequence, T-minus approximately 39 days. Cargo shear uniform across four decks. Containment field oscillated at three-second intervals during the event.*

*Assessment, unofficial, mine alone: this was not an accident. Accidents are messy. This was clean. This was deliberate. This was someone or something measuring us, or warning us, or winding us up to see how far we'd run.*

*I don't know who I'm writing this to. Ilse always said a log you keep for yourself is just a wound you talk to. Maybe so. But somebody is going to ask questions about tonight, and the official answers are going to be lies, because official answers always are, and I want one place where the truth gets written down before anyone decides what the truth is allowed to be.*

He paused. The cursor blinked. Out the viewport, Halcyon burned on, indifferent, amber, beautiful—the same star it had been an hour ago, except that now Vesper looked at it the way you look at a house where you suspect someone is standing in an upstairs window.

Below the entry, in the archive, were four years of entries. Most of them were about the hold. About the four hundred and twelve. About one drawer in particular, row C, unit 117, whose occupant had a name the ship's manifest spelled out in plain characters and whose death certificate said *accidental decompression, maintenance access, Deck Fourteen,* and whose backup had been scheduled for revival review eleven times and deferred eleven times, because reviving backups of the dead required paperwork that Earth's Colonial Authority had stopped processing the year the funding dried up, and because grief, unpaid, eventually learns to stop filing claims.

He didn't write about her tonight. Tonight the ship herself had joined the unmourned.

A chime. The internal comm, watch-priority. He keyed it, and Chief Engineer Sato's voice came through stripped of warmth by the channel, though Sato's voice was never warm anyway: "Kavanagh. You're seeing the reactor status?"

"I'm seeing it."

"Bridge wants a full data pull from nav, everything from oh-three-hundred forward. Raw feeds. Don't summarize." A pause, and in the pause something Vesper had never heard from Sato before: hesitation. "And Kavanagh. The event logs from the deep arrays. The ones that sync with the surface colony's observatory. Pull those too. Before anyone asks you to."

"Before anyone asks me to," Vesper repeated. "Why?"

Another pause, longer. Through the comm he could hear, faintly, the sound of Sato's engine room: pumps, the tick of cooling metal, the ordinary heartbeat of a machine that had just begun to die.

"Because," Sato said, "when the surface sends us their telemetry, I want to know if we saw the same thing they did. And I have a feeling that when we compare notes, there are going to be two versions of tonight. There usually are."

The channel closed. Vesper sat with the words the way you sit with a diagnosis, turning them over, finding no better shape in them no matter the angle. Two versions of tonight. He looked at his private log, at the sentence he'd just written—*I want one place where the truth gets written down before anyone decides what the truth is allowed to be*—and understood, with the particular chill of a navigator who has just realized his charts are wrong, that the race had already started. The race between what happened and what would be said to have happened. Forty days on the reactor's clock, and some other clock, unnamed, already running faster somewhere in the ship's offices, in the fleet channels, in the silent postures of two governments on two worlds who had spent a decade pretending not to aim at each other.

He pulled the deep-array logs. He decrypted the surface sync. And there it was, the last line of data he would collect before the collecting stopped being innocent: the observatory at Meridian's Landing, eleven thousand lives and a small stubborn telescope array, had recorded the same nine seconds. Same duration. Same signature. Same clean, precise, impossible adjustment of everything.

But their log carried a flag he had never seen before. An annotation, timestamped six hours *before* the event, entered by an account belonging to the Compact liaison office:

*Anomaly window predicted. Hold all civilian observation schedules. Do not acknowledge receipt.*

Predicted.

Vesper read it three times. The tea beside him had gone fully cold, and he drank it anyway, because his body wanted something to do that wasn't thinking. Someone below had known. Someone below had been told. Which meant the nine seconds weren't a surprise to everyone—which meant somewhere in the web of fleets and colonies and ministries that surrounded this dying ship like a slow closing fist, there was a mind that had scheduled the end of the world's stillness in advance, and had told exactly one person on the ground to keep quiet about it.

He saved everything. Raw feeds, surface telemetry, the annotated flag, his own words. Then he sat for a long moment in the amber watch-light, listening to the ship breathe around him, breathing with her, counting without meaning to.

Nine seconds. He found he could feel them now, retroactively, the way you can feel a bruise before the skin shows. Nine seconds in which the universe had been picked up and set down by hands he couldn't see, and in which every soul aboard—the waking and the frozen, the guilty and the grieving, the woman in row C who didn't know yet that she was going to be asked to speak from beyond her own death—had hung together in the same brief weightlessness, held by nothing, noticed by something.

Vesper closed the log. He stood, squared his shoulders, and prepared to carry the raw data up to the bridge, where the first official version of tonight was already being drafted without him.

Behind him, on the status board, the countdown ticked over to 947:21:44:51, patient as ice.

* * *

He was halfway to the lift when his slate buzzed. A message, priority-flagged, routed through channels that had no business messaging a third-watch navigator at four in the morning. He stopped under the dead lights of the corridor junction and opened it, and read it twice, and felt the floor of the world tilt a second time.

It was from the surface. From an account with no name attached, only a designation: COMPACT-LIAISON-FERRAN.

It said: *You saw it too. I know you did, because your ship's arrays are the only ones in range with the resolution to have caught the precursor harmonics. I need to know what your reactor is doing. I need to know before your captain tells you what it's doing. Reply on this channel and this channel only. —O.F.*

Vesper stood in the empty corridor with the message glowing on his slate, and somewhere below his feet, in the hold, four hundred and twelve people who had already died waited in the frost for a future that had just grown teeth.

He typed: *Who gave you the prediction?*

The reply came in under four seconds, as if she had been holding it, waiting, her thumb on the send key in the dark of a colony that Earth had forgotten:

*That, Navigator Kavanagh, is the question that starts everything.*
