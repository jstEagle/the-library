## 8. Waking the Dead

The thaw took eleven hours, and Vesper spent every one of them in the hold.

He told himself it was monitoring. Chief Engineer Sato had given him a tablet with the revival telemetry on it—cortical reintegration percentages, synaptic density curves, a dozen graphs that meant nothing to him and everything to the two med-techs who had drawn the short straw of the night shift—and he watched the numbers the way a man watches a tide gauge, as if attention alone could hold the water back. The truth was simpler and uglier: he could not be in his cabin. In his cabin there was silence, and in silence there was Ilse's voice, three years dead, saying his name in the memory he had worn smooth as river stone.

So he stood in the cryogenic hold among the four hundred and twelve, in the blue-white light that made every face look drowned, and he watched the one cradle at the end of Row C do the thing he had spent three years telling himself was impossible.

Frost crawled backward across the canopy. The gel that had held her drained away in slow amber threads. And beneath it, unchanged and impossibly unchanged, was her face—the strong jaw, the small scar above her left eyebrow she'd gotten falling out of a maintenance ladder in the Meridian's second year, the particular set of her mouth that meant she was dreaming about something she intended to argue with when she woke.

*Degradation is real,* Sato had warned him, the last thing she'd said before leaving him alone with the cradle. *The imprint was stored cold for thirty-one months past its rated window. Expect gaps. Expect confabulation. Expect her to be someone adjacent to who she was.* And then, because Sato was Sato and never left a wound unopened: *Expect her to know what you did.*

The med-techs had withdrawn to the antechamber at his request. He had told them it was for the subject's dignity. It was for his.

At 0437 ship time, the canopy hissed open, and Ilse Vann took her first breath in three years.

It was not graceful. She convulsed once, hard, her spine bowing off the cradle's liner, and the monitors shrieked a warning that Vesper silenced with a hand that did not shake, which surprised him, because everything else in him was shaking. Then she breathed again, and again, and her eyes opened—not all at once but in stages, the lids fluttering like something testing whether the world deserved to be seen—and she looked at the ceiling of the hold, at the pipes and the frost-rimed conduits, and said, in a voice like sand poured over glass:

"That's wrong."

"Ilse." His own voice came out as a stranger's. "You're aboard the Meridian. You're—"

"I know where I am." She sat up. It cost her; he could see it in the tendons of her neck, in the way she gripped the cradle's edge as if the ship might pitch. But she sat up, and she turned her head, and she looked at him with eyes that were hers and were not hers, and she said: "The gravity's wrong. It's a half-tenth heavy on the port side. The compensators are running a bias correction they shouldn't need." She frowned, and the frown was so exactly hers that his chest hurt. "And you're standing four meters away, which is wrong too. You never stood four meters away from me in your life."

"I didn't want to—"

"Overwhelm me." She said it flatly. "That's what the manual says, isn't it. Revival protocols. Give the subject space." She looked down at her own hands, turned them over, examined the nails, the lines of the palms, as though checking them against a memory. "Vesper. How long."

"Three years."

Something moved behind her eyes—not grief, not yet, grief required the context she was still assembling, but its shadow, its scaffolding. "Three years," she repeated. "And I died how?"

And there it was. The question he had rehearsed for eleven hours and had no answer for that was not a lie, and he heard himself give the lie anyway, smooth and rehearsed and immediately, catastrophically wrong: "Accident. A coolant line in the hydroponics bay. It was fast. You wouldn't have—"

"Stop." She said it quietly, and the quiet was worse than a shout. "You just lied to me in the first sixty seconds. That's a record even for you."

Vesper felt the floor tilt under him, though the gravity was steady. "Ilse—"

"Here's what I remember." She held up fingers, and he watched her count, watched the degraded imprint work like a lamp with a failing filament, flaring and dimming. "I remember the lattice. I remember being in the neural lattice suite at night, which I was never authorized to be in, and I remember that I found something, and I remember—" Her hand dropped. The filament went dark. "I remember that it mattered. I don't remember what it was. I don't remember the end. There's a hole where the end should be, and it's not a natural hole, Vesper, it's a *cut*." She looked at him. "And I remember you. I remember you the way you remember a language you dreamed in. So you're going to tell me the truth now, whatever it is, because I have spent—" she gestured at her own body, at the cradle, at the four hundred and eleven silent cradles around them, "—whatever this is, I have spent it already, and I will not spend it on your mercy."

The hold hummed around them. Somewhere in Row A a compressor cycled, a sound like a held breath released. Vesper became aware that he was gripping the tablet with both hands, hard enough to whiten his knuckles, and he made himself let go.

"You died in the lattice suite," he said. "That's the truth. The official record says hydroponics. The official record is a lie. I helped—" his throat closed; he forced it open, "—I helped maintain the lie. I signed the accident report. I was told it was for the mission, and I was a coward, and I have hated myself for three years in a way I thought was love, and maybe it was both, and I don't know anymore."

Ilse said nothing for a long moment. The monitors traced her heart, steady now, steady as it had always been in argument, and he remembered that about her with a violence of tenderness that nearly buckled him: she had never once raised her voice, because she had never once needed to.

"Say the rest," she said.

"There's a countdown on the reactor. Forty days, less now. There are two fleets outside this system that want to go to war over a gravity failure nine seconds long, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I built a lie to stop them. A third-party saboteur. Evidence, transmissions, a whole architecture of proof. And it needs an author, Ilse. It needs someone both fleets will believe was positioned to see the truth. And I—" 

"And you reached for me." Her voice was not angry. That was the terrible thing. It was almost gentle. "Three years dead. Unmourned. Filed. And the first thing you needed me for was the mission."

"You're the only one I trust."

"No." She swung her legs off the cradle, and her bare feet found the deck plating, and she stood—unsteady, gripping the frame, but standing, facing him across the four meters he had kept like a moat. "You're the only one who's *convenient*. There's a difference, and you know it, because you've always known it. You didn't wake me because you trust me. You woke me because I'm dead, and dead people don't get to say no."

The words landed the way she had always been able to land words—precisely, in the soft tissue, where the armor wasn't. And the worst part, the part he would carry into whatever came next, was that she was not entirely wrong, and he had known she would not be entirely wrong, and he had woken her anyway.

"Say no, then," he said.

She tilted her head. The drowned-blue light moved across her face. "Is that what you want?"

"No. I want your help. I need it. The reactor, the fleets, the colony below—there are eleven thousand people on Meridian's Landing who are going to burn if this goes wrong, and I have run out of honest ways to save them, and I hate that sentence, Ilse, I hate every word of it, but there it is." He heard his own voice crack and did not repair it. "I woke you to use you. And I woke you because I couldn't stand the hold anymore. And I woke you because I loved you and I never said it while there was time to say it. All of those are true at once. I'm not going to sort them for you and hand you the clean one."

For the first time since the canopy opened, something that was almost a smile touched her mouth. It was brief, and it was sad, and it was the most familiar thing he had seen in three years.

"That," she said, "is the first honest thing you've said. It's awful. Keep doing it."

She took a step. Then another. Her body remembered walking before her mind remembered wanting to, and she crossed the four meters and stood in front of him, close enough that he could see the fine tremor in her jaw, the cold still leaving her skin, and she looked up at him with the eyes he had grieved and said:

"Here are my terms. Not requests. Terms."

"Ilse—"

"You'll hear them." She held up one finger. "One. You tell me everything. Every lie you've told, to the fleets, to the crew, to yourself, in order, from the beginning. I don't work inside a lie I can't see the walls of." A second finger. "Two. When this is over—when the reactor is fixed or the ship is dead or the fleets have burned each other out of the sky—the truth comes out. All of it. The lattice, my death, the saboteur, your part in it. Not eventually-meaning-never. Eventually-meaning-a-date. You'll write the date down and I'll hold you to it." A third finger, and here her voice did change, dropped into something quieter and harder, bedrock under topsoil. "Three. If it comes to a choice between the lie surviving and the truth surviving, you come to me first. You don't decide alone. You've decided alone your whole life, Vesper, and look where the ship is."

The hold was very quiet. Four hundred and twelve people who could not object to anything listened to them negotiate over the body of the past.

"And if I refuse?" he asked.

"Then I'll stand in this hold and tell the truth to whoever walks in. Sato. The med-techs. The first ensign who comes looking for you. I'm a dead woman, Vesper. I have nothing left to lose, and that makes me the most dangerous person on this ship." She said it without heat, as a statement of engineering fact, and he believed her completely.

He looked at her for a long time. He was aware, distantly, of the countdown running in the reactor, of the two fleets taking their mirrored positions out in the dark, of the colony below turning through its short night, and all of it felt suddenly very far away, the way the world goes far away when the only real thing in it is standing in front of you asking you to be better than you are.

"Agreed," he said. "All three. I'll write the date down tonight."

"Tonight," she agreed. "And Vesper—" She reached out and took his hand, and her fingers were cold, and he did not pull away, and he understood that this too was part of the negotiation, a down payment, a show of good faith across a table where everything else was still in dispute. "I don't remember loving you. I remember that I did, the way you remember a proof without the steps. It might come back. It might not. The imprint is damaged, and I can feel the damage, it's like standing in a house where the walls are painted the wrong color." Her grip tightened, cold and strong. "So don't you dare grieve me in advance. Don't stand there deciding I'm already gone. I'm here. I'm *here*, and I'm furious, and I'm on your side, and those are three different things, and you're going to have to hold all of them at once. That's the price of what you did."

"Yes," he said. It was all he had. It was, he suspected, all she wanted.

She let go of his hand and looked past him, down the long blue aisle of the hold, at the cradles, at the four hundred and eleven sleepers in their legal limbo, and her expression shifted into something he recognized from a hundred briefings, a hundred watches—the navigator's look, the look that took in a system and saw the currents under it.

"How many?" she asked.

"Four hundred and twelve. Including you."

"Who else knows I'm awake?"

"Sato. Two med-techs under seal. Reyes thinks I'm reviving a 'technical consultant.' The fleets think the saboteur is real." He hesitated. "Ilse—when you're strong enough, there's something you need to see. The transmissions. The saboteur file. It's—" 

"It's me," she said.

He stared at her.

She turned back to him, and now the smile was fully there, and it was the smile he remembered from the night she'd caught him falsifying a fuel ledger to cover a subordinate's mistake—not the falsifying, but the catching, the terrible gentle delight of a person who has just understood you more completely than you understand yourself. "You said the lie needs an author both fleets will believe. You needed someone with my clearances, my access, my position. You didn't write a saboteur, Vesper. You wrote *me*. You've been impersonating me for—" she did the arithmetic without pausing, the navigator's gift, "—weeks. I've been dead for three years and somehow I've been committing treason for a month." She shook her head slowly. "I woke up and the first thing I learned is that I'm the most wanted woman in the Halcyon system."

"I can undo it," he said, and heard the hollowness in his own voice. "If you want. I'll tell the fleets the saboteur is dead. It'll cost us—"

"It'll cost us everything, and you'd do it, and that's the only reason I'm going to say what I'm about to say." She looked at him steadily. "I'll do it. I'll be your saboteur. I'll be the ghost in your machine. But I want to see every word you've put in my mouth first. Every transmission. Every forgery." Her eyes went briefly, terribly, to the cradles around them, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Because if I'm going to be a lie, Vesper, I want to be a lie that's load-bearing. I want to know exactly what's resting on me. And I want to be there when it comes down."

* * *

They walked out of the hold together at 0612, past the rows of the unmourned, and Ilse stopped once—at Row C, at the cradle she had just left—and read the tag on its frame. *VANN, ILSE MARGARET. STATUS: DECEASED. DISPOSITION: PENDING.*

"Pending," she said. "Three years of pending."

"The Compact and the Concord can't agree who has jurisdiction over the remains. So they stay."

"So they stay." She touched the frame once, lightly, the way you'd touch a grave marker you weren't sure was yours, and then she walked on, and Vesper followed her out, and the hold sealed itself behind them with a sound like a book closing.

In the corridor outside, under honest yellow light, she stopped again and looked at him with an expression he couldn't read, and said, "The gravity bias. Port side, half a tenth. I wasn't wrong about that, was I?"

He felt the question go through him like a needle finding a nerve. "No," he said carefully. "You weren't wrong. The compensators have been running a correction since the nine-second event."

"Since the *event*." She was quiet a moment. "Vesper. I spent two years calibrating this ship's mass distribution. I know her the way you know your own heartbeat. A gravity bias that appears suddenly, port side, after an anomalous event—that's not damage. Damage doesn't have a *signature*. That's a redistribution. Something moved, or something turned on, and the ship is compensating around it." Her eyes had gone sharp, fully awake now, the degraded imprint burning clean at its core. "Where is the lattice suite relative to the port compensators?"

The question landed in the silence between them and did not stop echoing.

"Directly beneath," he said.

Ilse Vann, three years dead, stood in the yellow-lit corridor of the ship that had killed her, and smiled the way people smile at the scene of an accident they've just realized they caused.

"Then before you show me your forgeries," she said, "you're going to show me what's under the floor."
