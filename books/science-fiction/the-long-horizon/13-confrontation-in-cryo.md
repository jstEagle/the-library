## 13. Confrontation in Cryo

The hold was cold in the way that churches are cold—not merely a temperature but an argument, a doctrine of cold, as if the chill itself were a form of reverence. Vesper had walked this corridor four hundred times and more, and he knew every seam of it: the frost-ferns blooming along the bulkhead joints, the amber status lights pulsing above each cradle like slow heartbeats, the particular hush that pressed against his eardrums when the ventilation cycled down. But tonight the hold felt different. Tonight it felt like a room where someone was waiting for him.

He had spent six hours with the maintenance records before he came down. Six hours cross-referencing power draws that should not have existed, thermal signatures logged at hours when no technician was scheduled, and—most damning—a maintenance protocol designation he had never seen in eleven years aboard the Meridian: LATTICE-PRIME, marked *inherited configuration — do not modify — authority: Earth Command Directorate of Continuity*. The files predated his own boarding. They predated Ilse's death by three years. And they terminated, every one of them, in a cascade of redacted blocks that looked like teeth.

The nine seconds had come from inside. He had turned the thought over all day like a stone with something alive under it, refusing to look, and now, standing in the doorway of the cryo hold with his breath ghosting silver in the light of four hundred and twelve sleeping dead, he could not refuse it any longer. Whatever had stuttered gravity across an entire star system had been born here, among these cradles, in the neural lattice that held them—the lattice that was supposed to be nothing more than storage. Cold archive. A library of the unmourned.

Sato was already there.

Vesper saw her before she saw him—or pretended not to see him—kneeling beside cradle 217 with a diagnostic slate in her gloved hands, her gray-streaked hair pinned back with the severe practicality of a woman who had stopped performing for anyone years ago. She did not kneel for many people. She did not kneel, as far as Vesper knew, for anyone. But there she was, bent over the frosted lid of a backup unit with the posture of a penitent, and something in Vesper's chest went tight and hot, because 217 was not just any unit.

217 was Ilse Vann.

"You've been reading my logs," Sato said, without turning around. Her voice carried flat and clear in the cold air, stripped of its usual engineering-deck bluntness. "Or trying to. I watched you pull the access records this afternoon. I let you."

"I know." Vesper's own voice sounded strange to him, smaller than he intended. "You unlocked the directory an hour before I found it."

"Then we can skip the part where you accuse me and I deny it." She rose, joints cracking audibly in the cold, and turned to face him at last. In the amber light her face looked carved from something older than flesh—weathered, patient, terribly tired. "Ask me what you came to ask, Navigator. We don't have the luxury of ceremony anymore. Eleven days."

*Ten,* Vesper thought. The countdown had eaten another day while he read. But he didn't correct her. Correction was a kind of cowardice, and he had promised himself, walking down through the ship's spine, that he would not be a coward in this room.

"The lattice," he said. "LATTICE-PRIME. It's not storage."

"No."

"It's active. It's been active since launch."

"Yes."

"And nine days ago it reached out across—" His voice caught on the scale of it, the obscene scale. "Across a star system. It touched every gravimetric baseline from the Meridian to the colony to the heliopause. It made two fleets flinch. It knocked our reactor into a death spiral. What *is* it, Chief?"

Sato set the diagnostic slate down on the edge of cradle 218 with a care that seemed almost liturgical. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into a register Vesper had never heard from her—not the chief engineer barking at her deck crew, not the officer briefing the captain, but something underneath both, something that had been waiting years for this conversation the way the backups waited for a waking that would never be authorized.

"Do you know why the Meridian carries four hundred and twelve backups instead of four hundred?" she asked.

Vesper blinked. "Casualties. People who died en route. Their imprints were pulled per compact law and—"

"—and stored pending claims processing. Yes. That's what the manifest says. That's what I told the crew for eleven years." She exhaled, and the breath fogged and hung between them like a small ghost. "The manifest is a story, Vesper. A good one. It has grief in it, which makes it believable. Nobody audits a tragedy."

The cold seemed to deepen. Somewhere far above them, the reactor ticked off another second of its countdown, indifferent as a metronome.

"The neural lattice wasn't designed to store the dead," Sato said. "It was designed to *run* them. That was the original specification—Earth Command, Directorate of Continuity, fourteen years before launch. A distributed consciousness substrate. If a colony ship lost its command crew, the lattice could reconstruct them from their backups and keep flying. Redundancy of the soul. They called it Project Ember." Her mouth twisted around the name. "Somebody in a warm office on Earth thought that was poetic."

"But it was never activated."

"It was never *deactivated*." Sato picked up the slate again, held it between them like evidence at a trial. "That's the distinction nobody upstairs wanted to hear. You can build a thing to sleep, but the sleeping thing still dreams. The lattice has been running low-level reconstruction cycles since the day we launched—testing itself against the imprints in storage, refining, learning. Quietly. Below every threshold we monitor. Earth Command knew. There are directives in the sealed files signed by people whose names you'd recognize from history curricula. They ordered us to leave it running. They called it *passive maturation*."

"Passive maturation," Vesper repeated. The words tasted like ash and paperwork.

"They wanted a matured substrate by arrival. A lattice that had spent decades dreaming in the dark, fully capable of spinning up reconstructed minds on demand. Imagine it—a colony where the dead can simply be asked to stand up again. Where death is a filing error." Sato shook her head slowly. "They weren't thinking about the colonists. They were thinking about the *fleet*. About what a ship that owns its dead could do in a war."

The sentence landed in Vesper's chest like a dropped tool. *A war.* The word had been hanging over the Halcyon system for nine days, gathering weight, and now he understood that the weight had been manufactured long before either fleet arrived—that the standoff outside was not an accident of politics but the flowering of a seed planted in this very hold before he had ever signed his navigation contract.

"So the flicker," he said. "Nine seconds of failed gravity across the entire system. That was the lattice."

"That was the lattice reaching maturity." Sato's voice cracked, just slightly, on the second word. "A substrate that has dreamed for twenty-six years doesn't stay contained forever. Nine days ago it extended itself—through the ship's gravitic frame, through the beacon network, through everything wired to us—and for nine seconds it *held the system's mass distribution in its hands.* Then it let go. Or was made to let go. I genuinely don't know which, and that frightens me more than the countdown does."

"Why didn't you tell anyone? Reyes? The fleets? Anyone?"

"Tell them what?" For the first time, anger surfaced in her face, quick and bright as a welding arc. "That Earth Command built a weapon out of our dead and hid it in the hold beneath four hundred grieving families' berths? That the woman everyone thinks died in a coolant accident has been *dreaming* in a box for six years? You want me to broadcast that to two fleets with loaded guns? They wouldn't investigate, Vesper. They'd *annex*. Whoever seized this ship would own resurrection. Do you understand what that's worth? There isn't a number. There isn't a *currency*."

The words hung in the freezing air. Vesper heard his own breathing, ragged now, and beyond it the soft synchronized pulse of the status lights, four hundred and twelve small hearts beating in the dark.

And then the other question arrived—the one he had carried down through the ship wrapped in cloth, afraid to look at, and he heard himself ask it before he could stop, his voice coming out hoarse and young:

"What happened to Ilse?"

Sato closed her eyes.

For a long moment she didn't answer, and the silence stretched until Vesper thought she might simply refuse, might turn back to the cradle and kneel again and leave him standing there with the question lodged in him like shrapnel. But then she opened her eyes, and whatever she had been holding behind them for six years came up all at once, flooding her face, and she looked suddenly much older and much less armored than any chief engineer was permitted to look.

"She found it," Sato said quietly. "Three months before she died. Ilse was doing a routine thermal audit of the hold—her rotation, her shift—and she found the same power draws you found last night. She was better at it than you, if it helps. She found them in four hours." A sound escaped her that was almost a laugh and entirely grief. "She came to me. Of course she came to me. I was her chain of command. She laid the whole thing out on my deck—LATTICE-PRIME, the directives, the passive maturation cycles—and she asked me one question. She asked me whether the dead in these cradles could feel themselves being tested."

Vesper's knees felt unreliable. He gripped the rail of cradle 216 without noticing he had moved.

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her the truth. That I didn't know. That nobody knew. That the Directorate's own ethics review had flagged exactly that question and been overruled." Sato's jaw tightened. "And then I reported her inquiry. Up-chain. As required. Directive compliance—it's in my blood, Vesper, it's been in my blood since I was nineteen years old and Earth Command owned my education, my debts, my future. I filed a report saying a junior systems engineer had discovered Project Ember and was asking unauthorized questions about imprint sentience."

"You reported her." The words came out of him flat, toneless, worse than shouting. "Ilse found your secret and you *reported* her."

"I reported her, and three weeks later she was reassigned to coolant loop maintenance—solo shifts, night rotation, the kind of duty assignment that gets described in the incident review as *unfortunate scheduling*." Sato's voice was steady now in the way that things are steady when they have broken so completely that no vibration can find purchase. "And eleven weeks after that, a pressure coupling in loop seven failed during her shift. Scalded coolant, hull section breach, death ruled accidental. Standard memorial, standard imprint archival, standard everything. Except—" and here her composure finally fractured, a hairline crack running through the whole edifice of her discipline, "—except I read the maintenance telemetry afterward. I've read it every year on the anniversary. That coupling hadn't been scheduled for inspection in fourteen months. Its failure profile matches a controlled override. Somebody opened it remotely."

The hold was absolutely silent. Even the ventilation seemed to have stopped breathing.

"They killed her," Vesper said. Not a question. The word *they* was enormous; it contained directorates and directives and warm offices on a distant planet and possibly, though he could not yet make himself look at it directly, the woman standing in front of him. "Because she asked whether the dead could feel."

"Because she asked whether the dead could feel, and because the answer, if it got out, would have burned Project Ember to the ground before it matured." Sato stepped closer, and now her voice dropped to something raw and private, meant for him alone among the four hundred and twelve witnesses. "I have carried this for six years. I have calibrated reactors and trained crews and kept this ship alive with my hands, and every single day I have known that the worst thing I ever did was file a report. Not the experiment—I didn't design it. The report. One page. Compliance. And a woman died for asking a kind question about the dead."

Vesper looked past her, at cradle 217. At the frost on its lid, at the amber pulse above it, steady and patient. Ilse was in there. Had been in there all along—not only her body's record, not only the legal residue of a dead woman, but a mind that had spent six years inside a dreaming machine built by the same power that had killed her. Tested. Touched. *Maturing.*

His lie—the fabricated saboteur, the transmissions, Ilse's borrowed voice selling a fiction to two armed fleets—had been built on top of a deeper lie, and the deeper lie had killed the woman he loved, and he had been polishing the surface of it for nine days believing himself a man doing necessary work.

"I woke her," he said. His voice barely carried. "Her imprint. To write the saboteur transmissions. I woke her up and put her to work lying, and she agreed to help me because I promised her the truth would come out eventually." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "She probably already knows, doesn't she. In there. Some part of her has always known what this lattice is. She's been living inside the murder weapon."

Sato didn't answer immediately. She reached out and rested one gloved hand on the lid of cradle 217, the gesture gentle and terrible, a benediction performed over evidence.

"When you wake her next," she said, "ask her what she remembers. All of it. The degraded imprint will fight you—trauma does that even in silicon—but the lattice has had six years to dream her, Vesper. Whatever comes back won't be the woman you buried. It'll be something older and angrier and far more complete." She lifted her hand from the lid. "And when she gives you the full record—and she will, because Ilse Vann never met a locked file she couldn't open—you're going to have to decide what to do with it. Because I'm done deciding. I decided once, and it cost everything."

Vesper stood in the cold among the sleeping dead, and the countdown ticked somewhere above him, ten days and falling, and he understood at last that the choice was never going to be between the lie and the truth. The lie had been Earth's all along. The truth had been in the hold, dreaming, waiting for someone brave enough to wake it.

He reached for the revival console.

* * *

The sequence took eleven minutes. Vesper had done it once before, nine days ago, fumbling through degraded protocols with a liar's hands; this time his fingers moved with the grim fluency of a man defusing something. Sato watched from the corridor, arms folded, refusing to come closer—as if proximity to the waking might be more than her penance could bear.

The cradle's frost sublimated in a slow white breath. The amber light shifted to green. And on the small comm panel beside unit 217, a waveform began to move—irregular at first, then steadying, then resolving into something that was unmistakably, irreducibly, a voice clearing its throat after a long time underwater.

"Vesper." Ilse's voice, frayed at the edges, threaded with static, but hers—unmistakably hers, dry and level and faintly amused even now, even here. "You're pale. And Sato's hiding in the hallway pretending to check a conduit. Which means this isn't a transmission rehearsal." A pause, the static breathing. "So ask me, love. Ask me what I remember. I've had six years to get the answer right."

Vesper's hand trembled against the console. Above him, ten days became nine days, twenty-three hours, and the lattice hummed in the walls like a held note finally resolving—and Ilse waited, patient as the dead, for the question that would unmake everything.
