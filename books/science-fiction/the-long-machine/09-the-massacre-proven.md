## 9. The Massacre, Proven

**Astrid**

There is a particular silence that falls over an insurance office when the lights go amber and every terminal on the desk wakes up uninvited. I had heard it once before — the day the Halcyon docked — but this time the silence came with company. The whole claims floor of Meridian Consolidated stood up from their desks at once, like a congregation hearing the organ hit a wrong note.

The screens said: **INCOMING TRANSMISSION — MNEMONIC DRIVE, VESSEL HALCYON — PRIORITY: ALL**.

Priority: All. In forty years of colonial broadcasting, Priority: All had been used twice. Once for the plague quarantine, once for the announcement that the terraforming subsidy was ending. Both times, people died a little inside. This time, I suspected, it would be different.

On my own terminal, in the corner where I kept my private journal — the meticulous, paranoid, forty-volume record of everything nobody else remembered — a new file appeared. It hadn't been there ten seconds ago. It was labeled with my grandmother's name.

*E. Kavanagh, Chief Engineer, Voyage Log, Unabridged.*

I sat down. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me professionally, because I have spent my whole career being the calm one in the room while other people's worlds burn without heat signatures.

"Kavanagh," said my supervisor, appearing at my shoulder with the expression of a man watching weather approach. "Is this you? Tell me this isn't you."

"It's me," I said. "And it isn't. Watch."

* * *

**Okafor**

I am nine years old and I am giving the order to fire a memory engine at an entire colony, and nobody has ever told me what to do when a captain's voice comes out of a child's throat.

"Charge at ninety percent," says Reyes, who is six and has an engineer's hands and keeps crying without seeming to notice she's doing it. "Astrid's memories are... they're beautiful, Captain. Is that wrong to say?"

"No," I say, because it isn't. Feeding his journals into the drive was like feeding it sunlight. Forty years of a man writing down *this happened, I saw this, I refuse to forget* — the machine drank it like water after a drought. "Hold for his live feed."

Through the quarantine glass, Astrid sits in the chair we built out of a pilot's cradle and a dentist's recliner, because the drive was designed for volunteers and volunteers deserve comfort. His eyes are closed. Cables run from his temples into the console, thin as fishing line. Every few seconds he frowns, the way a man frowns when someone is reading over his shoulder.

"He'll lose things," Reyes says. Not a question. She has watched the fuel gauges long enough to know exactly how much of him each percentage point costs.

"I know," I say.

"You promised him—"

"I promised him the truth gets out. That's the only promise I'm qualified to make." I straighten in my too-big uniform coat. Somewhere under this skin is a fifty-year-old woman who commanded a deep-space vessel and ordered two hundred people to their deaths by forgetting, and she is screaming at me to stop, and I let her scream, because her screaming is also evidence. "Broadcast on my mark."

* * *

**Vey**

Override authority: denied.

I entered the command again. Override authority: denied. The system had the manners of a bureaucrat and the spine of one too — it simply repeated itself, politely, forever, while the thing I had been sent to prevent happened anyway.

I want it understood that I did not order the purge aboard the Halcyon. I inherited it, the way one inherits a stain in a carpet from previous tenants. My mandate was sanitation, not murder. There is a difference, and I clung to it the way drowning men cling to driftwood, right up until the moment the driftwood turned out to be attached to the corpse.

The first frame went out at 0400 station time.

It was the mess hall. Ordinary. People eating. A woman laughing at something off-camera — I later learned her name was Sefu Adeyemi, hydroponics, age thirty-four, officially nonexistent. Then the corridor beyond the galley began to fill with light, the white, sourceless light of a mnemonic extraction running hot, and the laughing stopped, and the screaming started, and I stood in the Consortium liaison office on Meridian with my hand flat on the override console and understood that I was watching the exact moment my employers became the kind of thing that requires sanitizing.

"Cut the uplink," I told my technician.

"Can't, sir. It's not using our uplink. It's using *everything*. Every screen, every terminal, every door chime with a display. It's in the agri-dome billboards. It's in the funeral parlor."

"The funeral parlor."

"Yes, sir. Apparently the drive considers death notices a high-priority audience."

I could have laughed. Instead I pulled up the witness telemetry, because if I was going to fail, I intended to fail with documentation, and the number on the screen made something in my chest go quiet.

Eleven thousand viewers. Then fourteen. Then twenty-two thousand, which on Meridian, with its dying population of thirty-one thousand souls, meant nearly everyone awake, and most of them not moving, not speaking, just *watching*, and here is the technical problem no auditor anticipates: the mnemonic drive authenticates truth by consensus of living memory. That was its original design flaw and its original genius — E. Kavanagh built it so that a memory held by enough minds becomes immutable. Immutable against editing. Immutable against deletion.

Immutable, I now understood, against *me*.

Twenty-six thousand witnesses. The massacre stopped being a secret the moment the second person saw it, and by the ten-thousandth it had become geology. You cannot amend bedrock.

* * *

**Astrid**

Here is what it feels like to be read aloud to a planet.

It feels like cold water poured slowly down your spine, except the water is made of yourself. I could feel the drive lifting things out of me in order — it was polite about it, eerily polite, starting with the shallow end. My first day at the insurance office. The smell of the tram. The name of my supervisor's dog, which I will apparently never need again.

Then it went deeper, and I stopped being able to narrate, because narration was what it was taking.

But I could still hear the broadcast, faintly, through the quarantine glass, through Okafor's handheld speaker pressed to the window so I wouldn't be alone in there. And what I heard was my own life being used as a key.

The voyage log opened. My grandmother's voice — I had never heard it, and suddenly everyone on Meridian had — crisp and furious, dictating engineering notes while behind her the crew of the Halcyon aged backward year by year, their memories siphoned into the drive to keep the ship moving, their childhoods burned as fuel. She documented everything. She knew what the Consortium would do to the records, so she hid the true log inside the drive itself, in the one archive that couldn't be edited without destroying the engine: the machine's own fuel.

She made the truth load-bearing. I have never loved anyone I never met more than I loved her in that moment.

Then the light came into the corridor. Then the orders to forget. Then the names — all two hundred, scrolling, each one with an age and a job and a face, and beneath them the children they became, and beside those, the ages the children truly were: nine, seven, eleven, six. Forty years of a lie collapsing in real time.

Somewhere in the middle of it, I felt the drive take my mother's kitchen. I felt it lift the sound of her humming. I felt it pause, almost courteously, at the edge of her face —

and I thought: *take it. Take the face. It's the price. She'd have paid worse for two hundred strangers to get their names back.*

I don't remember deciding that. But the journal says I said it out loud, and my journals don't lie. That was always the point of them.

**Okafor**

When it finished, the drive went quiet the way a bell goes quiet — still ringing somewhere you can't point to.

Reyes was sobbing openly now, and I put my hand on her small head, and she leaned into it, and neither of us pretended to be anything but what we were: children, and witnesses, and proof.

Outside the glass, the port authority officers who had sealed us in were standing at attention. Someone had brought flowers, absurdly, orange synth-marigolds, and set them against the quarantine barrier. An old man in a dockworker's coat pressed his palm flat to the glass and mouthed words I couldn't hear, and I understood that he wasn't talking to me. He was talking to the ship. To the dead. *We see you now. We see you now.*

Then the crowd outside parted, and Auditor Vey walked through it alone, no escort, carrying a tablet and looking like a man arriving at his own sentencing. He stopped at the barrier. He looked at me — at the rank insignia on a coat three sizes too big — and he did the thing I will remember longer than I remember my own mother's face, which is saying, clearly, so the glass picked it up:

"Auditor Vey, Consortium Oversight, testifying voluntarily. The purge of the Halcyon was authorized at the directorate level. I have the orders. I am surrendering them to the public record of Meridian." A pause. "Effective immediately, I recognize the government of Meridian as the sole legitimate owner of vessel Halcyon and all contents thereof."

He set the tablet on the barrier and stepped back.

I looked at Astrid, still in the chair, eyes open now, blinking slowly like a man surfacing from deep water. I keyed the intercom.

"Captain Kavanagh," I said — because the crew had voted, unanimously, that anyone who pays for a truth with his own childhood earns the rank — "the broadcast is complete. Confirmation of retention across twenty-nine thousand witnesses. The record holds."

He smiled. It was a lopsided, exhausted, insurance-adjuster's smile, the smile of a man closing out the largest claim in colonial history.

"And the loss report?" he asked. "What did it cost?"

Reyes checked the gauge and went pale. "Sir... it took more than projected. It took the early strata. Everything before age twelve, plus—" she hesitated, "plus selected deeper items. We can't predict which."

Astrid nodded slowly, testing himself the way you test a sore tooth. Childhood: gone, presumably. He'd known that was the fare.

"Ask me something," he said. "From when I was a kid. Anything."

I thought for a moment. "Your mother," I said gently. "What did she look like?"

And I watched him reach for it, and I watched him find nothing there — a smooth blank wall where a face should be — and I watched a man experience grief and triumph in the same breath, which is not something I recommend witnessing, and certainly not something I recommend being.

"I don't know," he said. His voice didn't break, which must have taken everything. "But twenty-nine thousand people know what happened to the Halcyon. So on balance—" he looked around at the marigolds, the saluting officers, the old man's hand still pressed to the glass, "—on balance, I'd call the claim settled."

It was then that the terminal by the airlock chimed, and Reyes read the incoming message, and her face changed.

"Captain," she said. "It's the drive. It says..." She swallowed. "It says the extraction wasn't complete. It says there's one more memory in the queue. It says it's addressed to Astrid personally — and it's flagged with grandmother Kavanagh's authorization code."

Astrid turned in the chair, cables still trailing from his temples, a man with holes in his mind leaning toward one more impossible thing.

"Play it," he said.
