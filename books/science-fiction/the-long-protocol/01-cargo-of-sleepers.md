## 1. Cargo of Sleepers

There are three ways to be invisible in the contested orbits of the Kestrel Verge, and I have practiced all of them until they stopped being techniques and became a personality.

The first is paperwork. You file clean manifests, pay your tariffs on time, and never once give a customs officer a reason to remember your face. Boredom is camouflage. The second is mediocrity: you fly a ship so unremarkable that even pirates skip it on principle, because raiding the *Mild Interest* would be an admission that you've hit bottom. The third—the one nobody teaches you—is that you stop existing in any way that can be cross-referenced. No debts. No lovers. No drinking buddies who know your mother's maiden name. You become a signature on a delivery chit and nothing else.

Ten years I've been no one, and I've gotten very good at it. Good enough that sometimes, in the hour before sleep, I have to check.

The *Patient Liar*—my ship, my coffin, my one honest lie since it's registered to a man who doesn't exist—was hauling forty tonnes of agricultural desalination membranes from Halcyon Reach out to the refinery platforms at Ceres Deep. It was the kind of cargo that made customs officers yawn, which was the entire point. Membranes don't get stolen. Membranes don't fund insurgencies. Membranes are the ballast of legitimate commerce, and legitimate commerce is the best cover ever invented.

"Transponder check," said the Ceres Deep approach controller, a voice like wet gravel through four layers of compression.

"*Patient Liar*, registry KL-4471-Theta, outbound from Halcyon, inbound with agri-membranes, forty-two point six tonnes," I recited, in the flat tone of a man reading his own obituary. "Requesting lane assignment."

A pause long enough to be insulting but short enough to be procedural. "Lane seven. Keep your burn profile tidy, Theta."

"Tidy as a funeral," I said, and cut the channel before he could decide whether that was charming or suspicious.

I keep my burns tidy because untidy burns show up on other people's scopes as personality. A ship that drifts half a degree off-lane is a ship someone wonders about later, over drinks, and wondering is how men like me end up in shallow graves or deep ledgers. So I flew lane seven like it had been drawn on my soul, matched velocity with the refinery tugs, and let the great grinding machinery of commerce swallow me whole.

Invisibility, for the record, is not peaceful. People imagine it as freedom—no name, no past, no one to disappoint. What it actually is, is a job you can never clock out of. Every conversation is a performance. Every kindness has to be weighed against what it makes people curious about. You can't afford to be interesting, and it turns out most of what makes a person worth knowing is exactly the stuff that's interesting. So you sand yourself down. You become smooth. And then one night you're eating reconstituted noodles alone at your own galley table and you realize you can't remember the last time anyone laughed at something you said because they wanted to, rather than because you'd calculated the joke to be maximally forgettable.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. That kind of thinking is for the dark hours. In the light hours there is work, and work is the other thing I'm good at.

* * *

Ceres Deep took eleven hours to unload, which gave me eleven hours to do nothing, which is the most dangerous cargo I carry.

I spent the first two in the cockpit running maintenance diagnostics I didn't need, because diagnostics produce numbers and numbers are a respectable thing to look at when you're avoiding looking at anything else. Then I ate. Then I did the thing I always do at stations, which is walk the ring corridor once, slowly, letting the crowd wash past me—a man of average height, average build, average everything, wearing a jacket chosen specifically because three thousand other men on this station were wearing the same one.

It was working, the way it always works. Nobody looked at me twice. A dockhand asked me to help steady a crate and forgot my existence before I'd finished helping. A tea vendor overcharged me by exactly the amount she overcharges everyone. I was a ghost with a credit chip, and I felt the particular satisfaction that ghosts feel, which is to say none, which is to say safety.

On the way back I passed the public notice board, because passing notice boards is part of the ritual—you scan them without appearing to, in case your own description has gone up overnight. There was nothing about me. There was, however, a salvage syndicate posting, glossy and officious, offering finder's fees for "anomalous signal events" reported along the outer relay chains. Some corporate watermark I didn't recognize. A comm address. A number with too many zeroes in it.

Anomalous signal events. Which is syndicate-speak for *we heard something we shouldn't have, and we'll pay you to tell us if you heard it too.*

I read it twice, memorized it, and walked on with the unhurried gait of a man who has definitely not just memorized anything. My heart was doing something unsightly in my chest, but hearts are private organs and I've gotten good at keeping mine that way.

Here's the thing about carrying a secret for ten years: you stop thinking of it as a secret and start thinking of it as furniture. It's just there. You dust around it. You don't open the drawer it sits in. But every so often the universe slides the drawer open an inch on its own, just to watch you sweat, and you learn things about yourself—mainly that under all the smoothing and sanding, there is still a man in here who is terrified, and the terror has excellent posture.

I got back to the *Liar*, sealed the hatch, and stood in my own airlock breathing the recycled smell of my own life until my heart agreed to behave.

* * *

Every ship has a hold the owner doesn't talk about. Mine is aft, behind the water reclamation plant, behind a door I have legally, morally, and spiritually classified as structural.

Six cradles sit in that hold. Medical-grade stasis units, top of the line, bought used from a defunct research concern and paid for in ways I don't itemize even in my own head. Each cradle holds a sleeper. Each sleeper has my face.

I have never woken them. I have never sold them. I have never told a living soul they exist, including—especially—the man whose name is on my falsified registry, because he doesn't exist either, and a fictional man can't be interrogated about cargo he doesn't officially carry.

How they came to be there is a story with a massacre in it, and I don't tell stories with massacres in them. Not tonight. Tonight they were just six heartbeats' worth of telemetry on a maintenance display, green and slow and patient, drawing power from my reactor like six quiet accusations.

I checked them the way I check them every few weeks: remotely, through the diagnostic bus, without going aft. Cradle one, nominal. Two, nominal. Three through six, nominal. Six faces like mine, sleeping behind a bulkhead, dreaming whatever copies dream—which is nothing, I tell myself, nothing at all, because if it were something I'd have to think about what, and I have built ten years of silence specifically so I wouldn't have to think about what.

The telemetry was fine. The telemetry is always fine. I closed the display and felt the specific relief of a man who has checked a lock he knows is locked, and hated myself for the checking, and will check again in three weeks anyway.

* * *

We cleared Ceres Deep at 0400 station time with a partial return load—refinery catalysts, boring as sin, perfect—and turned our nose toward the long empty stretch between the refinery belt and the Meridian gate. Nine days of nothing. Nine days of me, the ship, and the furniture.

I like the empty stretches. That's the official version, anyway, the one I'd give if anyone asked, though no one asks, because asking would require noticing me. The truth is more complicated: I need the empty stretches the way an actor needs the interval between performances. Out here there's no audience, no manifest to recite, no lane to fly tidily. Out here I can almost relax, except that relaxing is its own performance now, a role I play for an audience of one, and I've begun to suspect the one is the toughest crowd in the Verge.

Day two, I resealed the membrane pallets that didn't need resealing. Day three, I recalibrated the nav array and found it didn't need calibrating, and did it anyway, because hands that aren't busy start remembering things. Day four, I allowed myself the luxury of music—an old Vessic lullaby cycle I keep on a chip with no label, played at low volume through headphones so the ship itself never hears it. The colony's dead tongue, sung by dead voices, for an audience of one man pretending he isn't homesick for a place that burned down around his childhood.

Vessic. Say the word and you get blank looks across three systems; the colony was small, remote, and two centuries erased, its language surviving nowhere outside my skull and a handful of encrypted archives nobody's bothered to crack. I was born there. Everyone I was born among died there, or scattered, or became someone else, the way I did. When the last Vessic speaker besides me dies, the language dies again, quietly, in a cargo hauler's headphones, and honestly that might be the most fitting burial available.

I listened to the lullabies until my eyes stung, then put the chip away, then sat in the dark being proud of myself for putting it away, which tells you roughly what my standards for victory have become.

* * *

Day five, the emptiness started talking back.

Not literally—I want to be clear about that, because a man who hears voices in contested space ends up on notice boards of a different kind. But there's a quality to deep-range quiet that isn't really quiet. The background hum of the cosmos, the static between stars, the faint signatures of relays whispering to each other across distances that make human lifespans look like rounding errors. After enough years alone you develop a feel for it, the way old sailors developed a feel for weather. You know what the silence should sound like.

At 1912 ship time, day five, the silence sounded wrong.

It started at the bottom edge of my passive array—a carrier wave where no carrier wave should be. Faint. Directionless. Coming, when I finally got a bearing on it, from somewhere out past the Meridian gate, toward the old relay chains at the edge of dead space, where nothing broadcasts because there's nothing left out there to do the broadcasting.

My first instinct was professional: log it, flag it, ignore it. Ghost carriers happen. Solar weather happens. Equipment lies. The correct response to an anomaly is to be a man who files reports, and I am, when it suits me, exactly that man.

My second instinct arrived about four seconds later and wore a different face entirely, because the carrier wasn't random noise. Noise doesn't have structure. Structure means intent. And this structure was repeating—short bursts, long gaps, a rhythm I recognized before I consciously recognized it, the way you recognize footsteps on your own stairs.

Someone was transmitting in pattern-groups. Old ones. The burst-and-rest cadence of Vessic longband protocol, the format we used for colony-wide alerts, drilled into every child born inside the dome because the assumption, back then, was that someday we'd all need to know how to listen for each other.

I sat in my cockpit with my hands hovering over the console and did not touch anything for a long time.

The rational explanations lined up like well-behaved passengers. Coincidence. Interference harmonics mimicking an archaic format. Some hobbyist archaeologist playing with antique protocols. Any of these was infinitely more likely than the alternative, and I am a man who deals in likelihoods, who has survived ten years by never once betting on the improbable.

But the improbable kept transmitting. Burst, rest. Burst, rest. Patient as tide. And underneath the carrier, when I let the array drink deep and pulled the signal apart, there was a modulation layer—dense, structured, deliberate—that no solar flare in history has ever produced.

I should have deleted the logs. I should have wiped the buffer, filed a routine interference report with the Meridian authority, and flown on to the gate as the invisible man, the tidy man, the man whose heart stays private. That was the protocol. My protocol. Ten years of protocol.

Instead I sat there in the dark with a dead language knocking on my hull, and I thought about six sleepers behind a bulkhead, and a notice board with too many zeroes on it, and a drawer I never open sliding an inch wider on its own.

Burst, rest. Burst, rest.

It wasn't addressed to anyone. That was the worst part. It was a general broadcast, screaming into the dark the way my whole extinct world used to scream into the dark, hoping anyone at all was listening.

And some traitorous, buried, unsanded piece of me—the piece I'd spent a decade convincing myself I'd amputated—leaned toward it like a plant toward a window.

I killed the passive feed. I sat in the silence I'd restored, which no longer felt like silence, and I told myself I'd decide what to do about it in the morning.

I knew, even then, that I was lying. It's the one skill I never lost.
