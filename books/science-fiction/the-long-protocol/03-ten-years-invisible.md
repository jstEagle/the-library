## 3. Ten Years Invisible

The ship cost me eleven thousand credits and a name.

Not the same transaction. I want to be clear about that, because the version of the story I tell at dockside bars—when I tell any version at all—is that the *Marrow's Grace* was a steal and the paperwork was clean. Both lies. The ship was overpriced and smelled of mildew and someone else's cigarettes, and the paperwork was a crime committed in a back room above a noodle shop on Perdition Row by a forger who charged me extra because I flinched when he asked what name to put in the registry field.

"Any preference?" he'd said. Pen hovering. A small man with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair and the patient eyes of someone who had heard every kind of silence.

I had a preference. My preference was Zephyrine Coldharbour, born Vessic Reach, son of Ilse and Marek, certified navigator, second class, survivor of nothing worth mentioning. My preference was to keep it. But keeping it meant keeping the trail that led from my name to the evacuation manifests, and the manifests led to the inquiry board, and the inquiry board led to questions I could not answer without lying, and lying under oath is a different crime than lying on a form. So I said, "Something forgettable."

He wrote *Zeph Marrow*.

"That's not forgettable," I said. "That's just short."

"Forgettable isn't nothing," he told me, tapping the pen against his teeth. "Nothing gets flagged. Forgettable gets ignored. You want to be the fourth most boring man in any room." He slid the chip across the desk. "Congratulations. You were never anyone else."

I have thought about that sentence more times than I can count. It was meant as reassurance. It landed like an amputation.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about cutting off a piece of yourself: the phantom limb keeps working. Ten years later I still reach for the name the way you reach for a light switch in a house you've sold—muscle memory aimed at a wall that belongs to strangers now.

Vessic. Say it and people either blink or go quiet. The colony died two hundred years before I was born, except that's the official story, the one carved into the memorial plinths on the worlds that absorbed its refugees: *lost with all hands, cause undetermined, let us observe a moment of silence.* What actually happened was slower and stupider and involved men in good suits deciding that a colony three jumps past the shipping lanes was a rounding error. By the time I was born there were maybe four thousand of us left, clustered in the old habitat rings, speaking a language the rest of the arm had already filed under extinct, running a navigation guild out of habit and pride because navigating was the one thing Vessic had ever been better at than anyone else.

You grow up like that, you learn two vocabularies. There's the one you use outside—the trade cant, the docking protocols, the cheerful flat accent of everywhere-and-nowhere. And there's the one you use at home, low, fast, full of words for snowfall inside a dome and for the particular grief of watching your sun get demoted to a dot. Vessic has forty words for that last one. I knew all of them once.

I know maybe nine now. I ration them. That's not a figure of speech—I genuinely catch myself hoarding them, letting the others erode, because every word of Vessic I still carry is a word that can be recognized, and recognition is a debt collectors come to collect.

* * *

The first year I owned the Grace, I nearly gave myself away twice.

Once was in a chandlery on Ceres Annex, when a supply clerk used an old Vessic loanword for "manifest"—*skarnet*, which properly means "the list of what survived"—and I answered him in kind before my brain caught up with my mouth. He stared at me. Not hostile. Worse: delighted. "Haven't heard that since my grandmother," he said. "You Vessic stock? Thought you were all—" and he made a gesture that managed to convey both *dead* and *dispersed*, which is a talent. I told him I'd picked it up from a textbook. He nodded the way people nod at textbooks, and I walked out with my gaskets and my heart going like a reactor approaching criticality, and I spent the next week deliberately mispronouncing common words so my mouth would stop trusting itself.

The second time was worse, because it wasn't a slip. It was a choice.

A woman came aboard at Tycho Transfer looking for passage to the Belt, cheap, no questions. Standard freighter economics; I said yes. She was maybe sixty, heavyset, with the forearms of someone who'd done decades of actual work, and on her second day in transit she stood in my galley drinking reconstituted tea and humming. Not a song. A cadence. The counting-cadence Vessic navigators use when they're doing burn math in their heads—the old guild rhythm, *one-two-three-hold, one-two-three-hold*, the beat our instructors hammered into us so the numbers would land in time even when the comms were screaming.

She was humming my childhood.

I stood in the galley doorway with a wrench in my hand and did not say a word. She hummed for maybe thirty seconds, trailed off, looked up, and saw my face. Whatever she saw there made her set down the cup very carefully.

"You know it," she said. Not a question.

"I've heard it somewhere," I said, and turned back to the engine room, and spent the rest of the transit finding reasons to be elbow-deep in machinery whenever she was awake. When she paid at the far end she paused at the airlock and said, quietly, in flat trade-cant so there could be no mistake: "Whoever you're hiding from, I hope they don't find you. And whoever you're hiding from yourself—" she tapped her own temple "—that one doesn't stay bought."

Then she was gone down the ramp and I never learned her name, and I have thought about her roughly once a month for ten years, which is a frequency I do not examine closely.

* * *

Let me tell you how you bury a name, since apparently I'm the expert.

It's not one act. It's maintenance. Like rust-proofing a hull, except the hull is your biography and the salt water is everyone you've ever met.

First you kill the paper trail. I pulled my own records—not hacked, *pulled*, which took eighteen months of small requests through small offices, each one boring enough to be invisible, each one shaving a fact off my file until what remained was a person who existed mostly as gaps. Gaps are fine. Gaps read as privacy. What reads as suspicious is consistency—a man whose story never changes is a man who rehearsed it.

So I built inconsistency. Three birthdates, deployed according to context. Two homeports. A work history with deliberate holes where I claimed to have been "between contracts," which spacers accept the way farmers accept weather. I got fired from two jobs on purpose—one for drinking, one for insubordination—because a man with blemishes on his record is a man nobody looks at twice. The drinking one stung, because I had to actually drink, publicly, convincingly, for six weeks, and I come from people who consider drunkenness a failure of navigation.

Then you kill the face. Not surgically—I couldn't afford surgical, and besides, faces that have been worked on have their own signature. No, you kill the face with behavior. I stopped standing the way navigators stand. Navigators stand still. We're trained out of fidgeting young, because a still body makes for steady hands at the console, and after ten years of that training you carry it in your spine forever. So I taught myself to slouch. To shift weight. To gesture too much, the way haulers do, all elbows and impatience. It felt like wearing someone else's coat for the first year. Now it's just my posture, and the navigator's stillness is the stranger's.

And then—and this is the part I'm least proud of—you kill the connections. Not dramatically. Nobody threatened anybody. You just... stop answering. Slowly. A message left unread here, a birthday missed there, until the people who knew Zephyrine Coldharbour conclude, correctly, that he has become someone who does not answer, and they stop sending things into the dark.

There were exactly two people I kept, and I'll defend that to my grave. One was Sef Calloway, my partner from the guild years, who had known me under the old name and agreed—drunk, weeping, furious, in that order—to pretend otherwise. "You're dead to me," he told me, meaning it as a kindness. "Zeph died. You're some guy I drink with." It was the strangest eulogy I've ever received and the only one I've needed.

The other was not a person. The other was a sealed hold.

* * *

I should talk about the hold. I'm going to have to eventually, and this chapter of my life—as the forger would put it, the chapter where I was never anyone else—is where it starts.

When I bought the Grace, the broker mentioned, almost apologetically, that she came with "legacy cargo infrastructure." Six cradles, medical-grade, cold-sleep rated, bolted into a reinforced compartment aft of the main hold. Expensive equipment. Nonstandard. He assumed I'd strip it out for parts.

I told him I'd think about it.

What I didn't tell him was that I knew exactly what those cradles were for, because I'd helped design the specification, back when I was someone else, back when the Vessic Navigation Guild—four thousand people and a language—had voted, near the end, on a last protocol. The Long Protocol, we called it, though half the council hated the name for being dramatic. Its premise was simple and terrible: a navigator's skill lives in a pattern too deep to teach quickly, and a people can survive losing everything except their navigators, but cannot survive losing those. So the protocol provided for copies. Backups. Sleepers carrying the full architecture of a trained mind, held in reserve against a day when someone would need to steer home.

Six cradles. Six patterns. All of them mine.

I installed them myself, over three nights, alone, telling myself the whole time that I was preserving heritage. Archival. Responsible stewardship of a dying culture's last technology. I told myself I would never wake them, that they were a library, not a promise. I told myself a great many things during those three nights, and the thing I told myself least often was the true one, which was that I could not decide whether the copies were children, siblings, hostages, or corpses, and that every possible answer was unbearable, and that a man who cannot bear an answer can always choose not to ask the question.

I sealed the compartment. I set the environmental systems to autonomous maintenance and locked the controls behind a code that is, I will admit, a fragment of Vessic counting-cadence, because if I was going to be haunted I might as well be haunted efficiently.

Ten years. I have opened that bulkhead twice. Both times I stood in the doorway, looked at the six shapes under their frost-fogged lids—my face, six times, slack and dreaming—and closed it again without walking fully into the room. You can't amputate something that's still attached. But you can refuse to look at the joint.

* * *

Which brings me, in the roundabout way of a man who has had ten years' practice avoiding straight lines, to why any of this matters tonight.

Tonight I am sitting in the Grace's cockpit with a cup of tea going cold beside the console, running the numbers on a routine haul—agricultural parts, Halcyon Drift to the refinery stations, eleven days, pay adequate, attention required minimal—and I am thinking about the woman who hummed, and the forger with his pen, and the particular exhaustion of being the fourth most boring man in every room for a decade.

Because invisibility works. That's the joke. That's the punchline I keep not laughing at. It *works*. Nobody has found me. Nobody is looking. I have successfully become no one, and no one is safe, and safety turns out to taste exactly like nothing, every single day, on schedule.

There's a moment I remember from the evacuation—I'll get to the evacuation properly someday, or I won't, that's rather the point of having buried it—from the last shuttle off the last ring, when a guild elder named Ondrat put his hand on my shoulder and said, in Vessic, the words I have spent ten years pretending I don't understand: *"A navigator alone is a compass in a drawer. Remember what you're for."*

I remembered. That was the problem. I remembered perfectly well what I was for, and I decided I would rather be a compass in a drawer than a compass pointed at a massacre.

Ten years of drawers. Ten years of *Zeph Marrow*, hauler, unremarkable, between contracts. And it has held. It has held right up until ninety minutes ago, when the long-range array picked up a distress signal on a band that has been legally silent for two centuries, broadcasting in a language with nine words left in my head and forty thousand in its bones—

—and the signal stopped mid-phrase, hung there like a held breath, and then began again, and I have not yet translated the second transmission, because translating it will require me to listen to it, and listening to it in Vessic will require me to be, for the first time in ten years, entirely and dangerously myself.

The tea is cold. The window to answer is closing at a rate I can calculate precisely, because calculation is the one thing they never managed to cut out of me.

I'm reaching for the console. Phantom limb, working fine.
