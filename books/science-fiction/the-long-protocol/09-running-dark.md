## 9. Running Dark

There's a particular sound a ship makes when she's about to do something stupid. It's not a warning, exactly. Ships don't warn you; they just change the way they breathe. The *Mercy of Small Debts* had been breathing differently since I keyed the corridor into her nav stack, and I'd spent the last six hours pretending I couldn't hear it.

Nineteen days. That was the window. One viable jump corridor from here to a relay station squatting at the lip of dead space, and the math said the corridor closed in nineteen days whether I was through it or not. The math also said I'd need to burn through a customs cordon to reach the jump point in time, and the math, as usual, didn't care how I felt about it.

I felt fine about it. That was the strange part.

I'd spent ten years building a life out of not being noticed. Falsified registry, laundered transponder, a cargo manifest so boring it could put a customs officer into a light doze mid-inspection. I hauled water reclamation filters and agricultural drones and, once, four hundred kilograms of someone's grandmother's furniture across three systems, and every single job was chosen for the same quality: nobody looks twice. Invisibility as a business model. I was very good at it. I'd had to be. Being good at it was the whole point of being alive.

And now I was about to light my transponder off, point my nose at a cordon, and burn.

You'd think a man would need to be dragged to that moment. Kicking, maybe. Negotiating. I'd imagined it often enough in the dark watches—some future where the past finally caught up and I had to run for real, and in every version of it I was terrified. I'd rehearsed the terror so many times I'd worn grooves in it.

But standing on my own bridge with my hand hovering over the mooring release, what I felt wasn't terror.

It was relief.

That frightened me more than the cordon did, if I'm honest. Because relief means you've been braced for something for a long time, and I'd been braced for ten years without once admitting what I was braced *for*. A man who lives his whole life flinching eventually starts to mistake the flinch for a personality.

*Noted,* I told myself. *File it under things to have a breakdown about later, when there's vacuum between me and the consequences.*

I ran the pre-departure checks one more time anyway. Habit, not caution. Caution had already lost the argument.

* * *

The *Mercy of Small Debts* was a Meridian-class hauler, forty years old if she was a day, with a cargo spine long enough that I'd stopped walking the full length of it years ago. Six cradles sat in the sealed aft hold, and I had not opened that hold's inner door in four years, three months, and some days I didn't count. The backups slept behind that door with my face and my memories up to the moment of their copying, and I paid their life-support bill the way you pay rent on a storage unit full of things you can't look at.

I told myself the same lie I always told myself: they were cargo. Inert. A financial liability I hadn't yet figured out how to dispose of.

The lie was getting harder to hold. The signal had done that. Somebody out at the edge of dead space had spoken Vessic into the dark—Vessic, the language that died with the colony, the language that existed now only in my head and, apparently, in whatever was using my stolen childhood as an encryption key—and the signal had stopped the moment I answered it, and then started again, addressed to me by my birth name.

Zephyrine Vess-Coldharbour. A name I hadn't heard spoken aloud in a decade. Hearing it had felt like a hand closing around the back of my neck.

So. Running dark.

The customs cordon around Ceres Anchorage was standard issue: six picket ships in a loose sphere, automated scan gates, and a customs authority whose institutional personality could best be described as *bribeable but proud of it*. Normally I'd have filed a departure manifest, paid the fees, and drifted out through the commercial gate with a wave and a forged signature. That was the plan until yesterday, when Ilsa Voss—dockmaster, occasional ally, professional cynic—had commed me on a hardline and told me a salvage syndicate crew had been asking after ships that received anomalous signals.

Asking. With money. The kind of money that turns asking into finding.

"They're not official," she'd said. "But they're carrying official-looking paperwork, and the kind of men who carry fake paperwork tend to get real upset when you point that out. I can lose your file for maybe six hours, Coldharbour. After that, even I can't help you."

Six hours. That had been fourteen hours ago. I'd spent the difference loading, checking the corridor math a third time, and standing in front of the sealed hold door for eleven full minutes without opening it. Then I'd gone to the bridge and started doing the stupid thing.

* * *

Departing a station without clearance is less dramatic than the holos make it look. There's no alarm, at first. There's just a ship that was docked and then isn't, and a gap in the traffic feed where a transponder used to be, and somewhere a duty officer frowning at a screen and deciding whether it's worth waking anyone up.

The trick is to be boring for as long as possible.

I cast off with station-keeping thrusters only, a whisper of gas, the kind of maneuver that reads as a docking error rather than a departure. The *Mercy* drifted off her moorings at Dock Nine like a leaf letting go of a gutter. I let her fall for two hundred meters before I touched the thrusters again, easing us down and away from the station's plane, into the cluttered volume where tugs and barges and garbage scows wandered in their slow orbits. Every ship in that volume was dark or dim or transponding as something it wasn't; Ceres Anchorage's lower traffic lanes were the maritime equivalent of a back alley, and I knew every crack in the pavement.

For forty minutes, I was nobody. A mass shadow among mass shadows. My heart rate stayed under eighty and I was quietly proud of that.

Then the scan gate.

The cordon's gates weren't physical—they were overlapping sensor fans, three pickets triangulating anything that moved through the commercial approach corridor, pinging you with a demand handshake that your transponder was supposed to answer. Answer honestly, and you got waved through with a fee deducted. Answer dishonestly, and you got waved through anyway if your forgery was good, and mine was very good. Refuse to answer, and you got a follow-up ping with a much less polite tone, and then, eventually, a picket ship with opinions.

I had no intention of answering at all. I was going under the fans, through the gap between the second and third picket where the sensor coverage had a seam—there's always a seam; sensors are built by committees and committees are built by compromise—and out into open space where the corridor to the jump point waited.

The seam was real. I'd bought the picket positions from a disgruntled traffic controller eight months ago, back when buying it had been paranoia rather than planning, and the geometry hadn't changed. I brought the *Mercy* around in a long, silent arc, main drive cold, running on thrusters and momentum, and slid toward the gap like a thief through a window left unlatched.

The strange freedom I'd been feeling on the dock was still there, but it had changed texture. On the dock it had been relief. Out here, in the dark, with my transponder silenced and my drive cold and the entire weight of the customs authority theoretically able to end my career with one lucky ping, it was something else. It was the feeling of finally matching the outside to the inside.

For ten years I had been a fugitive who pretended to be a freighter captain. Every honest departure, every paid fee, every boring manifest had been a costume, and I'd worn it so long I'd forgotten it was a costume. Now the costume was off, and underneath it was—what? The same man. The same fear. But the fear had somewhere to go now. It had a direction. It had a burn vector.

There's a version of running that isn't escape. I was discovering it in real time. Escape is when you run from something. This was running *toward*—toward a relay station at the edge of dead space, toward a voice speaking a dead language in my own stolen memories, toward whatever had said my name into the dark and waited for me to say it back. Nineteen days of corridor, and for the first time in a decade, every light-year of them was mine. Chosen. Aimed.

It should not have felt as good as it did. I noted that too. Filed it with the other thing.

* * *

The seam in the sensor coverage was exactly where the disgruntled controller had said it would be, which restored a small amount of my faith in human spite. I threaded the *Mercy* through at a quarter gravity of thrust, thrusters only, the ship moving with the ponderous grace of a loaded hauler, which is to say like a cathedral falling upstairs.

Halfway through, the second picket's scan fan brushed us.

Not a hit. A graze. A passive array sweeping wide on its patrol rhythm, its edge clipping my hull signature for maybe four seconds. Four seconds is nothing, normally. Four seconds is noise, clutter, one more anonymous mass in the anchorage's crowded skirt. But four seconds is also enough for a good sensor tech to pull a hull resonance profile, and hull resonance profiles don't lie the way transponders do. Every ship has a fingerprint in the way her frame hums. The *Mercy* had hummed her particular hum for forty years.

I held my breath for a count of sixty. The fan swept on. No ping, no handshake demand, no change in the picket's lazy patrol vector.

Probably nothing.

*Probably* is a word I had learned to respect the way sailors respect weather. I marked it and kept moving.

Beyond the cordon, space opened up the way it always does—suddenly, absolutely, the station's light falling behind and the stars doing what stars do, which is nothing, forever. I let the *Mercy* drift another ten minutes, dark and quiet, until the anchorage was a bright smudge against the belt's dust, and only then did I light the main drive.

The burn came on like a held breath released. Forty percent thrust, the deck pressing up through my boots, the old girl groaning through her frames the way she always did when I asked her for anything above a crawl. On the plot, the corridor unrolled ahead of me: nineteen days of burn-and-coast, a slanting line through three empty systems and the dark between them, terminating at a jump point on the edge of dead space where the physics got thin and the maps got honest about how little they knew.

I sat with it for a while. Just sat, in the pilot's chair, feeling the thrust, watching the numbers climb.

Ten years of careful, and I'd thrown it over in an afternoon. The registry forgery would be flagged within a day. The syndicate would have my departure from Dock Nine inside six hours—Ilsa would make sure of that, because Ilsa was many things but not suicidal, and a man who asks questions about you for money will sell your departure vector for more of it. They'd know where I was going. They'd have ships, and I had one old hauler with a sealed hold full of my own sleeping faces.

And Sef. I hadn't seen Sef Calloway in three years, and now he was back in my orbit wanting a piece of whatever the syndicate was hunting, and I'd had to look him in the eye and lie, and he'd let me lie, which was worse. Sef always let me lie. It was his way of keeping a receipt.

I'd deal with Sef later. I'd deal with the syndicate later. Later was a country I was very good at living in.

* * *

I slept in shifts, as always—four hours down, four up, the autopilot minding the burn. On my second watch, somewhere in the eleventh hour out, I did something I hadn't planned to do.

I went aft.

Not all the way aft. Not to the sealed door. But I stood in the cargo spine at the junction where the corridor split—forward holds to port, the sealed hold to starboard—and I looked down the starboard passage at the bulkhead door with its maintenance locks and its dust, and I let myself think about what was behind it.

Six cradles. Six sleepers. Copies made in the last bad year, back when I still told myself I was doing it for safety, back before I understood that what I'd actually done was amputate my past and then carry it with me, sleeping, everywhere I went. A man who runs from a massacre and brings six copies of himself along is not a man who has escaped anything. He is a man who has turned his escape into a cargo manifest.

The signal had used my childhood as a key. Someone had been inside those memories—my memories, the real ones, the ones I'd sold or lost or buried depending on which version of the story I told myself that day. And the signal had known my name.

Behind that bulkhead, six versions of me slept on, dreaming whatever backups dream, and I had never once asked myself the question I was asking now, alone in the cargo spine with the drive humming through the deck plates:

What if they weren't dreaming?

What if they were waiting?

I put my hand flat against the bulkhead. Cold metal. Nothing answered, obviously. Metal doesn't answer. But I stood there long enough that my tea went cold in the other hand, and when I finally turned away, I made myself say it out loud, in Vessic, the way my mother would have said it—*I'm coming*—because the corridor ahead was long and dark and I was going to need the practice.

The words sounded strange in my own voice. Rusty. But they came.

* * *

Day two out, the plot threw me a surprise, and not the kind I like.

I was recalculating the mid-course correction when the navigation system flagged an anomaly on the corridor: a transponder echo, faint and intermittent, riding the same slant line I was. Too far out to identify, too disciplined to be debris. Someone else was running the corridor to the dead-space relay, and they were running it quiet.

I watched the echo for six hours. It held station relative to the corridor the way a hunter holds a trail—patient, economical, making no effort to close the distance. It didn't have to close. We were both aimed at the same jump point, and the corridor was a funnel: every hour that passed, the geometry squeezed us a little closer together.

I ran the numbers on outrunning it. The math said I couldn't. The math said whoever it was had a faster ship and had entered the corridor ahead of me, and the only reasons to enter that corridor ahead of me and then wait were the reasons hunters wait: because they know where the prey is going, and they want to arrive first.

The syndicate had found the seam after all. Or they'd never needed it. Either way, the net wasn't behind me anymore.

It was ahead.

I killed my running lights, trimmed the burn to the minimum the corridor math would tolerate, and settled into the dark with my hand resting on the throttle and my eyes on that faint, patient echo.

Nineteen days to the relay. Seventeen days of being hunted through the empty dark, in a ship with my name written in her hull resonance and my past sleeping behind a bulkhead.

And somewhere in the cargo spine, in the silence between the drive's hum and my own heartbeat, I could have sworn I heard—though I knew, I *knew*, it was nothing, it was metal and vacuum and my own guilty conscience—

a sound from the sealed hold.

Soft. Rhythmic.

Like breathing.
