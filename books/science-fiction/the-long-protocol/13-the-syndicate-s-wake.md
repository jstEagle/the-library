## 13. The Syndicate's Wake

Brann Oduya believed in three things: ledgers, leverage, and the principle that everyone, eventually, tells you where they are. Everything else—love, loyalty, the various gods people kept like pets—was noise. Noise was fine. Noise could even be profitable, if you knew how to listen through it.

She was listening through it now, in the belly of the *Charitable Interest*, a salvage tug that had once been a naval corvette and still carried herself like one, all muscle under modest paint. The ship's name had been chosen by her employer's marketing department, which Brann considered the single most honest thing about the syndicate: they did think of themselves as charitable. They rescued valuable objects from neglectful owners. That the owners usually objected was a detail of logistics.

"Run it again," she said.

The signal analyst—a nervous young man named Pell whose hands were always slightly damp—replayed the intercept for the fourth time. On the main display, a waveform crawled across the dark like something alive and embarrassed to be seen.

"This is the anomalous burst from Dock Nine's approach lanes," Pell said. "Fourteen seconds. Encrypted, but not—well, that's the strange part. It's not encrypted the way we'd encrypt. There's no handshake protocol, no commercial cipher signature. It's—" He hesitated, which Brann allowed him to do for exactly two seconds before she raised an eyebrow and he hurried on. "It's structured like a distress call. Old format. Pre-Compact."

"Pre-Compact." Brann turned the word over the way she'd turn a stone to see what lived underneath. "Nobody's broadcast pre-Compact distress formats in two hundred years. The protocols were retired because the colonies using them stopped existing."

"Yes, ma'am."

"So either someone found a museum piece and got sentimental, or someone learned to speak dead languages." She leaned back in the acceleration couch, which creaked its objection; the couch had opinions about her weight and shared them freely, and she respected that in furniture. "Where did it originate?"

"That's the interesting part. Or the unhelpful part. The burst came off a relay buoy at the edge of the Halvard Deep—the dead zone past the Meridian shelf. But relays don't originate. They repeat. So the actual source is inside the Deep, or beyond it, and the relay just—" Pell made a vague gesture, damp hands catching the light, "—passed it along like a good little machine."

"And who received it?"

"We've been pulling dock logs. Fourteen ships in the corridor picked up fragments. Thirteen of them logged it as interference or ignored it entirely." Pell paused, savoring this the way he never savored anything else in his life. "One ship answered."

Brann felt the small cold click in her chest that she'd learned to trust more than any instrument. "Name."

"A cargo hauler registered as the *Quiet Interval*. Owner-operator. Flight history is—" he scrolled, frowning, "—oddly clean. Ten years of routine hauls through contested orbits without a single incident, citation, or insurance claim. No debts, no liens, no social footprint. No passenger manifests. No maintenance contracts with any major yard. It's like the ship exists only when someone's looking directly at it."

"Which means someone built it that way." Brann stood and crossed to the display, studying the *Quiet Interval*'s registry profile the way other people studied faces. Falsified registries were common enough; what was rare was falsification done *well*. Most people running from something overcorrected—they bought alibis, made friends, left traces of a personality like crumbs. This profile had no crumbs. This profile had been swept by someone who understood exactly what a broom was for.

People who swept that carefully were always hiding something worth finding.

"Pell. Cross-reference the *Quiet Interval*'s purchase. Where did the hull come from?"

"It was bought at a decommission auction eleven years ago. Cash equivalent, no financing. Seller was a scrap broker out of Ceres Gate." Pell's scrolling slowed. "Huh. The auction lot included a sealed cargo module. Listed as 'miscellaneous cryogenic equipment, condition unknown.'"

"Cryogenic equipment." Brann let the words sit in the room. Around her, the *Charitable Interest* hummed its low working hum, three decks of syndicate crew pretending very hard not to listen while listening with their whole bodies. She'd cultivated that atmosphere deliberately. A crew that eavesdropped efficiently saved her the trouble of repeating herself.

"The buyer paid for the hull," she said slowly, "and took delivery of a sealed module full of sleepers, and then spent ten years never opening it. You don't buy cryo equipment you don't intend to use. And you don't keep it sealed for a decade unless what's inside is either worthless or priceless."

"Or dangerous," offered Vess, from the doorway.

Brann didn't turn. She'd heard Vess Kettering coming for the last thirty seconds—he walked like a man announcing himself to every deck plate—and she let him arrive at his own pace. Her second-in-command was a broad, patient man with a salvager's calm and a debt-slave's history, and she trusted him precisely as far as his self-interest extended, which was a long way, and always in the same direction as hers.

"Dangerous pays too," she said. "Sometimes better. Dangerous things make sellers motivated." She tapped the display. "Here's what we know. Someone at the edge of dead space is broadcasting in a language that died with the Vessic colony. One ship in the entire corridor recognized it—and I mean *recognized*, because you don't answer a fourteen-second burst in an extinct protocol unless you can read it. That ship is carrying sealed cryogenic equipment it's never opened. And yesterday, our contact at Dock Nine reports a second party asking questions about anomalous signals. A woman traveling alone, paying cash, asking after the *Quiet Interval* specifically."

"You think she's competition?" Vess asked.

"I think she's a variable. Variables get resolved." Brann pulled up the star chart, the Halvard Deep spreading across it in the cartographer's traditional gray—the color used for places where beacons went to die. "Here's what matters. Whatever's broadcasting from inside the Deep wanted to be heard by exactly one person. That kind of message isn't noise. That's a key turning in a lock. And locks are assets."

Pell cleared his throat. "Ma'am, the Deep itself is the problem. No beacon coverage, no rescue infrastructure, comms degrade past the Meridian shelf. If we go in and something goes wrong—"

"Then we won't go in wrong." She said it mildly, which her crew knew to read as a promise rather than a comfort. "We're not explorers. We're not romantics chasing ghosts. We go in because there's a transaction waiting at the end of it, and transactions have structure. Six sleepers with a decade of accrued value, plus whatever technology is maintaining a live relay in dead space—that's a salvage run with a story attached. Stories add zeros."

Vess grunted. "And the pilot? The one who answers distress calls in dead languages? He's an asset too, or a problem?"

"He's both, which is my favorite combination." Brann studied the flight path data one more time. The *Quiet Interval* had filed nothing since receiving the burst—no new route, no departure manifest—but ships that ran silent still burned fuel, still drew supplies, still existed in the gravitational honesty of space. You could hide your intentions. You couldn't hide your vector forever. "He'll move. People who receive messages like that always move. The question is whether he runs toward the source or away from it, and I want an answer before he knows the question's being asked."

* * *

They caught the vector forty hours later, and Brann took a certain professional pleasure in how clean the work was.

Not her work—the pilot's. The *Quiet Interval* had slipped its moorings at Dock Nine during a shift change, burned cold for six hours on a trajectory that looked like nothing, and then threaded the customs cordon through a gap in the patrol schedule so narrow that Brann found herself nodding in genuine appreciation. Whoever flew that ship knew the cordon's rotation tables better than the customs office did. That wasn't luck. That was ten years of study, ten years of a man teaching himself the exact shape of the cage so he could stand very still inside it.

Until now.

"He's headed for the Meridian shelf," Pell reported. "Bearing puts him on a corridor into the Deep. There's one viable jump window through the shelf gravitics in the next month, and he's aimed at it."

"He's going to answer it in person." Brann sat with that for a moment, turning it over. Most people, receiving a signal from a place like the Halvard Deep, would do the sensible thing: report it, sell it, bury it. This man was doing none of those. He was spending his invisibility—the thing he'd clearly built his whole life around purchasing—to walk toward the voice. Which meant whatever was calling him outweighed everything he'd spent a decade protecting.

That was leverage. Leverage was just need, located.

"Set course for the shelf," she said. "Match his corridor, stay outside his sensor shadow. We don't want him yet. We want him at the far end, wherever the far end is, when he's tired and committed and the window behind him has closed."

Vess settled into the couch beside hers. "And if he doesn't come quietly?"

"Vess." She smiled, and was aware, distantly, that her smile had been described by three separate negotiators as the most frightening thing they'd ever seen on a human face. "Everyone comes quietly eventually. Some people just need the volume turned down first."

* * *

The transit to the Meridian shelf took nine days, and Brann spent them the way she spent all transits: working.

She worked the file. The *Quiet Interval*'s owner had a name on paper—Zeph Coldharbour, a bland, forgettable string of syllables that smelled of forgery the way old fuel smelled of solvent. She ran it against every database the syndicate could reach, legal and otherwise, and got back the digital equivalent of a shrug. Ten years of taxes filed punctually. Ten years of port fees paid in full. Ten years of a man being so aggressively ordinary that ordinary became a scream.

But paper trails had edges, and edges frayed. Eleven years ago, the trail simply began—a man with a clean record and no history, buying a ship with cash, in a system with lax verification and a broker who'd since retired to a moon with no extradition treaties. Before that: nothing. Not thin records. *Nothing.* Records didn't vanish on their own. Records were erased, and erasure cost money and skill and fear.

"Fear's the tell," she murmured to the display, on the fourth night, alone in her cabin with the lights down to working amber. "You weren't hiding from creditors. Creditors are patient. You were hiding from something that looks."

She thought about the Vessic colony. Everyone in the trade knew the shape of that story: a frontier settlement, two centuries gone, wiped out in whatever catastrophe the histories couldn't agree on—reactor cascade, plague, evacuation gone wrong, depending on which historian you paid. The colony had produced navigators of legendary quality, back when it had produced anything at all. And it had died so completely that even its language had gone extinct, surviving only in academic archives and the occasional nostalgic footnote.

A man who spoke Vessic. A signal in Vessic. Sleepers in a hold, sealed for a decade.

Brann didn't believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what people called patterns they hadn't finished pricing.

On the sixth day, Pell brought her a gift. He did it shyly, the way he brought everything, holding the datapad slightly too tight. "The private channel. The one the *Quiet Interval* used to answer the burst. Our listening posts caught the reply traffic before it went dark—it was short, but the channel metadata survived. Ma'am, the encryption handshake on that channel wasn't standard either. It was keyed to biometric memory patterns. Cognitive-signature authentication."

Brann looked up slowly. "Meaning what, in words?"

"Meaning the channel would only open for one specific mind. Someone built a communication line that literally required a particular person's memories to unlock." Pell swallowed. "Whoever sent that signal didn't just know the pilot. They had access to his cognition. His actual memory architecture. Enough of it to build a lock only he could open."

The cabin was quiet except for the ship's hum. Brann set down her tea, which had gone cold, and considered the implications the way she considered all large numbers: in installments.

Someone had been inside the pilot's head. Deeply, structurally, years ago—memory-pattern keys took time to harvest. Which meant somewhere in this story there was a second crime, older than the signal, older maybe than the ship. Memory theft was illegal in every jurisdiction that mattered and profitable in every jurisdiction that didn't. The market for cognitive signatures—identity cloning, skill duplication, the ugly trade in being someone else—ran hot in exactly the circles the syndicate moved in.

Six sleepers in a sealed hold. A man who'd spent ten years making sure no one ever looked at him. A lock built from his own mind.

"Oh, you're worth a fortune," Brann told the empty cabin softly. "You're worth several fortunes. You just don't know whose."

* * *

They crossed the Meridian shelf on the eleventh day, riding the same gravity corridor the *Quiet Interval* had cut through four days ahead, and the universe closed around them like a hand.

It happened gradually and then completely. First the beacon traffic thinned—commercial channels dropping away one by one, until the comm array hung silent as an unplugged instrument. Then the nav satellites' distant pips faded from the plot. Then, in the outer bands, even the static changed character: the Deep's background radiation had a texture to it, a soft granular hiss that old spacers swore sounded like breathing. Brann had always thought that was romantic nonsense. Out here, in the gray, she conceded it sounded a little like breathing.

"No rescue coverage past this line," Vess said, reading the same board she was reading. "Standard disclaimer."

"We're not standard." She watched the plot, where the *Quiet Interval*'s last known position drifted, stale and ghostly, four days cold. The ship had gone fully dark somewhere ahead—no transponder, no drive flare, nothing but the fading warmth of its wake in the interstellar medium. Good tradecraft. Better than she'd expected.

It didn't matter. Dark-running left wakes, and wakes left vectors, and vectors were honest in a way people never were. The pilot was heading deeper, toward the relay coordinates buried in the original burst, toward whatever had learned to say his name in a dead tongue. He thought he was escaping observation.

Everyone thought that, right up until the moment they were observed.

"Course holds," Vess confirmed. "We'll reach the relay's last known coordinates in six days. After that, we're navigating by his wake and prayer."

"We don't pray. We navigate." Brann rose and stood at the forward viewport, where the Deep spread out ahead of them—not black, exactly, but a color black would be ashamed of, a depth without floor. Somewhere out there, a man who had spent a decade becoming no one was about to discover that no one was a commodity, and commodities had buyers, and buyers had ships, and the ships were already moving.

She felt no malice about it. That was the thing people misunderstood about her work. Malice was inefficient; it clouded valuation. What she felt was closer to respect. The pilot was good. The pilot was careful. The pilot had built a fortress out of ordinariness and garrisoned it with silence.

But fortresses fell to patience, and patience was the one weapon Brann Oduya had never once run out of.

"Signal the fleet," she said. "Two support tugs, trailing distance. Nobody transmits. From here to the relay, we are a hole in space." She paused, watching the gray. "And Pell—if you find anything else about Vessic, anything at all, I want it within the hour. Because whoever built that signal knew our pilot down to the shape of his childhood. And I want to know what else they kept."

Somewhere ahead, in the breathing dark, a relay blinked its patient light, and a locked channel waited for one specific mind, and six sleepers dreamed whatever sleepers dream.

Brann Oduya set her course into all of it, unhurried, precise, and utterly certain that the ledger would balance in her favor.

It always did. Eventually, everyone told you where they were.
