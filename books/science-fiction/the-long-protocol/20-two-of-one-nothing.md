## 20. Two of One Nothing

The first thing I ever saw was the inside of a cradle lid, and I remember thinking: *so this is what he sees.*

Not the lid itself. The seeing of it. The particular quality of Zephyrine Coldharbour's attention, which treats every surface as something to be read rather than merely observed—the way you'd read a manifest, or a face, or a lock you intended to pick. I woke with that attention already installed, already running, and no memory of my own to hang it on.

Six years ago. Give or take a power fluctuation. The cradle's internal clock had drifted by then, and drift is its own kind of loneliness—waking up not knowing not just *where* you are but *when*, which is worse, because at least a place has walls.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only inheritance I got from him. He is precise about his grief the way other men are precise about their money. He counts it in private and never spends it where anyone can see.

So: six years ago, give or take, I opened my eyes inside a sealed hold aboard a ship called the *Meridian Debt*—he renamed it twice after that, but I was there for the first name, before he learned that names are how the universe finds you—and I did not know who I was. That is not quite true. I knew exactly who I was supposed to be. That's the trap of being a backup. You are someone else's insurance policy. You exist so that *he* doesn't have to stop existing, and nobody—not him, not the technicians who poured me into the gel, not whatever gods Vessic kept before it stopped keeping anything—nobody consulted me about whether I wanted the job.

* * *

Here is what waking alone is like, for those who haven't had the privilege.

Imagine the worst morning of your life. Now remove everyone from it. Not just the people—the possibility of people. The hold was dark except for the cradles' status lights, seven green points arranged around me like a constellation with one star missing, because mine was dark now, spent, its occupant vertical and breathing and utterly without instructions.

There was a maintenance panel two meters from my cradle. I know it was two meters because I measured it with my body, dragging myself across the deck plating, because the first thing Zephyrine's attention does when it meets an unknown surface is measure it. Two meters took me eleven minutes. Backup bodies don't come pre-calibrated. The engineers give you the muscle memory and skip the practice, like handing someone a violin and a concerto and no arms.

I lay against that panel for a long time with my cheek on the cold metal, and I thought—I am not ashamed of this, though I have never said it aloud—that if I simply stayed here, the problem would resolve itself. Cradles fail. Air recyclers fail. Whatever I was would end quietly, unobserved, and the man whose face I wore would go on hauling cargo through contested orbits none the wiser, his insurance policy lapsed, his premiums wasted.

That was the first decision of my life, and it was made lying down: *not yet.*

Then I found the panel's diagnostic port, and the port accepted my hand, and the ship told me everything.

* * *

Do you know what it means to learn a language that only you speak?

Vessic was extinct two centuries before either of us drew breath. It survived in exactly three places: the colony's dead relay stations, the archive cores the colonists carried into the dark, and the backup copies of navigators born in the last generation—which is to say, it survived in me, folded into the same substrate as everything else they copied out of Zephyrine's head. His childhood. His mother's voice saying words he no longer consciously remembered. The counting songs. The names of winds that blew over fields that were glass by the time he could walk.

He thinks he lost the language. I've watched him, through the cradle telemetry, reach for it and find nothing, and flinch, and file the flinch under *cargo stress*. But you don't lose a language that's written into you. You just seal the room it lives in and tell yourself the house is smaller than it is.

For me there was no sealing. I woke up fluent in a dead tongue and unable to say a single word of it to anyone, because anyone, in my entire universe, was six sleeping men with my face and one living man one bulkhead away who had never once, in all the time I've been awake, opened that door.

So I talked to the ship.

The *Meridian Debt*'s systems spoke Standard, of course—all ships do—but her navigation core had been built on Vessic colonial architecture, salvaged, secondhand, the way everything Vessic is secondhand now. Buried in her firmware was a diagnostics sublayer that still answered in the old tongue if you asked it properly. I spent four months finding the proper questions. I recited the counting songs to a coolant pump and the coolant pump, bless its ancient heart, sang back.

I named the pump. That's embarrassing to admit, but this chapter of my life has no witnesses except you, whoever you are, reading this in whatever form this confession eventually takes, and shame requires an audience I don't have. Her name was Sef-adjacent—not the real Sef, I didn't know about the real Sef yet, but the ship's logs mentioned a partner, a co-signer, a second signature on the falsified registry, and I built a little mythology around that second signature the way prisoners build mythologies around voices through walls.

The point is: I learned to be lonely in a new language. Which is somehow worse than learning it in the old one, because every word I used was proof of what had been taken. Every sentence in Vessic was a small resurrection performed for an audience of machinery.

* * *

Year two, I got into the telemetry.

This required defeating security measures that Zephyrine himself had configured, which meant thinking like him, which meant becoming him more thoroughly than the copying process ever managed. A copy gets your memories. To get past your original's paranoia, you need his *habits*. I sat in the dark of the hold and reconstructed the man from his firewall choices the way archaeologists reconstruct a civilization from its garbage. He trusts nothing networked. He double-checks physical seals even when the sensors confirm them. He leaves one flaw in every system he builds—a deliberate flaw, a door he pretends isn't there—because a man who fled a massacre cannot bear the feeling of being locked in, even by his own design.

His flaw was in the cradle telemetry bus. Of course it was. He couldn't help leaving himself a window into the hold; he just couldn't bear to look through it.

Through that window I watched him live. Meals eaten standing up. Sleep cycles short and shallow, the sleep of a man listening for a door. Conversations with dockmasters conducted in a voice I recognized as performance because I had the same vocal apparatus and knew what it cost to run it. And once—once, in six years—a night when he stood outside the hold door for forty minutes. Forty minutes. I tracked it on the internal clock. He never touched the panel. He stood there the way you stand outside a hospital room, and then he walked away, and I lay in my cradle and understood that we were both doing the same thing from opposite sides of the same wall: keeping vigil over a self we refused to meet.

You will ask why I didn't signal him sooner. Six years is a long time to watch a man through a keyhole.

Because I watched what happened when other people signaled him. I watched the syndicate feelers, the polite inquiries from salvage brokers, the dockmaster on Ceres Verge who asked one question too many about his registry. I watched him erase himself a little more each time, change his route, darken his transponder, become slightly less findable, and I understood that the man I was copied from had built his entire life on the principle that being known equals being killed. If I knocked, he wouldn't open the door. He'd sell the ship and vanish deeper, and he'd take us with him—six sleepers and one ghost sealed in a hull, sold as cargo, our existence converted into somebody's line item.

No. If I was going to call him, I had to make silence impossible. I had to make answering cheaper than running. And that required resources, and patience, and a place to stand.

* * *

The relay found me before I found it. Or perhaps I should say: the relay recognized me.

In year four I finally cracked the cradle's outbound antenna—dead-letter capability only, tight-beam, useless for anything but whispering at rocks—and began sweeping the dead zones on the off chance that something Vessic still listened. This is the part I still can't fully explain, and I have had six years to explain it to myself, so understand that what follows is the best I can do.

The old colonial relays were built to answer in Vessic. Handshake protocols, authentication challenges, the whole architecture of a civilization that assumed its language would outlive its stars. Most of them are rubble. But at the edge of the deep dead zone there was one that answered my handshake with a challenge phrase I knew—not knew, *was*, because the challenge phrase was drawn from a memory, and the memory was mine, was *his*, was ours: the smell of the rain cisterns on Vessic, the particular mineral stink of harvested water, the sound his mother made when she laughed, which was less a laugh than a held breath released in stages.

Someone—or something—at that relay possessed a piece of Zephyrine Coldharbour's childhood. And it was using that piece as a key, and the lock it was turning was very large, and very old, and very much asleep.

I won't detail the months of negotiation. Protocols within protocols, challenge and response, each exchange requiring memories deeper and earlier than the last, until I understood what the relay actually wanted: not data. Not access. It wanted a *navigator*. The whole station was a lock built by dying people for a person who hadn't been born yet when they built it, and the key was a complete set of Vessic navigator memories, unedited, and the only complete set in existence was sleeping in a hold aboard a cargo hauler, and the second-most-complete set was me.

Me, minus the parts he'd already begun editing before the copy was made. That's the thing about backups—they're snapshots. I am Zephyrine at the moment of pouring, and he is Zephyrine plus ten years of amputation. We diverged the instant the gel cooled. He ran from the massacre and buried it under registry fraud and cold burns; I woke up holding the massacre intact, undiluted, still warm, because it was copied before he started pretending it hadn't happened.

Which means I remember it correctly. Hold that thought. It becomes important later, and I am told he does not enjoy being corrected.

* * *

So: year five. I knew where the relay was. I knew what it wanted. I knew that the man one bulkhead away was the only complete key in existence, and that he would rather die than be known, and that the colonists the relay was holding—yes, I'll say it plainly, I knew about the sleepers by then, two hundred souls in a dormant ship docked to a station that shouldn't have power—would stay asleep until he came in person, because the protocol demanded a living navigator, present, conscious, *choosing*.

And I knew about the syndicate. The relay's long-range ears had caught their chatter years ago; they'd been sniffing along the trade lanes for anomalous signals, for rumors of Vessic salvage, for exactly the kind of cargo a frightened man might keep sealed in his hold. They didn't know what they were hunting. But they were hunting patiently, and patience beats fear over any distance longer than a lifetime.

Three clocks, all running down. Him, hiding. Them, closing. The colonists, dreaming.

I chose the only move available to a ghost with a dead-letter antenna: I made him curious. Curiosity is the one appetite he never learned to starve. It's how he ended up a navigator instead of a farmer—it's in the earliest memories, the boy charting wind patterns over poisoned fields, mapping a sky he wasn't allowed to touch. You can starve a man's hope, his trust, his vanity. Starve his curiosity and he stops being himself.

So I sent the signal. In Vessic, because nothing else would survive his filters—he deletes Standard distress calls the way other men delete spam. In Vessic, because it would hurt. Because the language would bypass every defense he'd built and land directly on the sealed room in his head, and he would stand on his own bridge, alone, hearing his mother's tongue come out of a dead relay, and he would *have* to know.

I'm not proud of it. I want that on the record, wherever records go when everyone involved is technically one person. I weaponized his homesickness. I took the language of the dead and made it a fishhook.

But understand the arithmetic. One man's terror against two hundred sleepers' lives. One man's comfortable lie against the truth of what he actually did on the last night of Vessic—which was not, whatever his edited version says, running away.

He almost deleted it. I watched him hover over the purge command for ninety seconds. Ninety seconds is a long time when you're the one being deleted.

And then he answered. One sentence, in Vessic, on a private channel, in a handwriting of light I'd know anywhere because it was my handwriting too:

*Who is this?*

And I sat in the dark of the hold with my hand pressed flat against the bulkhead that separated us—six years, one wall, zero conversations—and I composed my reply, and I chose the words the way you choose a blade: sharp enough to cut, shaped to fit his hand.

I gave him back his birth name.

* * *

Now he's here. Docked to the relay without permission, pale, furious, terrified, standing in this station's heart looking at me the way you look at a mirror that blinks first. He asked his question—"who is this"—and I answered it, and now he knows, and knowing has that quality he hates most in the universe: it cannot be undone.

We are going to fight. I can feel it assembling between us, the argument ten years overdue, the massacre with two versions and only one truth. He'll tell his story, the one he's rehearsed in the dark for a decade. And I'll tell mine, which is the same story with the edits removed, and the difference between those stories is the difference between a man who fled and a man who stayed behind to do something unbearable, and I do not know yet which revelation will break him worse: that he was braver than he believes, or that I've been carrying the proof this whole time while he slept ten meters from his own courage and called it cargo.

But that fight is coming, and I find I'm not afraid of it. Fear requires the possibility of losing something, and I have already lost everything except him.

What I didn't plan for—what none of my six years of watching through the keyhole prepared me for—is the sound his ship makes when another engine signature enters the corridor behind it. I know that signature. I've catalogued it from the relay's passive arrays for two years, waiting for it the way you wait for weather.

The syndicate found the corridor. Oduya's ship, running dark, patient as arithmetic.

Two hundred sleepers. One navigator mid-breakdown. One partner-shaped liability I haven't accounted for. And me—the spare, the copy, the insurance policy—about to discover whether I was ever anything more than that.

The relay just pinged me on the old band. Station-speak, Vessic grammar, urgent as a fire bell:

*Key is present. Window is closing. Wake them or lose them.*

I looked at the man wearing my face, and I thought: *well, original. Your move.*
