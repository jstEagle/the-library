## 25. Confrontation in the Cradle Bay

I knew something was wrong before I knew what it was. That's the navigator's curse—you spend your whole career listening to instruments tell you things your body already suspects, and eventually you can't tell the difference between a sensor fault and a feeling.

The feeling was this: the relay's hum had changed key.

Not much. A quarter-tone, maybe less, the way a room changes when someone else is breathing in it. Vess-1 had gone quiet in my earpiece twenty minutes ago—said he needed to check the colony ship's atmospheric reserves and signed off with a politeness I'd never heard from him before. My backup, being polite. That alone should have sent me running for the airlocks.

Instead I was walking the long spine corridor toward the cradle bay, following the changed hum like a blood trail, and telling myself that whatever I found at the end of it would be explainable.

It wasn't.

* * *

Sef Calloway was standing at the transmitter.

The old Vessic relay's transmitter—a thing the size of a shipping container, all copper veins and glass lungs, humming with enough power to shout across dead space—was bolted to the floor of what had once been the station's cradle bay. The colonists who built this place had kept their infants here, back when there were infants, back when there was a colony. Now it held six empty backup cradles shipped over from my hold at Vess-1's request, their lids open like waiting mouths, and one man hunched over a control panel with his hand on the transmit lever.

My partner. My stowaway. My debt of eleven years, standing in the light of a status board that said OUTBOUND TRANSMISSION — ACTIVE.

He didn't hear me come in. Or he did, and decided not to turn around, which would be worse. Sef had always been good at not turning around. It was practically his religion—the belief that if you never looked directly at a problem, it couldn't look back.

"Sef."

His shoulders did the thing they always did when I said his name like that. Not a flinch. A settling, like a man accepting weight he'd been expecting.

"Zeph." He still didn't turn. "You're supposed to be down in the core with your— with him."

"Him is standing right next to me, as far as I know. We were reviewing the wake sequence." I took another step. "What are you transmitting?"

Silence. The particular silence of a man doing math he doesn't like.

"Sef. What. Are you. Transmitting."

"The truth," he said finally, and turned around, and his face was terrible—not guilty, worse than guilty, *resolved*, which is guilt that's finished its paperwork. "I'm transmitting our position. Coordinates, ship registry, the works. To Oduya."

The floor didn't move, but I felt it move anyway. Dead space has that effect; when there's nothing real under you, everything imaginary gets a vote.

"When."

"Forty minutes ago."

"Why."

And there it was. The question I'd been carrying since Dock Nine, since he'd stowed away in my water reclamation module with a duffel bag and a grin, since the night on Vessic that neither of us had ever said out loud in the same room. Eleven years of *why*, compressed into two syllables, and I threw them at him like I expected an answer to hurt him more than me.

It didn't work out that way.

"Because you were going to die here," Sef said. "All of you. Every version of you. And I was going to watch."

* * *

Let me tell you about the argument we'd been not-having for a decade.

It has a shape. Most long arguments do—they accrete, layer by layer, like coral, until what started as a single sharp point becomes a whole reef you can't sail through. Ours began the night Vessic burned, or rather the night after, when I told Sef I was leaving and he asked me where I'd been during the evacuation, and I lied to him, and he let me lie because he loved me, and we both pretended the lie was load-bearing.

It wasn't. It never is. Lies don't hold anything up. They just get heavier.

"You think Oduya is going to save us?" I heard my own voice go thin and high, the voice I hated, the voice from the bad year. "You've met him. You watched him strip a salvage crew's teeth out over a mislabeled cargo manifest. You called him—" I searched for it, found it, threw it. "You called him *the taxidermist*. Your words, Sef. You said he makes things look alive so he can sell them dead."

"I know what I called him."

"And you handed him our coordinates anyway."

"He's four days out!" Sef's composure cracked down the middle, and what came out of the crack was louder and older than either of us. "Four days, Zeph. And you're down in that core feeding your memories into a machine that's waking up three hundred people who have been dead for two hundred years, and Vess-1 is talking about *merging*, about pouring both of you into one skull like it's a matter of pouring tea, and nobody—*nobody*—has said out loud what happens if it goes wrong!"

"It won't go wrong."

"You don't know that! Nobody knows that! There's no manual for this, Zeph. There's no protocol—" He stopped himself on the word, and I watched him stop, watched him realize what he'd almost said, and something in his face folded shut.

But I caught it. Of course I caught it. I'd been named for it.

"The Long Protocol," I said quietly. "Go ahead. Finish the sentence."

"No."

"Say it."

"*No.*"

"Then I'll say it." I walked toward him, slow, because fast felt like attack and I wanted this to be something else, though God knows what. "The Long Protocol. The standing order every Vessic navigator carried: no navigator travels alone, no memory goes unwitnessed, no colony gets lost twice. It's carved into the relay's foundation, Sef. It's in the colony ship's charter. It's why I exist in six copies in a hold I never opened—because some committee of dying people decided that a navigator's mind was too important to lose, even if the navigator disagreed." I stopped an arm's length from him. "And you want to call Oduya down on it. On all of it. Because it scares you."

"That's not—"

"Yes it is. Yes, it is exactly that, and the only reason you transmitted instead of talking to me is that you knew I'd say yes anyway. You knew I'd already chosen. So you went behind my back to save me from my own decision, and you dressed it up as rescue, and you want me to be grateful."

Sef's jaw worked. His eyes were wet, which was somehow the worst part, because Sef crying was like a bulkhead crying—it meant the pressure had gotten somewhere structural.

"You want to know the real reason?" he said. "The real reason, not the one that'll make you feel righteous?"

"Give it to me."

"Vessic." One word, and it landed like a dropped tool in vacuum—no sound, all damage. "You want to stand there and lecture me about decisions made behind people's backs? About dressing things up? *Zephyrine.* I was on the dock. I saw the shuttle manifest. I saw what you did."

* * *

Here is what I have never written down, never said, never let surface past the third hour of sleepless nights: I know what he saw. I know exactly what he saw, because I was there, and the version of it I've carried for eleven years is a version I edited, carefully, lovingly, the way you edit anything you can't survive intact.

The true version belongs to the massacre chapter, and that chapter isn't due yet. But Sef stood in the cradle bay with his hand still hovering near the transmit lever, and he looked at me with eleven years of held breath behind his eyes, and I understood that he was going to say it whether I was ready or not.

So I said the other thing first. The coward's priority: control the wound site.

"What did you hope would happen?" I asked. "Genuinely. Walk me through it. Oduya arrives, takes the backups, takes the relay's tech, maybe takes us—and how does that end with anyone alive?"

"Because he doesn't kill assets!" Sef's voice bounced off the cradle bay's curved walls and came back doubled, accusing itself. "That's the whole point of the syndicate, Zeph. They don't destroy value, they *move* it. I give him the coordinates voluntarily, I negotiate—I've done business with his people for nine years, I know what he pays for cooperation—and I buy you out. All of you. The copies go to him, the ship goes free, you walk away with a clean registry and enough money to disappear properly this time, somewhere the signal can't find you, somewhere *you* can't find you—"

"There it is."

"—and you get to live!"

"Alone," I said. "You're describing buying me back my loneliness. At scale."

"I'm describing keeping you *alive!*"

"And I'm telling you that's not the same thing anymore!" And then I was shouting, actually shouting, in a dead-space relay where sound should have felt obscene, and I couldn't stop, because eleven years is a lot of pressure and the valve had finally rusted through. "Do you know what I was doing before the signal, Sef? Before Vess-1? I was hauling refrigerated protein through contested orbits and sleeping four hours a night with a knife under my pillow, and I called that living, because the alternative word was *waiting*. I was waiting to be found. Ten years, Sef. I falsified my registry, I buried my name, I sealed my own face behind a bulkhead and never once—not once—opened that hold, because opening it meant admitting there was something to hide. And you know what I found out? You can't hide from yourself by hiding *copies* of yourself. They wait. They learn the language you abandoned. They grow up alone in the dark and then they call you home, and the home they call you to is the one you burned down, and it turns out it was never burned at all. It was *sleeping*. Three hundred people, Sef. Sleeping. Waiting for one living navigator's memories to wake them, and I am apparently the last navigator, and you want to sell that to a man who files people under inventory."

"They're not people yet," Sef whispered. "They're cargo in cold sleep. That's what you always said. Cargo."

"I know what I said." The anger went out of me all at once, the way it does, leaving the structural damage visible. "I said a lot of things for ten years. That was the whole disease."

* * *

We stood there in the quiet afterward, two men in a nursery for the unborn, surrounded by six open cradles and the ghost-smell of copper and antiseptic. Somewhere below us, deep in the relay's core, the colony ship's systems were drinking my memories through a fiber-optic umbilical, learning Vessic again, one childhood at a time. I could feel it faintly, the way you feel weather through a wall. Vess-1 had warned me it would feel strange. He hadn't warned me it would feel like being *read*—like the difference between remembering your mother's voice and hearing someone else remember it better than you do.

Sef sat down on the edge of an empty cradle. It creaked under him, offended.

"I wasn't trying to betray you," he said, to the cradle, to the floor, to the general concept of me. "I need you to believe that, even if you don't forgive it. I ran the scenarios for three days. Every scenario where I do nothing ends with Oduya finding this place on his own—he's been backtracing since Dock Nine, Zeph, you know he has—and arriving angry, unannounced, with you mid-merge or mid-wake or whatever the hell you're doing down there, and no leverage, no negotiation, nothing but a locked door between him and the biggest prize in the sector. Voluntary coordinates were the only card I had. So I played it."

"You could have told me."

"Could you have stopped me?"

I thought about lying. Old habit. Then I thought about the transmitter, and the forty minutes of head start I'd already wasted, and the fact that the truth was now the only currency in the room.

"No," I said. "I'd have locked you in the hold."

"Yeah." He laughed, and it was a broken little sound. "Yeah, you would've. See, that's the thing you don't get, Zeph. That's the thing you've never gotten. I'm not afraid of Oduya. I'm not even afraid of dying, particularly—I'm a salvager, we're all just pre-deceased. What I'm afraid of is watching. I watched Vessic. I stood on that dock and I watched the sky come apart and I watched you—" He stopped. Swallowed it. Started smaller. "I watched enough. I'm not doing it again. Not to you. If selling you out buys you out, fine. Hate me. Hate me in a clean registry with money in your account, hate me on some quiet rock on the far side of nowhere, hate me for fifty years. Just *hate me alive*."

The worst part—the part I'll carry into whatever I am after the merge—is that I understood him completely. That was always our tragedy, Sef's and mine. We understood each other perfectly and it never once helped.

"You transmitted our position to save my life," I said slowly, "by delivering me to the man hunting me."

"When you say it like that—"

"It's accurate. That's how I say things."

He rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them, he looked older than the war, older than the colony, older than grief. "There's something else," he said. "Something I haven't told you. About the transmission."

The changed hum in the walls picked that moment to change again—up a half-step this time, urgent, the relay equivalent of a raised voice. My earpiece crackled. Vess-1's voice, stripped of all its careful politeness:

*"Zephyrine. Come to the core. Now. Something answered the transmission—and it wasn't Oduya."*

Sef and I looked at each other across the cradle bay, and for the first time in eleven years, we were afraid of exactly the same thing, in exactly the same direction.

"What answered it?" I asked.

Vess-1 paused, and my own voice, coming back at me through six years of loneliness, sounded almost gentle:

*"Someone who knows the Protocol. Someone who says they've been looking for the last navigator longer than I've been awake."*
