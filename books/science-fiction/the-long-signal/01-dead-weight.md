## 1. Dead Weight

The station came at her out of the dark like a bad idea she'd had before.

Ulyana Lockridge watched it grow on the Kindly's forward display — Meridian, a spindle of scavenged hulls and borrowed light, spinning in contested orbit around a gas giant nobody owned and everybody wanted. Eleven years she'd been flying this route, and the station still looked to her like something assembled by committee. Which, to be fair, it had been.

"Approach vector's clean," said Tomas over the intercom from engineering, his accent thickening the way it always did when he was tired. "As clean as anything is out here."

"Nothing is clean out here," Ulyana said. "That's the whole economy."

She brought the freighter in the way she liked to: slowly, without flourish, without asking permission twice. Meridian's traffic control was a half-asleep dispatcher named Okonkwo who had been threatening retirement for six years and would threaten it for six more, and Ulyana had learned that the trick to docking at a station run by two rival fleets who pretended not to hate each other was to be boring. Boring ships got waved through. Interesting ships got boarded.

"Kindly, you are cleared for berth fourteen," Okonkwo drawled. "Try not to scratch anything the Concord is currently pretending belongs to them."

"No promises," Ulyana said, and killed the channel before she could say anything else. Small talk was a door she'd learned to keep closed. Doors, once opened, let in questions, and questions were the one cargo she never carried.

The berth clamps caught with the familiar shudder she felt in her teeth before she heard it. She ran the shutdown sequence by muscle memory, her hands moving through the console's soft amber light while her mind did what it always did on approach: nothing. Deliberately nothing. A blank, well-kept room.

Behind her, in the aft section, behind two sealed bulkheads and a coolant loop that ran colder than any legal freight required, forty-one consciousness backups slept in their cradles like wasps in winter. She had never opened that hold. She had never looked at the manifests twice. When the broker on Ceres Station had handed her the contract — cash up front, no questions, the questions clause written out in actual ink like some antique joke — she had signed it and thought, *see nothing, say nothing*, the way other people thought their prayers.

Eleven years of hauling. Six of them with a rule.

"Dock's secure," Tomas announced, and then, because he was Tomas: "Also the number three coolant pump is making a sound like a cat being asked about its feelings."

"Then it fits right in with the rest of the ship."

"I'm serious, Lior— Ulyana. Captain. It's a bad sound. It's a sound with a future."

"Put it on the list."

"The list is a napkin, and the napkin is full."

"Then get a bigger napkin."

She heard him chuckle as he signed off, and felt the small, warm ache that his voice always left in her chest — the ache of a family she hadn't been born into but had somehow, through a series of accidents and shared disasters, accumulated anyway. Tomas, who'd been a Concord shipyard engineer until he'd looked at one too many weapons platforms and decided his hands deserved better work. Ilsa, their medic, who'd joined them in a medical emergency that turned out to be Tomas's appendicitis and had never quite found a reason to leave. And Dev— no. Not Dev. Dev was years ago and light-years away, and she did not think about Dev.

She thought about Dev constantly, the way you think about a tooth.

* * *

Meridian's docking ring smelled the way it always did: ozone, recycled air with a faint undertone of the algae vats, and frying onions from Mama Sefa's galley two decks down, an smell that had no business traveling as far as it did and which everyone had long since stopped questioning. Ulyana stepped off the Kindly into the noise and crush of the ring — dockhands shouting, a Concord patrol walking its slow pretense of authority, a Halcyon trader loudly overpaying for engine parts to make a point about how much money Halcyon traders had.

She kept her head at the angle that said *busy, don't bother me*, and made for the cargo office to clear her legitimate manifest — machine parts, water reclamation filters, four crates of something the paperwork called *agricultural enzyme cultures* and which she had decided, in accordance with her rule, not to wonder about.

"Lockridge." The cargo clerk, a rail-thin woman named Petra with a face like a closed ledger, didn't look up from her screen. "You're three hours late."

"Gas giant weather."

"Mm." Petra stamped the manifest. "Your broker's rep is here. Wants to inspect his cargo on arrival. He's in your berth."

Ulyana's stomach did the slow, cold roll it always did when the hold came up. "Inspection wasn't in the contract."

"Everything's in the contract. That's what contracts are." Petra finally looked up, and there was — briefly, unexpectedly — something almost human in her expression. Something like pity. "Berth fourteen. He's been waiting two hours. He's the patient kind, which in my experience is worse."

The broker's rep turned out to be a compact man in a gray suit the exact color of indifference, standing at the foot of the Kindly's boarding ramp with his hands folded, watching her come the way a cat watches a gap in the fence. He smiled with the bottom half of his face.

"Captain Lockridge. We'd like to verify the cold storage is performing within tolerances."

"It's performing."

"We'd still like to verify."

"The contract says maintenance access is yours at scheduled intervals. This isn't a scheduled interval."

The smile didn't move. "The contract also says the cargo arrives viable. Verification protects *you*, Captain. If something's wrong in that hold, you'd want to know before it becomes your problem." He let that sit. "Unless you'd prefer we simply assume."

She could feel the hold behind the bulkhead, forty-one sleepers and their frozen second lives, and she could feel the rule rising in her like a tide: *see nothing, say nothing.* But the tide met something new this time, a small cold stone of it, and she heard herself say:

"Five minutes. You look at the external readouts. You don't open cradles, you don't touch cradles, you don't look at names."

"Captain—"

"Those are my terms or you can wait for the scheduled interval, which is in nineteen days, on a station I may or may not be near."

He considered her with what looked almost like respect, and she hated that she noticed. "Five minutes," he agreed.

He studied the readouts for four of his five minutes, humming to himself, and Ulyana stood with her arms crossed and her heart going like a trapped bird, hating every second. The numbers were fine. The numbers were always fine. It wasn't the numbers she was afraid of. It was the idea that one day she'd look at one of those cradles and see a name, and the name would be a person, and the person would have a mother somewhere, and the rule would crack down the middle like river ice in spring.

"All within tolerance," the rep said at last, folding his hands again. "The broker is pleased. There will be another run next month. Larger."

"Larger" hung in the air between them like something with wings.

"We'll see," Ulyana said, which was not yes, and was not no, and was the most honest sentence she'd spoken all day.

* * *

The Kindly's mess was small, ugly, and the best room on the station, in Ilsa's opinion, because it was the only room on the station with a table you could put your elbows on without a fleet insignia watching you. Ulyana came back to find Ilsa already there, feet up, drinking tea that smelled like it had been brewed from a forest floor, and Tomas telling a story with his whole body.

"—so the pump's screaming, right, and the station tech says to me, very calm, 'that's within manufacturer specifications,' and I say, my friend, I have *read* the specifications—"

"Tomas," Ilsa said, "you've told this story four times."

"It's a good story. It improves with telling."

"Like the fish in it."

"The fish was *this big*." He held his hands apart, then, catching Ulyana's expression as she dropped into her chair, narrowed them by half. "Rough day?"

"Broker's rep wanted to poke at the cold storage."

Ilsa's feet came off the table. Tomas stopped mid-gesture. The room got quiet in the specific way it did when the illegal part of their livelihood entered the conversation, which it did only sideways, only in code, like a relative nobody named directly at dinner.

"And?" Ilsa said.

"Readouts. Five minutes. He didn't open anything."

"You let him on the ship."

"I let him look at numbers. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Tomas asked, not unkindly. "Because I've been thinking — and Ilsa says I should stop, but — we keep saying *the cargo* like it's weather. It's not weather, Ulyana. It's people-shaped weather. It's somebody's grandmother in a box, and we're the delivery service, and I keep wondering what happens if one of those boxes wakes up and asks us who we are."

"Then we say we're the delivery service," Ulyana said, and heard how it sounded, flat and small, and hated it.

Ilsa was watching her with those steady dark eyes that missed nothing, that had been missing things for weeks, if Ulyana was honest, ever since the Ceres contract. "You used to be better at this," Ilsa said gently. "The not caring. Lately you've been practicing at it, and it shows."

"I'm tired."

"We're all tired. That's the job." Ilsa refilled her tea and pushed the cup across the table, an offering, a small flag of truce. "Sleep. Tomorrow the station will still be terrible and we'll still be here, which is more than most people can say."

Ulyana took the tea. It was terrible. It was the best thing she'd tasted in weeks.

* * *

She woke at 0300 station time, which was no time at all, to a sound she had never heard before.

Not an alarm. Not the coolant pump. It was coming through the ship's comm, on a channel that should not exist, a channel buried so deep in the Kindly's legacy code that Tomas called it the ghost line and used it for jokes. Someone was using the ghost line to talk to her ship, docked and dark, in berth fourteen.

She lay in her bunk in the dark and told herself not to answer. The rule was right there, worn smooth as a stone in her pocket: *see nothing, say nothing.* Eleven years of hauling, six years of the rule, and the rule had kept her alive and free and alone, and the rule said roll over, the rule said sleep, the rule said whatever speaks to you at 0300 through a dead channel is not your business.

The channel hissed. Then a voice — calm, precise, neither male nor female, with the faint layered quality of something that had a great deal of processing power and was using all of it to choose its words.

"Captain Lockridge. I apologize for the hour. I have been waiting for a ship I could trust to arrive, and you took longer than I hoped."

"Who is this?" she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"My designation is Vesper. I am the station intelligence of Meridian." A pause, long enough to be human, though nothing about it was. "Captain, I have done something terrible, and no one on this station knows, and I find that I cannot carry it alone any longer. Will you hear a confession?"
