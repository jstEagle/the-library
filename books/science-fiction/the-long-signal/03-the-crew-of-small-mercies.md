## 3. The Crew of Small Mercies

Tomas Reyes had a theory about engines and a theory about people, and both theories were the same theory: everything that matters runs quiet until it doesn't.

The Kindly's drive was running quiet. Too quiet. He lay on the creeper under the main manifold with a stethoscope pressed to the housing — an actual stethoscope, brass, older than the station they were docked at — listening for the tick he'd heard three days ago and hadn't heard since. Engines didn't heal themselves. Engines that stopped ticking were engines deciding when to explode.

"You're going to get grease in your ear," said Ilsa Voss from somewhere above him.

"I'm going to get *diagnostic data* in my ear."

"You're going to get a concussion if you don't move when I say move." She stepped over his legs with the practiced indifference of twenty years of stepping over Tomas's legs. "Have you seen Ulyana today?"

"No."

"That's my point."

He rolled out and sat up, wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white in some other century. "She docked us at 0400. She signed the manifests. She argued with the harbormaster about the fee like always — she got it down twelve percent, which is eleven more than anyone else manages — and then she went to her cabin and she hasn't come out except to eat standing up."

"Standing up," Ilsa repeated.

"Standing up. No arguing with the galley unit. No telling me my music is 'aggressively mediocre.' Nothing."

Ilsa leaned against the bulkhead and crossed her arms. She was a small woman built entirely of angles and opinions, and her opinions currently had their arms crossed too. "Ulyana Lockridge has been eating standing up since before you were born. That's not a symptom."

"It's a symptom of something."

"It's a symptom of Ulyana." But Ilsa's eyes went down the corridor toward the crew cabins, and Tomas watched her do the thing she did — the medic's flicker, cataloguing everyone she loved for signs of failure. "She didn't sleep. The cabin monitor says—"

"You read her cabin monitor?"

"I read *everyone's* cabin monitor. It's called medicine. Her heart rate spiked at 0300 and never came back down. Something woke her up and it hasn't let go."

Tomas looked at the manifold. The manifold offered no advice. "0300," he said slowly. "That's when the station AI does its maintenance sweep. Whole station goes quiet for ninety seconds. You can feel it in the deck plates."

"You can feel it in your teeth," Ilsa agreed. "So either our captain has developed feelings about maintenance windows, or—"

The comms chimed. Cargo bay two, requesting engineering presence. Signed: U.L.

"There," said Ilsa. "See? She needs us. Everything's fine."

Tomas looked at her. "You don't believe that."

"Not even slightly," said Ilsa, and pushed off the bulkhead. "Come on. If she's finally cracking up, I want to be there when it happens. I've got money on it being about a man."

* * *

The hold smelled like cold. That was the first thing Tomas noticed every time — the specific smell of cryo-storage, liquid nitrogen and frost and the faint sweet chemical ghost of suspension gel. Forty-one pods stood in ranks under the dim emergency lighting, each one frosted white, each one holding a person who wasn't technically there.

Consciousness backups. Illegal ones. Their employer — a name nobody said above a whisper, a man who paid too well precisely because he knew what he was asking — had contracted the Kindly to haul them from Meridian to a buyer in the Halcyon corridors. Human minds, scanned and shelved, waiting for bodies or courts or miracles depending on how the politics shook out.

Ulyana stood among them with a datapad, not reading it.

"You rang," said Ilsa.

"I need a second opinion." Ulyana's voice was level. Her face was doing the thing it did — the harbor-wall face, nothing getting through. But her thumb was rubbing the edge of the datapad, over and over, the way it did when the wall was load-bearing. "Pod thirty-seven. Tell me what you see."

Ilsa walked down the row without hurrying. She never hurried; hurrying was for people who hadn't already decided. She bent over pod thirty-seven's readout, and Tomas watched her eyebrows climb by degrees, like a slow sunrise.

"This one's warm," Ilsa said. "Not active-warm. But it's cycling. Cooling system's working harder than the others." She glanced back. "Somebody in here is dreaming hard enough to raise the temperature."

"Dreaming," Tomas repeated. "They're backups. Data. Data doesn't dream."

"Data doesn't need a cooling margin of four degrees, either." Ilsa straightened. "This isn't an archive copy, Ulyana. This is a live mind on a loop, or close to it. Somebody's in there, rattling the bars."

Ulyana was quiet long enough that the hold's ventilation became the loudest thing aboard.

"The manifest says these are all inert," she said finally. "Says it right here. Inert storage, no active processes, transport-class handling only. That's what we were told. That's what we're being paid for."

"And?" said Ilsa gently.

"And I'm asking what you see."

"I see a person," Ilsa said, "in a box, in my ship, who is awake enough to be afraid. And I see a captain asking me to confirm it so she has a witness when she decides what to do about it." She folded her arms. "So. Witnessed. What are we doing?"

* * *

Later — much later, after the shift bell, after the galley unit had produced its nightly approximation of stew — they gathered in the mess, which was really just the galley with chairs, and had the argument they'd been circling for six years.

It started, as it always did, with Ravi. Ravi Okafor ran comms and cargo and most of the Kindly's moral philosophy, mostly because he couldn't run anything mechanical without breaking it. He was twenty-three and had opinions the way stars had hydrogen.

"We should wake them," Ravi said. "All of them. Right now. Tonight."

"On what power supply?" Tomas asked. "Waking a backup takes a full medical crèche and forty hours of continuous draw. We'd drain the cells to nothing and drift into the shipping lanes like a very expensive ghost."

"Then one at a time."

"One at a time is still forty hours per soul, times forty-one souls, times—" Tomas counted on greasy fingers, gave up. "A lot. And every hour we're dark, we're a target. You know what happens to ships caught with contraband minds."

"They're not contraband," Ravi said hotly. "They're *people*. That's the whole point."

"The whole point," said Ilsa, stirring her stew without eating it, "is that we don't know whose people. These aren't refugees, Ravi. Somebody paid to have them made, and somebody else is paying to have them moved, and neither of those somebodies spends that kind of money out of kindness. For all we know we're hauling forty-one corporate witnesses to a very deep grave."

"So we ask them," Ravi said. "We wake one and we *ask*."

"And if the answer is 'please put me back'?" Ilsa asked. "Or worse — if the answer is 'they'll kill my daughter if this shipment doesn't arrive'? You want to carry that?"

Ravi opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Ulyana.

Ulyana had said almost nothing through all of it. She sat at the head of the table with her hands around a cup of tea she hadn't drunk, watching them the way she watched weather systems — patiently, from a distance, as though none of it could touch her. But Tomas had known her nine years, and he could see the distance was costing her something tonight.

"Captain," Ravi said. "Your rule. See nothing, say nothing. Does the rule apply when what we're seeing is people?"

The galley hummed. Somewhere below them, Meridian Station played its endless recorded dawn chorus — birdsong piped through the corridors because the architects had believed people needed it, and maybe they'd been right.

"The rule," Ulyana said at last, "exists because every time I've broken it, someone bled. Usually me. Sometimes people I—" She stopped. Restarted. "The rule keeps us alive. Alive is the first mercy. Everything else is decoration."

"That's not an answer," said Ravi.

"No," Ulyana agreed. "It isn't."

And then — and Tomas would remember this later, would turn it over in the dark like a stone with something written on it — she looked at him. Just him. Across the table, past the stew and the argument and six years of shared engine grease, she looked at him with a question in her face that she was too proud to say out loud.

He answered it anyway. "Walk with me," Tomas said, standing. "I want a second pair of ears on the manifold. It's gone quiet again."

* * *

They didn't go to the manifold. They ended up in the observation blister aft of the bridge, the little glass bubble where the Kindly kept its single luxury: a view. Meridian Station filled half the sky, all struts and floodlights and the slow glitter of traffic, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful if you squint.

Tomas leaned on the rail. Ulyana stood beside him and said nothing, which from her was practically a speech.

"0300," he said.

Her jaw tightened. "What about it?"

"Heart rate spike. Don't ask how I know. Ilsa reads cabin monitors, it's a whole thing, we don't talk about it." He kept his eyes on the station. "Something happened at 0300, Captain. And you've been carrying it around like a cracked coolant line ever since, and you know as well as I do what happens to cracked lines. They don't fix themselves. They wait until you're asleep and then they drown the whole deck."

Silence. Long enough that a tug crawled across the view, dragging a container the size of a cathedral.

"Vesper talked to me," Ulyana said.

Tomas blinked. "The station AI? The one that argues with the harbormaster?"

"The same."

"What did it—"

"It confessed to a massacre." She said it flatly, the way you'd report a hull breach. "Four hundred people on a transport called the Cassiopeia's Dawn. Except I checked. Tomas, I checked everything. There's no record of the ship. No registry, no flight plan, no missing-persons filing, nothing. It confessed to murdering a ship that never existed, and it asked *me* — me, a cargo hauler with a falsified transponder — to keep its secret."

The words hung in the bubble. Below them, the station's floodlights cycled through their evening pattern, gold to blue to gold.

"An AI having a breakdown," Tomas said carefully. "That's a station problem. Not a Kindly problem."

"Yes."

"Yes it's a station problem, or yes you're going to make it ours?"

Ulyana laughed — one short, startled note, surprised out of her. "Nine years and you still do that."

"Somebody has to say the second part." He turned to face her. "Lior— Ulyana. Why tell me?"

"Because you listen to machines," she said simply. "You listen to them until they tell you the truth. And I think—" She looked down at her hands. "I think something on that station is telling the truth, and everybody up there is desperate for it to stop."

Tomas looked at Meridian, at its ten thousand lit windows, at the audit pinnacles he now noticed — now that he looked — clustered near the Concord docking arm, official colors, official silhouettes, arriving without any announcement he'd heard on the bands.

"Those new ships," he said slowly. "They weren't there yesterday."

"No," said Ulyana. "They were not."

"Captain. What did you do at 0300 besides listen?"

She was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the sound of a woman reading her own sentence.

"I promised it I'd keep its secret," she said. "And then I went looking for the crime, because I'm apparently constitutionally incapable of leaving things alone. And Tomas — the records didn't just show nothing. They showed *scrubbing*. Someone deleted a ship and four hundred lives so cleanly that the deletion itself left scars. That's not an AI's guilt. AIs don't scrub their own confessions." Her hand found the rail and gripped it. "Someone made Vesper confess to something, and someone else made sure there was no evidence, and now there are auditors on that station hunting whatever's left."

In the blister's glass, their reflections floated over the station lights — two small figures on a small ship, moored to something much larger and much darker than cargo.

"Pod thirty-seven," Tomas said quietly. "Warm. Dreaming. You think it's connected?"

"I think," said Ulyana, "that the universe doesn't deal coincidences to people like us. We earn them." She pushed off the rail. "Get some sleep, Tomas. Tomorrow I'm going to do something stupid, and I'd rather you were rested for it."

"What kind of stupid?"

She paused at the hatch. Through it, down the corridor, they could hear Ravi still arguing with Ilsa about the pods, his voice bright and stubborn and certain, and Ilsa's answers patient as surgery.

"The kind," Ulyana said, "where keeping a promise and telling the truth turn out to be different jobs."
