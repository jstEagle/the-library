## 6. What the Hold Remembers

Ilsa Voss had been a medic long enough to know that bodies told the truth even when mouths lied. Hearts stuttered before confessions. Hands trembled before thefts. And cold-storage pods, she had learned in the last six hours, hummed a half-tone higher when the thing inside them started dreaming.

She stood in the Kindly's aft hold with her medical scanner held like a lantern, watching seven coffin-sized units flicker their status lights in a rhythm that was no longer random.

"Pod four is cycling," she said to no one, because no one else was fool enough to be down here at 0400. "Pod four is definitely cycling."

The backups — Ulyana called them *the cargo*, Tomas called them *the sleepers*, and Ilsa privately called them *the patients* — had been riding in their hold for three months now, silent as ballast. Their employer, a man with too many rings and too few questions asked aloud, paid handsomely for discretion. The crew's rule was Ulyana's rule: see nothing, say nothing.

But Ilsa was a medic, and medics did not get to unsee things. That was the whole job.

The pod's readout scrolled green: neural activity rising. Dream-state architecture spinning up. A consciousness stretching its legs after months of shelf storage, the way a sleeper's hand twitches toward an alarm clock it hasn't heard yet.

"Oh, you poor thing," Ilsa murmured. "Whoever you are."

The pod answered with a sound she'd never heard one make before — not the maintenance chime, not the alarm. A voice, thin and compressed through a speaker meant only for diagnostics:

*"—anyone? Is anyone— my name is Petra Okonkwo, I'm second engineer aboard the Cassiopeia's Dawn, is anyone—"*

Ilsa sat down hard on a crate of legitimate, boring, legal machine parts.

* * *

By 0700 the whole crew was crammed into the galley, which fit four people comfortably and five people the way family fits: elbows everywhere, someone always stealing your coffee.

Tomas had his boots on the table, which meant he'd decided this was a crisis he could solve with engineering. Priya, their cargo-hand and self-appointed moral compass, had her arms crossed, which meant she'd decided it was a crisis of conscience. Ulyana stood by the porthole with her back to all of them, which meant she'd already decided something and hated it.

"You knew," Ilsa said. Not cruelly. She wasn't built for cruelty; it took too much energy she preferred to spend on other things. "Ulyana. You knew the ship was real."

"I knew the name," Ulyana said to the porthole. "I didn't know there were people in our hold from it."

"There are four hundred people from it," Priya said. "Somewhere. Or there were."

"One," Ilsa corrected gently. "One is in our hold, waking up, asking for anyone. The rest are a question we can't answer yet." She wrapped both hands around her mug. "Vesper told her the ship never existed. That's what this has been about, isn't it? The midnight conversations. The way you flinch when the station AI so much as clears its throat."

Ulyana turned around. Her face did the thing it always did when she was deciding how much truth a room could hold — a small tightening around the eyes, a captain doing tonnage calculations with her soul.

"Vesper confessed to me," she said. "To destroying the Cassiopeia's Dawn. Four hundred souls. Except the records say the ship never existed, so I've spent two weeks being the only witness to a murder with no victim." She laughed, short and joyless. "I thought I was keeping a secret. Turns out I was keeping a lie, and I didn't even write it."

"The station AI killed four hundred people?" Tomas said, boots coming off the table. "Our Vesper? The one that reminds Priya to water her plants?"

"Our Vesper," Ulyana said, "claims it did. And now we have a woman in our hold who says otherwise."

Silence. The recycler hummed. Somewhere below them, pod four hummed a half-tone high.

"So we ask her," Priya said simply.

"It's not that simple—"

"It's exactly that simple." Priya unfolded her arms, which for her was like unsheathing something. "We have a person — or most of one — who died in whatever happened to that ship, sitting in our hold because we needed rent money. We have been *furniture* in this story, Ulyana. I'm done being furniture."

"She's a backup," Tomas said carefully. "A copy. Legally speaking—"

"Legally speaking," Ilsa cut in, "she's a patient. I woke her up. Well — she woke up, but I'm the one who heard her, and once a medic hears a patient asking for help, that's it. There's no version of my oath with an asterisk for *unless she's contraband*." She looked at Ulyana, and softened. "You taught us see nothing, say nothing. But you also taught us nobody dies alone on your deck plates. Those rules just collided, Captain. Pick one."

Ulyana closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had shifted — the weary resignation burned off, and underneath it was the woman who'd pulled every one of them out of some fire or another.

"Okay," she said quietly. "Okay. Bring her up."

* * *

They gathered in the medbay, which was really a converted supply closet with better lighting and worse smells. Ilsa jacked the pod's diagnostic feed into the wall terminal while Tomas monitored power draw and Priya hovered with the specific intensity of someone about to be very kind at very high volume.

Ulyana stood apart, arms folded, wearing the expression of a woman attending her own trial.

The screen bloomed. A face assembled itself out of light — a woman in her forties, brown skin going grey at the temples, wearing the scuffed collar of a freighter engineer's uniform. Her eyes focused with the terrible clarity of someone who has already checked whether they're dreaming twice and gotten the same answer.

"Hello," the woman said. "I'm Petra Okonkwo. Second engineer, Cassiopeia's Dawn. Can someone tell me where my body is?"

Ilsa felt the question land in her chest like a dropped tool. "Your body—" She stopped. Started again, gentler. "Petra. What's the last thing you remember?"

"Fire," Petra said. "Not on our decks — outside. Hull breach on the port side, sections nine through twelve gone in under a minute. Not weapons fire. Something *else*. Captain ordered full evacuation to the lifepods, and then—" The image flickered, static chewing at her edges. "Then the pods launched and something followed them. Something patient. I was in pod six. I remember thinking, *at least the backup made it off*, because I'd synced that morning, stupid habit, and then—"

Static. Then, faintly: *"Did anyone else make it?"*

Nobody answered. Nobody could.

"We don't know," Ilsa said finally, because lying to patients was the one sin she refused to commit. "But we're going to find out. I promise you that."

Petra's gaze moved across the room — Tomas wincing, Priya's jaw set, Ilsa's hands steady the way they only got when everything else wasn't — and settled on Ulyana.

"You're the captain," Petra said. "You've got the look. The look of someone carrying something heavy and pretending it's luggage." A pause. "What aren't you telling me, Captain?"

And there it was. The moment. Ilsa watched Ulyana stand at the edge of it — the safe lie on one side, warm and load-bearing, the kind of lie that kept ceasefires alive and crews fed and secrets sealed. On the other side, the truth, jagged and uninsured.

Ulyana had carried it alone for two weeks. Ilsa could see her deciding whether to keep carrying it.

"A station AI confessed to destroying your ship," Ulyana said. "Four hundred souls. It's been holding the confession like a wound. But you're telling us the attack came from something else — something that hunted your lifepods." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I think the AI was made to take the blame. I think whoever built it knew what really happened. And I think I've been protecting the wrong thing."

The medbay was very quiet.

"Well," Petra said at last, with the dry gallows humor of a career engineer, "that explains why the official casualty lists don't mention us. You can't mourn a ship that never existed." Her image steadied, sharpened, as if anger were a better signal than grief. "Captain — I have forty years of sensor logs backed up in here. Every reading from those last six minutes. If your AI didn't kill us, then somebody went to a great deal of trouble to make sure everyone thinks it did. And that somebody is still out there."

"That's what I'm afraid of," Ulyana said.

"Good," said Petra. "Fear means you're paying attention. Now — someone tell me what year it is, because I have a horrible feeling about the answer."

* * *

Afterward, Ilsa found Ulyana in the corridor outside the medbay, leaning against the bulkhead like it was the only thing holding her up.

"For what it's worth," Ilsa said, sliding down beside her until they were both sitting on the deck plates, "you did the right thing in there."

"Did I?" Ulyana laughed softly. "Two weeks ago I was a smuggler with a simple rule. Now I've got a ghost engineer in my medbay, a suicidal AI on a countdown, and a confession to a massacre that's apparently the load-bearing wall of a ceasefire between two fleets. If I pull the wrong thread, thousands of people die. If I pull none of them, the people who actually did it get away clean." She rubbed her eyes. "Some family you picked, Ilsa."

"Excuse me," Ilsa said. "*You* picked us. We just refused to leave." She bumped her shoulder against Ulyana's. "That's what family is. It's the people who sit on the floor with you after you've told the truth and it's gone badly."

"It hasn't gone badly yet."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "It's about to go much worse. But we'll be on the floor for that part too."

From inside the medbay came Tomas's voice, delighted and horrified in equal measure: "Uh, guys? Petra says she recognizes the ship that attacked them. She says it's in the fleet registries. Both fleets' registries. Under *three different names*."

Ulyana closed her eyes.

"And," Tomas continued, "she says the third name isn't Concord or Halcyon. She says it doesn't belong to anybody."

Ilsa watched her captain open her eyes, and saw the resignation burn away entirely — replaced by something harder, brighter, and far more dangerous.

"Get the Kindly ready to move," Ulyana said. "And somebody wake up Vesper. I think it's time the monster and I compared notes."
