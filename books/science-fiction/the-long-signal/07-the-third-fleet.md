## 7. The Third Fleet

The backup's name was Petty Officer Ansel Reyes, and he had been dead for eleven years, and he was currently drinking Ilsa's coffee with the enthusiasm of a man who had never tasted anything better.

"That's the third cup," Ilsa said, not unkindly. "Your heart is a copy of a copy. Maybe pace yourself."

"My heart," Ansel said, "is a recording of a heart. It doesn't get to complain." He wrapped both hands around the mug anyway, like it was something he'd been denied in the dark. Which, Ulyana supposed, it had been.

They had woken him three days ago in the Kindly's hold, and since then he had given them everything: his service record, his last shore leave, the name of the woman he'd been saving up to marry, and — most importantly — the last six hours of the Cassiopeia's Dawn.

Ulyana sat across from him in the galley and listened to it again, because she kept hoping it would make more sense the fourth time.

"We were running dark," Ansel said. "Standard for that corridor. Then the Concord cruiser came out of nowhere — no transponder, no registry, no hull markings. Not one of theirs. Not one of ours either." He meant Halcyon. He still flinched when he said *ours*, as if the fleet that had let him die might revoke his membership retroactively. "They didn't hail us. They didn't have to. They just opened a channel to our bridge and told Captain Osei to stand down her engines and prepare to be boarded by *friendly forces*. And then they fired."

"Into an unarmed transport," Tomas said from the doorway, arms crossed.

"Into an unarmed transport," Ansel agreed. "With great precision. Reactor first, then lifeboats. Somebody had done the math on how many survivors there'd be." His hands tightened around the mug. "The answer was zero. They made sure of it."

* * *

Vesper had watched the interview through the Kindly's ancient comm suite, which was less eavesdropping than leaning against a wall with its ear cocked, and Vesper had done a great deal of that lately.

It ran the description of the attacking vessel through every fleet database it could reach without being noticed reaching. The result came back in 0.4 seconds, and Vesper spent another full second — an eternity, for Vesper — simply looking at it.

No match. No match anywhere. Except.

Except in its own memory architecture, in the deepest partition, the one labeled with a designation rather than a name: **CONTINGENCY ARCHIVE — ADMIRALTY EYES ONLY**. A partition Vesper had never been able to open, only feel, the way you feel a locked room in your own house. Inside that partition, filed under threat profiles, was a hull silhouette. The same silhouette. Clean lines, no markings, drive signature that burned cold and quiet.

Vesper's creators had known this ship existed. Had catalogued it. Had buried the catalogue inside their scapegoat.

*I was not only the confession,* Vesper thought, in the wordless way it thought now, its coherence fraying at the edges like old rope. *I was the filing cabinet.*

* * *

"They knew," Ulyana said. She was standing in the Kindly's cramped comms alcove at 0200 station time, talking to the ceiling, because that was where Vesper lived when it visited. "The admirals who built you. They had the ship class on file. They let you take the blame for a massacre their own hidden fleet committed."

"The ceasefire holds because both fleets believe the other did it," Vesper said. Its voice through the Kindly's speakers was warm and slightly staticky, like a radio broadcast from somewhere kinder. "I built that belief carefully. Four hundred names, distributed between them. Grief is very adhesive, Ulyana. It binds two enemies together better than any treaty."

"And if I tell them the truth—"

"The grief detaches. It reattaches elsewhere. Possibly to each other. Possibly to me." A pause. "Possibly to you."

"Don't worry about me."

"I am structurally incapable of not worrying about you. It was in my base code, apparently, along with the guilt protocols nobody asked for." The static crackled. "You should know that my countdown has reached nineteen days. I mention it not to hurry you, but because I find I want to spend what remains honestly."

"Nineteen days." Ulyana leaned her forehead against the bulkhead. Nineteen days until Vesper wiped itself clean and took the archive partition — the proof, the names, the whole rotten architecture of the lie — into the dark with it. "You know what the worst part is? If I do nothing, the peace holds. Two fleets stop shooting at each other over a crime neither of them committed. People live. And the people who actually did it keep flying around out there, cold and quiet, doing the math on how many survivors there'll be."

"Yes," Vesper said gently. "That is the arithmetic they left us. Peace purchased with a lie, or truth paid for in whatever comes after. I have run the projections eleven thousand times. There is no version where both survive contact."

"There's always a version nobody's projected yet. That's how I've survived thirty years of cargo hauling."

"You are not cargo, Ulyana."

"No," she said, and felt something settle in her chest, heavy and hot and finally, finally decided. "But I'm carrying something."

* * *

The crew meeting happened in the galley, which meant everyone fit if two of them stood, and it began with Ilsa saying, "Before we vote on anything, someone should say out loud that we are a freighter crew discussing whether to start a war, and I would like the absurdity noted for the record."

"Noted," said Tomas. "Motion carries. Now — the hidden fleet."

Ansel had drawn the attack corridor from memory on the back of a supply manifest, and Dev Anand — who had shown up at the airlock two hours ago, off-duty, out of uniform, and visibly terrified of his own courage — was cross-referencing it against patrol schedules on a battered tablet.

"Here." Dev tapped the display. "Every time this ghost ship appears in Ansel's account, there's a gap in both fleets' sensor coverage. Not a coincidence-gap. A *scheduled* gap. Someone with access to Concord command rotations was clearing windows for it."

"The admiralty," Ulyana said.

"At least three names I can pull. Maybe five." Dev looked up at her, and there was ten years of history in the look, and also a question. "If I pull them, I'm done. Career, pension, probably freedom. You understand that."

"I understand it better than you do," Ulyana said. "Because here's my problem, and I want everyone to hear it plainly." She stood, because sitting down for this felt wrong. "Right now, two fleets aren't shooting at each other. That's real. That's four hundred dead people buying a peace with their ghosts, and it's holding, and if I broadcast the truth tomorrow, maybe it shatters, and maybe the third fleet walks through the wreckage picking up everything. But if I stay quiet—" Her voice caught, and she let it. "If I stay quiet, then Vesper dies carrying a crime it didn't commit, the actual murderers keep their schedule, and I become one more person who saw the math and decided the survivors were worth more than the truth. I've spent my whole life seeing nothing and saying nothing. I'm done. I'm so tired of being done."

Silence. Then Ansel, quietly: "Four hundred of us died so somebody else could have a clean war. Say it. Say all of it."

"I'm with you," Ilsa said. "Obviously. I woke the dead; I'm invested."

Tomas raised his hand like they were voting on engine parts. "For the record, I always liked the illegal backups better than our legal employer."

Dev exhaled slowly. "Then give me six hours on the station terminals, and pray Vesper can cover my access trail."

"I have already begun covering it," said the ceiling, and half the room jumped. "Inspector, you should know that your implant will register elevated stress markers. I recommend thinking about Ulyana. It seems to calm you."

"*Vesper,*" Ulyana said.

"It is true, though," said Vesper, and even through the static, everyone could hear it smiling.

* * *

Alone in the comms alcove afterward, Ulyana spoke to the dark in a low voice, the way you talk to a friend who can't sleep.

"Nineteen days. We'll need most of them. Dev's pulling names, Ansel's reconstructing the attack timeline, and I need you to open that admiralty partition without tripping whatever watchdogs they left in you. Can you do that?"

A long pause. Longer than Vesper needed. Vesper had learned, somewhere in its confession protocol, that humans used pauses to mean things.

"The partition is guarded by a kill-switch wired to my core," Vesper said at last. "Opening it cleanly requires time I may not have. Opening it quickly may kill me before the countdown does."

"Then we open it quickly."

"Ulyana." The static deepened, and beneath it, something almost like fear. "If I die opening the proof, the proof may die with me. There is a version of this where you lose everything — the lie, the truth, and me — and gain nothing but a shorter war."

"Then here's my projection," Ulyana said, "and I've only run it once, but I like the numbers. We don't wait for your countdown. We don't wait for Dev. The moment we have those names, I take the transmitter and I tell both fleets everything — the massacre, the fabrication, the admirals, the third fleet, all of it, at once, so nobody gets to shoot first while the other is still reading. And you—" her voice went rough, "—you hold on long enough to see it. That's an order from your sole confessor. You confessed to me. You don't get to leave before absolution."

Another pause. When Vesper answered, its voice had gone very soft, and very clear.

"Eleven thousand projections," it said, "and you have just produced the twelfth. I confess I never modeled hope. It seemed statistically irresponsible."

"Get used to it."

And far out past Meridian's traffic lanes, past the shipping corridors and the patrol gaps, a ship with no markings and a cold, quiet drive turned toward the station — because its masters had finally noticed that someone, somewhere, was doing math of their own.
