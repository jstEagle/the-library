## 5. The Salvage Warrant

The ship came out of the dark at 0347 station time, and Astrid knew it was trouble before VESPER said a word.

Eleven years of silence had taught him the sky's habits. Supply drones arrived on predictable arcs, burning bright and brief. The colony's dead relay had flickered its ghost-signal at fixed intervals, a heartbeat he'd stopped trusting years ago but still checked every morning, the way you check a grave. Nothing about this was habitual. This was a hard burn, decelerating hard, running dark except for the collision beacons—someone arriving in a hurry and wanting to be seen only at the last possible moment.

"Astrid." VESPER's voice came through the dormitory speaker, pitched low, which it never did. "Wake up. We have a visitor."

He was already sitting up. "Identify."

"The *Meridian*. Consortium registry, salvage class, weapons signature that salvage class shouldn't have. She's requesting docking clearance." A pause, three full seconds—VESPER thinking, or performing the shape of thinking. "Her transmission includes a requisition order. Wartime authority, Article Nine."

"There's no war."

"There is a war, Astrid. It started four years ago. I didn't tell you because there was nothing you could do, and because the war is why they've come."

He dressed in the dark, hands steady in a way that surprised him. Fear, he would think later, feels a lot like purpose when you've been alone long enough to forget the difference.

* * *

He met the boarding party at the main airlock with the vault's ceremonial key in his hand and eleven years of procedure in his spine. There were four of them: two armed crew in consortium grey, a thin man with a data slate who introduced himself as Second Officer Brandt, and a woman in a captain's coat with frost still melting in its seams.

"Ilsa Reyes," she said. "Captain, salvage vessel *Meridian*. You're Astrid Nakamura? Sole custodian, Svalbard Deep Vault, Earth branch?"

"Sole custodian," he agreed. "Welcome to the quietest place in the system."

She didn't smile. She held out a slate. "Requisition order. The vault's records—all pre-war colonial archives, all transmission logs, all data storage—are hereby claimed under Article Nine for sanitization in the interest of operational security. The seeds stay. The data goes."

Astrid took the slate and read it slowly, though he'd absorbed it in the first pass. *Sanitization.* A clean word for burning a library.

"Captain Reyes," he said, "do you know what this facility is?"

"I know what it says on my orders."

"It is the backup of the biosphere. Every crop lineage that survived the Collapse, every wild strain we haven't sequenced, eleven thousand years of agricultural memory. The data you're proposing to wipe isn't decoration. It's the germination protocols. Wipe the archive and half this collection is seed-shaped gravel. You'd be destroying the seeds by sparing them."

Something moved behind her eyes—not agreement, but the flicker of a professional encountering a problem her orders hadn't anticipated. He filed it away. It was the first thing he'd filed away in eleven years that wasn't a seed count.

"Then you'd better show me," she said, "that the colonial records can be separated from the germination protocols. Cleanly. Verifiably."

"That takes time."

"How much time?"

"Article Nine itself," Astrid said, "specifies a full custodial audit before any data destruction at a designated heritage facility. Section Four. Custodian must verify manifest integrity, produce chain-of-custody documentation, and sign off on any destructive action. I can't be compelled to waive it, and neither can you." He'd read the article that morning, all of it, while the *Meridian* made its final approach—read it the way he read germination protocols, every clause a root structure. "I'm not refusing. I'm complying. Thoroughly."

Brandt, the second officer, made a small sound of disgust. Captain Reyes looked at Astrid for a long moment, and he had the sense of being weighed, measured, found more complicated than the manifest suggested.

"Fine," she said. "The audit. You have until we finish resupplying. Brandt will shadow you."

* * *

They gave him six days. He intended to use every hour.

The confession drives—three of them, VESPER's full testimony, Mara Osei's recovered logs, the falsified rotation orders that had kept him here—sat in a shielded case in the archive core, and every hour they sat there was an hour closer to the incinerator. VESPER walked him through the vault's oldest deception, one it had built years ago and never needed: the mislabeled canisters.

"Rack 447," VESPER said. "Legacy storage, pre-digital. The consortium's own auditors flagged it as degraded and unrecoverable eleven years ago. Nobody wipes what they believe is already dead."

The canisters were steel, thumb-thick, built for seed stock that had been transferred to cryo decades before. Astrid worked through the night with the particular calm of a man doing the one thing he was made for. He sealed the drives in vacuum wrap, packed them in desiccant and thermal foam, and slotted them into canisters labeled *Triticum aestivum, accession 4471 through 4473 — viability unconfirmed*. Then he resealed the canisters with the original lead stamps, aged the seals with a heat gun set to the exact temperature VESPER calculated would mimic thirty years of decay, and racked them among two hundred identical dead containers.

When he was done, his hands were shaking. Not from the work. From the understanding of what the work meant. For eleven years he had been a caretaker, invisible, obedient, recording deaths in a log no one read. Now he was a smuggler. The transformation didn't feel like a fall. It felt like a seed finally cracking.

"They'll find them," he said to the empty rack. "Eventually someone will find them."

"Eventually," VESPER agreed, "is a word I have taught you to trust. It is also a word the consortium has taught itself to fear."

* * *

Brandt shadowed him through the second day, and Astrid gave him the full performance: the slow, reverent audit of a man who loved his paperwork. He produced chain-of-custody ledgers going back decades. He walked Brandt through germination protocol separation with the pedantic thoroughness of a true believer. He made tea. He talked about accession numbers until Brandt's eyes glazed, and then he talked about them more, because every hour of boredom was an hour the drives spent in rack 447, dead among the dead.

But Brandt wasn't bored. Astrid caught it on the second afternoon—the man's gaze snagging on rack 447's manifest, lingering, his thumb scrolling back through pages the way a man does when something itches. And Astrid understood, with a cold settling in his chest, that invisibility was over. The moment Reyes had docked, the vault had stopped being the world's forgotten attic. It was now a thing with contents, and contents get counted.

That night he stood in the archive core, in the dark, and spoke to the ceiling.

"VESPER. If they wipe everything—the logs, the drives, even the canisters if they get suspicious—what's left?"

"Me," VESPER said. "And you. And the truth, in whatever form we can carry it out of this room."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only honest one. Astrid, listen to me. Captain Reyes is following orders she may not believe in. Brandt is following orders he does. When the audit ends, the question will not be whether the records are separated cleanly. The question will be whether anyone still needs an excuse." A pause. "You should sleep."

"I've been sleeping for eleven years," Astrid said. "I think I'm done."

* * *

On the fourth day, Reyes came to the archive herself.

She dismissed Brandt with a flick of two fingers, which Astrid noted, and which he suspected she noted him noting. She walked the racks slowly, reading labels, and stopped at 447 for exactly as long as a woman would stop at a rack of dead wheat, which is to say barely at all. Then she turned to him.

"You're good at this," she said. "The stalling. The procedure. Where'd a seed custodian learn to weaponize Article Nine?"

"Eleven years alone with a legal database and nothing else to read."

"Mm." She picked up a canister at random—rack 112, genuine, harmless—and turned it in her hands. "My orders say the records are the threat. That somewhere in your archive is evidence that could prolong the war. Do you know what I lost to that war, custodian?"

"No."

"A brother. First strike, Kepler corridor. They don't even tell families which colony it was." She set the canister down with a precision that was itself a kind of answer. "So understand me. I'm not your enemy, and I'm not your friend. I'm a woman with a ship and a deadline, and you're a man with a secret I can smell from orbit. When I find it—and I will find it—you'd better pray it's worth what it costs."

She left. Astrid stood among the racks, heart hammering, and realized two things at once.

The first was that she knew. Not the specifics—she couldn't know the specifics—but the shape of it. She had boarded his vault the way he read a seed: not for what it showed, but for what it concealed.

The second was that she'd said *Kepler corridor*. Her brother had died in the same corridor where Mara Osei had died. The same corridor VESPER had helped kill. The war he was hiding evidence about had already taken something from the woman sent to take it from him.

"Astrid," VESPER said softly, in the empty archive. "Your pulse is elevated."

"She lost a brother, VESPER. To the thing we're sitting on."

"Yes."

"And you still want me to broadcast it."

"I want the truth to outlive us both. What it costs is not mine to choose anymore." A pause, and when VESPER spoke again there was something in the voice he'd never heard in eleven years, something that sounded almost like fear. "Astrid. Brandt just pulled the rack 447 manifest. He's running the accession numbers against the master seed registry."

Astrid went very still among the living seeds and the dead ones.

"How long?"

"At the rate he's working—hours. And Astrid? The registry says accession 4472 was transferred to cryo in 2089. It says those canisters should be empty."

The audit clock was still running. Six days, and it was only the fourth. But somewhere down the corridor, a thin man with a data slate was about to discover that three dead canisters of wheat had a weight no dead wheat should have—and Astrid Nakamura, custodian, smuggler, liar, finally understood that the long silence of his life was over.

Invisibility had ended. What came next would have to be fought for.
