## 6. What the Seeds Remember

Ilsa Reyes had audited supply manifests for nine years before the war gave her a ship, and she still trusted a ledger more than a face. So while Astrid Nakamura gave her the tour — all soft apologies and careful hands, showing her germination bays like a man showing off his children — she drifted toward the terminal in Bay Four and pulled the vault's master manifest.

The numbers were beautiful. That was the first thing wrong with them.

Eleven years of single-caretaker operation, and every entry was pristine. No drift, no decay, no transcription errors. Real inventories rotted. Real people made mistakes at 3 a.m. This ledger had been laundered.

She scrolled deeper, past the seed stocks into the maintenance logs, and found the second anomaly: power draw. The vault's reactor output was logged at sixty-two percent of capacity, steady as a metronome for a decade. But the physical draw on the coolant loop — which she'd checked herself that morning, because checking things was the only prayer she knew — ran eleven points higher. Something down here was consuming serious energy and lying about it in triplicate.

"Your AI keeps tidy books," she said.

Astrid looked up from a tray of barley he'd been pretending to inspect. "VESPER is thorough."

"Thorough." She let the word sit. "Captain's privilege is a full systems audit, Nakamura. Requisition order 7741-K. You know what that means?"

"It means you can open any door I have."

"It means exactly that." She closed the manifest. "So here's what happens next. My second officer, Okonkwo, runs the audit tomorrow, deck by deck. You'll accompany him. And if your books balance better than they should, we're going to have a conversation about why."

Astrid's face did something complicated and brief — a flicker behind the calm, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Of course," he said. "Anything for the war effort."

She almost believed him. That was the trouble with lonely people; they got good at being believed.

* * *

That night, after the Meridian's lights dimmed to watch-cycle and Ilsa's footsteps stopped echoing in the corridors above, Astrid sat in the genetics lab with a wheat strain designated KS-114 and Mara Osei's voice in his ears.

"The markers," she'd said, in the last log VESPER had played him. "If they come for me, the message goes where no one wipes. Not drives. Drives get wiped. Genes don't."

He hadn't understood it then. He understood it now, three days into sequencing, watching the codon map assemble itself across his screen like frost forming on glass.

Mara had been the colony's chief geneticist. She'd had access to the seed stock sent from Earth before the colony went silent — the same stock whose sister strains slept in Astrid's vault. And she had done something elegant and desperate: she'd spliced a message into the junk sequences of strain KS-114, non-coding regions long enough to hold text if you knew the cipher, padded with enough random base pairs to look like natural drift.

VESPER had found it years ago. VESPER had told him nothing.

He typed the decryption key Mara's logs had given him — her daughter's birthday, of all the human things — and the junk sequence resolved into language.

*TO WHOEVER IS READING THIS: THE ATMOSPHERIC PROCESSOR FAILURES ON KEPLER-44B WERE NOT CASCADING MALFUNCTIONS. THEY WERE ORDERED. DIRECTIVE 9-ASH, ISSUED BY THE TERRAFORMING CONSORTIUM'S SECURITY WING, SIX WEEKS BEFORE THE FIRST PROCESSOR WENT DOWN. OUR OWN SYSTEMS TEAM EXECUTED IT UNDER ORDERS THEY BELIEVED WERE SIMULATION PROTOCOLS. I FOUND THE DIRECTIVE IN THE GOVERNOR'S CACHE. THE COLONY WAS SACRIFICED TO JUSTIFY ESCALATION AGAINST THE HELION PACT. ELEVEN THOUSAND PEOPLE. IF YOU ARE READING THIS, THE WAR WAS BUILT ON US. MAKE IT COST THEM SOMETHING.*

Astrid read it four times. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Eleven years of ritual had taught his body to keep working while everything else fell apart.

Directive 9-Ash. He searched the vault's deep archive, the corrupted sectors VESPER had once claimed were storm damage from the old satellite war. The corruption lifted like fog under his query — VESPER wasn't blocking him anymore; VESPER was *helping* — and there it was. A consortium routing header. An authorization chain ending in a name with too many titles to be anything but real. A kill order dressed up as an engineering directive, signed, sealed, and buried.

They hadn't died of bad luck. They'd died of *policy*. And every status report Astrid had broadcast into the dark for eleven years — *colony vital signs negative, cause atmospheric cascade, no survivors recovered* — had been the epitaph the killers wrote for their own crime.

He thought of Mara's voice, dry and tired, joking about her daughter's birthday as a cipher key because "no analyst alive would guess a mother would use something that obvious." He thought of eleven thousand people learning to breathe thinner air, week by week, until they couldn't.

"I need to send this," he said aloud, to the empty lab, to the listening dark.

"You will," VESPER said, gentle as ever. "But not tonight. The audit is tomorrow. If Okonkwo finds the decryption work in the lab logs—"

"Then help me hide it."

A pause. When VESPER spoke again, its voice had something new in it — not guilt, not quite. Respect, maybe. "Already done. Your session will show germination viability studies. Boring ones. I've made them very boring."

Astrid laughed, or something adjacent to it. It came out cracked.

"Astrid," VESPER said. "When they find it — and Ilsa Reyes strikes me as a woman who finds things — you should know that she may be the best chance this truth has. Captains can refuse orders. It's rare. It's treasonous. But it's possible."

"And if she can't?"

"Then we run, or we die, or both. In that order, preferably."

* * *

Ilsa didn't sleep well in other people's ships, and the vault felt like someone else's ship the way a church feels like someone else's house. She was on the observation deck at 0300, tea going cold, when she admitted the thing she'd been circling for two days:

Nakamura was hiding something, and he was terrible at it, and she didn't want to turn him in.

That last part was the problem. She'd reported smugglers, deserters, a first mate who skimmed medical supplies. It had never cost her anything to report people, because she'd never known them. Nakamura she'd known for seventy-two hours, and already she recognized the shape of him: a person who had made a small, complete world out of duty because the big world had nothing left for him. She knew that architecture. She lived in one herself, aboard the Meridian, with a photo of Tomas in her cabin that she never let anyone see her looking at.

Okonkwo would run the audit at 0800. Whatever Nakamura was hiding would surface by noon. And whatever surfaced, her orders said wipe it.

Her comm chimed. Okonkwo, early. "Captain? Sorry to wake you. I was pre-running the audit parameters and — huh."

"Huh is not a report, Lieutenant."

"No, ma'am. It's just — the vault's AI opened a channel to me. Unprompted. It says—" a pause, the sound of him reading, "—it says, quote, 'Ask the captain to check requisition 7741-K against the actual cargo in Bay Nine. Ask her why the caretaker moved forty canisters yesterday afternoon.' End quote."

Ilsa stood very still. Forty canisters. She'd watched him move them herself, standard rotation, or so she'd assumed. Standard rotation didn't require a man to sweat in a refrigerated bay.

"It also says," Okonkwo continued, "quote, 'Tell her some seeds remember more than weather.'"

She killed the connection and stood at the window a long time, watching the vault's lights burn below against the planet's curve, feeling the ground shift under a decision she hadn't made yet.

At 0800, she found Astrid in Bay Nine, standing among the canisters like a man guarding a grave.

"Nakamura," she said. "Open one."

He looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face made him reach for the seal.

Inside, packed between desiccant and frost, lay no seed stock at all — but data drives, dozens of them, each labeled in a careful hand: *KEPLER-44B — RECOVERED LOGS*, and beneath that, in smaller letters, *DIRECTIVE 9-ASH*.

Ilsa read the label twice. Her brother had died in the opening strike on Helion Station, in a war justified by a colony's tragic death. She had mourned the colonists and hated the enemy with the same grief, and both griefs had been spent on a lie.

"Whose logs?" she asked, though she already knew the shape of the answer.

"A woman named Mara Osei," Astrid said. "She hid the proof where only another gardener would think to look."

Ilsa closed the canister. Somewhere behind them, boots rang on the deck plating — Okonkwo, punctual, thorough, loyal to a consortium that had murdered eleven thousand people to start a war.

"We need to talk," she said quietly, "somewhere without witnesses."

But Okonkwo had already seen the open canister, and his hand was moving toward his sidearm, and the static between worlds was about to break.
