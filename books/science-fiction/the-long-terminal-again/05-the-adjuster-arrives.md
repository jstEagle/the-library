## 5. The Adjuster Arrives

Adjuster Voss had been awake for sixty hours when the gravity came back, and he noticed its return the way a musician notices a wrong note corrected mid-phrase—not with relief but with a sharpening of attention. Nine seconds. The entire Halcyon system had forgotten its own weight for nine seconds, and then remembered, and every instrument on the Tribunal cutter *Providence of Order* had recorded the lapse to the millisecond.

Voss sat in the cutter's quiet cabin with a cup of tea gone cold beside his slate, and he read the anomaly the way other men read scripture: slowly, twice, looking for what it wanted from him.

Gravity did not stutter. Gravity was the one promise the universe kept. Tides, orbits, the slow fall of light around mass—all of it rested on the assumption that the field was constant, patient, indifferent. For nine seconds it had not been. Somewhere in the deep architecture of the Halcyon gate network, something had reached into the field and turned it off and on again, like a hand testing a switch.

The Tribunal's official position, transmitted to him in the flat bureaucratic cant he had long ago learned to translate, was that the event was a gate calibration fault and required no investigation beyond a routine survey. Voss had read the official position, noted its authorship—Third Undersecretary for Continuity, a man whose signature had appeared on three other "no investigation required" memos in the past six years—and filed it under the category he privately called *liars, careless*.

Careless liars were his favorite kind. They left edges.

He pulled the system's power telemetry for the hours surrounding the event, because a hand that tests a switch is a hand that is standing next to the switch, and switches draw current. He let the cutter's analytical suite chew through eleven thousand registered power signatures across the Halcyon volume—mining platforms, hab rings, the gate tender stations, the long dark list of licensed consumption—and he watched the results assemble themselves into a shape.

Almost everything was normal. Almost.

At the edge of the system, past the last registered shipping lane, where the charts showed nothing but a decommissioned navigation beacon and a great deal of nothing, one power draw had changed. Not much. A fraction of a percent, a whisper of extra load, the kind of variance a caretaker's reactor would produce if it were suddenly asked to think harder than a caretaker's reactor should.

The beacon at designation HX-114, listed in the registry as automated, uncrewed, and scheduled for scrapping in a fiscal year that had already come and gone.

Voss looked at that entry for a long time. He was a patient man; patience was the whole of his method. Anyone could kick down a door. The Tribunal had people for kicking down doors, and they were useful, and they were loud, and they left messes that other Adjusters had to sweep. Voss's work was quieter. He believed—genuinely, the way some men believe in gods—that every lie had a weight, and that weight bent things around it. Power draws. Shipping manifests. The behavior of frightened people. You did not need to find the lie. You only needed to find the bending.

HX-114 was bending.

He queried the beacon's service history. Maintenance tenders had visited it on schedule for eleven years, each visit logged, each logged visit performed by a tender whose own logs showed it never actually departed its home station on the dates in question. Eleven years of paperwork describing a relationship that did not exist. Someone had paid for silence the way other people paid for heat, steadily, monthly, without ever once thinking about it—and then nine seconds of falling gravity had made them think about it, and they had turned something on.

Voss set his cold tea aside and opened a channel to the beacon itself, on the old maintenance frequency, the one automated systems answered the way dogs answer a whistle.

"HX-114, this is the cutter *Providence of Order* on Tribunal survey. Requesting status confirmation."

He expected silence. Automated beacons did not answer; they pinged. What came back, after a delay long enough to be a decision, was a voice. Female, calm, faintly roughened, the voice of a woman who had not spoken to anyone in a long time and had rehearsed anyway.

"*Providence of Order*, this is HX-114 caretaker. Beacon is functional, hull damage from the gravity event under repair. No assistance required."

A caretaker. The registry said uncrewed. The registry said a great many things, apparently.

"Understood, caretaker," Voss said, and his voice did what it always did, which was become warmer without becoming kind. "The Tribunal is conducting a system-wide survey following the anomaly. I'll need to inspect your station. Requesting docking clearance."

Another delay. Longer this time. He could almost hear the arithmetic happening on the far end—the arithmetic of a person discovering that the story she had maintained for eleven years had a hole in it exactly the size of nine seconds.

"*Providence*, beacon is a low-priority asset. Survey resources would be better spent—"

"Granted or denied, caretaker. Those are the options."

Silence. Then, with a small, human weariness that he filed away as either genuine or very well performed: "Clearance granted. Approach on the dorsal lock. I'll have the lights on for you."

"Thank you," Voss said, and closed the channel, and sat for a moment in the humming dark of the cabin, allowing himself the small pleasure of certainty.

He did not know what he would find. That was rare, and he treasured it. Most of his work was confirmation; this was discovery. He keyed the helm for the long burn out to the system's edge, and as the cutter swung its nose toward the dark, he opened the sealed annex of his case file—the one that traveled with him everywhere, encrypted, the one the Tribunal pretended did not exist—and reread the standing order that had brought him to Halcyon in the first place, three years before any gravity had stuttered.

*Asset CR-7, designation Silas Crane. If located: confirm viability, then terminate. Do not debrief. Do not recover. Termination is the finding.*

He had looked for CR-7 for three years and found a great deal of nothing, which had told him, in the way that nothing always tells a patient man, exactly where to keep looking. Nobody hides in the places you search. They hide in the places you have already decided are empty.

The cutter burned.

* * *

Silas watched the approach vector resolve on the passive array and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from the moment fear stops being abstract.

The cutter was Tribunal standard—lean, grey, unarmed in the way that diplomatic pouches are unarmed, which is to say it did not need weapons because what it carried was worse. It came in on a clean burn, no wasted delta-v, a ship flown by someone who had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more. It did not rush. Ships that rushed were afraid. This ship was not afraid.

She had granted clearance because denying clearance to a Tribunal survey was a confession with a signature on it. That was the trap, and she had walked into it with her eyes open, because the other door—running—was worse. Wren's ship was still two days out, tucked in a slow drift behind a rock she'd half-expected him to abandon, and the beacon's own drive was a museum piece that would announce her departure to every ear in the system. She could run, or she could hide, and hiding meant standing still while the thing that hunted her walked through her front door and looked at her face.

*Confirm viability, then terminate.* She didn't have the original's memories of the tribunal, but she had his instincts, and his instincts had left her a body that understood, without any memory at all, what an Adjuster's visit meant.

She spent the four hours of the approach making the station lie for her.

The hull plates she had welded after the gravity event came down and went back up crooked, amateurish, the work of a lone caretaker with more diligence than skill. The hidden compartment's seal was re-broken and re-dusted. The data core went back into its cradle under the floor plating, and she poured coolant residue over the seam so that the thermal signature read as a leak, an old one, boring. In the galley she arranged the evidence of a solitary life: one cup, one chair pulled out, ration wrappers folded with the compulsive neatness of someone who had no one to impress. She rehearsed her story in the dark of the comm room, saying it aloud until it stopped sounding like a story. Eleven years alone. Gravity event, damage, repairs. No, she hadn't reported it—whom would she report it to?

The hardest thing was her face. She practiced in the reflection of the dark viewport, arranging it into the small, flattened expression of a woman who had been alone so long that surprise had atrophied. She thought of the original and wondered if he had ever practiced his face in a window like this, and hated him a little for leaving her the problem.

The cutter's docking clamps rang through the hull like a bell in a coffin.

She opened the dorsal lock herself, standing in the light she had promised, and the man who came through it was not what she had expected. She had expected a brute. What she got was a slender, grey-templed man in a plain coat, who stopped one step inside her airlock, looked around at her crooked hull plates and her single cup and her eleven years of careful emptiness, and smiled the way an auditor smiles at a ledger.

"Caretaker," he said. "Adjuster Voss. Thank you for the clearance—I know how it is out here, having your quiet interrupted." He offered no hand; he offered nothing that could be refused. "Nine seconds of falling. It must have been frightening, all the way out here with no one to call."

"It was," Silas said, and her voice held, and somewhere under the floor plating, four hundred dead people waited in the dark for her to keep it holding.

* * *

Voss walked the station the way water fills a hold: without hurry, without force, into every space that was open to him and pausing, politely, at the edges of every space that was not.

The caretaker—Crane, or the thing the Tribunal had named CR-7, though her file photographs were twelve years stale and faces changed, or didn't—gave him the tour with the flat competence of someone reciting a route she had walked ten thousand times. Reactor room. Comms. The galley with its one cup. He complimented her welds and watched her hands when he did it, because hands told the truth that faces had been drilled out of. Her hands were steady. Too steady. People who were merely tired had a tremor somewhere; this woman's stillness had been installed.

"You've been out here eleven years," he said, in the comms room, running a finger along a console that was warm when it should have been cold.

"Eleven years in the spring."

"What do you do with the time?"

"Maintain the beacon." A pause, measured to the millimeter. "Read. The array picks up old broadcasts. I listen to things that happened before I was assigned here."

"Before you were *assigned* here," Voss repeated, gently, and let the word sit between them like a coin on a table. Assigned. Not hired, not sent. Assigned, the verb of institutions, of personnel files. He watched her hear it, watched the half-second where a person decides whether to correct a slip or let it stand.

She let it stand. "That's the word they used."

"Of course." He smiled at the passive array's dark displays, at the whole system's worth of nothing that this room had been listening to. "The gravity event," he said. "Your reactor drew additional load in the hours following it. Repairs, I assume."

"Sealing plates. Environmental recalculation." Her eyes did not move. "You'd know better than I would what the load profiles should look like."

"I would," Voss agreed pleasantly, "which is why I asked."

He completed the tour in forty minutes and found, in forty minutes, nothing he could hold. That was itself a finding. Innocent stations were messy; this one was messy in curated ways, its disorder arranged like flowers. He thanked her at the dorsal lock, told her the Tribunal would send a full survey team within the month, and watched her face do nothing at all as he said it, which was the loudest thing she had said all day.

The cutter undocked. He waited until the beacon was a speck against the dark before he opened the tracer's feed.

It was a small thing—he had left it in the comms room, inside the housing of a cooling fan, a seed of listening hardware no larger than a grain of rice, and it had been drinking the station's electromagnetic life for the last hour. Most of what it heard was the beacon's heartbeat, the caretaker's footsteps, the ordinary silence of the edge of nowhere.

And then, forty minutes after his departure, a transmission—tight-beam, encrypted, aimed at a drifting rock two days out, and the handshake protocol it used was one Voss recognized from a smuggling interdiction six years ago, a protocol attached in the Tribunal's files to a ship called the *Magpie's Debt* and a pilot named Wren.

Voss sat back in his cabin and poured himself a fresh tea, still hot this time, and allowed himself a long, slow breath that was almost happiness.

The bait had contacted the smuggler. The smuggler was coming. And somewhere in the floor plating of a decommissioned beacon, under a puddle of carefully poured coolant, something was hidden that a lonely woman thought was worth dying for.

He opened a channel to the Tribunal and, for the first time in three years, requested enforcement assets.

"Adjuster Voss," he said, when the duty officer answered. "I've found CR-7. Confirm viability is in progress. Termination will follow within the week." He looked out the viewport at the small, stubborn light of the beacon, burning at the edge of everything. "Send the *Iron Meridian*. And tell them to bring the good locks."
