## 9. The Broadcast

The tracer's signal pulsed in her ear like a second heartbeat.

Silas had found it four hours after Voss left—a sliver of engineered lattice no larger than a fingernail, wedged behind the access panel in the galley where his fingers had lingered a half-second too long while asking about her water reclamation system. She hadn't destroyed it. Destroying it would have told him she'd found it, and the polite fiction of the inspection would have collapsed hours ahead of schedule. Instead she'd sealed it inside a tin of vacuum-rated sealant and buried it in the cargo locker farthest from the transmitter array, letting it sing its little song to a ship that was already turning back toward them with company.

She could see that company now on the passive array. Three hull signatures riding Voss's wake out of the dark: enforcement cutters, wedge-shaped and ugly, their drive plumes burning hotter than anything that intended to be gentle. The Cartel had stopped pretending. Whatever window of plausible deniability Voss had been operating within—the courteous inspector, the patient man—it was closed. They were coming to erase a beacon, a smuggler's ship, and eleven years of a life that had never legally existed.

And they were coming in ninety minutes. Maybe less.

The jump gate's window closed in three hours and twelve minutes.

Silas sat in the transmitter bay with the original's data core cradled against her sternum like something with a pulse, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for nineteen days. The burst transmission required every watt the beacon could produce. It required the failsafes down—every one of them, the thermal governors, the antenna stress limiters, the hard-coded kill switch that some engineer at Halcyon Yards had installed so that no single operator could ever turn a Class-4 beacon into a weapon or a scream. She had spent two days mapping those locks. She could break all of them. Breaking all of them would cook the array in under ninety seconds, and quite possibly cook her with it, because the transmitter bay's shielding was rated for scheduled maintenance loads, not for a beacon deliberately set on fire from the inside.

Her hands were steady. That was the strange part. Eleven years of quiet had taught her hands what to do when the world ended: work anyway.

*You were made for this,* said the voice that was not a voice, the residue of a dead man's certainty living in the architecture of her own skull. *Made to be found. Made to be deleted.* She had spent Chapter upon chapter—no, day upon day—hating that truth, hating him for it, hating the tribunal for writing her existence as a contingency clause. But hatred was a luxury for people with time. What she felt now was simpler and colder: if she had been manufactured as bait, then she would be the bait that bit back.

* * *

Wren found her kneeling among the open relay cabinets, bypass wire between her teeth, soldering iron in hand.

"You're going to burn it," he said. Not a question. He stood in the hatchway with the ship's toolkit still slung over one shoulder, and behind him the corridor lights were already cycling to emergency amber because she'd started pulling power from everywhere else in the station. The beacon groaned around them, a sound like a whale dying in slow motion, as systems that had run quietly for decades were asked to give everything.

"Probably."

"The array won't survive."

"No."

"And you—" His voice caught on it. He came closer, crouched beside her, and took the soldering iron from her hand not because he was taking over but because he needed his hands to have something to do while he said the rest. "Silas. There are other gates. Slower routes. We take the *Kestrel's Lament*, we run dark past the patrol line, we're in Verge space in six weeks and you broadcast from somewhere they can't reach in ninety minutes—"

"—and the gate closes in three hours, and the next window is a decade away, and in six weeks Voss's report has been filed, ratified, and laminated into official history." She looked at him. In the amber light his face was all angles and exhaustion, and underneath the exhaustion was the thing she'd been watching grow in him for nineteen days: he had stopped looking at her like a ghost. "They killed four hundred people and then they killed the record, and then they killed the man who kept the record, and then they grew me as a mop for the floor. If I run, I'm just the last draft of their erasure. Wren. I'm done being their draft."

He was silent for a long moment. Somewhere above them, a coolant line shuddered.

"I buried him," Wren said finally. "Twelve years ago. I put what was left of him in a box and I said words over it, bad ones, and I promised myself I'd never watch anyone die on my account again." His jaw worked. "So I'm not going to stand here and tell you not to do this. I'm going to hold the airlock door, and when they come through it—and they will come through it—you're going to keep transmitting until the array melts, and I'm going to make sure nothing with a Cartel insignia touches this room before the burst completes."

"That's suicide."

"We're all doing something suicidal today. At least mine has good odds. They'll expect a fight at the dock, not a man with a shotgun and nothing left to lose." He almost smiled. It didn't reach his eyes. "Besides. You don't get to decide how I grieve you twice."

The words landed in her chest like a dropped tool. *Grieve you twice.* As if the first grief had never finished—as if loving the copy was its own unfinished sentence—and she understood suddenly that he had been carrying both funerals since the day he opened the airlock and saw a dead man's face wearing a stranger's expression. She reached up and touched his cheek, once, briefly, the way you touch something you intend to remember.

"Burst sequence arms in twenty minutes," she said. "Go hold your door."

* * *

The failsafes fell one by one, and each one falling sounded like a small death.

Thermal governor: overridden. Antenna stress limiter: overridden. Power interlock between life support and transmission bus: overridden—which meant that when the array drew full load, the air scrubbers would starve, and the temperature in the station would begin climbing toward the melting point of insulation, and every minute of transmission would be a minute spent cooking in her own skin. She stripped off her jacket, then her overshirt, working down to the thermal undersuit, sweat already prickling at her hairline. On the bulkhead, the old brass plaque bolted there by some long-dead crew read HALCYON DEEP RELAY 7 — IN SERVICE OF SAFE PASSAGE. She wondered what the plaque's makers would think of safe passage being purchased this way.

The core went into the buffer at T-minus forty-one minutes. Four hundred casualty manifests. Sensor logs from Meridian Station's final ninety seconds. Testimony fragments—crew voices, civilian voices, a child's recording that Silas had listened to exactly once and would never listen to again. Her original's investigation notes, complete, timestamped, signed with a name the tribunal had spent twelve years pretending was never attached to anything. And at the top of the package, addressed to every receiver within range of the gate: a header in plain text. *This happened. Check your own archives. They will tell you it didn't.*

The gate itself was invisible from here—fourteen million kilometers out, a ring of impossible geometry holding open a throat in spacetime, and for three more hours it would accept whatever was pushed through it and scatter it across half the settled dark. After that, silence for ten years. Ten years in which four hundred dead people would remain officially unborn.

Not today.

T-minus thirty-eight minutes. The enforcement cutters crossed the beacon's defensive perimeter without slowing, without hailing. Of course they didn't hail. Hailing was for situations with survivors.

T-minus thirty-one minutes. The first cutter matched rotation with the docking arm, and Silas felt the magnetic kiss of grapples through the deck plates like a hand closing around the station's throat. Through the internal sensors she watched Voss come down the boarding tube first—he would insist on being first, the patient man, the certain man—and behind him eight figures in enforcement armor moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had been assured there would be no complications.

Wren's voice came over the suit channel, low and even. "Door's rigged. I'm at the junction. Go."

Silas pressed her palm to the transmit console. "Arming burst sequence."

The beacon screamed.

There was no other word for it. Every speaker, every resonant surface, every pipe and plate in the station picked up the harmonic of an antenna drawing nine hundred times its rated load, and the sound was not mechanical but animal, a rising shriek that she felt in her teeth and her lungs and the wet architecture of her inner ear. The lights died section by section as power fled toward the array. Heat rolled through the transmitter bay in visible waves, shimmering the air, and the temperature readout began climbing: sixty degrees. Seventy-five. Ninety.

On her display, the transmission progress bar crawled forward. Packet 1 of 2,847.

* * *

The first breach charge went off at packet 400.

She heard it as a concussion through the structure, felt it in the deck, and then heard Wren's shotgun answering—two shots, three, the heavy deliberate rhythm of a man conserving ammunition and buying seconds. The channel stayed open. He didn't speak. He didn't need to. Each shot was a sentence: *still here. still here. still here.*

Packet 900. The air scrubbers were dead; the air tasted of hot metal and her own exertion. Sweat ran into her eyes and she wiped it with her forearm and kept her other hand flat on the console, as if she could push the packets through by pressure alone. Ninety degrees outside the shielded core of the bay. A hundred and five. Somewhere below her, fire suppression was triggering in sections that no longer had power to suppress anything.

"They're through the junction," Wren said at last, breath ragged. "Falling back to secondary. Two minutes. Maybe three."

Packet 1,600.

Voss's voice arrived on the general channel, and even now—even with the station burning around them and his cutters venting atmosphere behind forced hatches—it was courteous. Unhurried. Almost kind. "Crane. Or whoever you are. This doesn't need to end the way you've decided it ends. Kill the transmission, open the bay, and I give you my word you walk out of this."

She keyed the channel, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Your word. From the Cartel Tribunal's Adjuster. To a person who doesn't exist."

A pause. When he answered, the courtesy had thinned, and beneath it she heard the thing she'd sensed during the whole long dance of his inspection: certainty, curdling. "You were a contingency. You know that. Everything you are was authored by the same office that wrote the erasure order. You're transmitting their document."

"No," Silas said. "I'm transmitting *his*. You just never bothered to read the difference."

Packet 2,300. The array's outer casing was glowing dull orange on the thermal feed. The progress bar flickered, stuttered—her heart seized—and held. One hundred and fifteen degrees in the bay. Her undersuit was soaked through; her vision swam at the edges. She thought, absurdly, of the note in the compartment, the dead man's careful handwriting: *whoever they grew next—I'm sorry it's you. Make it count.*

It counted. God help her, it counted.

Breach charges at the secondary junction. Wren's shotgun, then not his shotgun. Then his voice, close to the pickup, rough and alive: "Bay hatch, thirty seconds! Silas—"

Packet 2,700.

The hatch blew inward in a bloom of white fire and decompression fog, and through it came the armored shapes, and through them—impossibly, stupidly, gloriously—came Wren, bleeding from the scalp, reloading as he walked, planting himself in the doorway of the transmitter bay like a man who had finally found the one door in the universe worth holding.

"Keep going," he said, not turning around. "I've got the door."

Packet 2,800. 2,820.

The lead enforcer raised a weapon. Wren fired first. The bay filled with muzzle flash and screaming metal and the smell of scorched polymer, and Silas did not look up, did not flinch, kept her eyes on the bar as the heat pressed down on her like a hand and the array sang its dying song—

Packet 2,847 of 2,847.

**TRANSMISSION COMPLETE.**

The burst tore through the jump gate fourteen million kilometers away and scattered into the dark beyond it—into Verge relays, into independent receivers, into ten thousand hands the Cartel could never find fast enough—four hundred names, ninety seconds of sensor truth, a dead detective's signature, and a header that would outlive every tribunal that had ever tried to bury it: *This happened.*

Silas slid down the console as the array's casing split open above her in a fountain of sparks, expecting the shot, expecting the dark, expecting to die with the progress bar at one hundred percent and finding that she did not mind, that she had never minded, that this was the ending her original had written for whoever they grew next and she had made it true—

But the shot didn't come.

Because Wren was still standing in the doorway. Because the enforcers had stopped, weapons lowering, faces turning upward toward their own helmet feeds as something arrived on *their* channels—something that made the whole boarding party go still as statues in the burning light of the ruined array.

And Adjuster Voss stepped over the threshold, looked at the melted transmitter, looked at her, and said quietly:

"Well. That changes things."
