## 10. Whoever They Grew Next

The signal reached the inner system in four hours.

Silas knew this because Wren had rigged the beacon's passive arrays to listen for the echo of its own voice—bounce-backs, retransmissions, the chatter of relay buoys waking up one by one like a nervous system remembering itself. She sat in the cramped observation blister with a cup of recycled tea going cold in her hands and watched the traffic indicators bloom across the Halcyon system: freighters altering course, courier drones scattering, the jump gate's control cluster suddenly alive with authentication requests it had no protocol to answer.

Four hundred names. That was all it had ever been, underneath everything. Four hundred people who had lived and worked and died on Meridian Station, whose deaths had been filed out of existence by men in good suits. Her original had carried their names for years inside his own skull, and then he had written them into a data core and hidden them under a derelict beacon at the edge of nowhere, addressed to *whoever they grew next*.

She had said them aloud once, all of them, during the nineteen days. It had taken most of a night shift. Wren hadn't interrupted. He'd just sat across from her in the galley, drinking bad coffee, keeping her company in the dark the way you keep a vigil.

Now the names were everywhere. On every screen in the system. In every mouth that mattered and ten thousand mouths that didn't.

"They can't pull it down," Wren said from the doorway. "I've been watching the attempts. Every time the Cartel scrubs a mirror, three more go up. It's like trying to unspill something."

"Water," Silas said.

"What?"

"It's like trying to unspill water. Unspilling implies the spill was ever a discrete thing." She turned the cup in her hands. "It was always everywhere. They just built a very expensive fiction on top of it."

Wren came into the blister and folded himself into the seat beside hers, knees up, the way he sat everywhere—like a man perpetually about to leave. Except he never left. Eleven days since the breach, since Voss's enforcement team had come through the airlock expecting a corpse and found instead an empty station, an open transmitter, and two people already gone through the maintenance tunnels to the smuggler's ship tucked in the beacon's shadow. Eleven days of hiding in plain sight while the system tore itself apart over what she'd done.

Over what *he* had done, eleven years ago, and what she had finished.

"The tribunal on Ceres Anchorage voted to dissolve this morning," Wren said. "Third one this week. There's footage of Adjusters turning in their credentials. One of them burned his coat in a fountain, which I feel is theatrics, but the crowds loved it."

"And Voss?"

"Gone quiet. Officially recalled for review." Wren's mouth twisted. "Unofficially? I made some calls. The Cartel doesn't recall an Adjuster because they failed. They recall them because they're a liability. Voss let you live, Silas. Whatever report they wrote, somebody read between the lines."

Silas looked out at the stars. The beacon hung below them, still transmitting its ordinary navigation pulse, still pretending to be the dumb, faithful machine it had always seemed. She had thought she would burn it when they left. She'd stood in the core chamber with the demolition charges in her hands and found she couldn't.

"I keep thinking about what he would have wanted," she said. "The original. Whether he'd have wanted the station destroyed. Erased. Another thing filed out of existence."

"You think he'd have wanted it kept?"

"I think he hid his evidence in the foundation of it. I think he trusted the most boring object in the system to keep his secret safe for a decade." She smiled, small and tired. "No. We leave it standing. Let it be a lighthouse again. Let some other lonely soul tend it and never know what slept under the floor."

* * *

The legal question resolved itself faster than either of them expected, and in a way neither of them could have predicted.

There was no record of Silas Crane. Not anymore. The Cartel's identity registries—the same archives that held no record of Meridian Station's dead—had purged her original's file as part of the cover-up, and the clone program's records had gone into the tribunal dissolution along with everything else. She was, legally speaking, nobody. A person-shaped absence. The tribunals that remained were too busy fracturing and testifying against each other to manufacture a new crime out of a woman who did not exist.

"You understand what that means," Wren said, sprawled on the bunk in his ship's narrow cabin while she sat cross-legged on the floor, sorting the original's case files into physical hardcopy. Old habit. Paper couldn't be remotely scrubbed. "You're free. Completely. No name, no number, no file. You could be anyone."

"That's what I was before," she said quietly. "Anyone. A caretaker with a borrowed face and a fake registration, tending a light at the edge of everything so no one would look twice." She smoothed the edge of a page. "Being no one isn't freedom, Wren. It's just a smaller cage."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "So pick a name."

"It's not that simple."

"It's exactly that simple. It's terrifyingly simple. You stand in front of a registry clerk on any station in the outer belt and you say 'my name is' and whatever comes after that sentence is yours. Nobody checks. Nobody cares. Half the people out here invented themselves twice." He propped himself up on one elbow. "The only person who has to believe it is you."

She looked down at the files in her lap. Her original's handwriting marched across the margins—dense, impatient, brilliant. *Check the docking manifests against the life support draw. Four hundred souls means four hundred sets of lungs.* He had been so certain the truth would matter. He had died three days before he could find out if he was right.

And she had spent eleven years being the contingency. The bait in the trap, grown in a vat so that if the evidence ever surfaced, there would be a convenient copy of the inconvenient man to delete along with it. Voss had come to kill her. Voss had stood in her galley drinking tea and asking polite questions, and every word had been a blade looking for a place to land—and at the end, standing in the airlock with the tracer planted in his sleeve, Voss had looked at her and said nothing at all, and let her live, and she still didn't fully know why. Maybe even Adjusters got tired. Maybe four hundred names weighed something even on a scale built by the Cartel.

Maybe Voss had simply recognized what she was still learning to recognize: that she was not him.

"I'm not going to take his name," she said.

Wren blinked. "I didn't ask you to."

"I know. But I want to say it out loud, so it's real." She set the files aside. "Silas Crane was a detective. He was sharp and reckless and he died for a story he believed in, and I honor that. But I'm not him. I'm not the copy of him, either. I'm the person who spent eleven years learning patience in the dark. Who rationed air and decrypted a dead man's last words and chose to broadcast knowing it might be the last thing she ever did." She exhaled slowly. "Whoever they grew next—that's what the note said. Whoever they grew next. But they didn't grow me next. They grew me *instead*, as a failsafe. And I outlived my purpose. So now I get to decide what I am."

"And what's that?"

She looked at him—at the smuggler who had buried a man twelve years ago and fallen in love with his ghost's reflection without ever once calling her by the wrong name. Who had held the airlock door with a cutting torch in his hands while she screamed the truth into the gate.

"Alive," she said. "For now, that's enough of an answer."

* * *

They left on the nineteenth day after the broadcast, though the window mathematics no longer mattered; the gate stayed open now, its schedule suspended while the whole apparatus of the Cartel's authority came apart around it. Wren insisted on the date anyway. "Nineteen days," he said. "We leave on nineteen days. Symmetry matters to people like us."

"People like us," she repeated.

"Runners. Survivors. People who know exactly how long a window stays open and plan accordingly."

The beacon's final systems check took her six hours. She walked every corridor one last time, ran her palm along the bulkheads she had polished and patched and cursed at for a decade, and left the lights on. All of them. Let it burn bright and stupid and visible across the dark, a lighthouse with nothing left to hide.

In the foundation chamber, she placed the note back where she'd found it—her original's handwriting, *to whoever they grew next*—and beneath it she added a page of her own. Just one line, in handwriting that was similar but not the same, the way a river is similar to its source:

*I was here. I finished it. I left.*

Then she sealed the compartment, climbed the ladder, and didn't look back.

Wren had the engines warm. She dropped into the copilot's seat—a seat she had never once occupied in eleven years, because the beacon had no copilot—and strapped in, and felt the old familiar shudder of thrust as the ship fell away from the station she had called a prison and a home and, finally, a grave she'd dug herself out of.

"Course?" Wren asked, hands hovering over the board.

She had thought about this for eleven days. The original's case files held more than Meridian. Three other investigations, unfinished, their threads trailing off into the same corporate darkness. Names of witnesses scattered across the outer belt. A pattern, half-glimpsed, that he had died chasing and she had inherited—not as a duty, not as a destiny, but as a choice. She could burn the files. She could disappear into the belt and be gloriously, permanently no one.

Or she could be someone. Someone specific. Someone with a name she hadn't chosen yet and work that mattered and a ship and a partner and the rest of her life, however long the Cartel's ghosts allowed it to be.

"Set course for Ceres Anchorage," she said. "There's a witness list in file seven. Forty-one names. Thirty-nine are still unaccounted for."

Wren grinned, slow and real. "And the other two?"

"One of them sent a message two days ago. Encrypted, buried in a shipping manifest, keyed to a phrase only the original would have known." She pulled the printout from her jacket and laid it flat on the console between them. "Except I'm not the original. And whoever sent it knows that. Because the message wasn't addressed to Silas Crane."

She tapped the single line of text at the bottom of the page.

"It was addressed to *whoever they grew next*. And it says: *Come find me. I've been waiting eleven years to finish what we started.*"

Wren looked at her. "Any idea who?"

"No," Silas said, and felt something that was not dread move through her chest—something lighter, sharper, almost like joy. "But they knew the phrase. They knew the case. And they've been waiting in the dark, same as me."

She watched the beacon shrink behind them, a bright point against the black, still burning, still honest.

"Course laid in," Wren said.

"Then let's go meet the future," she said. "It's been waiting long enough."
