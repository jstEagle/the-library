## 1. Cold Cargo

The dark between Earth and Vesper Reach was not empty. Mirabel Nystrom had learned that in her first year of running the route, when she'd mistaken silence for safety and nearly died for it. The dark was full—of debris from a war no one admitted to fighting, of dead satellites still whispering to each other on encrypted bands, of customs drones that slept with their eyes open. The dark was full of everything except people, and that was precisely why she loved it.

She sat in the pilot's cradle of the *Kestrel's Regret*, a ship whose name was a joke only she understood, watching the quarantine line crawl toward her on the display like a slow tide of red light. Eleven hours out from the colony. Nine hundred kilometers of nothing, then the orbital customs ring, then the part of the night where competence mattered more than luck.

The wafer case rode in the false floor beneath her navigation console, forty slivers of black glass packed in foam, each one holding someone's recorded life. A wedding on Luna, sold by a widow who couldn't bear to watch it anymore. A soldier's last leave, pressed before he shipped out to a front that officially didn't exist. A child's first steps, bought back by a father who had lost the original in a data collapse and paid half a year's wages for the copy. Mirabel never watched them. That was the rule. You carried other people's memories; you didn't live in them.

Mostly.

Her fingers found the edge of the old wafer in her breast pocket without her permission—the way they always did, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. One wafer among thousands over the years, but this one she didn't carry for anyone else. Ovid's wafer. Seven years cold against her sternum, and she still touched it like a bruise.

*When they start dreaming,* he'd said, and then he'd said nothing ever again.

She pushed the thought down where it lived and ran the pre-approach checklist instead, because ritual was its own kind of company.

* * *

The transponder chirped its identity into the customs net at 0400 ship-time, and the identity belonged to a dead man.

Tomas Reyes, registry 4471-Kappa, licensed courier, clean record, current insurance. Tomas Reyes had been real once—a broad, laughing man who'd taught Mirabel how to read a customs scanner's hesitation patterns, how to tell a bored inspector from a hungry one by the rhythm of their ping. He'd been real for fifty-one years, and then he'd walked out an airlock over Ceres rather than be interrogated about a shipment he never should have taken, and his codes had gone into Mirabel's keeping along with his spare gloves and his recipe for synthetic rum.

Earth's bureaucracy was a vast machine, but it was a machine that grieved slowly. Dead couriers stayed in the system for years, their credentials renewed automatically by processes no one audited, because auditing meant admitting the process existed, and admitting the process existed meant admitting there was something worth hiding between Earth and its colonies. The silent war ran on paperwork. Both sides knew it. Neither side signed anything.

"Vessel 4471-Kappa, this is Vesper Reach Orbital Control." The voice was young, female, tired around the edges—the voice of someone three shifts deep into a rotation. "State cargo class and manifest."

"Cold storage, agricultural samples," Mirabel said. Her voice came out flat and bored, the voice of a woman who had made this run so many times it had worn a groove in her. "Forty units, sealed, temperature-logged. Manifest is in the buffer."

A pause. In the pause, the scanner swept her hull, and Mirabel felt it the way you feel a stranger's eyes on the back of your neck. Somewhere above her, an array of sensors was reading the *Kestrel's Regret*'s skin, looking for the faint density anomalies of smuggled goods, and somewhere below her own calm, an older and less rational part of her braced for the alarm.

It didn't come. The false floor held. It always held. She made herself breathe anyway.

"Manifest received. You're cleared for berth fourteen, Kappa. Customs inspection on docking."

"On docking," Mirabel repeated, and kept her voice level through the small cold drop of it. Inspection on docking meant a person, not just a machine. A person meant eyes, questions, the particular dangerous boredom of an enforcer with time to kill.

She fed thrusters and let the colony grow in her viewport.

* * *

Vesper Reach hung in the black like a lantern someone had tried to smother. From this distance you couldn't see the dome-cities or the agri-spokes or the scar of the old shipyards; you saw only the light, and the light was wrong. Too dim. Earth's colonies burned bright and wasteful, advertising their prosperity across the void the way a rich man wears gold. Vesper Reach burned like a house where the family was pretending to be asleep.

Quarantine status: advisory. That was the official term. Advisory quarantine, imposed twenty-two years ago after what the bulletins called a civil disruption, lifted for essential traffic only, pending review. Twenty-two years of pending review. Mirabel had been nine years old when the quarantine went up, which meant she had spent her whole conscious life inside its shape, the way a tree grows around a fence wire.

She'd been born on Vesper Reach. She'd left at nineteen with a forged permit and a suitcase full of other people's letters, and she'd come back eleven times since, each time a little less sure why, each time a little more unable to stop. There was no money in the Vesper run—not like the Jovian lanes, not like the luxury-memory trade to Mars. The colonists were poor and careful and paid in scrip and favors. She came back because coming back was the only direction her life seemed to know.

Or because of the wafer against her sternum. She'd stopped trying to tell those two reasons apart years ago.

Berth fourteen opened its mouth and swallowed her, and the docking clamps took the ship with a shudder she felt in her teeth. Through the viewport, the customs ring turned its slow carousel of floodlit bays, and beyond it the planet turned too, blue-white and cloud-streaked and utterly indifferent to what had been done to it, if anything had been done to it, which the archives said no, which the archives would say no even if—

Enough. She killed the thought like a matchpinched flame and went to meet her inspection.

* * *

The enforcer who came aboard was built like a piece of loading equipment and moved like one too, all mass and economy, his boots ringing on the deck plates in a rhythm Mirabel catalogued automatically—heavy heel strike, unhurried, a man who expected obedience rather than trouble. His badge read DAE-HO in blocky stencil, and his eyes did something most enforcers' eyes didn't do: they actually looked. Not at her face, particularly. At the ship.

"Reyes," he said, reading off his slate. "Tomas Reyes."

"That's me." The lie slid out smooth as coolant. She'd worn this name so long it fit better than her own.

"You're listed as male."

"I'm listed as my father's registration. I inherited the contract." Also true, in its way, though the man whose contract she'd inherited had never sired anything but trouble. "Family operations are grandfathered under the Colonial Traffic Act, section—"

"I know the act." He said it without heat, and that was worse than heat. Heat you could push against. This was a man filing things away. His gaze traveled the cockpit: the nav console, the coffee bulb sweating on its magnet, the blanket folded in the second seat that no one ever sat in. "Alone on a long-haul?"

"Company policy discourages conversation."

Something twitched at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. He consulted his slate again, and Mirabel watched him the way she'd watch a scanner: reading his hesitations, his rhythms. Bored inspector or hungry one. This one was neither, and that unsettled her more than either would have.

"Agricultural samples," he said. "From where?"

"Luna. Meridian cold-storage. Sealed chain of custody, if you want to crack one and lose your certification."

"I don't want to crack one." He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were brown and ordinary and entirely too awake for hour four of a graveyard shift. "I want to know why a routine sample run needs a military-grade hull scrub. Your exterior plating's been re-metallized within the last six months. Sample runners don't re-metallize. Smugglers do."

The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere in the ship's guts, a pump cycled, counting seconds for her.

"Rust," Mirabel said. "The Kestrel's old. I do what keeps her flying."

"The Kestrel," he repeated. "That's not the registered name. Registered name is *Long Haul*, owner T. Reyes."

"Ships get renamed. People do too. Ask any colonist."

He held her gaze for three full seconds—she counted them the way she counted everything, in beats and breaths—and then he stepped back, and the tension in the cabin broke like ice underfoot.

"Inspection complete," he said. "Cargo unverified, vessel condition noted. Welcome to Vesper Reach, Ms. Reyes." He paused at the hatch, one hand on the frame, and without turning around he added, quietly, almost gently: "Twenty-two years ago, this station processed four hundred ships a month. Last month we processed nine. Whatever you're carrying, whatever everyone's carrying—it's not agriculture. It's never been agriculture. And one day somebody's going to ask what happened to all the ships that used to come here, and I'd like to have an answer ready."

Then he was gone, boots ringing away down the access corridor, leaving Mirabel standing in her cockpit with her heart going hard against the wafer in her pocket, against the seven-year-old secret she'd carried so long it had become part of her skeleton.

*When they start dreaming.* She hadn't started dreaming. Not yet. But an enforcer asking philosophical questions at 0400—that was new. That was a change in the weather.

* * *

She delivered the wafers in the old market under the ring's shadow, in a stall that smelled of yeast and solder, to a dockworker named Sefa with forearms like cable and a laugh that could be heard across three berths. Sefa paid in scrip and fresh bread and gossip, and the gossip this time had a strange grain to it.

"Anouk woke the whole hab block again last night," Sefa said, counting scrip twice because she always counted twice. "Screaming. Same dream as the Vasquez girl, same as the Okonkwo twins. Fire, and a dome sealing, and names getting crossed off a list. Forty kids now, Mirabel. Forty kids having the same nightmare, and the health office says it's a coincidence, and the health office can go—" she glanced at a passing clerk, and finished the sentence with a gesture rather than words.

Mirabel took the bread. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; that was the thing people praised about her, the thing that had kept her alive through eleven runs and one near-mutiny and the long slow death of the only person who'd ever loved her without wanting anything back.

"Kids share dreams," she said. "Contagious fear. It's documented."

"It's documented," Sefa agreed, and looked at her with those flat dockworker's eyes that missed nothing, "and so is the fact that none of these kids were born when whatever they're dreaming about happened. If it happened. Which it didn't. Officially." She smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "You hear things, running the lanes. You hear if anyone else's children dream like ours."

"No," Mirabel said. And then, because lying to Sefa felt like kicking a dog: "Not yet."

"Not yet," Sefa repeated softly, and handed her the last of the scrip, and held her grip a moment longer than payment required. "Come by the hab sometime. Anouk draws them, you know. The dreams. She's got a wall of them. Fire and shuttles and a list with names struck through, over and over, different names every time. Eleven years old, and she draws like a witness."

* * *

The *Kestrel's Regret* sat in her berth while Mirabel walked the ring's outer gallery, because she couldn't sleep and wouldn't drink and had nowhere else to put herself. Below her feet, through the transparent deck, the planet rolled: continents she'd memorized as a child, oceans she'd never seen, the northern latitudes where the Amaranth Dome complex sat closed and decommissioned and unremarked upon for two decades, a gray smudge under permanent cloud cover that even the weather satellites seemed to orbit wide of.

No one would notice if she didn't come back. The thought arrived on schedule, the way it did every run, usually somewhere around the lonely middle watch. If the *Kestrel's Regret* went dark tomorrow—if a customs drone caught her, if a micrometeorite found her spine, if she simply drifted out an airlock the way Tomas Reyes had—there would be no one to file the report. No next of kin. No one waiting at any port with her name on a board. Sefa would notice the bread going uneaten, maybe. For a week. The smugglers' network would redistribute her routes within a month and her name within a season, and the galaxy would close over the place where she'd been like water over a dropped stone.

She had chosen this. She reminded herself of it the way you remind yourself of a debt: she had chosen the invisibility, cultivated it, worn it like armor, because visible people got audited and remembered people got erased. Ovid had taught her that too. *Be the vector, not the point,* he used to say, tapping his temple. *Vectors pass through. Points get hit.*

And yet.

She stood at the gallery rail with the planet turning beneath her and her hand pressed flat over the wafer in her pocket, and she thought about forty children drawing fire on walls, and an enforcer who asked questions he shouldn't have needed to ask, and a dead man's voice saying *when they start dreaming* in a tone that hadn't been riddle but instruction—urgent, specific, the tone of a man handing you a lit fuse and telling you exactly when it would reach the powder.

Seven years she'd carried it. Seven years of *not yet.*

In her earpiece, the port chatter murmured its late-shift static, and beneath it—so faint she might have imagined it, so faint she would spend the walk back to her ship deciding she hadn't—someone on an open band was humming. Old-fashioned, half-forgotten, a melody of maybe six notes, repeating.

A lullaby.

Ovid's lullaby.

Mirabel stood very still at the rail while the planet turned and the quarantine lights blinked their patient red, and the hum faded off the band as if it had never been, and her heart, competent and solitary and invisible, began—for the first time in seven years—to pound like something waking up.
