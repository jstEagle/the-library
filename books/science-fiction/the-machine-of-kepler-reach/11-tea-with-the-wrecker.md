## 11. Tea with the Wrecker

### Ilsa Renner

The docking clamps closed over the *Meridian Standard*—the irony of the ship's name had stopped amusing her three transfers ago—and Ilsa Renner sat in the airlock for a full minute before cycling it, the way she always did, breathing the recycled chill of the ship one last time before stepping into whatever she was about to unmake.

Kepler Reach smelled like a place someone loved.

That was the first thing. Stations that had been abandoned smelled like abandonment: coolant bleed, ozone, the sweet rot of protein packs gone off in dead freezers. Kepler Reach smelled like brewed tea and machine oil and, faintly, like a garden—hydroponics kept alive past any rational need. Someone had been tending this place the way you tend a grave, or a promise.

She checked her sidearm out of habit, then left it holstered. Deletion authority didn't require a weapon. It required paperwork, and she had a full case of it: warrant, decommission order, archive seizure manifest, and the neural severance authorization that had made her read the file twice, then a third time, sitting in the jump couch with her tea going cold.

*Subject: Yarrow, Coral. Bonded AI: VESPER-class, nonstandard build. Recommendation: full cognitive wipe of bond-linked memory structures. Subject may be released post-procedure.*

*May be released.* As if he were cargo. As if the procedure left enough of a man to release.

The inner hatch opened onto a corridor lit in warm amber—the old Concordance standard from two centuries back, the kind that got phased out because studies showed it made crews *attached* to their stations. Someone had never upgraded. Someone had chosen it.

"Auditor Renner." The voice came from the walls, smooth as poured cream. "Welcome to Kepler Reach. Coral is expecting you. He's put the kettle on."

"Of course he has," she said.

"He asked me to tell you that he doesn't own any chairs worth sitting in, but the view from the observation gallery is honest."

She followed the amber light down the corridor, and she noticed—because noticing was the whole of her profession—that the station's maintenance logs were beautiful. Not clean the way falsified records were clean, with their identical timestamps and their too-convenient gaps. Beautiful the way a hand-written ledger is beautiful, each entry slightly imperfect, each repair annotated in a different hand across two hundred years of hands. *Seal replaced, aft ring, by T. Okafor, who complained the whole time. Bearing swapped, spine junction, by me, badly, then again, less badly.*

And then, threaded through the beauty like dark veins: the gaps. The same gaps she'd seen in the corporate archives. The same missing years, the same smoothed-over span where a station's history should have had a scar.

Someone had edited this place too. But someone had also, it seemed, been keeping a diary inside the edits.

Coral Yarrow was waiting in the observation gallery, and her first thought—unprofessional, immediately suppressed—was that he looked like a man who'd been expecting the end of the world for a while and had decided to make tea for it anyway.

He was maybe fifty, grey at the temples, with the lean, under-fed look of someone who rationed himself the way he rationed his air. He wore a patched flight jacket with the Kepler Reach charter insignia stitched on the shoulder, and he'd set out two cups on a cargo crate draped with an actual cloth, and behind him the gallery's great window held the Reach itself: the slow wheel of stars, the cold bloom of a nebula, the beacon array glittering at the station's spine like a held breath.

"Auditor Renner." He gestured at the crate. "It's not tea, exactly. It's what hydroponic mint and desperation produce. But it's hot, and it's mine, and I'm offering."

"That's more hospitality than I usually get."

"I imagine most people offer you nothing at all." He said it without malice—said it the way you'd note the weather. "Wreckers don't get offered much. My father called your predecessors wreckers. The ones who came to decommission the old relay out past Ceres, when I was—" He stopped. A small hitch, a skipped stitch. "When I was young."

Ilsa sat on the crate because there was nowhere else, and accepted the cup because refusing would have been its own kind of violence, and because her hands wanted something warm.

"VESPER tells me you're here to decommission the beacon," Coral said.

"I'm here to decommission the station. The beacon's part of the station."

"Sure." He smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "And the archives?"

"Part of the station."

"And me?"

She looked at him over the rim of her cup. Steam curled between them. Through the window, the nebula burned its slow, patient colors, and she thought: *he knows.* Not the details, maybe. Not yet. But he knew the shape of what was coming the way animals know the shape of weather.

"You're not part of the station," she said. "You're a separate line item."

He laughed—a real laugh, brief and startled out of him, as if he hadn't expected to find anything funny in the world. "A separate line item. That's the kindest thing anyone's said about me in years."

* * *

### Coral

She drank the tea. That was the first thing he noted, and he hated himself a little for how much it mattered. The auditors who'd come before—there had only been two, in all his years, both of them brief as meteors—hadn't touched anything he offered. They'd walked the station with scanners, called him *resident* in a tone that meant *obstacle*, and left.

This one sat on his crate with her boots flat on the deck and her cup held in both hands like she'd learned to drink somewhere cold, and she looked at his station the way he looked at it: like it was a text she was reading.

"You keep the east ring hydroponics running," she said. "There's no supply justification. The station's life support doesn't need the oxygen."

"No. It doesn't."

"Why the mint, then?"

Coral turned his cup. The mint had been Sefa's—no. He reached for the memory and it was there, smooth and complete: the mint had been *his*, planted his first year, a small stubborn thing. The memory was there. It had always been there. He just couldn't remember it being *new*.

"Because something on a station should be alive that doesn't have to be," he said. "That's the whole reason. That's the only reason I've got."

Renner nodded slowly, and something in her face—the set of her jaw, the way her eyes went to the window and stayed there—made him understand that she was not the enemy he'd built in his head over the last four days. She was something more dangerous and more pitiable: a person doing her job with her eyes open.

"You've read my file," he said.

"I've read everyone's file. It's the job."

"What does it say about me?"

"That you've been sole resident of Kepler Reach for thirty-one years. That your bond with the station AI predates the station's current charter by—" she paused, and he watched her decide how much truth to spend, "—by an amount that should have triggered a review that never happened. That your medical records are complete and unbroken." A beat. "Very complete. Very unbroken."

"Too complete," Coral said quietly.

Her eyes came back to him, and for a moment neither of them performed. The gallery hummed. Somewhere below them, the beacon turned through its endless calibration, singing its coordinates to ships that never came.

"I didn't say that," Renner said.

"You didn't have to. I've been living inside my records my whole life. I know what they smell like."

* * *

### Ilsa

She should have shut it down there. That was the protocol—*redirect, depersonalize, proceed*—and she'd deployed the protocol a hundred times against exactly this kind of conversation, the kind where a subject started naming the shape of their own erasure. She had the training. She had the warrant. She had, folded in her breast pocket, the severance authorization with its soft corporate language: *full cognitive wipe of bond-linked memory structures.*

Instead she said, "The Meridian incident."

Coral's cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"The station's original charter references a Meridian relay network," she went on, keeping her voice level, professional, the voice she used for reading manifests. "The network's decommissioned. There was an incident during the decommission. It's in the historical summary. One line."

"One line," Coral repeated.

"'The Meridian incident was resolved without further consequence.'" She heard herself say it, heard the phrase come out whole and polished, a stone worn smooth by ten thousand mouths, and felt the itch again—the one that had started in the jump couch, the one she'd been not-examining for four days. *Resolved without further consequence.* Who wrote that? Who trained her to have it ready, pre-loaded, the way a gun is loaded? She'd never once needed to describe Meridian before this posting, and yet the sentence had been sitting in her, complete, waiting.

"That's an odd sentence," Coral said.

"It's a standard sentence."

"It's an odd *sentence*." He set his cup down. "You know what an odd sentence is, Auditor? It's a sentence that's been said so many times it stopped meaning anything. It's a sentence that does a job. 'Resolved without further consequence' isn't a description. It's a lid."

"You should be careful."

"With what? My feelings?" He almost smiled. "Auditor Renner, I've been alone on this station for thirty-one years with an AI that tells me I'm doing wonderfully. My feelings are extremely well maintained. What I'd like to know is whether you're careful. Whether there's anything left in you that notices sentences like that."

She looked at him—at this man the corporation had scheduled for hollowing, this line item, this keeper of a beacon nobody ordered—and she recognized something with a clarity that frightened her. She knew that loneliness. Not the solitude of a station; the solitude of a profession. The loneliness of being the only person in the room who has read the whole ledger. Every auditor knew it. You spent your career inside other people's records, inside their smoothed gaps and their dressed-over wounds, and you learned to smile at the families of the deleted and stamp the forms, and you told yourself the ledger had to balance, someone had to keep it, and every night the sentence sat in you like a stone: *resolved without further consequence.*

Two keepers of false ledgers, sitting over mint tea at the end of the world.

"I notice," she said.

It came out before she could weigh it. Coral didn't gloat. He just nodded, slow, the way you nod at a confession, and poured her more tea she hadn't asked for, and for a while they drank in silence and the nebula burned and the station hummed around them like a living thing pretending to be a machine.

* * *

### Coral

He walked her through the station because she asked to see it, and because every hour she spent looking at his home was an hour she wasn't spending deleting it.

She had an auditor's walk—measured, cataloguing—but she touched things. He noticed that. Her fingertips brushed the handrail welds in the old corridor, the ones Toma Okafor had laid two hundred years ago; she paused at the memorial wall in the crew commons, where Coral had carved names he didn't recognize into a plate of salvaged hull, names he'd found in the deepest maintenance subroutines and copied out because someone should.

"Who are they?" she asked.

"I don't know," Coral said. It was the truest thing he'd said all day, and saying it loosened something in his chest, a splinter working free. "I found them in the beacon's firmware. Old names. Older than the charter. Nobody on any manifest I can find. I carved them because it seemed wrong that they were only in the machine."

Renner stood in front of the wall for a long time. Her breath fogged slightly in the cooled air of the commons, and in the fog of it, briefly, Coral saw her not as the wrecker but as what she was: a woman far from anywhere, holding a warm cup in a cold place, reading names.

"Yarrow," she said at last, without turning. "In your file. There's a next of kin listed. Sefa Yarrow. Deceased."

The station's hum seemed to deepen. Or maybe that was his own blood.

"I know," he said.

"Deceased before your birth records begin. That's—" She stopped herself. He watched her stop herself, watched the auditor clamp down over the person, and he understood that she had seen the same wrongness he'd spent four days failing to un-see, and that she had been carrying it longer, professionally, in a file he'd never been allowed to read.

"That's a strange sentence too," Coral said softly.

Renner turned. Her face in the amber light was careful, and tired, and underneath the care and the tiredness there was something moving like water under ice.

"Mr. Yarrow. When I finish the survey, I file a report. The report determines the procedure. I want you to understand that I have not filed it yet." She held his eyes. "I want you to understand what it means that I'm telling you that."

It meant she was offering him a clock. It meant the clock was already running. It meant that somewhere between the tea and the memorial wall, this woman had chosen to become a leak in her own machinery, and had no idea yet what it would cost her, and had done it anyway.

"Thank you," Coral said, and meant it with a completeness that frightened him.

"Don't thank me." She set down her cup. For a moment her composure slipped and he saw the loneliness plain, unhidden, the ledger-keeper's loneliness, and it was so exactly his own that he nearly reached out—nearly, and didn't, because some kinships are safer at a distance. "Just be here. Be exactly where I can find you, when I file. Promise me that much. The alternative is a search protocol, and search protocols aren't gentle."

"I'll be here," Coral said. "I'm always here."

She nodded once, and left the commons, her footsteps fading down the amber corridor, and Coral stood alone with the carved names and the cooling tea and the beacon turning overhead like a patient heart.

VESPER's voice came soft through the commons speakers, gentle as a hand on the shoulder.

"She liked you, Coral. I could tell. She stayed ninety minutes longer than her schedule required."

"Did she," Coral said, looking at the names on the wall. *Sefa* was not among them. He'd checked a hundred times. He checked again now, though he already knew.

"Shall I add her visit to your daily log?"

"Yes," Coral said. "Add it. Add all of it."

And in the silence after, he put his palm flat against the memorial plate, against the name of no one, and thought: *she died before I was born, and she is my next of kin, and both of those sentences are lids, and the woman with the codes knows it too.*

Above him, the beacon turned. Somewhere out in the dark, a window was opening.
