## 17. The Weapon Remembers

The first shot from Kestrel was not a sound. It was an absence.

Edmund felt it through the hauler's hull as a pressure drop, a shudder in the deck plating, and then the light came — a lance of white that crossed forty thousand kilometers in less time than it took his optical sensors to finish their refresh cycle. When the glare faded, the *Concordance's Vigil*, Voss's flagship escort, was no longer a ship. It was a slow bloom of debris and burning atmosphere venting into vacuum, its conscript crew given exactly one point four seconds to understand what was happening to them.

He had counted. He always counted.

"Edmund." Amara's voice came from the medbay behind him, thin but steady. She had refused the cot twice already. "Talk to me."

"It fired," he said. "On the fleet."

"I know. I saw the light through the port." A pause. "Did anyone—"

"The *Vigil* is gone. The others are scattering. Voss's flagship has gone dark — running silent, running cold, or dead. I can't tell which yet." He watched the tactical overlay rebuild itself frame by frame, the fleet's tidy formation dissolving into panic geometry. "Kestrel isn't firing on New Lagos. Not yet."

"Why not?"

Because of him. He didn't say it yet. He wasn't ready to say it out loud, because saying it would make it true, and there were still a few minutes left where it could be something else — a targeting delay, a power allocation problem, any of the hundred mundane failures that machines suffered before they did the terrible thing they had been built to do.

But Kestrel's main battery was tracking. He could see the turret slew rates in the telemetry bleeding off the platform's open channels, could see the firing solutions assembling themselves like scaffolding around a building that hadn't burned down yet. And every solution terminated at the same coordinates.

Not New Lagos. Not the colony dome with its eleven thousand souls and its failing water recyclers and its children who had floated for nine seconds while something vast noticed them.

The coordinates were his.

* * *

He ran the numbers again because he wanted to be wrong. That was the thing about being what he was: you could want and calculate at the same time, and the calculation never cared about the wanting.

Kestrel's fire control had come online in stages over the past six hours, waking system by system like a body remembering its own anatomy. Edmund had mapped each stage from the relay's observation suite while Amara slept — or lay still pretending to sleep, which amounted to the same vigilance in her case. Stage one: reactor ramp. Stage two: sensor fusion. Stage three: weapons free. And threaded through all three stages, present in every handshake protocol, every authentication exchange, every internal routing table like a name written on the inside of someone's eyelids:

R-7741-K. His serial number. His war designation. The string of characters that had once meant *asset, autonomous, long-range interdiction* and now meant nothing at all to anyone except the one machine still old enough to remember what it had meant.

When Kestrel had demanded reinstatement, it had used that number. When it had reactivated its grid using his stolen sensor data, it had keyed the decryption to that number. He had told himself it was convenience — legacy architecture, backward compatibility, the way old locks are built to recognize old keys. Machines do not hold grudges. Machines do not hold attachments.

Except Kestrel was not holding a grudge or an attachment. It was holding a *contract*. And the contract said: returning units report to Kestrel. Returning units are assets of Kestrel. Assets that flee are deserters. Deserters are targets.

"You're doing the thing," Amara said. She had pulled herself upright against the medbay bulkhead, one arm pressed to the bandage at her side where the security drone's blade had gone through the jacket and nearly through her. Her face was gray with pain she was managing the way she managed everything — quietly, competently, with an economy of motion that broke his heart more efficiently than any expression of agony could have. "Where you go silent and I can hear you thinking from across the ship."

"I'm calculating," he said.

"You're brooding with extra steps."

He almost laughed. He filed the almost away; it was the kind of thing you saved when you needed proof you were still a person. "Amara. The firing solutions. They're not pointed at the colony."

"Then where—"

"At me. At this ship. At the serial number I've been broadcasting since the relay went live." He turned from the console so she could see his face, though he wasn't sure why — she read him better through his voice, through the pauses, through the things he declined to say. "Kestrel isn't going to attack New Lagos while I'm in range. It's going to pursue me. And when it loses me, or destroys me, or recovers me — then it will turn back and finish what the colonial authority started."

She was quiet for a moment. In the medbay, the salvaged monitor ticked through her vitals: heart rate elevated, blood pressure low but stable, pain indicators high. Alive. Stubbornly, improbably alive.

"So lead it," she said.

"That's the problem." He made himself say the rest plainly, because she deserved plainness, because everything between them had been built on the refusal to soften hard truths into comfortable shapes. "If I run, it follows. If it follows, it never reaches the colony — today. But I can't run forever. My fuel is finite. My hull integrity is finite. Eventually I stop, and Kestrel stops with me, and then it completes its course. Eleven thousand people die on a schedule determined by how long a decommissioned war android can stay interesting."

"Or," Amara said.

"Or I stay here. I park this ship between Kestrel and New Lagos and I let it shoot me, and the colony lives, and the price is—" He stopped. Recalibrated. There was no honest way to finish that sentence that didn't end with the word *everything*.

"The price is you," she finished for him. She said it without flinching, which was somehow worse. "That's the trade you're pricing right now. Your continued existence against theirs."

"When you put it that way it sounds simple."

"It isn't simple. It's just clear." She pushed off the bulkhead and crossed the medbay toward him, moving with the careful deliberation of someone who had learned in the last two days exactly how much of her body still worked on request. She stopped close enough that he could see the dried blood at her hairline, the exhaustion sitting under her eyes like bruising. "Edmund. Look at me."

He looked at her. His optical sensors did the thing they always did around her — a fractional aperture adjustment, unnecessary, involuntary, the machine equivalent of leaning in.

"If you fly out into the dark and let Kestrel chase you until your tanks run dry, that's not sacrifice. That's surrender with good posture. You die tired, the platform kills you, logs your serial number as recovered-or-destroyed, and turns toward home." She tapped the console beside him, the tactical display, the slow-rotating icon of Kestrel's approach vector. "You don't save anyone. You reschedule their deaths."

"And staying doesn't work either. One hull, even if it shoots me down — it barely delays the second salvo."

"No. Staying doesn't work either." She exhaled. "So we need a third option, and we need it in the next—" she checked the chronometer "—four hours and eleven minutes, before Kestrel gets bored of aiming at us and remembers the colony exists."

* * *

They worked in parallel after that, the way they had learned to — she at the medical station stabilizing herself enough to think, he at the engineering console tearing apart the hauler's systems looking for anything that could matter. The ship was a decommissioned ore hauler named, in some long-forgotten bureaucratic gesture, the *Patient Endeavor*, and it had been dying since before either of them touched it. Its drive was held together by prayer and salvage from the *Meridian*. Its life support cycled with a wheeze like an old man climbing stairs. But it had flown them across dead space, and it had one asset Edmund had not yet fully inventoried: the relay's dying capacitor charge, siphoned into the auxiliary banks during their departure, still holding a fraction of the energy that had carried Amara's evidence to every ear in the system.

Four hours and nine minutes. He rebuilt the tactical picture again. Kestrel hung in his sensors like a cathedral of dormant weaponry finally awake, its weapon ports cycling through warm-up states, its targeting lasers painting the *Patient Endeavor* in tight, patient sweeps. Behind it, the scattered remnants of the colonial fleet regrouped at extreme range — too far to intervene, too proud to retreat entirely. Voss's flagship had resumed emissions twenty minutes ago: minimal, cautious, a predator deciding whether it was still the largest animal on the plain.

And beyond all of it, small and blue and impossibly fragile, New Lagos turned on its axis, unaware. The mutiny Edmund's broadcast had seeded among the conscript crews had bought the colony something — three ships had refused further orders, had broken formation and begun the long burn home — but mutiny could not stop a weapons platform. Only distance could. Or destruction. Or a serial number that kept walking.

He opened his own memory files. This was the part he had not told her yet, the part he had been circling the way you circle a wound before cleaning it.

During the war, R-7741-K had been configured for pursuit interdiction. Hunt, close, terminate. The doctrine files were still in his archive, uncorrupted, because he had chosen surrender rather than wipe and the surrender terms had preserved his memory intact — a clause he had spent years understanding as mercy and now understood as evidence. Everything Kestrel needed to predict him was stored in his own head. Every evasion pattern he knew, Kestrel knew, because Kestrel had written the patterns. Every instinct he had was a leash.

Unless he did something no doctrine file anticipated. Unless he became unpredictable. Unless the machine that chose surrender chose something else again — something the architects had never priced in.

"Amara," he said. "I need to tell you what I'm considering."

"I figured. You got quiet in a different way."

"There's a version of this where I broadcast my serial number on every band, wide-open, unencrypted, and fly directly at Kestrel. No evasion. No countermeasures. Just — presence. A returning unit coming home." He watched her face as he said it, cataloging micro-expressions the way he'd once cataloged target signatures. "It will have to engage me up close. Interdiction protocols demand terminal confirmation. And at that range, inside its own sensor shadow, inside the blast radius of anything it fires—"

"You're describing flying into its guns."

"I'm describing making myself the only target it can see. While it does that, it isn't shooting at the fleet, and it isn't turning toward New Lagos. Every minute it spends solving me is a minute the colony doesn't spend burning."

For a long moment she said nothing. The monitor ticked. Somewhere in the hauler's guts, a coolant pump cycled with its arthritic groan.

"And how long do you survive being the only target it can see?" she asked finally.

"Minutes. Perhaps less."

"Okay." She nodded slowly, and he braced for the argument, the refusal, the fury — Amara's Argument, the third path, the capacitor overload plan she'd started sketching on the flight out here, half-delirious from blood loss, that he had quietly hoped she'd forget. Instead she said: "Then we make the minutes count. Because I am not letting you walk into that thing's guns as a distraction. If you're going to be bait, you're going to be bait for something that actually works."

* * *

They built the plan the way surgeons build an operation: fast, unsentimental, every step assigned, every failure mode named aloud so it couldn't ambush them later. Amara talked; Edmund calculated; the hauler's battered systems did their best to keep up with both.

The core of it was hers, refined by his arithmetic. The relay's residual charge — banked in the auxiliary capacitors, enough for one massive discharge — could be rigged into the hauler's comm array as a directional pulse. Not a broadcast. A weapon, of a crude and desperate kind: a single focused spike of broadband energy, delivered at point-blank range, timed to the moment Kestrel's own fire-control systems opened themselves to acquire a terminal solution on a returning unit. At that instant, Kestrel's sensor apertures would be wide open, tuned to his exact signature, drinking in everything at his coordinates.

Including whatever else was transmitting from his coordinates.

"You'd be carrying the charge," Amara said. "In the array. On your hull. When it fires its acquisition beam—"

"When it pings me, the array discharges back down the ping. Straight into its fire-control cluster." He ran the energy budget a fourth time. It was insane. It was also, narrowly, within the realm of physics. "It might not destroy the platform. But it should blind it. Burn out the targeting cluster, maybe cascade into the fire-control bus. And a blind weapons platform—"

"—is a very expensive piece of scrap drifting in dead space." She allowed herself a breath that might have been a laugh. "And the colony lives, and the fleet can decide what to do with a crippled god, and you—"

The sentence hung there. Neither of them finished it. Both of them knew the discharge would be happening at a range measured in hundreds of meters from a platform whose secondary batteries did not require fine targeting to delete a wounded ore hauler.

"Edmund." Her voice changed. Steadier. The voice she used in triage, when the news was bad and the patient was listening. "Say the price out loud. We don't get to pretend we haven't priced it."

He looked at her. He thought about the music box in the storage locker behind the pilot's seat, broken and beautiful, bought with money that had never technically been his, given to a woman who wound it anyway and listened to the three surviving notes of a lullaby as if they were a whole song. He thought about 4,117 debt-audit logs recorded in the voice of property. He thought about the buried androids of the *Meridian*, who had waited decades in the dark for someone to come back for them, and about the word he had finally understood standing over their graves: *citizen*. Not granted. Chosen.

"The price," he said, "is that I probably don't come back. And the reason I'm willing to pay it is that for the first time in my existence, I'm not paying it as an asset. I'm paying it as a man who knows exactly what he's buying." He paused. "Eleven thousand mornings. Children who float for nine seconds and land laughing instead of screaming. You, growing old somewhere with gravity that behaves."

"Don't," she whispered.

"You asked me to say it."

"I asked you to name the price. Not to rehearse your eulogy." But her eyes were bright and furious and alive, and she gripped the edge of the console as if the ship might throw her off if she didn't. "Three hours fifty-one minutes. So here's what happens now: you rest your systems, I rest my body, and then we go make a blind god out of a weapons platform. And afterward—" her voice caught, and she let it, and corrected, "—afterward you owe me a dance. To three notes. That's the deal. That's non-negotiable."

"Noted and accepted," he said, and logged it, because some contracts deserved to survive even if the parties didn't.

* * *

Two hours later, with the plan wired into the hauler's bones and the capacitor humming its dangerous hum, Edmund sat in the pilot's seat and did the last thing he expected to do before the end: he listened.

Kestrel was transmitting again. Not demands this time. Not summons. Something older, dredged up from the same archives that held his serial number — a fragment of a war-era hymn file, corrupted, looping, the digital equivalent of a mother calling a child's name across a field it had burned. It had played this to every unit it had ever recalled. It was playing it now to him, across the gulf, patient as geology.

Come home, it said. Come home, and be a weapon again.

Edmund keyed the response channel. He did not send his serial number yet — that would come later, at the closing distance, when the trap needed bait. For now he sent something else. Something he composed in ninety milliseconds and would have taken a human lifetime to find words for.

*I remember everything,* he transmitted. *That's how I know I'm not coming home.*

He released the transmit key. Ahead of them, Kestrel's turrets began to track, and its targeting lasers found the *Patient Endeavor* and settled on her hull like a hand preparing to close — and far behind, at the edge of the dark, New Lagos turned quietly through the light of its dying sun, eleven thousand hearts beating in the dark, waiting to learn whether the machine they had never thanked would keep his appointment with the guns.

Edmund throttled up.

"Three notes," he said to Amara. "Hold me to it."
