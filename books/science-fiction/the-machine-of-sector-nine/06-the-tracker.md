## 6. The Tracker

The tracker was the size of a grain of rice and cost more than a freighter's annual profit, and Voss had carried it aboard the *Perihelion's Daughter* in the lining of his warrant folder, which was the one place a smuggler would never think to search because no one had ever hidden anything in paperwork before.

He had watched Cyrus Okonkwo sign the inspection log with a hand that did not quite tremble, and he had thought: *there it is.* Not the signature itself—the signature was flawless, better than flawless, the kind of penmanship you only get from someone who has practiced being another man for fifteen years—but the pause before it. A half-second hesitation at the line where the original's name waited in printed type. Most people signed their own names without looking. Okonkwo looked every time.

Voss had been doing this work for nineteen years. He had learned to read people the way customs officers read manifests: not for what they declared but for what they weighted oddly, what they stacked too carefully, what they kept near the airlock. And everything about the man calling himself Cyrus Okonkwo was stacked too carefully. The forged Compact transit papers were excellent—Voss had run them twice against the registry and they'd held—but excellence was its own tell. Real documents lived hard lives. They got coffee-stained and corner-bent and re-laminated by bored clerks on Ceres Annex. These papers were pristine in the specific way of something that had been made all at once, recently, by someone who cared very much.

And then there was the blood.

The medical scan during boarding had been routine—radiation exposure check, standard for long-haul crews—and Voss had flagged the sample for deep sequencing without telling anyone, including his own team, because he had learned that the most dangerous thing in intelligence work was a colleague who wanted to help. The results had come back while he was still aboard, humming up through the ship's encrypted channel like a patient insect: genetic profile consistent with Cyrus Okonkwo, deceased, age-adjusted. Match confidence 99.7 percent.

The remaining 0.3 percent was telomere drift. Clones always drifted. Someone had done beautiful work correcting for it—someone with access to Compact-grade gerontological sequencing, which meant this was not some back-alley vat job in the Syndicate underlevels. This clone had been built by professionals, or by grief, which sometimes produced the same quality of work.

Voss had stood in the *Daughter*'s cramped corridor, holding his warrant folder against his chest like a hymnal, and made the decision that would define the rest of his life, though he did not know it yet. He did not arrest the man. He planted the tracker in the folder, wished Okonkwo a pleasant haul to Tycho Relay, and walked back down the docking arm with his heart beating slow and even, the way it always did when he was most afraid.

* * *

Compact Intelligence Station Meridian hung in high orbit over Halcyon Colony like a paperweight holding down a map, and Voss reported to Admiral Kessler in person because the admiral did not believe in intermediaries for matters she classified as *load-bearing*. That was her term. She used it for information that held up other information—the way a single beam holds up a floor, so that if you pull it out, everything above it comes down.

Her office was small, deliberately, and bare except for a display wall currently showing the sector's traffic patterns in amber threads. Kessler herself sat behind a desk with nothing on it. She was sixty-one, silver-haired, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since been repurposed entirely into function. People who met her expected coldness and were instead unsettled by her warmth, which was real, and which she deployed the way artillery commanders deployed ranging shots.

"Sit down, Elias," she said. "You look like a man carrying good news badly."

"The Okonkwo lead is confirmed, ma'am." He took the chair across from her. "The man hauling seed grain out of Halcyon Lower is a genetic match to the original. Clone-grade profile, telomere correction consistent with Compact laboratory standards."

Kessler did not move for a moment. Then she leaned back, and something crossed her face that Voss had learned to recognize over six years of service under her command: not surprise, but the settling of a long calculation into place. The look of an equation resolving.

"Fifteen years," she said. "Fifteen years quiet, and now he surfaces hauling contraband grain through a Syndicate checkpoint like any other ghost with debts." She turned to the display wall. The amber threads shifted. "What triggered him, do you think?"

"We don't know yet, ma'am. There's—" Voss hesitated. He had debated this the whole shuttle ride up, whether to include it, and decided that withholding from Kessler was like withholding from gravity. "There's an anomaly in the colony. Forty-one children in the lower habitat have reported an identical recurring dream over the past three weeks. Same imagery. A dark corridor, a sealed door, nine scratches. The station medical office logged it as mass psychogenic illness. My office pulled the file because one of the children's parents works in the relay maintenance pool."

"And?"

"And the dream geometry maps onto Halcyon-9. The derelict. The corridor matches the ring-section schematics. The door with nine scratches corresponds to a sealed compartment aft of the reactor housing—one that doesn't appear on any survey we have on record."

The silence that followed had texture. Voss had served under officers whose silences meant *you have disappointed me*, and officers whose silences meant *think faster*, but Kessler's silences meant she was letting him watch her think, which was either trust or a test, and after six years he still wasn't sure which.

"The original Okonkwo," she said at last, "was on the *Meridian Star* when it was lost. He was the last person to transmit from it. His final packet was corrupted—partially recovered, mostly noise. We wrote him off as a victim." Her eyes stayed on the amber threads. "Now a copy of him walks out of nowhere, and children start dreaming about a door on a dead station. You see the shape of it."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Say it anyway. I want to hear how you say it."

Voss folded his hands. "The original found something. Something worth killing a freighter full of people to bury, or worth burying himself before he could be killed properly. He hid it on Halcyon-9, behind a door he marked so it could be found again—not by adults, not by instruments, but by whatever means he had available. And somehow, fifteen years later, that mark has started reaching into the dreams of children." He paused. "Which should be impossible, ma'am. Which I have triple-checked and cannot explain."

"Nothing is impossible," Kessler said mildly. "Some things are just expensive." She rose and walked to the display wall, and the traffic threads parted around her reflection like water around a piling. "Elias, do you know why the war hasn't started yet? The real one. Not the border skirmishes, not the checkpoint shootings we call incidents. The war."

"Because neither side can afford it."

"Because neither side can *prove* anything." She turned. "The Syndicates believe—we believe they believe—that the Compact burned civilian habitats on Ereshkigal Station during the retreat two years ago. They've said so in every channel short of open declaration. But belief isn't casus belli. Belief is just anger with nowhere to go. If hard evidence of that operation ever surfaced—authenticated logs, orders in my handwriting or my predecessors', names—it wouldn't start a war. It would end one, catastrophically, in the Syndicates' favor, because every neutral habitat in the sector would side with them inside a month. The Compact's position in Sector Nine rests on ambiguity. Ambiguity is our armor. Do you understand?"

"I understand, ma'am."

"So when a dead man's clone starts moving toward a machine that may be capable of broadcasting authenticated proof of anything to anyone—" she let the sentence hang, unfinished, the way she did when finishing it would make it too real to handle calmly, "—that is not a law enforcement matter. That is structural."

Voss felt the weight of the word settle into his chest. Structural. In nineteen years he had heard men described as assets, liabilities, expendable, deniable. Never structural. It was worse than all of them, because structures weren't blamed when they came down. Structures were simply demolished, and everyone understood it was necessary, and no one filed a report.

"What are your orders, ma'am?"

Kessler came back around the desk but didn't sit. She stood over him, and her voice dropped into the register she reserved for things she never wanted repeated outside the room.

"You will not detain him. You will not approach him. You will let him run. Whatever spooked him out of hiding—and I want you to find out what, quietly—he is going to lead us to it. To the machine, or the vault, or the evidence, or whatever the hell the original buried behind that door. Fifteen years of searching turned up nothing because we were looking for a thing. Now we have a compass. The clone *is* the compass, Elias. He knows where north is, even if he doesn't know he knows."

"And when he leads us there?"

"Then we take custody of the site, recover or destroy the contents, and close the account." She said it the way she might have said *then we requisition fuel*. "Everyone involved. The clone, anyone he's contacted, anyone who's dreamed the dream if it comes to that—I want the perimeter tight. No tribunal, no paperwork, no families asking questions at memorial services. A clean erasure. The sector keeps its peace, and forty thousand people on Halcyon Colony keep breathing, and nobody ever learns how close they came to burning."

Voss kept his face still. Nineteen years of practice. But somewhere beneath the practice, something shifted its weight, the way ballast shifts in a hold.

"The children, ma'am. Some of them are under twelve."

Kessler looked at him, and here was the terrible thing, the thing he would turn over at 0300 hours for the rest of his career: her expression was compassionate. Genuinely. He had seen her comfort widows of officers lost in the Ereshkigal withdrawal; the warmth was not performed.

"Elias," she said gently, "I have read every casualty projection for a full-sector war. Every one. The lowest estimate puts colony deaths at two hundred thousand in the first year alone. I have also read the file on those forty-one children, and I grieve what has to be done about them, I promise you that I do. But arithmetic does not care about grief. One station of witnesses against two hundred thousand dead—that is not a moral question. It stopped being a moral question the moment the numbers got large enough. Morality is for peacetime. I am trying to buy us peacetime." She straightened. "That is the load-bearing truth of this entire sector, and I am the only one willing to carry it. So carry your piece, Officer, and carry it well."

"Yes, ma'am."

"One more thing." She returned to her desk and, for the first time, picked something up from it—a slate, thin and dark, which she slid across to him. On it was a personnel file. Voss recognized the name before he finished reading it: Mara Ilic. Station medic, Halcyon Lower. Former partner of the late Cyrus Okonkwo. Flagged in the system fourteen years ago and cleared, flagged again last week when her name appeared adjacent to the dream reports.

"She was the original's partner," Kessler said. "She'll be the clone's first stop, if she isn't already. Watch her. Don't touch her yet. If the clone reaches the site, she goes on the erasure list with everyone else. If she can be turned—if she loves him enough to give him up—she becomes more useful alive." A thin smile. "Love is the oldest intelligence asset we have. Cheaper than satellites, too."

Voss took the slate. His hands were steady. He noted that his hands were steady, and hated them a little for it.

* * *

His office on Meridian was four meters by three, windowless, decorated with a single photograph of a coastline on a planet he had never visited and probably never would. Earth, someone had told him once. Pacific coast. He kept it because everyone needed one impossible thing in their field of view.

He sat down and opened the tracker feed.

A green point moved across the sector chart, crawling along the shipping lanes out of Halcyon Lower with the unhurried patience of a man who had spent fifteen years learning to be invisible and was now, unknowingly, glowing like a signal flare. The *Perihelion's Daughter*. Registered owner: Cyrus Okonkwo. Cargo: seed grain, destination Tycho Relay.

Voss watched the green dot for a long time.

He thought about the handshake at the end of the inspection. Okonkwo had offered his hand—unusual for a smuggler, who usually couldn't wait for an inspector to leave—and his grip had been dry and firm and brief, and his eyes had done something Voss hadn't catalogued until now. They had apologized. There was no other word for it. The man had shaken Voss's hand and looked at him with an apology already loaded behind his eyes, as if he knew exactly what was in Voss's pockets and forgave him for it in advance.

Or as if he knew what was coming for both of them.

Voss pulled the dream files again. Forty-one statements, transcribed in the flat language of child interviews. *It's dark and cold and there's a door and there are nine scratches on it and someone is behind the door waiting for us to count right.* He read them twice. Then he opened the schematic overlay and laid the children's descriptions across the derelict's ring section, and the correspondence was exact—not approximate, not suggestive, exact, down to the spacing of bulkheads none of these children could possibly have seen.

Someone had put something behind that door that could reach into sleeping minds across eleven thousand kilometers of vacuum. And the original Cyrus Okonkwo had known about it, and had died—or been murdered—with that knowledge locked in his skull, and had trusted the lock to hold until it didn't.

Voss closed the files. He entered the standing order into the tasking system: surveillance only, no interdiction, per Kessler. The system chirped its acknowledgment. Somewhere below, a green dot crawled toward Tycho Relay, and somewhere beyond it, a door with nine scratches waited in the dark for children to finish counting.

He looked at the photograph of the coastline. The waves in it were frozen mid-break, permanent, harmless.

"Lead us there," he said quietly, to the dot, to the dead man, to whoever was listening. "That's the order. Lead us there, and then—"

He didn't finish it. He saved the draft of nothing, shut down his console, and sat in the windowless dark listening to the station breathe, doing the arithmetic he had been ordered to do, waiting for it to come out clean.

It didn't. It never did. But he entered the next shift's schedule anyway, and set the tracker feed to alert him the moment the *Perihelion's Daughter* changed course—because it would, because a compass always swings eventually, and because Elias Voss had spent nineteen years following orders and had just discovered, in the silence of his own office, that he was no longer entirely sure which direction north was.
