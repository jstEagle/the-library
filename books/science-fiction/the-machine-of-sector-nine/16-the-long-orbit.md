## 16. The Long Orbit

The message from Voss arrived at 0347 station time, riding a burst transmission so tightly compressed it made the *Magpie's* antenna hum like a struck tuning fork.

Cyrus read it twice in the dark of the cockpit, the words glowing against his retinas long after he'd closed the display. *Strike fleet departs Kessler anchorage at 0600. Target: Halcyon-9 and all Syndicate vessels in support radius. Timetable confirmed by two independent sources. You have approximately nine hours before they glass the station and everyone on it.*

Nine hours. He did the math the way he did everything now—half as a smuggler calculating burn windows, half as a dead man's echo trying to finish what the living man started.

He woke Mara with a hand on her shoulder, gentle, because some habits you learn even when the person you learned them for is gone.

"Mara. We have to move."

She came up out of sleep like a diver surfacing—fast, disoriented, one hand already finding the sidearm she'd taken off a Syndicate corpse two days ago. Her eyes found his in the dim emergency lighting and something in them settled when she saw it was him. He tried not to think about how much that meant to him. He failed.

"Voss?" she said.

"Voss." He handed her the tablet. "Read it. Then help me do something insane."

* * *

They plotted in the cockpit with the *Magpie*'s engines cold and the hull ticking as it bled heat into vacuum. The chart of Sector Nine spread across the main display was a smuggler's map, annotated in three different hands across fifteen years—the original's neat vector lines, Mara's cargo-route corrections, and Cyrus's own additions, scrawled in the margins like confessions.

The problem was simple and impossible. The Loom needed a broadcast path, and every live relay in the sector was either Compact-controlled or Syndicate-controlled, and both sides would cut the signal the moment it went up. Kessler had spent fifteen years making sure of that. The sector's communications grid wasn't infrastructure anymore; it was a muzzle.

But dead relays didn't answer to anyone.

"There," Cyrus said, tapping a cluster of icons drifting along the sector's northern margin, beyond the shipping lanes, beyond anything worth patrolling. The Relay Graveyard. Eleven ancient broadcast platforms, decommissioned before the Compact even existed, left to tumble in slow decaying orbits around a brown dwarf nobody had bothered to name. "If we chain them—wake them one at a time, hand the signal up the line—they can carry it across the whole sector. Old tech. Analog guts. No kill switches."

Mara leaned over his shoulder. Her hair smelled of engine coolant and the medicinal soap of the station infirmary where she'd gotten her shoulder stitched. "Can you even interface with hardware that old?"

"I grew up—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "*He* grew up pulling salvage out of exactly this kind of wreck. And I have his hands. His muscle memory, if any of it took." He flexed his fingers, watching them move with a fluency that felt borrowed. "It took."

"And the timing?"

Cyrus pulled up Voss's timetable and overlaid it on the orbital plot. The strike fleet's projected path was a red wedge opening across the sector like a wound. Halcyon-9 sat inside it. So did the Syndicate flotilla anchored at Ceres Verge. So, if the math was ugly enough, did everything else.

"We broadcast at 1400," he said. "That gives us six hours to reach the first relay, four hours to chain the rest, and maybe ninety minutes of margin if nothing goes wrong." He looked at her. "Something will go wrong."

"Something always goes wrong." She said it without drama, the way she'd say *the patient is stable*. Fifteen years of station medicine had burned the drama out of her. "What's our approach vector to Relay One?"

"Through the debris shoal off Thessaly Minor. It's the only route that keeps us out of Compact sensor range, and it's also the only route where a freighter our size has a thirty percent chance of coming apart." He paused. "I've flown worse odds. He flew worse."

"You keep doing that," Mara said quietly. "Talking about him like he's a separate person and the same person in the same breath."

Cyrus stared at the chart. "Because that's what I am. A separate person wearing the same life. And right now the life is more useful than the person." He tapped the debris shoal. "We go through."

* * *

The debris shoal off Thessaly Minor was what remained of a mining colony's evacuation fleet, forty years gone—hulls split open like seed pods, cargo containers drifting in slow constellations, a child's pressure suit tumbling end over end with the helmet visor still fogged from the inside. Cyrus flew through it at a quarter thrust with the *Magpie*'s running lights dark, reading the field the way the original had taught no one but himself: by feel, by the way the debris thinned and thickened like current around stones.

Mara sat in the second seat with the nav board and a medical kit between her feet, because she was the kind of woman who kept a trauma kit in a cockpit.

"You're good at this," she said somewhere past the halfway point. Not a compliment. An observation, delivered with a frown, like a symptom she couldn't diagnose.

"I'm good at being him."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "I watched him fly. He was brilliant and he knew it—he flew like a man showing off to an audience of one. You fly like a man trying not to be seen. That's not the same thing at all."

Cyrus threaded the *Magpie* between a ruptured fuel skiff and a slab of hull plating the size of a chapel, and let the silence stretch until it stopped being an argument and became something closer to an answer.

"He hid for fifteen years," Cyrus finally said. "I've hidden for fifteen years. Maybe that's the only part of him I got honest."

Ahead, out of the shoal's gray drift, the first relay resolved against the stars—a skeletal tower three hundred meters tall, solar panels hanging off it like torn wings, its beacon dark for longer than Cyrus had been alive.

Relay One.

* * *

Docking with a derelict was a two-person job done badly by one person, so Mara held the *Magpie* steady while Cyrus went EVA. The suit was old Syndicate issue, patched at the knees, and it smelled of someone else's sweat preserved in the lining. He crossed the forty meters to the relay's maintenance collar on thruster puffs, clipped in, and put his palms against the access panel.

For a moment he just hung there, looking down the length of the tower into the dark below it, the brown dwarf a dull coal far beneath, and felt the specific vertigo of standing on something everyone else had abandoned.

Then he opened the panel, and his hands moved.

He watched them move. Stripping a corroded coupler, bridging a dead bus with a jumper he'd fabricated from scrap, waking the relay's power plant in the exact sequence some engineer had designed a century ago and the original Cyrus Okonkwo had performed dozens of times in another life. There was no thought in it. There was memory in it—not *his* memory, not images or feelings, just the shape of the work living in his tendons like a song stuck in the body instead of the head.

The relay's status lights climbed from amber to green, one deck at a time, and the great dish overhead began a slow, grinding rotation, shedding decades of dust in a silver halo.

"Cyrus." Mara's voice in his ear, tight. "It's transmitting a handshake. Anyone listening will see it light up."

"It's supposed to light up. That's the whole point." He hauled himself along the truss toward the transmitter core. "Kessler will see it too. Which means from this moment on, we're not hiding. We're announcing."

"Announcing what?"

"That the dead relays are alive. That someone's building a road through the graveyard." He reached the core housing and popped it open. Inside, the transmitter's guts glowed faintly with standby warmth, older than both of them put together. "By the time anyone figures out what the road is *for*, the signal will already be walking down it."

He spliced the Loom's interface module—salvaged from Halcyon-9, wrapped in insulation, cradled in a padded case strapped to his chest like a bomb—into the relay's input bus. The module's indicator blinked awake. Somewhere inside it, in architecture no one fully understood, the machine that had spoken to forty children in their sleep waited to speak again.

"Relay One is hot," he reported. "Chain position confirmed. Six hours to broadcast window. Get us to Relay Two."

* * *

They ran the graveyard like a lit fuse.

Relay Two was easier—closer docking, better preservation, a transmitter core that woke almost gratefully. Relay Three fought them, its attitude thrusters frozen, its orbit decaying into the brown dwarf's upper haze, and Cyrus spent ninety minutes of their shrinking margin physically hauling the dish back onto bearing with a winch and his own shoulders while Mara talked him through the load numbers from the *Magpie*'s sensors. When it locked, he floated in the black with his arms shaking and his breath ragged in the helmet and laughed once, a short ugly sound, at the absurdity of a clone of a murdered man doing manual labor on a century-dead radio tower so that a dead man's evidence could outlive them both.

Between relays, they slept in shifts. Or tried to.

During his off-shift on the leg to Relay Five, Cyrus found Mara in the hold, sitting beside the Loom's case with her knees drawn up, staring at it.

"It's warm," she said when she noticed him. "You can feel it through the padding. Like an animal."

"The children said it dreamed at them. Maybe dreaming runs hot."

She didn't smile. "I've been thinking about what happens after. If it works. Every channel in the sector carrying Kessler's name and the freighter's manifest and the footage of the *Meridian*'s last transmission." She looked up at him. "Fifteen years I built a life on top of a grave. If the truth comes out, the grave opens. Everything I told myself about why he died, why I stayed, why I didn't look harder—it all gets re-litigated in public. And I asked for that. I *chose* it. But knowing that doesn't make it hurt less in advance."

Cyrus lowered himself to sit beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched through the thermal layers.

"When he recorded that message," Cyrus said, "he said *you were never supposed to carry this.* He was talking to me. But I think he wrote it for you too. For whoever ended up holding it. He knew carrying truth costs. He paid in advance."

"And what does it cost you?" she asked. "You're the one broadcasting it. Your face, your voice, your—his name, attached to it forever. After today you'll never be invisible again. Isn't that the thing you've been buying with fifteen years of forged papers?"

He considered lying to her, and found he didn't want to. That was new. That was hers.

"Yes," he said. "And I'm going to spend it anyway. Because I finally figured out the difference between him and me. He carried secrets because he chose to. I've been carrying one because I was *born* into it. Today I get to choose. First real choice of my whole life." He exhaled. "Turns out it's the same choice he made. Maybe that's the only inheritance that matters."

Mara studied him for a long moment, and then she did something she hadn't done since the docking bay on Ceres Verge: she leaned her head against his shoulder and let it stay there.

"Don't die," she said. "I've done the other version. It's worse."

* * *

Relay Seven sat in the shadow of a shattered moonlet, and it was not alone.

Cyrus saw the drive flares first—three of them, tight formation, running dark-to-dark the way only people who'd grown up in the Syndicate's smuggling culture ever managed. His stomach dropped through the deck. He cut thrust and killed every emitter, and the *Magpie* drifted silent among the rocks while the contacts closed.

"Mara. Suit up. If they board, you take the Loom case and go EVA toward Relay Eight. Don't argue."

"Noted," she said, already moving. "And if they shoot you?"

"Then the truth dies with me and the sector keeps its peace. Fly safe."

The comm crackled before she could answer. A woman's voice, dry and unhurried, with the flat vowels of the inner colonies: *"Unidentified freighter, this is Syndicate patrol vessel* Kestrel's Debt. *Your transponder is dark, your course matches a Compact interdiction profile, and you are loitering near critical infrastructure. You have thirty seconds to explain yourself before we treat you as a Compact advance element."*

Cyrus keyed the mic. "This is the freighter *Magpie*, Captain Oduya. I'm the one who's been waking your dead relays. And in five hours I'm going to broadcast something on every channel in the sector that Admiral Kessler has spent fifteen years and a lot of murders burying."

Silence. Long enough that he could hear his own pulse in his ears.

Then: *"Oduya. That's a dead man's ship registration. That's a* dead man's *name."*

"It's a complicated situation," Cyrus said.

Another silence, and then a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. *"Hold position. Do not touch anything. I'm coming aboard."*

* * *

Commander Sefu Oduya came up the *Magpie*'s boarding collar alone, which was either a gesture of trust or a demonstration that she didn't need backup, and Cyrus genuinely couldn't tell which. She was fifty-ish, heavyset, with a colonist's build and a Syndicate officer's eyes—eyes that catalogued exits the way other people breathed. She stopped in the hold, looked at the Loom's case, looked at Mara's drawn sidearm, looked at Cyrus's face, and went very still.

"You're him," she said. "Or you look like the recordings. My grandmother served on the *Meridian*. She died in the evacuation that never happened." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "Every kid in my family dreams about a door with nine scratches on it. I dreamed about it when I was nine years old. I still dream about it. So before you say another word, copy-paste—you tell me what's in that case, and you tell me why I shouldn't space you for wearing a dead hero's face."

Cyrus met her eyes and gave her the only currency he had.

"The original Cyrus Okonkwo found proof that Admiral Kessler ordered the *Meridian* destroyed—with your grandmother aboard—to cover Compact war crimes against Syndicate civilians. He hid that proof in the machine in that case, on Halcyon-9, and he died for it. In five hours, Kessler's strike fleet burns Halcyon-9 and every Syndicate ship in reach to bury it a second time. I'm chaining these relays to put the truth on every screen and into every dream in the sector before her guns arrive." He spread his hands. "I can't stop her fleet. I can't fight her fleet. All I can do is outrun her clock. So I'm asking—not as him, as me—for your ships to stand between us and whatever she sends."

Oduya looked at the Loom case for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register—still quiet, but no longer aimed at him. Aimed at something behind the world.

"My grandmother's last transmission made it out. Four seconds of audio. Screaming, and then a woman's voice saying 'Compact vessel, cease fire, we are civilians,' and then static. I've heard it ten thousand times. The Compact called it a reactor accident for fifteen years." She turned to the hatch. "I have eleven ships. Most of them are garbage. All of them are crewed by people who've had the dream." She paused at the airlock, and for just a moment she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the mission. "The* Kestrel's Debt *will screen your run to Relay Nine. The rest will follow as they can. Whatever happens after your signal goes out—war, peace, the whole rotten board flipping over—we'll have chosen it with our eyes open. That's more than the Compact ever let us do."

She stepped through the collar, then turned back once.

"For what it's worth, copy-paste—I don't care whose face you wear. My grandmother used to say the dead don't need their names back. They need the *truth* back. Finish it."

* * *

At 1330, with the broadcast window ninety minutes out, Cyrus stood in the cockpit of the *Magpie* and watched the tactical plot assemble itself into a shape he'd never expected to see.

Eleven Syndicate ships, running lights blazing for the first time in the sector's history, formed a loose crescent between the Relay Graveyard and the deep black where Kessler's strike fleet would emerge. Garbage ships, like Oduya said—old freighters with bolted-on gun mounts, tugs with missile racks, a survey vessel whose only weapon was a very good sensor array. They were not a fleet. They were a wall made of people who had all, at some point in their lives, dreamed of a door with nine scratches on it.

Beyond them, at the edge of detection range, the first drive flares of the Compact vanguard bloomed like malignant flowers.

"They're early," Mara said.

"Or Voss's timetable was soft." Cyrus's hands hovered over the controls, steady, which surprised him. "Doesn't matter. The chain is hot. Relays One through Eight are synced. Nine and Ten in twenty minutes. The Loom rides the last leg to Eleven and broadcasts from the highest platform in the graveyard." He glanced at her. "Whatever happens in the next hour, the signal goes out. That part is fixed. The only variable left is whether we're alive to hear it."

Mara checked her sidearm, checked the med kit, checked him—a quick clinical sweep that somehow contained more tenderness than any embrace.

"Then let's go be variables," she said.

Cyrus brought the *Magpie* around and burned for Relay Nine, and behind them the Syndicate wall held its crescent against the coming dark—and ahead, on the plot, a new contact appeared where no contact should be. Small. Fast. Running straight at them out of the Compact fleet's heart, squawking a code that made Cyrus's blood go cold, because he recognized it.

It was Voss's personal authentication string.

And Voss was not heading for the Syndicate wall.

He was heading for the *Magpie*.
