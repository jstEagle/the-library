## 17. Boarding Action

The station told her they were coming before any sensor did.

Mara felt it in the deck plates first—a faint tremor through her boots, rhythmic, patient, like a pulse she hadn't learned to read yet. Halcyon-9 had been dead for twenty years, and dead things didn't shiver. She crouched at the junction outside the vault corridor, one hand flat against the cold alloy, and listened with her whole body the way the original Cyrus had once taught her to listen to a ship's hull: *the ship talks, Mara. You just have to stop talking long enough to hear it.*

Then the comms panel above her head crackled to life, spitting static and a voice she'd been dreading for six hours.

*"Unidentified vessel and personnel aboard Halcyon-9. This is Major Okafor, Compact Marine Expeditionary Unit Three. You are in violation of Emergency Directive Nine. You have ten minutes to surrender the station and all data assets. Resistance will be met with lethal force."*

The voice was calm. That was the worst part. Calm voices meant people who had already done the arithmetic on your life and found the remainder acceptable.

Mara keyed her wrist unit. "Cyrus. They're here."

Static, then his voice, thin and strained through the suit relay: "How many?"

"Couldn't tell. The breach alarms are dead, but the station's been groaning since they docked at the upper pylon. I'd guess a full platoon. Maybe more."

A pause. She could picture him exactly—knees on the deck behind the reactor housing, the Loom's ancient cabling spread around him like the spilled guts of some enormous machine, splice kit in his teeth, sweat freezing on his neck in the failing heat. She'd kissed that neck an hour ago, in the dark, before either of them said anything, because there hadn't been time for words and there might not be time for anything else.

"Ten minutes," he said. "Maybe twelve if I'm fast. The grid coupling is fused—half this station's wiring is older than I am."

"Older than the original, you mean."

"Older than both of us, Mara. Just—hold the door."

*Hold the door.* As if it were that simple. As if a woman with a welding torch and a stolen sidearm could hold a door against Compact marines with mag-rifles and breaching charges and fifteen years of institutional certainty behind them.

She checked the sidearm anyway. It had come off a Syndicate patrolman back in Chapter Eleven of her life—the one she didn't tell stories about—before the patrol commander, Okonjo, had pointed her ship toward Halcyon-9 and said, *Go. We'll buy you the sky.* The pistol was ugly, unbalanced, and its charge read sixty percent. She'd fired maybe two hundred rounds with it in her life, most of them at targets, none of them at people.

She thought about that number. Two hundred rounds, zero people. She thought about the marines on the other side of the station, who had probably each fired more than that at people, and who had been ordered here to erase a machine full of truth along with everyone who'd touched it.

Then she thought about Amara Okoye, ten years old, who had stood in the colony's comms hub three days ago with forty other children and told the whole habitat, in a voice that didn't shake, *the machine said to tell you it's almost ready.* Mara thought about the look on the girl's face—not brave, exactly. Something past brave. The look of someone who had already decided that the adults were going to fail, and had made her own arrangements.

*You hold the door,* Mara told herself. *Because they can't.*

* * *

The corridor outside the vault was a throat of dead machinery. Emergency strips glowed a sullen orange every twenty meters, running on whatever trickle the station's dying batteries still produced. Frost furred the walls. Somewhere deeper in, the reactor ticked and sighed, and behind the reactor, behind a vault door that had taken Cyrus and a plasma cutter four hours to open, the Loom waited—older than the Compact, older than the Syndicates, maybe older than the war itself, humming a frequency that forty-one children had carried in their skulls like a second heartbeat.

Mara had chosen her ground carefully. The corridor narrowed thirty meters out from the vault junction, funneling through a bulkhead hatch that hadn't sealed since the evacuation. She'd welded a cargo skid across half the gap, angled so it funneled attackers into a kill-lane she could cover from the junction. It wasn't a fortress. It was a delay. Every minute she bought was a minute Cyrus needed, and she'd made her peace with the arithmetic of that a long time ago—in a medic's bay on Meridian Station, holding the hands of people she couldn't save, learning that sometimes the only victory available was *not yet.*

*Not yet* was the whole strategy. Not yet, not yet, not yet. Until the truth was loose in the sky and no one could pull it back.

The tremor in the deck changed. She felt it before she heard it: boots. Dozens of them, moving in the synchronized cadence of people trained to walk like one animal.

She killed her headlamp and pressed into the shadow of a coolant conduit, torch in her left hand, pistol in her right, and waited.

They came around the corner in a wedge—four point marines in matte-grey armor, visors down, weapons up, moving with the terrible unhurried confidence of people who had never once been the ones outgunned. Behind them, more. She counted nine before the corridor's bend swallowed the rest. Nine visible. Call it fifteen. Call it twenty.

The point man stopped at her skid barricade. Tilted his head—she saw the motion, the visor panning—and raised a fist. The wedge froze.

"Contact front," he said quietly. "Barricade. One position, maybe two."

"Light it up," said a voice from behind. Okafor, she guessed. The major herself, up front with her people. That was something, at least. Officers who led from the front at least had the decency to be shootable.

A marine's shoulder lamp snapped on and swept the junction. Mara was already moving—low, along the conduit's shadow, to the secondary position she'd marked. The beam found the skid, the frost, the empty air where she'd been standing.

"Contact lost. Movement, left side."

The first shot came without a warning burst. That was the answer to her question, then. *Resistance will be met with lethal force* hadn't been a formality. They'd been authorized to kill a station medic over a machine and a message, and everyone in that corridor had signed off on it.

Mara fired twice at the shoulder lamp and missed both times—the pistol kicked harder than she remembered—and dove behind the skid as the corridor erupted. Mag-rifle rounds hammered the barricade, shrieking off alloy, punching through the skid's thin plating in bright punctures she could see by the light of her own sparks. Frost rained down. Something above her head burst in a gout of coolant steam, and the corridor filled with white.

*Good,* she thought, wild and cold. *Good, can't shoot what you can't see.*

She crawled through the steam on her elbows, found the edge of the junction, and put three rounds down the throat of the corridor at the muzzle flashes. One marine screamed—not hit, she thought, just screaming, the way people screamed when the math of their own invincibility suddenly came back wrong. The wedge broke. She heard Okafor's voice cutting through it, sharp and controlled: *Discipline! Suppress and advance! They're one person!*

*One person,* Mara thought, and laughed, and hated the sound of her own laugh.

* * *

The next hour existed afterward in her memory as a series of fragments, the way bad nights always do.

The skid barricade, half-melted by a breaching charge, which she'd heard coming—a whine, a click—and survived by being ten meters further back than any sane person would have retreated, because she'd decided in the first minute that she wasn't holding a line, she was spending distance, meter by meter, the last currency she had.

The marine who came around the coolant conduit and found her face to face at four meters, both of them too surprised to shoot, and the long half-second in which she saw his eyes through the visor—young, terrified, a boy from some Compact core world who'd been told he was securing a derelict and had discovered instead that he was storming a tomb—and the moment he raised his rifle and she shot him in the shoulder, and he went down screaming, and she screamed *stay down, stay down, I don't want to*—and meant it, meant it with everything, and would have shot him again if he'd moved.

The torch. God, the torch. When her pistol's charge died at forty minutes—sixty percent, she'd had, sixty percent, and she'd spent it all—she'd stood up from cover with the welding torch burning in her fist like a sword out of a story her grandmother told, a blue-white star at the end of her arm, and she'd advanced on three marines in a steam-choked corridor, and two of them had actually *retreated*, because there is something in the human animal that flinches from fire, some older fear than training, and for eleven seconds she was the most terrifying thing on Halcyon-9.

Then the third one shot her.

It wasn't cinematic. It didn't even hurt, at first. There was a sound like a door slamming somewhere far away, and her left leg folded, and the deck came up and hit her in the hip, and she was lying on her back in the frost and the steam looking at a ceiling she'd crawled through an hour ago, and the torch was still burning in her hand, hissing against the deck, and she thought, with absurd clinical detachment, *that's a through-and-through, left thigh, femoral's intact or I'd already be dead, pressure, get pressure on it.*

She got pressure on it. She was a medic. Her hands knew what to do even when the rest of her was a woman lying on her back in a dead station, watching marines advance on her through the steam with their rifles leveled, and knowing, with the last of her arithmetic, that she had maybe one more minute in her, maybe two, and Cyrus needed twelve.

"Cyrus," she said into her wrist unit. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "How close?"

His voice came back shredded with static and effort: "Two couplings. Ten minutes. Mara, your vitals just—your suit's reading blood loss—"

"I know what it's reading. I'm the one leaking." She dragged herself backward, one-armed, torch still in the other hand because putting it down felt like dying a little early. "Ten minutes. I'll give you ten minutes."

"Mara—"

"Cyrus Okonkwo." She said the whole name, the dead man's name, the name he'd carried like a debt for fifteen years. "You were never supposed to carry this. That's what he told you. Well, I was never supposed to carry a gun, and here we both are. Ten minutes. Hold up your end."

The marines were close now. She could see them through the thinning steam—Okafor's voice behind them, *take her alive, the admiral wants witnesses processed*—and Mara propped herself against the conduit, pistol empty, torch burning, leg pouring warmth down into her boot, and aimed the torch at the first one the way she'd once aimed a scanner at a wound, with the calm of someone who understood exactly what was broken and how.

The first marine stopped. They all stopped. A corridor full of the best soldiers in the Compact, halted by a bleeding woman and a welding flame.

"Major," Mara called, and her voice echoed down the throat of the dead station. "Ask your admiral why a freighter pilot had to die in a cargo bay fifteen years ago. Ask her what was in the cargo. Go on. Ask her on an open channel. I dare you."

Silence. Steam hissed. Somewhere behind the reactor wall, faint as a thought, the Loom hummed—and Mara would have sworn, then and ever after, that the whole station leaned in to listen.

Okafor's voice, when it came, had lost its calm. "What do you know about—"

The lights died.

All of them. Every emergency strip in the corridor, every lamp on every rifle, snuffed in the same instant, as if the station had closed its eyes. And in the absolute dark, from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls and the deck and the marrow of the world, came a sound Mara had heard only in dreams: a deep, rising tone, like a held breath finally released, like a door with nine scratches swinging open.

The children's frequency. The Loom was awake, and it was *loud*, and it was pouring through every piece of metal on Halcyon-9 into every skull in the corridor, and the marines were staggering, clutching their helmets, and in the dark a woman's voice that was not broadcast on any channel said, very quietly, to all of them at once:

*You're on the wrong side of this door.*

Mara laughed—really laughed this time, pain and wonder in the same breath—as the tone swelled, and somewhere beyond the reactor wall a splice closed, and the station's dead relays lit up one by one down the dark like a runway, pointing at the sky, pointing at everything, and Cyrus's voice cracked through her wrist unit, half sob and half triumph:

"Mara. It's live. It's *live*—get in here, we have to see it go out—"

She was already crawling toward his voice, leaving a thin dark line on the frost behind her, torch still burning, still burning, all the way down the corridor the station had opened for her.
