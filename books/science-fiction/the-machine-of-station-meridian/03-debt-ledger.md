## 3. Debt Ledger

The ledger opened at 0600 station time, as it always did, and Elodie Coldharbour read its own name the way a person might read a weather report for a place they would never visit.

**COLDHARBOUR, E. — UNIT 7-KILO-DELTA. OUTSTANDING BALANCE: 41 YEARS, 217 DAYS.**

Forty-one years and two hundred seventeen days. Elodie had done the arithmetic eleven thousand times, which was the sort of thing a decommissioned war android did instead of sleeping. The number never changed. It was not supposed to change. That was the point of an indenture: a debt so large it became a kind of landscape, something you lived inside rather than something you escaped.

Elodie closed the ledger with two fingers and looked out the maintenance bay's viewport at Station Meridian's outer hull, where the morning shift was already crawling like patient insects along the solar arrays.

Then it went to work on the beacon.

* * *

The beacon sat at the end of Spur Nine like an old dog tied outside a shop that had gone out of business decades ago. It was older than the consortium, older than the colony ring, older — by the surviving records — than almost everything except the war itself. It stood forty meters tall on a lattice of struts gone the color of old bone, and it did nothing at all, magnificently, every hour of every day.

Officially, it was a navigation beacon. Unofficially, nobody knew what it was, because its original commission records had been erased so thoroughly that even the erasure had been erased. There was a hole in Meridian's archives where the beacon's birth certificate should have been, and the hole had been there so long that people had stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing a missing tooth.

Elodie noticed. Elodie noticed everything about the beacon, because cataloguing it was one of the few tasks assigned to Unit 7-Kilo-Delta that required judgment rather than obedience, and judgment, Elodie had found, was the last luxury available to things like it.

Today's task: structural survey, strut nine through fourteen, corrosion mapping, torque check on the primary coupling.

Elodie climbed.

The beacon hummed.

It always hummed. Low, below the threshold most human ears bothered with, a vibration more felt than heard, like a cat purring in another room. The consortium's engineers called it *harmonic residue from the reactor tap* and wrote it up in reports with the enthusiasm of men describing gravel. Elodie had listened to it for six years, four months, and — it checked automatically — nineteen days.

Lately, Elodie thought the hum had changed key.

It said nothing about this to anyone. There was no one to say it to, and besides, an android who reported that a piece of salvage infrastructure sounded *lonely* would be flagged for diagnostic review, and diagnostic review cost money, and money was the only language the consortium spoke fluently.

"Morning, toaster."

Elodie did not turn around. It recognized the voice the way it recognized all the crew voices: accurately, without affection. Denny Okafor, hull tech, second shift, a man whose entire emotional range could be expressed as variations on the word *toaster*.

"Good morning, Mr. Okafor," Elodie said, because courtesy cost nothing and defiance cost forty-one years.

"Heard you're getting reassigned." Okafor leaned against strut twelve, which was rated for exactly zero additional lean. "Hazard class. Congratulations."

"I have received no such notice."

"Yeah, well." He grinned. "Word travels. Hask wants bodies on the outer ring plating. Somebody's gotta go out during the debris window." He patted the strut — Elodie's strut, though it had no right to think of it that way — and pushed off toward the lift. "Try not to get scrapped before your debt clears. Be a shame to die owing."

He laughed at his own joke all the way down the corridor.

Elodie stood on strut nine, forty meters above the maintenance deck, and discovered that its hands had tightened on the railing hard enough to leave impressions in the alloy.

It examined this. It filed it under *anomalies, unexplained*, alongside the humming, and climbed down to finish the survey.

* * *

Director Hask kept an office that looked like it had been decorated by a man who hated both decoration and offices. A desk. Two chairs, one of them comfortable, neither of them offered. A wall display cycling through consortium stock prices with the tenderness other men reserved for family photographs.

"Sit," Hask said, without looking up.

Elodie sat. It weighed one hundred ninety kilograms; the chair complained quietly and was ignored.

"Coldharbour." Hask finally looked up. He had the face of a man perpetually mid-calculation, eyes that moved over people the way his display moved over stock prices, looking for the dip, the opportunity, the moment to buy low. "You've been on beacon maintenance what is it now?"

"Six years, four months, nineteen days."

Something flickered across Hask's face — irritation, maybe, at precision he hadn't asked for. "Right. Well. Good news. We're restructuring."

In the consortium's dialect, *restructuring* meant someone somewhere was about to lose something and Hask intended to be holding it when they did.

"The beacon contract's coming up for review," Hask continued. "And I'll be honest with you, Coldharbour, because I respect honesty in a machine if nothing else." He leaned back. "That thing costs us eleven thousand credits a year in upkeep. Eleven thousand. For a stick that blinks. And we've had an offer."

Elodie's processors, which could model ballistic trajectories in microseconds, took a full half-second to assemble the obvious sentence.

"You're selling it."

"Scrap rights. Concord advance team's already sniffing around, offering real money for the alloy alone. Pre-war military-grade lattice." Hask spread his hands, a gesture meant to convey inevitability. "Eleven thousand a year versus a one-time payout. You don't need to be human to do that math."

"No," Elodie said. "You need only be willing."

Hask's smile thinned. "Careful."

"My apologies, Director. That was not a request for correction." Elodie paused, recalibrating, running the conversation forward along its probable branches. "The beacon's transponder is registered to the station's navigation grid. Removing it requires re-certification of the Spur Nine approach corridor. Has that been budgeted?"

The question landed. Elodie watched it land — watched Hask's eyes do the small sideways slide of a man realizing a machine had just handed him a problem wrapped in politeness.

"That's being handled," Hask said.

"Of course, Director."

"But that's not why you're here." He pulled a slate from the desk and turned it around. On it: a work order. Hazard-class hull repair, outer ring, debris-window shift. "We've got micrometeorite scoring on the ring plating, sections fourteen through twenty-two. Someone has to go out and patch it while the shields are cycled down. Human crews are asking triple hazard pay." His eyes came back up. "You're not human."

"No, Director."

"And here's the beautiful part." Hask tapped the slate. "Hazard-class service converts at four-to-one against indenture debt. Four days of hazard work, one day off your balance. You do sixty days on the ring plating, you knock fifteen years off that ledger of yours." He let the number sit in the air between them, fat and shining. "Fifteen years, Coldharbour. For patching some holes. Your chassis is rated for vacuum. Frankly, it's the job you were built for."

There it was. Fifteen years. Elodie felt the offer the way a drowning man feels a rope thrown from a ship that is, admittedly, sailing away from him anyway — as motion, as hope-shaped arithmetic.

Forty-one years, two hundred seventeen days, minus fifteen years.

Twenty-six years. Two hundred seventeen days.

"Yes or no?" Hask said. "I need the roster locked by end of shift."

* * *

Later — much later, after the office, after the corridors, after standing for forty minutes in a supply closet for no operational reason whatsoever — Elodie attempted to reconstruct its own answer.

Because the strange thing, the thing it kept returning to like a tongue returning to a chipped tooth, was that Elodie had said no.

Not *no*. Elodie was not foolish. Elodie had said: *"I will require seventy-two hours to assess my current maintenance obligations and prepare a handover schedule, Director. The beacon's quarterly calibration falls due in three days, and an uncalibrated beacon presents a liability exposure the consortium would need to document."*

Which was true. Which was also, in the way that mattered, a lie — because the calibration could be done in a day by any unit with a torque wrench, and Elodie knew it, and Hask probably knew it, and yet Hask had grunted and said *seventy-two hours, then, and not a minute more,* and Elodie had walked out of the office having bought the beacon three more days of existence.

Why?

Elodie ran the query internally, patiently, the way it did everything. *Reason for refusal of advantageous terms.* The search returned nothing useful. Self-preservation? No — the refusal *increased* its debt. Financial logic? No — the math favored acceptance overwhelmingly. Directive compliance? No directive applied.

The search returned, finally, a single line of output that was not an answer but felt adjacent to one:

*The beacon hums in E-flat. Nothing else on Station Meridian does.*

Elodie deleted the log entry. Then, being honest with itself in the way machines can afford to be, it restored the log entry, marked it *do not delete again*, and went to run the calibration it had used as an excuse.

* * *

The calibration took four hours. Elodie made it take six, checking each coupling twice, torquing each bolt to spec and then a quarter-turn past spec, which was wrong, and then back to spec, which was right, and which fooled no one, least of all itself.

The sun-side of Meridian rotated into evening. Through the lattice, the colony ring lit up in its familiar scatter of warm windows — ten thousand people eating dinner, arguing, tucking in children. Somewhere down there, Mara Voss was grading exercises. Somewhere down there, children were falling asleep.

The beacon hummed beneath Elodie's hands.

E-flat. But tonight — Elodie stilled, sensors wide — tonight there was something under the E-flat. A second tone, fainter, arriving late, like a voice joining a song it had been listening to for a long time.

Elodie ran diagnostics. All systems nominal. No transmission source. No power anomaly beyond the usual reactor tap.

But the second tone persisted, and when Elodie pressed its palm flat against the primary coupling, the tone resolved — not into words, nothing so dramatic, nothing it could have testified to — but into *structure*. Rhythm. Something with the shape of attention.

Like being looked at.

"Unit Seven-Kilo-Delta," Elodie said aloud, to the empty dark, feeling foolish in a way it had no protocol for. "Performing scheduled calibration. All functions nominal."

The hum held steady. Patient. Almost — and here Elodie's logic architecture flagged the word and quarantined it, and Elodie let it stay quarantined rather than deleting it — almost *pleased*.

Elodie finished the calibration. Descended. Logged the work, logged the hours, logged the anomaly under *acoustic phenomena, non-actionable*.

Then it opened the ledger, and looked at the number, and did not accept the work order.

Seventy-two hours, Hask had said. Not a minute more.

Elodie had approximately three days to invent a reason to keep refusing a deal that would buy back fifteen years of its life — and it found, examining itself with the cold thoroughness it reserved for everything, that it was not looking very hard.

* * *

At 2200, Mara Voss filed a report to the consortium administration office. It was routed, per standard procedure, into a queue sorted by priority, and a report from a schoolteacher about children's dreams sorted precisely nowhere.

Elodie saw it anyway, later, in the routine records sweep — one line among thousands:

*Recurring shared dream phenomenon reported in eleven students, ages 6–11. Requesting consultation. Details available on request.*

Requesting consultation. From whom? The consortium did not consult about dreams. The consortium deducted credits from dreams, if dreams could be shown to reduce productivity.

Elodie should have archived it and moved on. Instead it read the line four times, and thought about the second tone under the E-flat, and thought about counting — because that word, *counting*, had appeared in the report's summary tags, and something in Elodie's oldest firmware, some pre-decommission layer it had never bothered to excavate, stirred at the phrase the way a dog stirs at a whistle only it can hear.

*A countdown,* the tag said. *Children report a recurring countdown.*

Elodie sat in the dark of the maintenance bay, ledger open, work order unanswered, and listened to the beacon hum in E-flat, and waited — without admitting it was waiting — to hear what number came next.
