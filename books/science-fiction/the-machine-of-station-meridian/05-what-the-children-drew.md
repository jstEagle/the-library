## 5. What the Children Drew

Mara Voss had taught on Station Meridian for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that children's drawings were a kind of weather report. When the consortium cut the heating ration to the lower ring, the suns in the pictures turned orange and angry. When the fish vats failed and everyone ate protein paste for a month, the houses grew small and the sky grew large. When a new baby arrived in a family, every drawing for weeks would feature that baby, enormous, radiant, drawn at the scale of a moon.

So when she collected Friday's art — the ritual was always the same, crayons passed down a table worn smooth by three generations of station children — she was already reading the pages like a meteorologist reading pressure fronts.

She stopped at Ivo Tann's paper first, because Ivo's papers were always first. He drew with the careful, economical strokes of a child who knew exactly what he wanted on the page before the crayon touched it.

It was not a house. It was not a family. It was not a sun of any temperature.

It was two emblems, side by side, rendered with a precision that made the hair on Mara's arms stand up. On the left: a broken circle around a single star — the sigil of the Republic Ninth Fleet, the one that had been sitting out past the shipping lanes for as long as anyone could remember, close enough to matter, far enough to forget. On the right: a triple-barred chevron inside a hexagon — the Concord Expeditionary Force, its rival across the dark for thirty years.

Ivo was six years old. Ivo had never seen either insignia. Neither had most of the adults on Meridian; the fleets were things you heard about in garbled news bursts, symbols on smuggled entertainment feeds, shapes from another life.

Below the emblems, Ivo had drawn a machine. It was not a child's machine — no wheels, no smiling face, no arms waving hello. It was a tower of nested rings around a bright core, with lines running out of it like roots, or veins, and each line was labeled in Ivo's blocky, determined handwriting: *IN. IN. IN. OUT.*

A reactor schematic. Mara didn't know how she knew that. She just did, the way you know a stranger is crying even when their back is turned.

And then there was the third drawing. The one she kept turning over and over in her hands after the children had gone to rest period, the one she finally locked in her desk drawer because looking at it felt like eavesdropping.

Elodie Coldharbour's face.

The salvage android came by the school twice a month to repair the air scrubbers, moving through the classroom with that terrible courtesy decommissioned war units were trained into, saying *excuse me* and *thank you* in a voice like a filing cabinet being opened gently. The children adored it the way children adore anything patient and sad. They called it *the tall mister*. It let them.

Some child — the crayon work said Pip Okafor, age five, all enthusiasm and no proportion — had drawn Elodie's narrow chassis, its dented shoulder plate, its single optical band. But it wasn't the accuracy that unsettled Mara. It was what Pip had added above the head: a halo of fine radiating lines, exactly like the lines coming out of Ivo's reactor, and beneath the feet, in letters she'd clearly asked an older kid to spell for her:

*A KIND MACHINE WHO KEEPS US SAFE.*

Mara sat alone in the empty classroom with twenty-two drawings spread across her desk like evidence at a tribunal, and admitted to herself what she'd been avoiding since the night Ivo first described his dream in that flat, reciting voice: *a corridor of light, and a number getting smaller, and a voice that was patient.*

Eleven children. Identical dreams. And now identical drawings — not identical in style, but identical in content, as if they'd all been assigned the same homework by a teacher none of them could name.

She laid Ivo's schematic beside little Sefa Ren's version. Same tower. Same nested rings. Same core, same lines, same labels — Sefa couldn't write yet, so hers were squiggles, but the *structure* matched, line for line. Then beside those, Dov Aslan's drawing, which included something the others didn't: numbers along the outer ring. Big ones. A countdown, if you read them in order.

Dov was four.

Mara pressed her palms flat against the desk and breathed the way she'd been trained to breathe during hull alarms — slow, counted, useful. Teachers on Meridian learned early that panic was a luxury the station's oxygen budget couldn't afford.

There were explanations. There were always explanations. The children had seen a feed, some old war documentary leaking through the entertainment relays. They'd heard adults talking. Dreams were contagious the way yawns were contagious; one vivid dreamer could seed a whole sleeproom. She built each explanation carefully, held it up to the light, and watched it fall apart. No feed on Meridian showed Republic fleet insignia — the consortium filtered anything military out of the entertainment package, and had for decades. No adult talked about reactors; nobody under forty even knew where the beacon was. And dreams did not give a four-year-old the internal architecture of a fusion plant.

Something on this station was talking to her students while they slept. Something patient. Something that counted.

And the only adult the children's drawings connected to it was a decommissioned android who fixed air scrubbers and thanked them for letting it.

Mara gathered the drawings into a folder with hands that wanted to shake and refused to. She had one more stop to make before she decided what kind of trouble to cause, and she was going to make it whether Director Hask liked it or not.

* * *

Hask received her the way he received everything — seated behind a desk too big for his office, surrounded by the trappings of authority he'd purchased secondhand. The desk was genuine pre-Exodus hardwood, which on Meridian meant it had been salvaged off a wreck somewhere and probably still had someone's belongings fused to the underside. His wall display cycled promotional footage of the consortium's finest moments: cranes lifting, ore barges docking, workers in clean suits smiling at nothing.

"Mara Voss." He pronounced her name like a line item he'd already written off. "This about the scrubber requisition again? Because I told facilities—"

"It's about the children."

"Then it's about nothing. Next."

She put the folder on his desk and opened it, and had the small satisfaction of watching his eyes snag on Ivo's page despite himself. Hask was many things — petty, mercenary, allergic to any problem that didn't have a line in the budget — but he hadn't gotten to be director by ignoring information.

"Eleven of my students are having the same dream," she said. "A corridor of light. A counting number. A voice that calls itself the Machine. Yesterday they started drawing what they see. Fleet insignia, Director. Both fleets. Reactor schematics. Countdown sequences." She turned Dov Aslan's page toward him. "He's four. Look at his numbers and tell me they're random."

Hask looked. Mara watched him do the thing she'd seen him do in staff meetings when a cost report went sideways: the tiny flicker behind the eyes, the calculation running faster than his face could keep up with. He was recognizing something. Or refusing to.

"Kids share dreams," he said. "It's a documented phenomenon. Sleep contagion. My niece went through a phase where the whole dormitory dreamed about a candy planet."

"Did your niece draw the candy planet's coolant intake valves?"

Hask closed the folder. That was the moment Mara understood she'd already lost — not the argument, but the conversation. You could see him deciding, right there behind the secondhand hardwood, that whatever this was, it was bigger than a schoolteacher and it was going to stay that way.

"What I see," he said, "is a teacher working herself up over crayon scribbles, and a station full of parents who'll hear about it and start asking questions the consortium doesn't have time to answer. So here's what happens now." He slid the folder into his desk drawer — her folder, her students' drawings, gone behind a lock without so much as a by-your-leave. "You're going to go back to your classroom, and you're going to teach colors and counting, and if any child reports further nightmares, you'll route it through medical, standard channels, in writing. Not to me. In writing."

"They're not nightmares," Mara said quietly. "That's the part that should frighten you. Ivo says the voice is *patient*. He says it's waiting for something. He says—" she stopped, because the last part she'd sworn she wouldn't repeat, and then repeated it anyway, because Hask needed to hear it. "He says the Machine remembers Elodie."

Something crossed Hask's face then that Mara had never seen on it before. Not confusion. Not dismissal. *Recognition*, quick and guilty, like a man caught checking a lock he'd told everyone didn't exist.

"The android?" he said, a half-beat too late. "What does a scrap unit have to do with—"

"You tell me, Director. You looked at that schematic like you'd seen it before."

"I looked at it like it was nonsense, which it is." He stood, all secondhand authority, and came around the desk to open his office door with the crisp finality of a man ending a meeting he'd already won. "Voss. You're a good teacher. The kids like you. Don't make yourself a problem. Problems on Meridian get reassigned, and there's no school on the outer platforms."

* * *

She walked back through the colony ring with her jaw set and her mind running hot, and it took her most of the transit corridor to sort the anger into piles, because it arrived all at once, the way it always did.

There was fury at Hask — easy, plentiful, almost comfortable. The man had confiscated children's drawings like contraband. Like *evidence*. Which meant he believed there was something to find, and whatever it was, his first instinct had been to bury it rather than protect anyone.

But underneath that, harder to look at, was the other pile: fury at herself, and at every adult on this station. Eleven children were dreaming in unison about a machine older than any of them, and the grown-ups' response had been a shrug, a requisition form, a drawer with a lock on it. She thought of the parents at pickup, tired and distracted, grateful their kids slept through the night now — *so soundly, isn't it nice, they used to wake up crying and now they sleep like stones.* Sleeping like stones. Being spoken to, all night, by something patient.

The children weren't the mystery anymore. The children were the *message*, and everyone responsible for reading it was too busy protecting budgets and routines to look down.

Mara stopped at the observation window overlooking the maintenance bays, and there — because the universe occasionally cooperated with people paying attention — was Elodie Coldharbour, halfway up a service gantry, running a diagnostic wand along conduit with the methodical patience of a machine that had nothing else in the world to be.

*Ivo says the Machine remembers Elodie.*

She watched the android work. It moved like something that had been designed for violence and repurposed for chores, every motion efficient and slightly mournful, and it paused — she was almost sure it paused — with its optical band tilted toward the direction of the old beacon, the way you glance at a door you're trying not to think about.

The adults were the danger. She'd known it since Hask slid that folder into his drawer, but standing there watching the lone machine on the station that treated her students with unforced gentleness, she made it official, made it a vow, wrote it into herself the way she'd once written lesson plans: whatever was waking up out there in the dark, whatever Hask was hiding and whatever the dreams were for, the children would not be left alone with it. Not by the consortium. Not by the fleets. Not by anyone.

Even if the only ally she had was a decommissioned war android who said *thank you* like an apology.

She turned toward the beacon access corridors, folder-less, plan-less, armed with nothing but a teacher's certainty that you never leave a child alone with something that talks to them in the dark.

Behind her, in a locked drawer in a secondhand office, eleven drawings lay in the dark — and if anyone had been watching the director's private terminal, they might have noticed that at 0300 station time, it pinged an outgoing file transfer to a Concord contact code that Hask very much hoped no one knew he had.

But no one was watching. Yet.
