## 6. Two Fleets on the Horizon

Director Corvin Hask had a philosophy about bad news, which was that bad news was like a leak in a cargo hold: manageable, provided you found it before the passengers did. He had built a career — a modest, comfortable, well-upholstered career — on the principle that most disasters could be talked down to inconveniences if you got to the intercom first.

So when the night-shift sensor tech, a nervous young man named Pell who chewed his own sleeves, knocked on Hask's office door at 0300 station time and said, "Sir, you're going to want to see this," Hask did what he always did. He put on his jacket. He straightened his collar. He smiled in a way that had taken twenty years of practice to make look effortless and reassuring and only slightly like a man who had never once been surprised by anything.

"Show me," he said.

Pell's station was crammed into a closet of a room on the observation ring, all humming consoles and cold coffee and a window that looked out on the slow wheel of Meridian's night side. The colony lights glittered below, eleven thousand sleeping souls in their little heated boxes, none of whom paid Hask's salary and all of whom, technically, depended on it.

"Long-range array picked this up forty minutes ago," Pell said, pulling up the plot. "I ran the filters twice because I didn't believe it the first time."

The display bloomed. Hask saw it and felt something in his stomach perform a slow, professional somersault.

Two fleets.

Not debris fields. Not sensor ghosts. Not the ghost of a sensor ghost. Fleets — capital ships in formation, drive flares burning steady and deliberate, the kind of burns you only made when you had stopped caring about fuel economy and started caring about arrival time. The Republic task group was coming around the long curve of the Verge, forty-one hulls in a textbook spearhead. The Concord squadron was sliding in from the opposite quadrant, thirty-three ships in a tighter, meaner wedge.

Both converging on Meridian.

Both, by every chart Hask had memorized in twenty years of not thinking about them, supposed to be on the other side of the Armistice Line, where they had sat glaring at each other through three decades of stalemate like two dogs chained to opposite sides of a yard.

"Pell," Hask said carefully. "When did they change course?"

"That's the thing, sir." Pell wiped his palms on his trousers. "They didn't change course. The archive says these vectors have been building for eleven days. Slow corrections. Tiny ones. We only see the shape of it now because the geometry finally closed."

"Eleven days." Hask stared at the plot. Eleven days ago he had been worried about a water reclaimer contract and whether the canteen's protein printer could be coaxed into making something that wasn't loaf. "What are they responding to?"

"That's the other thing, sir." Pell's voice dropped, as if the room itself might be listening. "There's no traffic. No diplomatic burst, no fleet courier, nothing on any band. It's like they both just... decided."

Hask looked at the two converging wedges of military hardware, and then — because he was Corvin Hask, and because his mind had always been a ledger before it was anything else — he did the arithmetic.

Eleven days ago.

He thought of the beacon. He thought of the power draw anomalies his own maintenance reports had flagged and he had filed under *non-critical, revisit Q3*. He thought of the decommissioned android up there on the ridge, cataloguing bolts in the dark, and the way the consortium's own records on that beacon had a hole in them where a commissioning document should have been.

He thought, with a sensation like cold water finding the seam of his boot, that he had been meaning to sell that beacon for scrap.

"Sir?" Pell said. "Should I wake the watch officer?"

"No," Hask said, too quickly, and then smoothed it over. "I mean — not yet. Let me look at this first. If this is a sensor calibration error, I don't want the whole station losing sleep over a ghost."

"It's not a calibration error, sir."

"I know it's not a calibration error, Pell." Hask exhaled through his nose, long and slow, the way he did when he was buying himself time. "Which is exactly why we're going to be very careful about who hears it first. Do you understand me?"

Pell looked at him. He was maybe twenty-three, and he had the particular earnestness of young men who still believed institutions worked. "The Republic fleet is coming here, sir. And the Concord fleet is coming here. Shouldn't the crews — shouldn't somebody —"

"Somebody," Hask said, "is me. I'll handle it. You did good work, Pell. Excellent work. This conversation stays in this room until I say otherwise. That's an order, and it's also a kindness — you don't want to be the one who started a panic on the colony ring. You've seen what eleven thousand people do when someone shouts 'fire' in a corridor."

Pell had, in fact, seen it. Last year a coolant scare in Hydroponics had produced three broken arms and a woman who tried to sell her apartment at a loss in under four minutes. He nodded, mollified, and Hask clapped him on the shoulder and told him he had a future in this business, which was true in the sense that a fish has a future in a barrel.

* * *

Hask did not go back to bed. He went to his office and locked the door and pulled up everything the consortium had on the beacon, which took four minutes, because there was almost nothing.

That was the part that had always nagged at him, in the small hours, the way a loose tooth nags at a tongue. Meridian's consortium had inherited the station from the colonial authority, which had inherited it from the war administration, which had dissolved in a rain of paperwork and unpaid pensions. Somewhere in that chain of custody, the beacon's file had been — not lost. *Trimmed.* The physical beacon sat on the ridge above the colony like a lighthouse that had forgotten what it was for, and the consortium's ledger listed it as an asset of unknown provenance, estimated scrap value, and a maintenance obligation Hask had been trying to shed for a decade.

He had always assumed the missing records were the usual bureaucratic rot. Now, watching two fleets sail toward his station across a chart that hadn't moved in thirty years, he found himself wondering what kind of machine you had to bury so thoroughly that even its owners forgot it.

He called up the power logs. The anomalies were still there — a slow, rhythmic draw from the beacon's core, too regular to be leakage, too patient to be a fault. He had flagged it twice. Both times, the maintenance queue had eaten the ticket. Both times, the android — Coldharbour, the war-surplus one with the ledger of debts — had requested access to the sealed core, and both times Hask had denied it, because sealed cores were consortium property and consortium property did not get opened by indentured hardware with more curiosity than was cost-effective.

*Eleven days,* he thought. *The vectors have been building for eleven days.*

He searched the logs for when the power draw had changed. It had changed eleven days ago. It had *increased* eleven days ago — a step up, clean as a switch thrown.

Hask sat very still in his chair for a long moment, listening to the station breathe around him. Somewhere below, eleven thousand people were sleeping. Somewhere above the ridge, a machine nobody had ordered was humming to itself, and two fleets that had spent thirty years not moving were moving, both of them, toward the hum.

He was not a stupid man. He was, in his own estimation, a very intelligent man whose intelligence had been cruelly underutilized by the universe, and he understood exactly what he was looking at. He also understood, with equal clarity, what it would mean to say it out loud.

Because here was the problem with the truth: the truth had no owner. If he reported that Meridian's beacon might be a live war-orchestrator of pre-Armistice vintage, the report would go to the colonial authority, which would go to the Republic, which would go to the Concord through channels that leaked like the canteen's protein printer, and inside a week both fleets would know that Meridian was either the most important piece of real estate in the system or the most dangerous, and both of them would come to the same conclusion: *better us than them.*

And Hask — Hask would be a footnote. *Station director during the incident.* He could already see his name in someone else's report, in someone else's memoir, in the past tense.

Whereas if he kept it quiet. If he kept it *contained.* If he could find out what the beacon actually was, and who else knew, and what it was worth — well. Information was an asset. Assets had value. And value, in Hask's long experience, was the only kind of peace that held.

He drafted three messages that night and deleted all three. The first was to the colonial authority. The second was to a Republic naval contact who owed him a favor. The third was to no one in particular, just the shape of a plan, which he kept in his head where plans belonged.

Then he did the thing he was best at. He went to the crew mess at 0600, bought coffee for the watch, and told everyone, warmly, that long-range had picked up some convoy traffic on the Verge and the consortium was looking at a possible salvage windfall, nothing to worry about, plenty of work to go around. He watched the relief move through the room like weather. He was very good at this. He had always been very good at this.

* * *

By the second day, the fleets were close enough that the civilian telescopes could see them, which meant the colony could see them, which meant the lie acquired a shelf life.

Hask managed it. He called it "joint naval exercises," a phrase that had soothed colonial populations since before his grandfather's time, and he posted it on the community board with a cheerful graphic of two stylized ships. Mara Voss — the schoolteacher, sharp-eyed, the one who had been making noise about the children's dreams — looked at the graphic for a long moment in the corridor and then looked at Hask with an expression he did not enjoy, the expression of a woman filing something away.

"Exercises," she said.

"Joint exercises," Hask confirmed. "Very routine."

"Two fleets that haven't moved in thirty years, exercising together. Over us."

"Near us," Hask said. "The distinction matters, Ms. Voss. It's the difference between weather over a town and weather in it."

She held his gaze a beat too long, then walked on, and Hask found himself, unaccountably, sweating.

That night he took a shuttle up to the ridge himself. He told the pilot it was a routine asset inspection. He stood in the beacon's shadow with his hands in his coat pockets, and it was colder up there than he remembered, and quieter — the kind of quiet that had weight to it, like the quiet inside a held breath.

The beacon hummed. He had never noticed it hum before. He was almost certain it had never hummed before.

"Coldharbour," he said, because the android was there, of course, at the base of the tower with its maintenance kit, its optical sensors catching the shuttle's running lights. It turned with that unhurried, mechanical patience that had always struck Hask as vaguely insolent.

"Director Hask. This is outside your usual inspection schedule."

"I own the schedule," Hask said. "I'm inspecting the asset. Anything to report?"

The android was quiet for a moment. Later, Hask would turn that moment over many times, the way you turn a coin to check both faces. "Routine maintenance," it said at last. "The core remains sealed, per consortium directive."

"Good," Hask said. "Keep it that way. And Coldharbour — there's naval traffic in the system. Exercises. If anyone asks, you've seen nothing, because you've been up here cataloguing bolts, which is all you've seen. Am I understood?"

"Understood, Director," the android said, and Hask could have sworn — could have *sworn* — that something behind those optics was measuring him, the way he measured assets.

He flew back down and told himself the cold on the ridge was just the cold. He told himself the hum was thermal expansion. He told himself he had time — time to understand the asset, time to establish provenance, time to position himself as the indispensable man between two fleets and a machine that might be worth more than the station, the colony, and everyone in it combined.

In the sensor closet, Pell chewed his sleeves and watched the drive flares brighten. In the colony ring, eleven thousand people went about their evening. In the shielded dark of the beacon's core, something patient continued to count.

And on the third day, the Republic fleet's lead ship flashed a direct hail to Station Meridian — not to the consortium, not to the colonial authority, but to the *station*, addressed in a cipher Hask did not recognize and could not read, requesting confirmation of armistice status.

Hask stared at the message for a long, long time.

It was addressed to the beacon.
