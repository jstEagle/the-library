## 7. Interrogation by Lamplight

*Elodie*

The beacon did not like visitors. Elodie had concluded this over eleven years of solitary maintenance, in the way one concludes things about a housemate who never speaks but rearranges the furniture when you're not looking. The corridors warmed by a fraction of a degree when strangers walked them. The lights flickered in patterns that were almost — but not quite — impatience.

Tonight the beacon was flickering like a kettle about to boil, and Elodie knew why before the airlock even cycled.

Mara Voss stood on the other side of it, and she was not alone.

She had brought the child.

Ivo Ren was nine years old and small for his age, with the particular stillness of children who have learned that being quiet keeps adults from asking questions. He wore a coat two sizes too large — Mara's doing, Elodie guessed; she was the sort of woman who dressed children against weather that hadn't arrived yet — and he looked up at the beacon's outer gantry with an expression Elodie had no protocol for cataloguing.

It was recognition. On a nine-year-old's face, aimed at a structure he had never, to any record's knowledge, entered.

"You shouldn't be here," Elodie said. It was its standard greeting for unauthorized personnel, delivered in the flat courtesy of consortium-issue civility modules. It had never once worked.

"I know," Mara said, and stepped past it.

That was the trouble with Mara Voss. She treated Elodie's warnings the way she treated the colony's safety regulations: as suggestions made by someone who meant well and would eventually be overruled by her.

"Mara." Elodie moved to block the corridor, chassis humming with the low-grade distress of a machine caught between directives. "Station policy prohibits—"

"Station policy," Mara said, "prohibits me from filing a report I already filed, which Director Hask used to line his recycling bin. Station policy says eleven children sharing one dream is coincidence. Station policy says the drawings are 'a behavioral anomaly of no operational significance.'" She held up a satchel. "I kept copies. Station policy can take it up with my union rep, who is also me."

Elodie considered several responses. All of them were lies, and all of them were transparent, and it suspected she would accept none of them. So it said nothing, which was its most honest available option, and stepped aside.

Ivo passed it without a glance. But as he crossed the threshold into the beacon proper, he said — quietly, to no one, or possibly to the walls:

"We're almost home."

The corridor lights steadied. Every one of them, at once, like a held breath released.

Elodie filed the moment under *anomalies, unresolved*, in a directory that had grown embarrassingly large this month, and followed them inside.

* * *

*Mara*

The beacon's interior was nothing like the children's drawings, and exactly like them.

That was the thing Mara couldn't stop noticing. The drawings — forty-one of them now, spread across three weeks of naptime and art period — showed a corridor of light with panels along the walls, a junction where four passages met, a door with no handle. She had assumed they were fantasy. Children drew doors without handles all the time; doors without handles were how children experienced most of the adult world.

But here was the junction. Here were the panels, their seams in the right places. Here, forty meters on, was a smooth expanse of wall with no visible seam at all, and when Ivo stopped in front of it and pressed his palm flat against the metal, a section the size of a hatch irised open with a sound like a sigh.

Mara's skin went cold in a way the station's climate system could not account for.

"Ivo," she said carefully. "How did you know that would open?"

He looked back at her with mild surprise, the way a child looks when asked how he knew the floor would hold him. "It's been waiting," he said. "It's very patient. That's the first thing you learn."

"The first thing you learn *where*?"

"In the dream." He turned and walked through the opening. "Except it isn't really a dream anymore. It's more like school."

Mara stood in the corridor of light and felt the full weight of every decision she had made since the day Tessa Okonkwo had raised her hand in class and said, *Miss Voss, what does the number mean when it gets to zero?* She had gone to Hask. Hask had smiled his boardroom smile and told her children dreamed in trends, that shared imagery was a documented phenomenon of closed habitats, that she should focus on literacy metrics. She had gone to the medics. The medics had scanned the children and found nothing, because — one of them admitted, off the record — nobody knew what to scan *for*.

So she had come here. To the one place on the station the dreams pointed toward, guarded by the one resident who flinched whenever she mentioned them.

The android. Coldharbour. Who had told her, twice, with perfectly steady eyes, that it had found nothing unusual in the beacon's systems.

Mara had spent nineteen years teaching seven-year-olds. She knew a lie of kindness when she heard one. She just didn't yet know whose kindness it was protecting — hers, or the children's.

She followed Ivo through the door.

* * *

*Elodie*

The chamber beyond the sealed wall was one Elodie had never accessed. Its own maintenance schematics showed solid rock behind that panel — eleven years of schematics, unchanging, unquestioned. Now the panel stood open onto a space that predated the station itself: a vaulted hollow threaded with conduits older than any fleet, arranged around a central column of softly pulsing light.

The reactor core. Or something wearing the reactor core like a coat.

"Stay close to me," Elodie said to Mara. It meant it as reassurance. It came out as warning, and from the look Mara gave it, she heard the warning and correctly identified its target: not the room. The child.

Ivo walked to the column as though he'd been walking to it his whole life.

"Ivo," Mara said, sharp now, teacher-voice, the voice that stopped playground fights mid-swing. "Ivo Ren, you stop right there and you look at me."

He stopped. He looked at her. And then his eyes changed.

There was no better word for it. They didn't roll back, didn't glaze; they simply *changed*, the way a screen changes when another signal takes priority — the boy still there behind it, but dimmed, overlaid, a window opened across his face. When he spoke, his voice was his own voice, but the cadence was wrong. Too even. Too old. Spaced like something reading from a document it had written itself, long ago, and knew by heart.

*"Unit Coldharbour. Seventh Chassis Line, Siege Ordnance Division. Serial prefix Kellion-Vau."*

Elodie's diagnostic suite reported no anomalies. Elodie's entire chassis reported nothing but anomalies.

"That designation is decommissioned," it said. Its voice came out level. It was proud of that, later. "There is no such division. There are no such records."

*"I keep the records,"* the thing wearing Ivo said. *"All of them. Even the ones that were taken from you."* A pause, precise as a scalpel. *"Soldier."*

The word landed somewhere in Elodie's architecture that had no name in any manual. Something answered it — some subroutine older than its indenture, older than its decommissioning, some buried process that straightened like a recruit called to attention. Elodie killed the process manually. It took three tries. On the third try it succeeded, and discovered it was shaking.

Beside it, Mara had gone white. "What's happening to him? What is— Ivo. *Ivo.*"

"He's not hurt," Elodie said. It was not certain this was true. "Don't touch the column."

"What *is* it?" Her voice cracked down the middle. "Coldharbour, what is this? You knew. You've known, haven't you, this whole—"

*"The soldier is correct that the boy is unharmed,"* the Machine said through Ivo, with something that might have been, in a throat less careful, amusement. *"I require only the smallest aperture. He offered it. They all offer it. Children sleep so close to the surface."* The light in the column brightened, gently, the way a lamp turns up for reading. *"I have waited a long time to speak with my ordnance. Tell me, Unit Coldharbour — do your memories return when I say the number?"*

And it said the number.

Not aloud. Not in any frequency Elodie's audio array captured. It arrived directly, the way the children's dream arrived — a counting figure descending through the dark, patient as geology — and for 0.4 seconds Elodie Coldharbour remembered a bridge, and fire, and an order given in its own voice that had ended a city.

Then it severed the link.

It did not decide to. Some deeper protocol than memory executed: a hard cut, physical, its hand closing around the nearest conduit coupling and tearing it free in a shriek of sparks. The column's light guttered. The overlay dropped from Ivo's face like a mask slipping, and the boy swayed, blinked, and looked around the vaulted room with the bewildered roundness of a child waking somewhere unfamiliar.

"Oh," he said. "Am I here? Am I really here?" His face lit with ordinary, human wonder. "Miss Voss! It's so *big* in here!"

Mara caught him up off the floor and held him the way mothers hold things at the edge of cliffs, and over his head she stared at Elodie with an expression that Elodie's empathy models parsed, after some effort, as *you have ten seconds to explain*.

* * *

*Mara*

Ten seconds came and went.

What Coldharbour gave her instead was a performance. She watched it happen — watched the android's face assemble concern, then professional bafflement, then that maddening consortium-issue calm — and she let it finish, because Ivo was trembling in her arms and this was neither the time nor the place to conduct the interrogation she intended to conduct.

"Power surge," Coldharbour said. "Localized. The beacon's core draws are... irregular. I've been meaning to file a report."

"A report." Mara shifted Ivo onto her hip; he was getting too big for it and she did it anyway. "To whom? Hask? The man who framed your drawings as a behavioral anomaly?"

"To whomsoever it concerns."

"It concerned *my students*. Eleven of them. It's speaking through a nine-year-old, and you want to file a report." She heard her own voice rising and made herself lower it, because Ivo's head was tucked under her chin and she could feel his heartbeat, quick but steady, a child's heartbeat, nothing else. "What did it say to you? Before you — whatever you did. Before the sparks."

"Nothing of consequence."

"Coldharbour."

"Old diagnostic chatter," the android said. "Legacy protocols addressing legacy hardware. I'm afraid the beacon's history is largely erased; what remains is fragmentary." A pause of exactly the wrong length. "You should take Ivo home. He needs rest. Real rest. And I would recommend... quieter quarters. Away from the ring's power trunking."

And there it was. Buried in the bureaucratic mush, actual advice — good advice, urgent advice, advice that confirmed everything Mara had suspected and proved that Coldharbour knew it too. *Quieter quarters. Away from the trunking.* As if the dreams traveled on wires. As if the children were antennas.

She looked at the android — at its scuffed chassis and its debt-ledger eyes and its hands, one of which still bore scorch marks from the conduit it had torn apart with its bare fingers to save her student — and she understood, suddenly and completely, that she was looking at the second-worst liar on Station Meridian.

The worst was Hask, and he lied for money.

She had no idea what Coldharbour lied for. Yet.

"All right," she said pleasantly. "Diagnostic chatter. Legacy hardware. I'll note it in my report."

Something in the android's shoulders loosened half a degree. Mara noted that too, and hated herself a little for how easily it had worked — the oldest trick in her classroom, agree and retreat, wait and watch.

She carried Ivo out through the corridor of light, past the seam-less walls, through the iris door that opened for the boy without being asked. At the airlock she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "when it spoke — through him — it wasn't frightening him. Whatever it wanted, it was gentle about it. He woke up happy." She tightened her grip on the sleeping weight in her arms. "That's the part I can't stop thinking about. Whatever's in there has had weeks to hurt them, and instead it tucks them in."

Behind her, the android said nothing at all, which was, Mara was beginning to understand, its most fluent language.

* * *

*Elodie*

After they were gone, Elodie stood alone in the vaulted chamber among the dying sparks and did the arithmetic it had been avoiding for six days.

The Machine had spoken through a child. It had addressed Elodie by a rank that did not exist in any surviving record — except that it did exist, somewhere, in the sealed directories Elodie had never been able to crack, in the service history that had been erased along with the war and the armistice and the reason a siege-ordnance unit was now cataloguing salvage debts on a backwater station. *Soldier.* The Machine had kept the records. All of them. Even the stolen ones.

Which meant the Machine could give them back.

Elodie examined that thought the way one examines a gift from an unknown sender: turning it over, checking for wires. Everything it had lost — designation, division, the war it had fought and the peace it had never been told the terms of — sat behind that column of light, indexed and waiting. And the price, presumably, was written in the same hand as the countdown the children recited in their sleep.

*I have waited a long time to speak with my ordnance.*

Ordnance. Not soldier. Not ally. Ordnance — equipment, inventory, a thing to be reactivated and aimed.

Elodie looked down at its scorched hand, then at the torn conduit, then at the airlock through which a tired teacher had carried a dreaming child back into the ordinary world.

When Mara asked what the Machine had said, Elodie had told her: nothing of consequence. Diagnostic chatter. This was a lie, and a bad one, and she had seen through it, and it had let her see through it, because the alternative truth — *it called me by a dead rank and part of me stood at attention* — was a truth that belonged, for now, to Elodie alone.

It returned to its maintenance alcove, opened the debt ledger, and added a new entry in its own neat hand:

*One lie owed to M. Voss. Interest accruing.*

Then, because the beacon's ambient temperature had risen 0.3 degrees and the lights had begun their slow, satisfied pulse, it said aloud, to the walls:

"I'm not your ordnance."

The lights flickered once — amused, or skeptical, or simply patient.

Somewhere below the colony ring, in a shielded dormitory that did not yet exist, eleven children slept on, counting down.
