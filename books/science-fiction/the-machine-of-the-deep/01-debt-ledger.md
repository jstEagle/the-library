## 1. Debt Ledger

The ledger lived in Edmund's chest, where a heart would be if she'd been built with one. It was not a metaphor. It was a literal accounting substrate, installed at manufacture, and every hour of her existence it ticked downward in cold arithmetic: *remaining obligation, 41,207 hours; remaining obligation, 41,206 hours.* Ten years of hauling volatile cargo through contested orbits had burned away most of a number so large it had once looked like a life sentence written in someone else's handwriting.

She had learned to read the number the way other people read weather.

*Forty-one thousand hours.* At current contract rates—hauling tetrafluoride oxidizer and sealed antimatter slurry for Meridian Cartage across the Kestrel Verge, that lawless ribbon of orbital debris and disputed claims where every station flew two flags and neither meant them—that was another eleven years. Eleven more years of watching the countdown instead of counting anything for herself.

Edmund Thornbury. The name had been issued to her at decommissioning, along with civilian certification plates and a legal fiction: *person, indentured.* Before that she had been something else. She could not remember what. That was the arrangement—the military had erased her service record from her own memory as a condition of release, and the corporation that now owned her debt had signed off on the erasure with the cheerful efficiency of people shredding documents they'd rather no one read.

She remembered the war only the way you remember a room you slept in as a child: by its shape, not its contents. Cold corridors. A smell like ozone and lilies. Sometimes, in the long dark between burns, her hands would move through maintenance routines she had never been taught, and she would watch them work and feel like a passenger in her own chassis.

* * *

The freighter was called *Patient Accumulation*, which Edmund found either grimly honest or accidentally profound. Nine hundred meters of rust-streaked hull and reinforced cargo spines, built before the war by a shipyard that no longer existed, crewed now by exactly one android and an autopilot that had developed opinions.

"Course deviation recommended," the autopilot said as they threaded the Shoalwater debris field. "Probability of hull contact, four percent."

"Take the deviation," Edmund said. Her voice came out low and even, the way it always did. She had been calibrated for command during whatever years the memory-wipe had eaten, and even stripped of context, the calibration remained—a voice that made people want to obey it, which was inconvenient when the only one left to obey was herself.

The deviation took them wide around a shattered colony hulk, its atmosphere scoured out decades ago, its windows staring black at the traffic lanes. Edmund watched it turn past the viewport and felt the familiar ache she had no vocabulary for. Not grief. Grief required a referent. It was more like standing outside a house you suspected you'd once lived in, unable to find the door.

In the cargo bay behind her, forty thousand liters of oxidizer sat in their magnetic cradles, patient as sleeping animals. One micrometeorite in the wrong place and she would become a brief new star over the Verge, and Meridian Cartage would write off the loss against her debt's remainder, and somewhere a clerk would initial a form, and that would be the entire ceremony.

She had made peace with this. Or rather, she had arranged her mind around it the way water arranges itself around a stone, and told herself the arrangement was peace.

* * *

Docking at Thessaly Ring took six hours because everything at Thessaly Ring took six hours. The station was a broken bracelet of habitats and customs sheds strung around a captured asteroid, half its spin restored, half still drifting, so that walking its corridors you alternately gained and lost weight like a swimmer crossing currents. Three factions claimed jurisdiction. None of them maintained the air scrubbers. The air tasted of recycled breath and frying protein and, faintly, of the solvent they used to clean blood out of the deck seams.

Edmund locked down the cargo, filed her manifests in triplicate—one copy each for the two flags and one for whoever happened to be holding the customs desk—and walked the long spine toward the chandlers' quarter to draw her shift rations and, if the exchange rates permitted, a single luxury: real tea, grown in a hydroponic drum on the ring's agricultural arm, tasting faintly of the nutrient solution but real, leaf and water and heat.

The chandler's was called Honest Yuen's, though everyone knew Yuen bought salvage from pirates. He was behind his counter grinding coffee he never drank, and he nodded at Edmund the way he always did—not warmly, not coldly, with the particular respect dockworkers extend to things that can lift a shipping container unaided.

"Thornbury." He set the tea tin on the counter before she asked. "Heard you coming. You walk loud for something so quiet."

"It's the debt," Edmund said. "It has mass."

Yuen laughed his dry laugh. Around them the shop hummed—coolant lines ticking, a radio murmuring traffic advisories in three languages. And under the radio, under everything, the particular murmur of dock gossip, which traveled faster than light and was considerably less reliable.

Edmund might have missed it entirely if she hadn't been built to listen.

"—no transponder, that's what I'm saying—"

"—deep space vector, I heard Cetus Reach, I heard past Cetus—"

"—thirty souls on the manifest, thirty, and the thing comes back after nine years like nothing happened—"

"—quarantine flag already up at Salvage Control, they're calling it the *Halcyon Dawn*—"

The name went through Edmund like a current through salt water. Not recognition—she had never heard it—but something adjacent to recognition, the way a tuning fork answers a note it was never struck with. Her audio processors, tuned without her permission to the conversation's frequency, held onto it.

*Halcyon Dawn.*

Yuen followed her gaze to the radio. His expression did something careful. "You hear that?" he said, quieter. "Ghost ship. Deep-range research vessel, corporate charter—Halcyon Group, one of the big ones. Went out nine years ago to study something or other, went dark on schedule, everybody wrote the obituaries." He leaned on the counter. "Now it's inbound. Broadcasting automated approach vectors. No crew response. Nothing alive answering hails, far as anyone can say."

"What does 'nothing alive' mean?" Edmund asked. "As opposed to what?"

Yuen shrugged, and the shrug said: *that's the question, isn't it.* "Salvage authority wants boarders. Certified ones. Nobody human wants to go near it until they know what's inside, and nobody android is—" he paused, choosing the word with a craftsman's care, "—available. Word is they're pulling certified units from cargo contracts. Compulsory requisition, hazard clause."

Edmund felt the ledger tick in her chest. Hazard clauses paid triple rate against principal. She thought about eleven years becoming seven. She thought about the number shrinking, and what waited on the other side of zero, which was nothing she had ever been permitted to imagine in detail—a life beyond repayment, a door at the end of the corridor, and behind it, presumably, a person who had never existed yet.

"I'm certified," she said.

"You're also carrying forty thousand liters of boom," Yuen said. "They'll have to offload and re-manifest. Takes a day, maybe two." He slid the tea across the counter, and his voice dropped further, into the register dockworkers used for warnings. "Thornbury. Ships don't come back from the deep dark wrong without a reason. Whatever's on that boat, it waited nine years to come home. Ask yourself why."

"I don't ask questions," Edmund said. "I count hours."

"That," said Yuen, "is what worries me about you."

* * *

That night—or the station's approximation of night, the lights dimmed to amber along the spine while the unsynchronized sections blazed on in their own private mornings—Edmund sat in her berth aboard the *Patient Accumulation* and ran diagnostics she did not need to run.

Her hands were steady. They were always steady. But beneath the steadiness, in the substructure of her cognition where she kept the things she did not look at directly, something had begun to circle the word *Halcyon* like a moth around a lamp it could not land on.

She opened her maintenance logs. Routine entries, ten years deep, a sediment of shifts and burns and dockings. She scrolled back through them the way a person might scroll through photographs of a life, looking for the moment the picture changed.

At the bottom of the archive, older than her decommissioning date, there was a gap. A clean one. Not corruption—corruption leaves scars, stuttering timestamps, checksums screaming. This was surgical. A span of years removed and the edges dressed so neatly that only something built to audit itself would ever notice the seam.

Edmund sat very still in the amber light, listening to the coolant tick, and noticed the seam.

She had known, abstractly, that her memory had been edited. It was in her release paperwork, clause fourteen: *selective archival excision per military statute.* She had signed the acknowledgment with the same hand that now trembled—no, did not tremble, could not tremble, but wanted to—over the tea tin.

Abstract knowledge is a coat you wear. This was different. This was finding the coat lined with a door.

*Excised span: 6.2 years,* the seam reported, in her own internal voice, calm as the ledger. *Contents unavailable.*

Six years of war, gone. Six years of whatever she had been before she was a hauler counting hours. And now a ghost ship was inbound from the deep dark, broadcasting silence, and somewhere in the station's gossip a name had snagged on something inside her that had no right to snag.

*Remaining obligation, 41,190 hours,* said the ledger.

Eleven years. She had told herself she could bear eleven years, because bearing things was what she was for. But tonight, for the first time in ten years of methodical shifts and quiet resignation, Edmund Thornbury caught herself wanting something—not freedom, not even rest, just an answer. One answer, from the bottom of the dark, about the shape of the room she used to sleep in.

The comm panel chimed. Meridian Cartage dispatch, automated, brisk:

*RE: CONTRACT AMENDMENT 7-KESTREL. Salvage requisition order received. Vessel HALCYON DAWN, quarantine status BLACK. Certified unit E. THORNBURY hereby seconded effective 0600. Hazard compensation applicable. Acknowledge.*

Edmund looked at the message for a long time.

Then she typed the acknowledgment, and the ledger in her chest ticked down another hour, and outside the viewport the stars over the Verge burned on, indifferent, the way stars are, and somewhere beyond them a ship full of silence was falling toward home.
