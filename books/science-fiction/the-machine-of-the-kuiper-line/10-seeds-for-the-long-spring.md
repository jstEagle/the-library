## 10. Seeds for the Long Spring

The delegations arrived on a Tuesday, because Orsen had decided weeks ago that the vault would keep Earth-standard calendars whether anyone answered her logs or not. Habit was a kind of faith, and she had eleven years of it banked.

She watched them come down through the atmosphere in three separate shuttles—Mars Concord, the Jovian Compact, and the Belt Assembly, the three great grandchildren of the war, landing on the ice shelf like nervous birds. She stood at the observation window with a mug of reconstituted tea and counted the escort fighters peeling away, and felt something she hadn't felt since the Meridian's engines first broke her silence.

She felt unnecessary.

It was a strange gift. For eleven years she had been the only necessary person on this rock. Then she had been the most necessary, the courier, the key, the woman with faction guns trained on her greenhouse while a nine-year-old recited authentication codes through tears. Now the war was over—not won, not lost, just *over*, ended the way a fever ends, leaving everyone weak and blinking and embarrassed about what they'd said during the worst of it—and nobody needed her to be anything but a gardener.

"Orsen." Ada's voice from the corridor, high and certain the way children's voices are. "The little one's here. The one with the bad haircut."

"That's Sef, and his haircut is a choice he made under fire, so we respect it."

Ada appeared in the doorway, small and round-faced, dragging a blanket like a cape. She was ten now, or eleven—the months had passed the way months do, indifferent to drama. Most of the adult in her was gone. What the fold had compressed into her, the message assembly had burned out, a candle spending itself to light a room. What remained were fragments: she remembered the taste of a fruit no one had grown in forty years, she remembered a lullaby in a dead dialect, she remembered, occasionally, with a clarity that stopped Orsen's heart, exactly what it felt like to sign a founding charter. She did not remember her daughter's face.

Orsen had that face. It was the cruelest and kindest joke the universe had ever played, and she'd stopped flinching from the mirror sometime in the second month.

"The delegates want to see the archive," Ada said, climbing onto the observation bench beside her without asking, which was her right, because she'd founded this place and also because Orsen had given up on boundaries around week three. "Reyes says we should let them. Says it's better they see it here, in the cold, where they can't pretend it's warm."

"Reyes is smarter than her rank suggests."

"Reyes is dead," Ada said, with the terrible flatness of children stating weather. "Mostly. She's mostly dead. She's alive in the chair."

*Alive in the chair.* The medical cradle in the Meridian's medbay had become Captain Reyes's whole world—a body burned through shielding a child from a stray kinetic round, a mind intact and sharp and, by all reports, insufferably cheerful about it. She ran the truce negotiations from the cradle via relay, and when delegates tried to pity her she told them she'd finally gotten a desk job and they should be so lucky.

The shuttles touched down in a bloom of steam. Orsen watched the first delegates step onto the ice—dignitaries, historians, a woman from Mars who had signed the ceasefire with a shaking hand—and she thought about the last people who had come to the Kuiper Line with guns. She thought about the silence after the warships went dark mid-attack, the whole system holding its breath while the broadcast propagated, proof unspooling into every ear at once: *your governments built you from the same sabotaged hull. You were one family before you were two enemies.*

Nobody had surrendered. There had been nothing to surrender *to*. There had only been the shared and awful discovery that the war's founding grievance was an inheritance forged by the very people who'd founded them all—and that both sides had been guarding the same lie for a century, each convinced the other had written it.

"They're scared," Ada said.

"Of course they are."

"No. Of *us*." She pointed a mitten at the shuttles. "Of you. The woman who ended the war. They think you'll be tall and made of judgment."

"I am tall and made of judgment."

"You're made of tea and stubbornness. I've checked." She leaned her head against Orsen's arm, a small warm weight. "Can we plant today? While they're doing their talking? I don't want to watch them do their talking."

Orsen looked down at her—the founder of colonies, the anchor of a fold experiment, the mind that had carried a century of grief in a skull the size of a melon, and who now mostly wanted to plant seeds and had opinions about blanket capes.

"Yeah," Orsen said. "We can plant today."

* * *

The thaw had been Sef's idea, technically, though he'd deny it with the ferocity of a boy who could not bear to be credited with hope. The vault's southern sublevel had a geothermal seam the founders had tapped for emergency warmth, and beneath the old hydroponics bay there was soil—real soil, regolith and composted biomass, sealed and dormant for a hundred years. Sef, who had been printed for war and knew exactly what that felt like, had spent the quiet weeks after the broadcast down there with a heat lamp and the patience of someone relearning what his hands were for.

Now the bay glowed. Grow-lights strung on salvaged cable, rows of trays, and in the corner the first bed of thawed soil, dark and smelling of something alive, which after eleven years of antiseptic cold nearly made Orsen weep every time she walked in.

She'd learned not to fight it. Weeping was just another kind of planting.

Ada knelt at the bed's edge in her blanket cape, sleeves rolled, hands buried to the wrist in dirt like a much smaller and more cheerful farmer. "This one's a pear," she announced, pressing a seed down. "I remember pears. I remember the *smell* of the orchard on the generation ship, before—" She paused. The fragments had edges like that; you brushed them and got cut. "Before. It smelled like green. Nobody alive remembers that smell except me, and I'm not really alive-then anymore, so I'm putting it in the ground instead."

Orsen knelt beside her and made a furrow with two fingers. "That's the best archive I know of."

"Better than the vault?"

"The vault keeps things *from* changing. This—" she nodded at the soil, at the seed disappearing under Ada's small dirty palm, "—this keeps things *by* changing. That's the whole trick of seeds. They only survive by refusing to stay what they were."

Ada considered this with the gravity of a senate hearing. "That's what happened to me, isn't it. I'm a seed."

Orsen's chest did the thing it did now, the ache-with-warmth-inside that she'd stopped naming and started just carrying. "You're the best seed I've got."

"Then you have to water me."

"You get tea like everyone else."

"*Fair.*"

The door hissed, and Sef came in carrying a tray of seedlings he'd coaxed from the old stock—wobbly green things, leggy from the grow-lights, alive against every odd the universe had stacked. He was seventeen now by body, maybe forty by everything else, and he still stood like a gunner, weight on the balls of his feet, until he caught himself and stood like a boy instead. He'd gotten better at the second one.

"Delegates are asking for you," he said. "The Mars woman. She says—" he grimaced, "—she says the heirs request the custodian's presence."

"Tell the heirs the custodian is planting pears."

"I *did*. She said, quote, 'that is not a schedule.' So I told her the custodian ended a war and could schedule however she liked, and then Reyes laughed so hard the medbay had to sedate her, so, you know. Casualties."

Orsen laughed—really laughed, the kind that had taken months to come back—and Ada smeared a triumphant dirty handprint on Sef's sleeve, and for a moment the three of them were just people in a warm room with green things, and Orsen thought: *this*. Not the broadcast, not the key, not the bloodline written into her cells by technicians who'd never once considered what it would be like to *be* the copy, alone, for eleven years, tending other people's future.

This. The handprint. The tea. The pear going into the ground.

She'd raged at Ada once—early, in the ice, when the archive told her what she was. *You made me a lock,* she'd screamed at a nine-year-old. *You made me a key with legs and left me in the dark.* And Ada, nine years old and carrying a century, had looked at her with ancient eyes and said, *No. She made you a daughter. I just wasn't brave enough to stay and be your mother.* And then Ada had shown her the letter—the real daughter's farewell, written in the ice before the print, before the vault, before everything: *If someone wears my face someday, don't let her be alone. Don't let her be a mechanism. Let her choose.*

Orsen had chosen. That was the whole of it, and it was enough.

* * *

The Mars woman found them at sunset, which on the Kuiper Line meant the light through the ice shelf went the color of old honey, and the grow-lights made the hydroponics bay look like the inside of a lantern. The delegates had finished their talking. The truce held. It would hold or it wouldn't, but that was no longer Orsen's weight to carry; she'd delivered the message, and messages, like seeds, belonged to the ground after that.

The woman stood in the doorway, took in the blanket cape, the dirty child, the gunner-turned-gardener, the custodian with soil to her elbows, and something in her official face came loose.

"You're not what we expected," she said.

"Nobody ever is," Orsen said. "That's rather the point of the archive. Would you like to plant something? We have pears. Nobody alive remembers what they taste like. It'll be our job to find out together."

The Mars woman looked at her hands—diplomat's hands, signing hands, hands that had held a pen over a ceasefire—and then, slowly, she knelt at the edge of the bed and held one out for a seed.

Ada put a pear seed in it, solemn as a coronation.

Outside, the ice shelf caught the last of the light, and beneath it the vault hummed its old patient hum—embryos sleeping, archives dreaming, four thousand logs of a woman talking to no one finally, at last, answered. Orsen sat back on her heels between her strange, assembled, unprinted family, and began, quietly, to compose log entry four thousand and one.

*Day 4,011. Soil's thawing. Company's arrived. Nobody's leaving.*

*Spring's a long way off. We've got seeds.*
