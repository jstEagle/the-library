## 2. The Dreaming Children

Wren woke with a word in her mouth that did not belong to her.

She lay still in the dark of the bunkroom, listening to the wind worry the plastic sheeting over the window, and said the word again to be sure it was real. It came out smooth and strange, four syllables stacked like stones: *keth-ru-ahn-sol*. Her tongue knew it. Her mouth made it without stumbling, the way her mouth made *bread* or *mother* or *Gideon*. But she had never heard it before in her life. Nobody at Corvid's Rest had ever said it. She was sure of that the way you are sure of your own hands.

She sat up. The cot across from hers was empty—her brother Tam had already gone out to the morning chore line—and the room smelled of ash and cold iron, the way everything at the colony smelled, the way the whole rusted world smelled if you were honest about it.

*keth-ru-ahn-sol.*

It meant something. That was the worst part. It didn't feel like gibberish rattling around her skull; it felt like a word she'd set down somewhere and forgotten, like a coat left on a hook. And underneath it, faint as water under ice, there was the number.

The number from the dream.

She couldn't hold it while she was awake. Every time she reached for it, it slipped sideways, too big for her head, a number with more digits than there were stars anyone bothered to count. But in the dream it had been simple. In the dream, a voice—not cruel, not kind, just patient as geology—had been counting down from it. Not fast. Slowly. One decrement per heartbeat, maybe, or per day, or per year. The voice hadn't said what would happen when it reached zero. It had only counted, and Wren had listened, and seven other children had listened with her, all of them standing in the same dream on a plain of black glass under a sky the color of an old bruise, and none of them had been afraid until they woke up.

She got dressed. She said the word one more time, quietly, into her cupped hands, like checking whether a coal was still alive.

It was.

* * *

By midmorning, everyone knew.

That was how Corvid's Rest worked. Two hundred souls crammed inside a fence line that had been salvaged, welded, patched, and welded again from the bones of some pre-collapse depot, and no secret survived longer than it took to walk from one end of the compound to the other. Wren heard it first from Tam, who'd heard it from the Kessler twins, who'd heard it from old Marta Osei, who ran the wash-house and therefore ran the world.

"The Aldous girl," Tam said, catching up to her outside the greenhouse, his voice pitched low and thrilled. "Petra Aldous. She woke up talking yesterday morning. Words nobody knows. Her mother thought she was having a fit."

"I know," Wren said.

Tam stopped walking. "You *know*?"

Wren looked at him. Tam was eleven, two years younger than her, and he worshipped her in the way younger brothers do—completely and with constant resentment. He would tell their mother in an hour no matter what she said. Their mother was on the council. So Wren told him the truth, because the truth was going to come out anyway, and because saying it out loud might make it smaller.

"I dreamed it too," she said. "The same dream. The counting. I woke up with a word in my mouth." She gave him the word, and watched his face go pale in the flat morning light.

"That's—" He swallowed. "Petra's word was different. But Marta says it sounded the same. Like it was from the same place."

"It is from the same place," Wren said, and she didn't know how she knew that either, only that she did, the way she knew the number existed even though she couldn't hold it. "There are seven of us. I can feel it. Seven kids dreaming the same dream."

"You can't *feel* that."

"Ask around," she said. "Go on. Ask."

He asked. By noon he came back to her looking like a boy who'd put his hand into a hole in the ground expecting a rabbit and found something else. Seven. The Aldous girl, both Kessler twins, Juno Park, little Samet who was only six, a shepherd boy named Rho from the far paddocks whom nobody had ever seen speak more than ten words in a row—and Wren. Seven children, scattered across the compound, none of them kin, most of them strangers to each other, all of them waking between three days and one night ago with foreign syllables on their tongues and a countdown ticking behind their eyes.

The colony did what colonies do with things they can't explain. It got afraid. Then it got angry.

* * *

The council convened in the evening, in the big hall with the cracked dome window, and because there was nowhere else to put the children while the adults argued, the children were brought along—which meant, as it always meant, that the children heard everything.

Wren sat on the bench beside Petra Aldous, who was nine and kept her fists clenched in her lap like she was ready to hit the next person who called her cursed. The Kesslers sat together, shoulder to shoulder, identical and grim. Samet cried quietly until Juno Park, who was twelve and the toughest person Wren knew, let him lean against her arm.

Her mother stood at the front. Asha Voss, hydroponics chief, council member, a woman who had once pulled the entire colony through a blight winter on half-rations and stubbornness. Right now she looked tired in a way Wren had never seen, and it frightened her more than the dream had.

"—three nights now," her mother was saying. "Seven children. Same dream, same symptoms, waking with language none of them have been exposed to. I've ruled out contamination, I've ruled out the water, I've ruled out every plant in my own houses. Which leaves one variable we haven't accounted for."

Councilman Dreech didn't even look up from his slate. "Say it, then. Everyone in this hall is already saying it."

"The beacon," her mother said. "The vault machine."

A murmur went through the hall like wind through dry stalks.

The beacon. Wren's chest went tight. Everyone at Corvid's Rest knew about the beacon the way everyone knew about the weather—it was simply there, a fixture older than any of them, humming away out past the north fence where Gideon Almeida tended it alone, year after year, decade after decade. Twenty years, the old-timers said. Twenty years winding that machine, keeping its light turning, trading seeds for salt and spare parts at the fence line every month like clockwork. The beacon was supposed to call home a fleet that had gone silent before Wren was born. Nobody expected it to work anymore. Nobody thought about it much at all, except as the reason the Almeida woman existed, a strange solitary figure in a heavy coat, always polite, never quite belonging.

And now, apparently, it was in their heads.

"It makes sense," Dreech was saying, warming up, his voice carrying the particular relish of a man who has found a thing to be right about. "It's a transmitter, isn't it? A great big transmitter sitting forty kilometers out, broadcasting who-knows-what into who-knows-where, and now our children are waking up *speaking in tongues*. You want coincidence? I don't believe in coincidence anymore than I believe in ghosts. That machine is doing something. Maybe it's broken. Maybe it's doing exactly what it was built to do and we're finally close enough to hear it. Either way—"

"Either way what?" her mother said sharply. "You want to march out there and smash it? That beacon is the only link we have to the fleet. If the fleet ever comes back—"

"If the fleet were coming back it would have come back twenty years ago!" Dreech's fist came down on the table, and Samet flinched against Juno's arm. "We keep a light burning for ships that sank a generation ago, and meanwhile the thing is *in our children's skulls*, Asha. I've got parents coming to me saying their kids won't sleep. Saying they hear counting. Counting! You know what counting means in the old stories. Countdowns mean endings. Curses count down. Bombs count down."

"It's not a curse," Wren said.

She hadn't planned to speak. The words just came out, the way the word itself had come out that morning, fully formed, certain. The hall went quiet. Two hundred faces turned toward the children's bench, and Wren felt Petra's small hand grab her sleeve, and she stood up anyway, because someone had to.

"It's not a curse," she said again. Her voice shook and she hated that, but she kept going. "Curses don't talk to seven people at once. Curses don't need witnesses. Whatever this is, it's a *message*. Somebody is trying to tell us something, and they picked us—the kids—because—" Because why? She didn't know yet. But she could feel the shape of it, the way she could feel the number, huge and true and just past the edge of holding. "Because we were the ones who'd listen."

Dreech's face had gone the color of old meat. "Sit down, girl."

"She's right," said a voice from the back, and it was Rho, the shepherd boy, the one who never spoke. His voice cracked with disuse but it carried. "It's not a curse. It's *lonely*. Haven't any of you ever been lonely?"

Nobody answered him. That was answer enough.

The vote wasn't even close. The council resolved to send a delegation to the vault—to confront Gideon Almeida, to inspect the beacon, and if inspection confirmed what Dreech called "the obvious," to silence the machine by whatever means necessary. Her mother voted against it. It didn't matter. Fear had the numbers, fear always had the numbers, and by the time the hall emptied, the decision had already hardened in the colony's mind into something older and less reversible than a vote.

Superstition moves faster than procedure. By full dark, Wren could hear it spreading door to door: the machine was cursed, the keeper was a witch, the children were marked. Someone had chalked a crooked sign on the north gate—a circle with a slash through it, the old sign against the evil eye—pointing out toward the dark horizon where, forty kilometers away, the beacon turned and hummed and counted.

Wren stood at the dormitory window a long time, looking north.

*keth-ru-ahn-sol,* she whispered. And beneath it, patient as geology, the number ticked down another beat.

They were going to go out there and break it. She understood that now. They were going to destroy the only thing that had ever chosen to speak to her, and they'd call it protection, and they'd sleep fine afterward.

Unless somebody got there first.

She looked over at Petra, awake in the next bed, wide-eyed in the dark. Petra looked back. Neither of them said anything. They didn't need to. Seven children had been given the same dream, and tomorrow, Wren decided, seven children were going to give the colony an answer—one that started with a long walk north, past the fence line, toward the keeper of small green things and the machine that remembered too much.

The countdown, after all, was still running.
