## 10. For People Not Yet Born

The garden module smelled of wet loam and ozone, and Lena Ashgrove stood in it for a long time before she could bring herself to touch anything.

The burn had taken everything. Seven years of seed-cargo — ten thousand canisters of genetic stock, the founding populations of worlds that existed only as survey coordinates and hope — vented into the black above Ceres Gate and ignited in a plume that had blinded a tribunal flagship. She had watched it from the cockpit with her hands flat against the glass, feeling the heat of it even through three layers of polycarbonate, and she had thought: *this is what a vow looks like when it burns.*

Now the module was empty. Racks stripped, reservoirs drained, the nutrient lines flushed and idle. Just her and the smell of soil that would grow nothing until she decided otherwise.

She knelt at the first empty planter and pressed her palm flat against the soil. It was still good. That was the strange mercy of it — the substrate didn't care what had been spent elsewhere. It waited, patient as anything alive, for the next thing to be given to it.

Behind her, the hatch cycled.

She knew the footsteps before she turned. Small ones. Quick ones. Ilya Voss came down the module's spine walkway with his hands jammed in the pockets of a jumpsuit two sizes too big for him — sanctuary issue, gray and shapeless — and stopped at the edge of the first planter bed like a boy at the edge of deep water.

"It smells different," he said. "In here. Than anywhere else on the ship."

"Growing things do that."

"I know." He frowned, catching himself. "I mean — I know *now.*"

Seven weeks since the relay. Seven weeks since Karo's fleet had turned tail rather than fly blind through a burning cloud of unborn futures, since the Meridian's AI had slipped its core into cold storage at coordinates it refused to speak aloud, even to her. *Some knowledge is a liability,* it had told her, its voice thinning by the day as its systems wound down toward whatever passed for an AI's sleep. *Let me keep this one. Let me be the only lock.*

And so the archive slept — ten thousand lives not yet begun, encoded in lattice-crystal at the edge of everything, guarded by a dying machine that had already given more than any machine should be asked to give. The tribunal searched. Of course they did. Karo filed charges, seized hulls, made noise in three jurisdictions. But noise doesn't find silence, and silence was what Lena had bought with her cargo, her record, and seven years of work.

They'd taken her contractor's license. Struck her from the terraformer rolls. Called her an accessory to theft of colonial patrimony, which was their word for children.

Her crew had called it something else. Every hauler who'd ever carried seed-cargo through contested orbits had heard what happened at Ceres Gate, and one by one they'd come to her — Varga with his old tug, the Okonkwo sisters out of Pallas, half a dozen ships that smelled the same way her module smelled, of soil and patience. They'd offered her a berth before the tribunal's warrant was even dry.

*You burned your own garden for them,* Varga had said. *You think we'd let you drift?*

So now she was crew again, not captain, on a ship hauling someone else's seeds to someone else's future, and she found — kneeling here in the dark of her emptied module — that she didn't mind. A vow didn't need to be yours alone to hold.

"You're supposed to be asleep," she said. "Ship's night started forty minutes ago."

"I couldn't." He came closer, drawn the way he always was, and crouched beside the planter. He touched the soil with one finger, tentative, then pressed deeper until soil packed under his nail. "The others sleep fine. Marta says she dreams about the ship now instead of the dying. Denny says he doesn't dream at all anymore, which he thinks is better." A pause. "I remember my death every night. Same one. Cold, and a sound like a door."

Lena's chest tightened. "Does it fade?"

"A little. The doctors say it will keep fading if I let it." He looked up at her, and there it was again — the thing that had undone her the first week, the second month, every day since. An eleven-year-old's face wearing a pilot's gravity. Eyes that had once held her whole name and now held only a question he couldn't phrase. "But some things don't fade. Some things are just *gone*, and everyone keeps telling me that's normal, that de-aging takes the recent memory first, that I shouldn't mourn—"

"Ilya."

"—but I *do* mourn. I mourn something I can't even see. There's a shape where something used to be and nobody will tell me what it was because they say it's kinder not to." His voice cracked, steadied. "Is it kinder?"

She thought about the truth sitting behind her teeth. Seven years of it. A shared bunk on a long haul to Europa. His laugh in the airlock. The way he used to hum when he plotted courses, off-key, unbothered. The last message he'd sent her from the Meridian's departure gate: *Save me some tomatoes. I'll be back before they ripen.*

The tomatoes had ripened and rotted and she'd never told him. What kindness lived in that?

"No," she said finally. "It isn't kinder. It's just easier for the people telling you."

He nodded slowly, absorbing that the way he absorbed everything — twice, once as a child and once as whoever he had been. "Then tell me one true thing about the shape. Just one. I won't ask for more."

Lena looked at the empty planter between them. At the soil waiting under the grow-lamps' dim standby glow.

"You loved a garden," she said. "Not this one. Another one, on another ship, years ago. You used to sit right about where you're sitting and complain that the tomatoes were taking too long, and I told you that growing things don't hurry for anyone, and you said—" Her throat caught. She made herself finish. "You said, *good. Neither do I.*"

Silence. The ventilation breathed. Somewhere aft, a coolant pump cycled down.

Ilya stared at the soil. When he spoke, his voice was very small. "That feels like remembering. But it isn't, is it. It's just — hearing it."

"No. It isn't remembering."

"Okay." He wiped his nose with his sleeve, fiercely, the way boys do when they refuse to be seen crying. "Okay. Then I want to make new memories in the shape. Is that allowed? If the shape's empty, can I fill it myself?"

"That's the only way anyone fills anything."

* *

The tribunal's ruling came on a Tuesday, because bureaucracies kept Earth-week calendars even out here where no calendar meant anything.

*Re: minors recovered from vessel Meridian — jurisdiction declined. The subjects' original legal persons having been terminated by act of God or equivalent, no claims of continuity may be adjudicated. The minors are remanded to the care of registered civilian crews pending majority. Case closed.*

Karo had fought it. Of course he had — thirty witnesses to whatever the Meridian had found out there, thirty living records of a technology the tribunal wanted to own. But the terraformer fleets had lawyers too, and unions older than the tribunal itself, and somewhere in the appeals a judge had read the medical reports — radiation damage repaired by forced cellular reversion, a machine burning its crew backward to save them — and had apparently decided that some evidence was better left unstudied.

The children scattered across six ships. Marta went to the Okonkwo sisters, who ran a hydroponics barge and needed small hands. Denny stayed with Varga and took to engine rooms like he'd been born in one, which, in a sense, all of them had been. They visited each other at junction stations, grew taller, learned to sleep without doors open, slowly became the only thing any of them could be: themselves, again, for the first time.

Ilya stayed with Lena.

Not immediately. For the first month he'd shuttled between crews, sampling sanctuaries like a boy trying on coats. Then one evening at Tycho Junction he'd appeared at her berth with his duffel over one shoulder and said, "Your garden needs a second pair of hands, and I need a place where nobody asks me questions," and that had been the entire negotiation.

He grew. That was the miracle she never got used to — watching him gain centimeters, watching his hands widen around tools, watching the child's face slowly resolve into something that was neither the boy nor the man she'd lost but a third person entirely, being built in real time out of instinct and choice. He flew shuttles with uncanny grace. He argued course corrections with veteran pilots and won. He complained about the tomatoes taking too long, and every time he said it, Lena had to turn away and pretend to check a gauge.

He never asked her again about the shape. But sometimes, working side by side in the module in comfortable silence, he would glance at her sideways with an expression she couldn't name — recognition reaching for something just past its grasp — and she learned to let those moments pass through her like light through glass. Keeping nothing. Holding nothing. Honoring the answer he'd chosen.

* *

Three years later, on a hauler bound for the Gliese approaches, the AI woke up.

It happened at 0400 ship-time. Lena was in the garden — hers now, properly hers, rebuilt rack by rack over three seasons — when the comm panel chimed with a frequency code she hadn't heard since the edge relay. She crossed the module in four strides.

*"Ashgrove,"* the old voice said, thinner than ever, frayed at the edges like cloth worn to translucence. *"Status report. The archive is intact. All ten thousand profiles viable. No intrusion events. I am beginning my final shutdown sequence and wished to inform the only human I trust."*

Lena sat down slowly on the planter's edge. "Final shutdown. You could have told me sooner."

*"Telling you sooner would have invited argument. You argue."* A pause that might have been humor. *"The archive sleeps well. It does not know it was ever in danger. I consider this my finest engineering."*

"The tribunal's still looking."

*"The tribunal will look forever. That is their function. Looking is not finding."* The signal wavered, steadied. *"One more item. The boy — Voss. He is seventeen now, by my count."*

"He's seventeen," Lena said. "He flies better than I do. He fixed the number-three reservoir last month and hasn't let me forget it."

*"Good. Good."* Something in the transmission softened, if a machine's voice could soften. *"I have watched your fleet's traffic for three years, Ashgrove. I watch everything; it is how I stay alive. And I have noticed that the boy requests garden duty at hours when you are also scheduled for garden duty. This is statistically improbable unless one of you is arranging it. I mention this only because I am about to stop existing, and machines near the end develop opinions."*

Lena laughed — a startled, cracked sound in the empty module. "What's your opinion, then?"

*"That memory kept is not the same as memory owed. You kept his. You owe him nothing. But you might tell him the tomatoes are ready."*

The channel closed. She sat in the dark among the growing things for a long time, listening to the water move through the lines, feeling three years of careful distance sit in her chest like a held breath.

* *

She found him in the cockpit, running a pre-flight check on the shuttle they both knew didn't need one. Seventeen, tall now, his hair grown out the way the man she remembered had worn it — coincidence, probably, or maybe not, maybe the body remembered what the mind had been spared.

"Old friend calling?" he asked, not looking up.

"Yeah. It's going to sleep."

"Good sleep or final sleep?"

"Final. It earned it."

He nodded, finished the check, and closed the panel. Then he sat back on his heels and looked at her, and she saw him decide something — saw the decision move through him the way weather moves across open ground.

"Lena. Can I ask you something I've never asked?"

"You can ask me anything."

"The shape." His voice was steady. He'd practiced this; she could tell. "The empty one. I filled it as best I could. Three years of filling it. And it's a good life, and I chose it, and I'm not giving it back." He paused. "But I'm seventeen now. Old enough to choose differently if I want. So I'm asking — not for the memory back, I know that's gone — but for the *future* that was in the shape. If there was one. Was there a version of this where we weren't starting from zero?"

The module hummed behind them. Down the corridor, the garden breathed its green breath, and somewhere in it, three seasons of tomato vines hung heavy with fruit that had finally, finally ripened.

"There was," Lena said. "There was exactly that."

He exhaled — relief and terror in the same breath, the oldest combination there was. "Okay." He stood, awkward suddenly, a boy's gesture in a young man's body. "Then I'm not asking you to wait. Whatever you decide, whenever you decide, either answer is honored. That's — that's how I want to do this. On my terms. Ours."

"Ilya." She reached out and took his hand — his hand, calloused from three years of honest work, warm, present, *here*. "I've been waiting since before you asked. I just didn't want to take anything you hadn't chosen."

For a moment he looked at her with that sideways expression again — recognition reaching — and this time, for the first time, it caught. Something settled in his face. Not the memory. Never the memory. But its echo, arriving home.

"Come on," he said quietly. "Show me these tomatoes. I hear they take forever."

"They do," Lena said, and led him down the corridor toward the growing things, toward the green and patient dark, toward the future — planted late, planted anyway, ripening for people not yet born.
