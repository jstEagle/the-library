## 1. Actuarial Grace

The transport was called the *Charitable Intent*, and Rafael Lockridge thought the name was the funniest thing about it.

She'd been aboard for eleven days, long enough to learn its rhythms: the way the gravity plating stuttered in the aft sections every fourth hour, a half-second drop that made her tea float and her stomach lurch; the way the recycled air tasted faintly of ozone and old upholstery; the way the corridor lights dimmed to amber at what the ship's clock insisted was evening, though outside the viewport there was only the long, featureless dark of the transit lanes and the distant, indifferent smudge of stars.

The *Charitable Intent* was a second-generation fleet hull, decommissioned from passenger service and reassigned to what the manifest called *Administrative Transit* — which meant it carried auditors, adjusters, inspectors, and the occasional disgraced executive being ferried to a hearing. The paint was peeling in the corridors. The carpet in the observation lounge had worn through to the substrate in a pattern shaped, if you squinted, like a map of nowhere. Everything about the ship whispered the same quiet truth: the fleet had better places to spend its money, and this was not one of them.

Rafael had a cabin to herself, which was a small mercy. It had a bunk, a desk, a washbasin with a temperamental tap, and a viewport that looked out on nothing in particular. She had hung nothing on the walls. She never did. Eleven years in the Investigative Division had taught her that attachments to rooms were a liability; you could be reassigned mid-sentence, your belongings forwarded in a crate, your life packed up by strangers while you were still in transit.

She sat at the desk now, in the amber false-evening, with the file open before her.

The file on Pale Meridian was thick in the way files were thick when someone wanted them to look thorough rather than be thorough. Four hundred pages, most of it boilerplate: colony charter amendments, production quotas for the lithium extraction works, personnel rotation schedules, water reclamation reports. She'd read all of it twice. The real content fit in a single appendix, and the appendix fit in a sentence:

*Forty-one workplace fatalities and catastrophic incidents in thirty-six months. All investigated. All ruled coincidental.*

She turned to the incident summaries again, though she knew them by heart by now. She'd made herself a private index on her slate, cross-referenced and color-coded, because the fleet's own filing system was designed — she was increasingly convinced — to make patterns invisible. Each accident had its own folder, its own investigator, its own ruling, its own closed case number. No one had ever looked at them side by side. Or if someone had, they hadn't written it down.

Accident one: a pressure seal failure in the water reclamation plant, killing a maintenance foreman named Odile Ferran. Ruled: material fatigue, accelerated by improper storage. Coincidental finding: the seal had passed inspection nine days earlier.

Accident seven: a cargo lifter in the spaceport freight yards, its failsafe engaging four seconds late, crushing a dock supervisor. Ruled: firmware anomaly. Coincidental finding: the dock supervisor had filed a grievance about shift scheduling the week before, and had been scheduled to testify at an arbitration hearing.

Accident nineteen: a decompression event in a residential module, killing two — a schoolteacher and her partner. Ruled: micro-fracture propagation, undetectable by standard survey. Coincidental finding: the schoolteacher had been collecting affidavits for a class action over the colony's water quality. The affidavits were destroyed in the decompression. All of them. Every copy, in a colony where every document was supposed to be triplicated.

Accident thirty-three: an elevator in the administrative tower, plunging eleven floors with a senior records clerk inside. Ruled: cable wear. Coincidental finding: the clerk had been compiling the annual safety statistics — the very statistics that would have aggregated the thirty-two accidents before his into a single reportable pattern.

Rafael had a habit, when she read files like this, of imagining the dead as they must have been in life: tired, particular, fond of small things. Odile Ferran had kept a plant on her workbench, according to a colleague's statement; it had survived the pressure failure and been given to her sister. The schoolteacher, Ama Sorel, had coached a junior debate team. The records clerk, whose name was Petros Ilyin, had been three years from pension.

She imagined them because the fleet did not. The fleet reduced them to line items: *incident, cause, finding, closure.* Weighing a human life was the first thing they taught you in the Division, and the last thing you were supposed to forget how to do — a life had an actuarial value, a number, and the number was never the point but always the starting point. But the number was a door, not a room. Behind it were people who had wanted things. Odile Ferran had wanted, presumably, to see her plant bloom. Petros Ilyin had wanted his pension. Ama Sorel had wanted clean water for her students and had died of the absence of it, in a manner the fleet called coincidence.

Forty-one coincidences in three years, in a colony of eleven thousand people.

Rafael had done the arithmetic on the second day. The baseline industrial fatality rate for a colony of Pale Meridian's size and profile was six per year, and that was for colonies with aging infrastructure, which Pale Meridian emphatically had. Forty-one in three years was more than double the ceiling. And the distribution was wrong — not clustered by site, not clustered by system, not clustered by shift. Clustered by *person*. The dead were not the ones working the dangerous jobs. The dead were the ones who *talked*: grievance-filers, affidavit-collectors, statistics-keepers, union organizers, a journalist, two auditors from the colonial comptroller's office, a chaplain who had asked too many questions at too many funerals.

Someone, she thought, was curating randomness.

She didn't write that sentence down anywhere. Eleven years in the Division had taught her that too.

* * *

The comm chimed at 2100 ship's time, which meant her supervisor had waited a decorous interval after the amber lights came on, so the call would feel like an evening call rather than a business one. Denzil Okafor was careful that way. He was careful in every way; it was what had kept him alive and promoted through thirty years of fleet service.

His face resolved on her cabin's screen: broad, gray at the temples, wearing the expression of a man who had already rehearsed this conversation and found it distasteful in rehearsal.

"Lockridge. You're still in transit, yes?"

"Day twelve of fourteen. You have the manifest."

"I have the manifest." A pause, in which he arranged whatever came next. "I wanted to touch base before you arrived. Informally."

"Informally," Rafael repeated. She kept her voice flat and pleasant, the voice she used for depositions. "Go ahead."

Okafor leaned back, and the camera caught the bookshelf behind him — the real books, the paper ones, the collection he was famous for showing off on calls precisely so people would remember he was a man of culture and judgment and not, despite the rumors, a man who made problems disappear. "Pale Meridian is a difficult assignment," he said. "Not technically. Technically it's simple. A declining colony, an aging plant, a string of bad luck. The kind of place where things fail because everything is failing. You've seen a hundred of them."

"I've seen a hundred of them," Rafael agreed.

"The difference is that this one has *attention* on it. The truce council's renewal negotiations are coming up, and the last thing the fleet needs is a story about a colony falling apart. There are delegations that would use it. There are journalists — actual journalists, not the colonial bulletin kind — who would use it. So the Division wants this handled cleanly and quickly. You go in, you confirm the actuarial picture, you issue a consolidated finding, and you come home. Two weeks on the ground, three at the outside."

"And if the actuarial picture doesn't confirm?"

Okafor smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, the smile of a mentor. Rafael had been a junior investigator once, green and hungry, and she remembered what that smile had meant then: *you're one of us, you're being trusted, don't spoil it.* She was not green anymore, and the smile meant something else now, something with a lock on it.

"Then you write the finding anyway," he said gently, "because the actuarial picture *will* confirm. Forty-one incidents, Lockridge, over three years, in a colony running on equipment two generations out of date. The statistics are not mysterious. Old colonies have accidents. That's not a cover-up; that's entropy. Your job is to put entropy on paper in language the council can read." He tapped the desk, once, a period at the end of a sentence. "Find nothing, Lockridge. That's not an order. That's a *forecast*. The best investigators I've ever known — and you were the best of the last generation, I'll say that to your face — the best ones know the difference between a question worth asking and a question that only makes noise. This one makes noise."

"And if it isn't entropy?"

The smile stayed exactly where it was, which was its own kind of answer. "Then you'll have a very quiet career ahead of you, because you'll have learned to keep the difference to yourself. Look —" and now his voice softened into something almost paternal, and that was worse, that was so much worse than the threat, because it might even have been sincere — "I've read your file. I know what you cost the Division on Meridian Station, chasing that ferry disaster, three months of your life on a theory about sabotaged ballast pumps that went nowhere. I know what it did to you. I'm telling you as someone who respects the work: don't spend yourself on Pale Meridian. It's a dying rock at the edge of the lanes. Nobody out there is worth the burning."

"Everyone out there is worth the burning," Rafael said, before she could stop herself, and then hated the sentence for its sentimentality, its exposure. "That is," she amended, in the flat deposition voice, "the finding should reflect what's there. That's the job."

Okafor regarded her for a moment. Something moved behind his eyes — she'd never been able to name it, that flicker, whether it was disappointment or recognition or the weary affection of a man watching someone walk toward the thing that would ruin them. "The colony administrator will meet you at the port," he said. "A man named Voss. Cooperate with him. He'll have the access you need, and he'll have the colony's full cooperation, which is more than you usually get. Take the tour. Read the reports. Issue the finding." A beat. "And Lockridge — send me your preliminary notes through the standard channel. *Only* the standard channel. The relay out there is old and I want to make sure nothing gets garbled in transit."

"Of course," Rafael said. "Wouldn't want anything garbled."

"Good. Get some sleep. You land in two days." The screen went dark, and the amber light came back, and Rafael sat for a long time looking at her own reflection in it, a pale woman in a small room at the edge of everything.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She pulled up the appendix instead, all forty-one summaries, and read them the way she'd been taught to read them twenty years ago, in a classroom on Meridian Station, by a man who had made her the investigator she was.

*An accident is a story someone paid for.* Corvel Ashen had stood at the front of that classroom with his sleeves rolled up, chalk in hand — actual chalk, an affectation he'd kept his whole career — and he'd said it the way other men said their prayers. *Every accident has an author. Most of the time the author is entropy, or negligence, or the universe's indifference. But sometimes — and this is the only reason our job exists — sometimes the author is a person, and the payment is invisible, and the story is written so that no one will ever read it as a story. Your work is to read it anyway.*

She'd been twenty-three. She'd written it in the margin of her notebook and underlined it twice, and she had built her entire career on the underlining.

She built the pattern now, on her slate, where no standard channel could see it. Forty-one incidents, plotted by date. She'd expected clustering around audit cycles — that was the classic signature, violence timed to oversight — and it was there, faintly, incident after incident landing in the weeks before a scheduled review. But that wasn't the structure. The structure was the *witnesses*. She pulled each victim's file, each survivor's statement, each grievance and affidavit and testimony, and laid them out in a lattice, and the lattice resolved into something that made the back of her neck prickle in the cold recycled air.

Every dead person had been a thread. Pull back far enough and each one had been connected — loosely, tangentially, through committees and petitions and shared corridors — to a question the colony had stopped asking. Not the water. Not the shifts, not the quotas, not any of the surface grievances that had flared and died over three years. Something older. Something underneath. The chaplain who died in accident twenty-two had kept a private journal, seized and sealed by the colonial administration after his death; Rafael had read the seizure order four times, trying to understand what a chaplain's journal could possibly contain that required an administrative seal rather than an evidentiary one. The two comptroller's auditors, accidents twenty-nine and thirty, had both, in their final weeks, requested archival access to founding-era records. *Founding-era.* Two hundred years back. Before the colony had even been a colony — when it had been a survey claim, a line on a chart, a place where something had happened that the histories, as far as Rafael could remember them, did not mention.

The lattice had a hole in its center. She could see the shape of the hole by the way the threads bent around it, the way iron filings bend around a hidden magnet. Something at the founding. Something the dead had brushed against without, perhaps, ever fully seeing. Something worth forty-one lives to keep buried, worth a supervisor's careful call, worth a colony's rehearsed silence.

Rafael closed the slate and pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars that had nothing to do with the viewport.

She was tired. That was the truth under everything — she was fifty-one years old, eleven years in the Division and twenty in the field before that, and she was tired in the way that iron is tired, structurally, all the way through. She had buried a marriage in a transit crate. She had buried her mother between assignments. She had spent her whole life reading other people's disasters and calling it a living, and somewhere along the way the hunger that had brought her into the work — the young, stupid, magnificent hunger to *know*, to pull the thread and follow it into the dark no matter what was waiting there — had gotten ground down into professionalism, which was hunger wearing a gray coat and keeping its hands in its pockets.

But it was still there. She could feel it now, awake and ashamed of itself, sitting up in the back of her chest like something that had been sleeping for years and had smelled blood in the water.

*Find nothing,* Okafor had said. And the terrible thing, the thing she would not say to anyone, was how reasonable it sounded. How easy it would be. She could land at Pale Meridian, take Voss's tour, read Voss's reports, issue her consolidated finding — *aging infrastructure, statistical anomaly within tolerance, no evidence of systemic cause* — and be home in three weeks with her pension intact and her hands clean, and forty-one coincidences would stay coincidences, and the hole in the center of the lattice would stay a hole, and the dead would stay line items forever.

And then she thought of Odile Ferran's plant, blooming on someone else's windowsill. Of Ama Sorel's debate team, arguing in a colony where the water was killing people. Of Petros Ilyin, three years from a pension, falling eleven floors with a year of statistics in his head.

The amber lights dimmed toward ship's night. Rafael opened her slate one more time, created a new file — unencrypted, innocuous, titled *Colony Infrastructure Survey Notes, Draft* — and beneath that title, where no standard channel would think to look, she began to copy the appendix. All forty-one. Every name, every date, every finding, every coincidence, every thread.

It was, she reflected, the first dishonest thing she had done in eleven years of honest work, and it felt like the first honest thing she had done in eleven years, too.

Outside the viewport, the stars wheeled slowly as the *Charitable Intent* adjusted its heading, and somewhere ahead of her, at the end of the transit lanes, a colony that had forgotten itself was waiting to be audited.

Two days later, it would come up over the horizon of her viewport like a pale thumbprint on glass — a gray world, a shuttered world, a world with its windows dark — and Rafael Lockridge would stand at the glass with her slate against her chest and understand, with the certainty she'd been trained never to trust and never quite managed to kill, that she was not arriving at an investigation.

She was arriving at a story someone had paid a great deal to keep unread.
