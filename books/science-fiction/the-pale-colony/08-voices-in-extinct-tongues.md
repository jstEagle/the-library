## 8. Voices in Extinct Tongues

The dark down here had texture.

Rafael Lockridge had learned that in the first hour of her descent — that darkness was not an absence but a substance, thick as felt, pressing against her eyes until they ached with the effort of seeing nothing at all. Her headlamp cut a cone through it that seemed less like light than like permission: this much, and no farther. Beyond the cone the tunnel went on breathing, and she went on walking, and the two acts had begun to feel like the same act.

Her boots found the rhythm of old rail ties beneath the dust. The mine cart rails ran ahead of her into blackness and behind her into blackness, twin lines of rusted iron that some crew had laid down two centuries ago and that no one, apparently, had ever bothered to tear out. That was its own kind of evidence. Fleet procedure was ruthless about asset recovery; when a site was decommissioned, it was stripped to bedrock. But these rails remained. Which meant either the fleet had forgotten this place existed — or someone had wanted it forgotten so thoroughly that even the paperwork of forgetting had been suppressed.

She stopped at a junction where three tunnels met and let her lamp play across the walls. The rock was veined with pale mineral streaks — the colony's namesake color, she supposed, the pallor that had given Pale Meridian its name. Someone had scratched marks into the soft stone near the junction, low to the ground, at the height of a kneeling person. Not letters. Tallies, maybe. Or a script she didn't recognize.

She crouched and touched one groove with her gloved fingertip. The edges were smooth. Old. Older than the colony's official founding, if the corrosion patterns meant anything, and she had spent eleven years learning what corrosion patterns meant.

*Forty-one accidents,* she thought. *Forty-one witnesses erased before they could testify, and every single file closed with the word "coincidence" stamped in certified fleet typeface.* And underneath all of it, sealed for reasons nobody says aloud, a mine complex that breathed.

Because it did breathe. She had felt it an hour ago, standing in the collapsed ventilation shaft she'd squeezed through against every protocol Corvel Ashen had ever beaten into her: a current of warm air moving upward through the broken ductwork, carrying the smell of wet stone and — impossibly, unmistakably — woodsmoke. Nothing down here burned wood. Nothing down here should have been alive at all.

She stood, checked her oxygen readout — six hours, plenty — and chose the left-hand tunnel, because its floor showed drag marks, recent ones, the scuffed signature of cargo moved by hand rather than machine.

Two hundred meters on, the speakers woke up.

* * *

It began as static, a dry hiss from somewhere above and ahead, and Rafael froze with her hand on her sidearm out of pure reflex before remembering that sidearms were useless against sound. The hiss swelled, stuttered, resolved into a carrier tone — the old analog band, pre-digital, the kind of signal that hadn't been standard fleet equipment since before the founding war. Her suit's comm suite identified it automatically and then, bizarrely, failed to identify it, cycling through decryption protocols and returning null results like a dog circling a door it couldn't open.

Then the voice came through.

It was a woman's voice, or had been once — thin now, warped by whatever ancient amplifier carried it, but steady, rhythmic, reciting. The words were nothing Rafael knew. They had a shape she didn't recognize either: not the clipped efficiency of Standard Fleet, not the rounded formality of the colonial trade languages, not anything from the forty-odd tongues her translation suite claimed fluency in. The syllables rose and fell in long rolling phrases, each one ending on a falling note, like waves, like a litany, like someone counting.

Her wrist display filled with the translation suite's honest confession: **UNKNOWN LANGUAGE. NO MATCH IN DATABASE.**

Rafael stood very still in the dark and felt the hair rise along her arms inside her suit.

She knew why there was no match. She knew it the way you know a thing you've been refusing to know, the way Sefa Idris had known it in his careful non-answers, the way the whole colony knew it in the silence around the sealed mines. There was no match because the language was dead. It had been dead for two hundred years. It had died in the founding war, in the relocations that weren't relocations, in the campaign the histories called *pacification of the Kessari territories* and never described further, as though pacification were a weather event, as though a people could be smoothed flat like a wrinkle in a sheet and no one would need to say what the iron had been.

A dead language was speaking to her out of a dead mine, in a living voice.

"No," she said aloud, to herself, to the tunnel. "No, that's not possible."

But her training — Corvel's training, the whole hard catechism of it — answered her the way it always did: *An accident is a story someone paid for. A coincidence is a story nobody bothered to write. When the impossible presents itself, stop asking whether it's possible and start asking who made it possible.*

Someone had kept this signal alive. Someone had maintained analog equipment, ancient equipment, in a sealed complex officially abandoned before the colony's first landing party came down. Someone had recorded that voice, or trained that voice, or *was* that voice. And none of those possibilities fit inside any report Rafael was authorized to write.

She keyed her recorder. "Investigator Lockridge, private log, timestamped and hashed. I am approximately nine hundred meters below the surface in the sealed section of the Meridian Deep complex. I am receiving an audio transmission on legacy analog frequencies in an unidentified language. My translation suite returns no match across all indexed human languages." A pause. "I want the record to show that my hands are shaking."

* * *

She followed the signal.

It wasn't difficult. The voice repeated on a loop — she timed it, four minutes and eleven seconds per cycle, with thirty seconds of silence between repetitions, and in that silence she could hear, faintly, beneath the carrier hiss, other sounds. Footsteps? Machinery? Once, unmistakably, a child's voice, high and quick, saying something sharp and brief before an adult hushed it.

A child. Speaking the dead language. Fluently, unselfconsciously, the way children speak the only language they have.

Rafael walked faster.

The tunnel descended in long switchbacks, the rails groaning under her boots, and the air grew warmer and wetter until her faceplate fogged at the edges and she had to wipe it with her glove. Condensation beaded on the walls. Somewhere water dripped in a slow percussion, and threaded through it all the voice went on reciting its four minutes and eleven seconds, patient as geology, as though it had been saying these words into the dark for years and expected to go on saying them for years more.

And maybe it had. Maybe it had been saying them since before Rafael Lockridge was born. A distress signal didn't expire just because no one was listening. That was the whole terrible grammar of a distress signal — it assumed a listener would eventually come, and it refused to die before one did.

She thought about the forty-one files again, and this time she saw them differently. Not as a pattern of deaths but as a perimeter. Every accident had occurred within the upper colony, among people whose work brought them near the mines' administrative records, the survey data, the old geological reports. Witnesses to paper, all of them. Nobody had ever died *in* the deep complex, because nobody official had ever gone into the deep complex. The killings weren't random violence. They were maintenance of a boundary — the line between the world that knew and the world that must never know.

And she had just crossed it.

The thought arrived without fear attached, which frightened her more than fear would have. She catalogued it, the way she catalogued everything, filed it under *anticipated consequences*, and kept walking. If they had wanted investigators dead on arrival they'd have arranged her shuttle's failure over the ocean. They had let her land, let her dig, let her find the seam in the story — which meant either they were arrogant, or they were watching her right now and deciding what she was.

The tunnel opened.

* * *

It opened without warning, the way caves do — one step the walls were close enough to touch both sides, and the next step the dark simply gave up and became a space so large her headlamp couldn't find its far wall. She stood at the mouth of a cavern that had been a mine once and was something else now, and her light swept across the evidence of the something else and her breath went shallow and stayed there.

Terraces. Cut into the rock in ascending tiers, faced with salvaged plating and woven fiber and, on the lower levels, actual masonry, fitted stone, the kind of work that takes generations. Walkways stitched the terraces together with rope bridges and cable rails. Grow-lights glowed in long troughs — dim, amber, carefully rationed — over rows of hydroponic greens and pale fungus beds and something climbing on trellises that might have been beans. Water ran in channels beside the walkways, channeled from some source deeper in, feeding cisterns wrapped in insulation. Laundry hung on lines. Tools hung on pegboards. A pair of boots sat outside a doorway, neatly paired, one sole worn through at the heel.

It was a village. It was a village two hundred years old, built downward instead of up, hidden in the body of a mountain beneath a colony that had been built on top of it like a lid.

And it was empty.

Not abandoned — empty the way a house is empty when everyone has stepped into another room. The grow-lights were on. The water was running. A pot sat on a heating element in an open-air kitchen terrace, still steaming. Whatever lived here had heard her coming — had probably heard her from the moment she'd dropped through the ventilation shaft, had tracked her every step down every switchback — and had withdrawn, all of them, taking only themselves and leaving everything else exactly in place.

Leaving, Rafael realized, the lights on. On purpose. So she could see.

So she would understand what she was looking at.

The speakers crackled again, closer now — she found the source bolted to a strut near the cavern's entrance, an analog unit older than the fleet itself, its casing patched with solder and tape across decades of repair. The woman's voice poured out of it, four minutes and eleven seconds, and this time, standing beneath it, Rafael caught the structure of the recitation. It wasn't random. It had sections. A rising sequence, a list — names, she thought, it's a list of names, hundreds of them, thousands — then a lower, slower passage that sounded like instruction, like directions, and then a final phrase, repeated three times, that needed no translation at all because the cadence of it was universal:

*Come. Come. Come.*

A distress beacon that was also a map. A lament that was also an invitation. Two hundred years of a dead language saying *come* into the dark, addressed to whoever finally came, and the cruelty of it closed around Rafael's throat — because for most of those two hundred years, the message had been true and hopeless at once. Sent by survivors who believed no one was left to hear it. Or sent *to* survivors, telling them where the others had gone, generation after generation updating the list of names, keeping the count of the dead current, teaching the children the language not as inheritance but as evidence.

You keep a language alive for one of two reasons, Rafael thought. Because you love it. Or because you expect someday to have to prove it existed.

She looked up at the highest terrace, where the darkness gathered thickest, and spoke — not loudly, the way you speak in a sickroom.

"I'm not armed. Well. I'm armed, but I'm not — " She stopped, corrected herself. "I'm an investigator. Insurance division. I came down because the numbers didn't add up, and now I'm standing in the part that isn't in any number." Her voice echoed off stone and plating and came back to her sounding smaller than she'd intended. "I don't know your language. I'm sorry. I don't know anything except that someone has been killing people upstairs to make sure no one ever found you, and I think you already know that, and I think that's why you're hiding."

Silence. The water ran. The pot steamed. Somewhere a grow-light buzzed at the edge of its cycle.

Then, from the dark of the highest terrace, a sound she would carry for the rest of her life: a voice answering hers — an old voice, cracked and deliberate, speaking Standard Fleet with an accent she had never heard, the accent of a tongue that officially did not exist, pronouncing each common word as though it were a foreign dignitary being introduced in a hostile court.

"We know," the voice said. "We have always known. The question, investigator, is what *you* know — and who sent you."

Rafael's hand drifted away from her sidearm, deliberately, and lifted instead into the light, palm open, empty.

"My name," she said, "is Rafael Lockridge. And I was sent to find nothing."

A pause. Then the old voice, dry as the dust on the rails, almost — almost — amused:

"Then you have exceeded your assignment."

Movement on the terrace now, shapes detaching from the dark — three figures, four, descending the stair cut into the rock, unhurried, their faces resolving as they came into the amber glow. The one in front was ancient, straight-backed, wearing a coat made from the lining of a much older coat. Behind him, a woman with a child half-hidden in the folds of her clothing, the child's eyes enormous and unafraid in a way that made Rafael's chest hurt, because unafraid was not what two hundred years underground should have taught anyone.

The elder stopped at the edge of the light and studied her the way she had studied forty-one accident files: thoroughly, without mercy, looking for the seam.

"You came down through the ventilation shaft," he said. "Alone. Without authorization. Your own people will call that trespass." He tilted his head. "Or they will call it opportunity. We have waited a long time to learn which word the fleet prefers."

Behind him, the woman said something soft in the dead language, and the child laughed — actually laughed, bright and sudden, echoing off the terraces of the hidden village — and Rafael understood, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with the depth of the mine, that the signal she'd followed down into the dark had not been a distress call at all.

It had been a test. And somewhere above her, in the upper colony, in offices with certified locks and pre-approved findings, someone had just heard the same broadcast she had — and was already deciding what to do about the fact that an insurance investigator had answered it.

The elder extended his hand, palm up, mirroring hers.

"Come and sit," he said. "You have sixty days. So do we. Let us spend the first of them well."
