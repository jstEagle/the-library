## 21. Cut Lines

The message left the enclave at 0400, and by 0417 it was dead.

Rafael learned this the way she learned most things on Pale Meridian — not through announcement but through absence. She had been awake anyway, sitting in her rented quarters above the shuttered customs house with Sefa's smuggled logs spread across a secondhand terminal, when the uplink indicator in the corner of her screen flickered from amber to grey. Not red. Red would have meant failure, an error she could interrogate, a fault with a code she could chase down some bureaucratic corridor until someone admitted it existed. Grey meant nothing at all. Grey meant the channel simply wasn't there anymore.

She tried the packet again. The transmission window opened, thought about itself for eleven seconds, and closed without comment.

She tried the backup relay on the old survey mast above the port. Dead. She tried the emergency band, the one every colonial charter guaranteed as inviolable — *reserved for distress, immune to administrative control*, the founding documents said, in the same confident legal prose that had once promised the Kessari relocation rather than extermination. The emergency band answered with a carrier tone and then, more insultingly, with music. A soft orchestral loop the fleet piped to colonies during scheduled outages, something pastoral and string-heavy, chosen by some committee to be soothing. Rafael sat in the dark listening to violins reassure her that everything was fine, and understood that she was listening to the sound of a door being locked from the other side.

* * *

By dawn she was in the records office, where the outage notice had already arrived, printed on actual paper because paper could not be forwarded or archived inconveniently. It was a masterpiece of its genre. *Scheduled maintenance of interplanetary communications infrastructure,* it read. *Duration: indefinite. We apologize for any inconvenience.* There was no work order number. There was no maintenance crew manifest. There was no date of completion, which in fleet accounting was roughly equivalent to a confession signed in blood, because the Office of Continuity's accountants never authorized open-ended expenditures — except, apparently, when they did.

Administrator Voss stood in his office doorway watching her read it, and his face was doing something complicated. He had aged visibly since her arrival, she realized; the rehearsed warmth of their first meeting had burned off like morning fog, and what remained underneath was a man performing calm the way a drowning man performs floating.

"Investigator Lockridge," he said. "I trust the disruption hasn't affected your work."

"My work is transmission," Rafael said. "My findings are worthless if they stay on this rock."

"Then I'd recommend patience." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing he'd shown her yet. "These systems are old. Older than anyone likes to admit. Sometimes they need... attention."

"Indefinite attention."

"These things take the time they take." He turned back into his office, then paused, one hand on the frame, and for a moment — half a moment — he looked at her with something that was not quite apology and not quite warning. "Miss Lockridge. Whatever report you're preparing. I'd think carefully about how much of it needs to leave this planet at all."

Then the door closed, and Rafael stood alone in the corridor with the maintenance notice crumpling slowly in her fist, and understood that Voss had just told her two things: that he knew what she had found, and that whatever came next would not be an accident. The era of accidents was over. Accidents were for people who still believed in coincidence.

* * *

She went down the shaft that afternoon.

It was becoming a habit she didn't examine too closely — the descent, the squeeze through the collapsed ventilation throat, the long dark drop where certified maps said there should be suffocation and instead there was moving air, cool and mineral-tasting, breathing like something alive. Her headlamp carved a cone out of the blackness. Somewhere below, speakers crackled in a language two centuries dead, and two hundred people who officially did not exist went about the business of staying alive.

Maren Kesseth met her at the junction chamber, the elder's face emerging from shadow like a moon rising. Behind her, the enclave had transformed in the weeks since Rafael first saw it — the chamber walls now hung with cable runs and salvaged panels, children's chalk marks climbing toward ceilings no daylight had ever touched. But today the mood was wrong. She felt it before anyone spoke: the particular silence of a community that has received bad news and is deciding how much of it to tell its guest.

"You've heard," Rafael said.

"The uplink died in the night," Maren said. "Sefa's contacts in the port confirmed it before your fleet even posted the notice. Maintenance, they call it."

"They call it that."

"And you know what it means."

Rafael looked around the chamber — at the elders seated in their loose crescent, at Ilan and the other young messengers who had been rehearsing testimony in Kessari for the council that would now, possibly, never hear it, at the data core sitting in its padded case like a sleeping animal everyone was afraid to wake. Fifty days. That was what she'd told them. Fifty days until the truce expired, until the war's legal framework dissolved and force became lawful again, until every grievance in the fleet became an argument that could be settled with ordnance. They had fifty days to move a message off a planet whose every channel out had just been amputated.

"It means," Rafael said slowly, "that someone read my earlier transmissions. Or intercepted the ones I thought I'd sent. And decided the message couldn't be allowed to arrive." She heard her own voice go flat and professional, the investigator's register, the one that kept fear at arm's length. "It means we're not trying to beat a deadline anymore. We're trying to escape a trap."

An old man near the crescent's edge — Torvath, the former soldier, the one who argued the fleet only understood bodies — made a sound that might have been a laugh. "We have been inside the trap for two hundred years, investigator. The only difference is that now you're in it with us."

"I'm sorry—"

"Don't apologize." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the burn-scarring along his jaw, old damage from a life Rafael knew only in fragments. "Apologizing is what people do instead of acting. Tell us instead: what channels remain?"

"None. Legal ones." She counted them off because counting was how she kept panic organized. "The uplink is severed. The relay masts are dead. The emergency band is playing lullabies. The port has three ships docked and all three are under fleet charter with fleet crews, and my credentials were flagged yesterday — I checked, quietly, through a contact in cargo scheduling. As of 1800 hours I can't book passage, can't file a flight plan, can't so much as rent a groundcar without a supervisor's countersignature." She paused. "They haven't arrested me. That's the part that worries me most. Arrest is a legal act. This is something else. They're building a box around me one regulation at a time, and when the box is finished, whatever happens inside it will be very hard to call a crime."

Silence took the chamber. In it Rafael could hear the enclave's own infrastructure — water moving through salvaged pipe, the hum of generators Sefa had kept alive for thirty years with parts smuggled down in maintenance crates. All of it dependent, she realized, on the same fleet logistics that had just sealed the surface. If the shipments stopped—

"Sefa," she said. "His supply runs. If the port locks down fully—"

"He has six months of medicine cached," Maren said. "He planned for this. He has planned for everything for thirty years." Something moved across the old woman's face, grief and pride tangled together. "He always said the fleet would cut the lines eventually. He said a lie can only survive in the dark, and the day the truth tried to leave, they would make sure nothing else could either."

* * *

She found him in the machine gallery, elbow-deep in a coolant manifold, and told him everything she'd told the elders and a few things she hadn't. He listened while he worked, which was his way — Sefa Idris processed catastrophe with his hands busy, as if the world's horrors were simply another class of mechanical failure awaiting diagnosis.

"So," he said finally, wiping forty years of grease onto a rag older than both of them. "No uplink. No ships. No credentials. No emergency band. And fifty days." He said it without drama, inventorying the trap the way he'd inventory a parts shortage. "You know what I did the last time the fleet sealed these tunnels?"

"You've never told me."

"No," he agreed. "I haven't." He set down the rag and looked at her, and in the lamplight his eyes were very clear. "There were more of us then. Before the plagues, before the collapses. And the fleet sealed the shafts and posted a quarantine notice and waited for us to become a rounding error. What saved us wasn't courage. It was redundancy. Every system they cut, we'd built a second one they didn't know about. Every line they monitored, we'd dug a parallel. The lesson I'd give you, investigator, is this: when they cut all the lines, stop looking for lines. Start looking for what isn't a line at all."

"Meaning what? There's no ship on this planet they don't control."

"There's no *fleet* ship they don't control." His mouth did something dry. "But this colony was a mining settlement before it was anything else. And mining settlements accumulate junk. Decommissioned hulls. Survey shuttles written off in the ledgers but never actually hauled away, because hauling costs money and dead metal doesn't complain. There's a boneyard past the east slag fields. I've been keeping one hull airworthy for eleven years — not to escape, understand. I stopped believing in escape decades ago. I kept it running because keeping something running is a form of hope, and an old man needs his hobbies."

Rafael stared at him. "You have a shuttle."

"I have a *decommissioned survey platform*, registration number K-7741, stricken from fleet rolls in a paperwork error I may have personally arranged." He was smiling now, a small crooked thing. "It has no transponder worth spoofing, no armament, a drive rated for planetary orbit and maybe a whisper beyond, and it is, as of tonight, the only vessel on this world that exists in no one's ledger but mine."

"It'll never reach the truce station."

"No," Sefa said. "Probably not. Its range is a fraction of the distance. But range is a problem for after launch, and launch is a problem for now, and I've survived two hundred years of history by refusing to solve tomorrow's problem before today's." He picked the rag back up. "Go back up. Watch Voss. Watch your mentor, when he comes — and he'll come, investigator, men like Corvel Ashen always come when the trap closes, because the trap is partly for you. And when the moment ripens, you come find me, and we'll see whether an insurance investigator can forge a clearance good enough to fly a ghost."

* * *

She climbed back toward the surface through the dark, and somewhere in the middle stretch of the ventilation shaft, wedged in the narrow throat where the collapsed rock narrowed to a crawl, she stopped and let herself feel it — really feel it, for the first time since the grey light on her terminal. The trap closing. Not with violence, not with soldiers at her door, but with paperwork. With maintenance notices and flagged credentials and a violin loop where a distress band used to be. Whoever was doing this had learned from the forty-one accidents: never leave a wound. Just remove the possibility of movement, one certified system at a time, until the victim was standing in a perfectly lawful room with no exits, and then let physics or bureaucracy do the rest.

Her wrist comm buzzed against the stone. One line, unencrypted, from a port scheduling clerk who owed her a favor and was about to regret it:

*Unscheduled heavy transport inbound. Fleet registry, Continuity charter. Landing clearance requested for 1900 tomorrow. Requesting officer listed as C. ASHEN.*

Rafael lay in the dark of the earth with the message glowing against her palm, and above her, two hundred meters of rock, the man who had taught her everything was coming down through the sky.

She started climbing again. Faster.
