## 22. Ashen's Choice

The order arrived at 0400 station time, which Corvel Ashen had learned, over thirty years in the fleet's employ, was the hour reserved for things the fleet did not want witnessed. It came encrypted on a channel that officially did not exist, addressed to a rank he had not held since the founding war commemorations, and it was signed by a director whose name appeared in no public registry. He read it twice standing at his cabin viewport, watching Pale Meridian turn beneath him — a brown-and-gray coin of a world, its night side stitched with the dying lights of a colony everyone had already buried.

*Lead sanitation team six. Objective: sublevel complex, former Kessari mining works. Method: total structural denial. Timeline: seventy-two hours. No survivors, no records, no exceptions.*

No survivors. Three words doing the work of a paragraph.

He set the tablet down on the desk with the exaggerated care of a man who did not trust his own hands, and poured himself a measure of the whiskey he kept for occasions he hoped would never come. The glass trembled — not much, but enough. He was sixty-one years old and his hands had been steady through two wars, through the identification of four hundred bodies after the *Vantage* decompression, through the moment he'd told a young investigator named Rafael Lockridge that her father's death had been ruled an accident and watched her decide, silently, to make a career of proving otherwise.

The whiskey burned going down. It did not help.

* * *

He spent the morning doing what he did best: stalling with the appearance of diligence.

Sanitation teams were assembled from Continuity's deep roster — men and women whose service records existed only as checksums, whose loyalty had been purchased with debts forgiven and pasts erased. Corvel had trained three of them himself, years ago, back when he still believed the Office's work was a necessary surgery performed on the body politic. He requisitioned equipment with deliberate slowness. He filed a geological survey request for the mine complex that would take, if he padded it correctly, thirty hours to process. He scheduled the team briefing for 1900 and then moved it to 2100, citing a supply delay from the orbital depot that he invented himself and confirmed himself through a procurement officer who owed him a favor and asked no questions.

It was, he reflected, remarkable how much friction a single conscientious man could introduce into a machine built for efficiency. Every falsified form was a small act of treason dressed as bureaucracy. Every delayed signature bought the tunnels another hour of breathing.

But friction was not refusal. Seventy-two hours was seventy-two hours, and the clock did not care about his conscience. Somewhere beneath those brown hills, two hundred people were living in the dark because his employer had failed to kill their grandparents thoroughly enough, and in seventy hours or less, men with shaped charges and thermal lances were going to finish the job.

Unless someone warned them.

Unless someone warned *her*.

He thought of Rafael the way you think of a wound you've stopped looking at directly. She had been his best student — not the most gifted, perhaps; there had been others with quicker intuitions — but the one who took doubt seriously, who treated every certified fact as a hypothesis awaiting its autopsy. He had taught her that an accident is a story someone paid for, and she had taken the lesson so completely that she had eventually turned it on the fleet itself, and then on him, and now here he stood, holding an order to erase the last living witnesses to the fleet's founding crime, understanding at last that the lesson had never been his to give. It had belonged to the truth all along. He had merely been its negligent custodian.

* * *

At noon he walked the spaceport district under a gray sky that smelled of dust and recycled rain.

Pale Meridian in daylight was worse than at night. The shuttered arcades, the boarding-house windows papered over with fleet notices about truce renewal schedules, the old colonial fountain at the district center running dry except for a thin green trickle of algae water — the whole town had the look of a place practicing its own disappearance. A few residents nodded to him as he passed. They saw a fleet inspector, senior, gray-coated, unremarkable. They did not see a man carrying a death warrant for two hundred souls in a sealed folder against his ribs.

He found what he needed in a surplus shop off the freight concourse: a maintenance transponder, secondhand, its registration scrubbed, broadcasting on a frequency that the port authority logged but nobody monitored. Fifteen credits. The instrument of his treason cost less than lunch.

Back in his cabin, he sat down to compose the warning, and discovered that this was the hardest part — harder than the falsified schedules, harder than facing the team at 2100. Because anything explicit was fatal. Continuity monitored his outgoing traffic; they had done so since the day he landed, and he'd have been insulted if he weren't so frightened. Whatever he sent Rafael had to be deniable even under interrogation. It had to be nothing — a fragment, an echo, a piece of their shared past reassembled into meaning only by the person who shared it.

Their training days. Twenty-six years ago, the investigation academy at Corvus Reach, the long winter of practical exercises. He closed his eyes and let the memory come up clean.

They had run a simulation called the Sealed Room: a staged accident scene, all evidence planted, and the students' task was to find the story underneath. Rafael had solved it in nine hours by refusing every official document in the file. When he'd asked her afterward how she knew where to look, she'd said something he had never forgotten: *"You told me once that whoever cleans a scene always leaves the cleaning itself as evidence. So I looked for the absence. What's missing is the message."*

Absence as message. That was the code. Not what he wrote — what he failed to write.

He opened a secure channel to her field address and typed a routine supervisory memo, the kind he had sent her a dozen times across her career: schedule confirmations, procedural reminders, boilerplate. Then, in the middle of it, he introduced the absences.

A standard memo of this type contained seven sections. He included five. The missing two were sections four and seven — and section four in their academy numbering had always been *Witness Protection*, section seven *Site Preservation*. To anyone else, a truncated memo, sloppy, forgettable. To Rafael, who had memorized the academy manual the way other students memorized song lyrics, two missing sections in that exact pairing could mean only one thing:

*They are coming for the witnesses, and they will destroy the site.*

He added a timestamp reference — 72:00:00, formatted as a maintenance interval, meaningless to any auditor — and signed it with his standard closing, except he dropped the final character of his authorization code. Another absence. Another message: *I am compromised. Trust nothing signed complete.*

He read it eleven times. He imagined the interrogation: *Explain the missing sections, Inspector Ashen.* And he heard himself answer: *An oversight. I'm old. My memos have gaps.* Plausible. Barely. Enough, maybe.

His thumb hovered over the transmit key for a long moment. Once sent, there was no version of this that ended with him retiring to a garden world. Either the fleet fell and forgave him, or the fleet held and erased him — and either way, Rafael Lockridge would know, with certainty, which side he had finally chosen.

He thought about Sefa Idris, though he'd never met the man. The old engineer's name had surfaced in Continuity's files years ago, flagged and inexplicably never actioned — a mercy Corvel had never understood until now. Thirty years of smuggling medicine into the dark. Thirty years of small treasons, each one deniable, each one insufficient, all of them together keeping two hundred heartbeats alive. Maybe that was all any decent man could manage inside the machine: not revolution, but sabotage by increments. Friction. Absence. Gaps in the memo.

Corvel Ashen pressed transmit.

* * *

The team briefing ran at 2130, an hour later than rescheduled, because the geological survey had come back — he'd made sure of it — with ambiguities requiring review.

Six faces around the table in the transport's operations room, lit flat by the tactical display. He knew four of them. Vasko, the breacher, who collected vintage firearms and cried at his daughter's recitals. Imara Chen, the signals specialist, who had once bought him coffee for a month when his wife was dying, saying nothing, just leaving the cup. The others were newer, blanker, chosen by the Office precisely because they were blank.

He briefed them on the objective in the sanitized language of the trade: structural remediation of a compromised sublevel, hazard abatement, site denial. He watched Vasko take notes and felt ill. These were not monsters. That was the oldest lie the fleet told itself — that the work attracted monsters. The work attracted tired people with mortgages and secrets, and it made the monstrousness administrative, distributed, deniable. Six ordinary people would descend into those mines in fifty-one hours and end two hundred lives, and each of them would go home able to sleep, because the order had come from above and the language had been clean.

"Timeline?" Vasko asked.

"Seventy-two-hour window opens at 0600 tomorrow," Corvel said. "Survey review first. We do not breach until geology signs off." He let the words carry the weight of procedure, the sacred inertia of checklist. "Nobody moves early. Nobody improvises. This goes by the book or it doesn't go."

Chen looked up sharply — she was sharp, that was the trouble with training people well — and he met her gaze with the bland face he'd worn through two wars. She nodded slowly and went back to her notes.

Afterward he sat alone in the operations room, the display cycling the mine complex schematics: the ventilation shafts, the collapsed galleries, the deep workings marked *UNSURVEYED — SEALED 197 YRS*. Somewhere in that unsurveyed dark, a language that the fleet had declared extinct was being spoken aloud to children. He found himself trying to imagine it — the sound of it, rising through rock, patient as groundwater — and could not, and the failure shamed him more than the order had.

Fifty-one hours. His delays had bought time, but time was a currency that only spent in one direction. If Rafael received the warning, if she understood it, if she moved fast enough — three ifs, chained together like climbers on bad rope.

He opened the channel log and watched the transmission status change: *DELIVERED — ACKNOWLEDGMENT PENDING.*

Pending. He stared at those two words for a long time, and then, because a man can only bear so much uncertainty in one day, he pulled up the academy manual, section four, and read the old text he'd helped draft twenty-six years ago:

*The protection of witnesses supersedes all investigative convenience. A case without witnesses is not a case; it is a verdict rendered in absentia by whoever remains standing.*

He had written that believing it. He had spent a career betraying it. And now, in the last hours available to him, he was spending everything he had left — rank, record, reputation, the whole careful architecture of a life — on a truncated memo and a delayed survey, betting that one student had learned the lesson better than the teacher ever had.

The acknowledgment light stayed gray.

Outside the viewport, Pale Meridian turned through its long night, and beneath its surface, in tunnels he had helped map a lifetime ago, the clock was already running.
