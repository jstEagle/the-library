## 4. Voices in Contested Orbit

The hails came in at 0317 station time, both of them, simultaneously, which was itself an impossibility Edmund had to sit with for a moment before doing anything about it.

They were sitting in the galley with a cup of reconstituted coffee that tasted like someone had described coffee to a machine over a bad comm line. The gravity had been steady for six hours. The ballast anomaly sat in their logs like a stone at the bottom of a glass — you could stop looking at it, but you always knew where it was. Edmund had spent those six hours drafting maintenance report 4471-A, *Anomalous Mass Reading, Keel Section, Non-Critical*, and then deleting it, and then redrafting it, because there was no category for what they'd found and no one left to read it anyway.

Then every comm channel on Pale Anchor lit up at once.

Not the routine traffic — the automated handshake pings from the Meridian relay chain, the weekly scheduling bursts from Compact logistics, the carrier waves of dead networks still broadcasting to nobody. All of that hummed along underneath like weather. This was different. This was two priority-one transmissions arriving on two separate encrypted bands at the same second, each one flagged with authentication codes Edmund hadn't seen used since the war scare of their third year, when both sides had parked battle groups within missile range of each other for eleven days and everyone had pretended it was an exercise.

Edmund set down the coffee. Their hands were steady, which surprised them.

"Station log," they said to the empty galley, because talking to the recorder was as close as they got to talking to anyone. "0317 hours. Receiving simultaneous priority traffic from Meridian Combine relay *Kestrel-9* and Halcyon Compact vessel *Perpetual Vigilance*. Both channels requesting immediate response." A pause. "Both channels requesting immediate response," they repeated, because saying it twice made it slightly more real.

Twelve years on Pale Anchor and Edmund had been hailed directly maybe forty times. Half of those were wrong numbers. The rest were cargo manifests, docking clearances, a single apologetic message from a Combine quartermaster who had shorted them three pallets of protein stock and wanted it noted that it wasn't personal. Nobody hailed Pale Anchor. Pale Anchor was a waypoint, a fueling stop, a line item. It was the orbital equivalent of a restroom off a highway — useful, unremarkable, beneath notice.

Two powers noticing it at once meant one thing: whatever was under the keel wasn't Edmund's secret anymore.

* * *

They played the messages in order of receipt, which was meaningless since they'd arrived in the same instant, and which already felt like taking a side.

The Meridian transmission came through crisp and corporate, video and audio, a woman in a Combine fleet uniform with the collar tabs of someone who mattered. She was seated, composed, backlit by the blue-gray wash of a command deck. Her name tag read VOSS, I., CAPTAIN, MCS *TENACITY*.

"Pale Anchor Station, this is Captain Ilsa Voss, Meridian Combine Navy, commanding the Third Patrol Group. Your station registered a system-wide gravitational event at 1842 local yesterday. We have independently confirmed the event's origin point as your position." A beat, perfectly measured. "We are aware that you have also detected anomalous mass readings consistent with our own instruments' findings. I want to be direct with you, technician, because I suspect you've had enough of people who aren't."

Edmund felt something twist in their chest at that — flattery aimed straight down the barrel, and accurate enough to land.

"The Combine is dispatching a vessel to your position. It will arrive in approximately fifty-one hours. You are not required to do anything before then except remain safe and keep your station operational. However, I am authorized to offer you protection, compensation, and — if the anomaly proves what we believe it may prove — a role in what comes next that no one else can offer you. You have been alone out there a long time, Joubert. That ends soon, one way or another."

She let that last sentence sit for a full second before signing off. Edmund stared at the frozen frame of her face. They didn't know how she knew their name. Then they remembered: the station registry was public. Everything about them was public. It had just never mattered to anyone before.

The Compact message was audio only, and it was colder for it — a synthesized voice with a human's cadence grafted onto it, the kind of voice Compact bureaucracy used when it wanted to sound impartial while being anything but.

"Pale Anchor Station, this is Admiral Reyes, Halcyon Compact Orbital Command. By authority of the Compact Assembly, we formally notify you that the gravitational anomaly originating at your coordinates falls under Compact jurisdiction per the Kessler Accords, Article Fourteen, governing pre-colonial artifacts discovered within contested volumes."

*Pre-colonial artifacts.* Edmund's stomach dropped. Not an equipment malfunction. Not a sensor ghost. An *artifact*. Someone on both sides already knew more than they did, and both of them had skipped past *what is it* and gone straight to *who owns it.*

"You are directed to refrain from any interaction with the anomaly, including physical descent into sublevel structures, until a Compact survey team arrives aboard the CSV *Perpetual Vigilance*, ETA sixty-two hours. Be advised that unauthorized contact with protected artifacts constitutes a violation carrying civil penalties under Compact law. Be further advised—" and here the synthetic voice did something almost human, almost gentle, "—that the Meridian Combine has also dispatched assets to your position. The Compact cannot guarantee your station's neutrality will be respected by Combine forces. We can guarantee ours. Respond on this channel to confirm receipt."

Edmund sat in the galley for a long time after both messages finished, listening to the station breathe around them — the air handlers, the coolant pumps, the soft tick of thermal expansion in the hull. Fifty-one hours. Sixty-two hours. Two fleets converging on a station whose entire defensive capability consisted of Edmund and a fire suppression system designed by the lowest bidder.

They thought about not responding at all. Just letting the silence stretch, staying invisible, staying nobody. It had worked for twelve years.

But invisibility, they understood now, was over. The only question left was what shape being visible would take.

* * *

They spent the morning doing the thing they knew how to do: work. There was comfort in it, real comfort, the kind they'd built their whole life around. Filter flush on the water reclamation loop. Torque check on the docking ring clamps, because if two military vessels were going to try to park on their station, the clamps would damn well hold. They ran diagnostics on the long-range array and found it healthy, which was its own small horror — healthy meant the fleets hadn't needed to guess about anything. Every reading Edmund had taken, every log entry, every automated status burst the station sent out on schedule to a network that mostly ignored it: all of it had been sitting in plain sight, waiting for someone important to look.

At 1140 they pulled up the orbital traffic display and looked at what was coming.

The Combine group was four signatures out past the Kestrel relay chain, burning hard — *Tenacity* and three escorts, running dark otherwise, all business. The Compact response was smaller on paper, one heavy cruiser and a tender, but the Compact always kept a picket closer to Pale Anchor than either side admitted to publicly, and Edmund could see the tender's escort detachment peeling away from its patrol lane and falling in behind the cruiser like ducklings. Eleven ships total, give or take drones. Around a station with one crew member.

Eleven ships. For a rock that processed forty containers a week.

"They're not coming for me," Edmund said aloud, to the galley, to the recorder, to whoever might someday listen. "I'm just the door."

And then, because the thought arrived fully formed and refused to leave: *and doors get kicked in.*

* * *

The afternoon went to the question of the reply. Both messages demanded confirmation. Neither specified what confirmation should contain. Edmund drafted six responses to Voss and five to Reyes and deleted all of them, and finally understood that the problem wasn't wording. The problem was that any reply at all was a move on a board they hadn't known they were standing on. Confirm receipt to Voss first, even by ninety seconds, and somewhere in a Combine intelligence shop a junior analyst would timestamp it and file it under *cooperative*. Answer Reyes first and the mirror image happened. Stay silent and both sides would read hostility, or worse, read that someone else had gotten there first.

There was no neutral answer. There was only the appearance of one.

So Edmund did the most technician thing possible: they answered both with the same message, same words, sent within the same minute, stripped of everything but fact.

*"Pale Anchor Station confirms receipt of transmission. Station systems nominal. Crew complement: one. Station remains available for lawful docking under existing commercial agreements. Joubert out."*

No acknowledgment of claims. No acknowledgment of jurisdiction. No mention of the keel, the sublevels, the nine seconds, or the fact that Edmund had already been down there and touched things. That last part they told themselves was an omission of convenience rather than concealment, and they were honest enough with themselves to know it was a lie, and tired enough not to care.

Both sides confirmed receipt within minutes. Both confirmations were warm. Both warmth made Edmund's skin crawl.

* * *

It was during the second watch, alone in the observation blister with the system spread out below them like spilled jewelry, that the feeling finally found its name.

They'd expected dread. Dread was accounted for; dread was just fear with a schedule attached, and Edmund had lived with a low ambient hum of it since the flicker. What they hadn't expected was this other thing, this stranger moving through their chest, and it took them most of a shift of turning it over before they could look at it directly.

It was grief.

Not for anything lost yet. Grief in advance — mourning the version of their life that had ended at 0317 without asking permission. Twelve years of being beneath notice, and they had hated it, had catalogued its costs in a thousand private ledgers: the sister who stopped writing, the holidays logged as work days, the birthday they'd spent replacing a failed bearing in pump three and marking the occasion by eating the dessert ration. They had hated being invisible the way you hate a chronic pain — constantly, functionally, without ever quite believing life could be otherwise.

But invisible had also meant *free*. Unclaimed. When Meridian and Halcyon drew their maps, Pale Anchor sat in the gray space between the lines, and so did Edmund. No one's citizen. No one's soldier. No one's asset. The loneliness had been the price, and the price had been terrible, and Edmund had paid it every day for twelve years without missing a payment — and now two governments had arrived simultaneously to inform them the debt was called due, that the gray space was being annexed, that the map was being redrawn through the middle of their life whether they consented or not.

Being noticed, they understood, watching the approaching drive-flares crawl across the display, was not rescue.

It was capture. Slower and politer than a boarding action, but capture all the same. Voss had promised the solitude would end "one way or another," and she hadn't been offering companionship. She'd been announcing custody.

"I liked being nobody," Edmund told the stars, and heard immediately how thin it sounded, how much of a lie, and laughed — a short, ugly sound in the sealed air. Because that wasn't true either. Nobody *liked* this. But nobody had chosen it either, and choice was the whole difference between exile and freedom, and Edmund had spent twelve years telling themselves the story where those were the same thing.

Below them, the anomaly waited under the keel, patient as geology. Somewhere in the dark between here and the Kestrel chain, eleven warships burned toward them carrying two versions of the future, each one certain it was the only legitimate one. And in between — in the gap, in the gray — there was just Edmund, one crew complement, holding a station neither side would let them keep and neither side could take without them.

That was the thought they fell asleep on, strapped into their bunk with the traffic display still glowing against the closed eyelids: *they need me to open the door.*

It should have been leverage. It felt like a target.

* * *

At 0248, ninety minutes before either fleet entered hailing range, the station woke Edmund up.

Not an alarm. Nothing so crude. It was the gravity again — not a stutter this time, nothing so dramatic. A lean. A fractional tilt, half a degree, sustained, like the whole station was a held breath leaning toward something. Edmund floated out of the bunk's straps and caught the handrail and checked the telemetry with their heart going hard, and the readings were clean, impossible, and unmistakable.

The mass under the keel had moved. Not much. A few meters, deep down, deliberate — the way something shifts its weight when it's been lying still a long time and has decided to pay attention.

And on the console, in the queue of incoming traffic, one new item had appeared. Not from the Combine. Not from the Compact. Origin: internal. Source node: keel.

A single line of text, assembled in Edmund's own handwriting font, lifted from twelve years of maintenance logs:

***THEY ARE COMING FOR THE DOOR. COME DOWN BEFORE THEY ARRIVE.***

Edmund looked at it for a long time. Out past the observation blister, the first drive-flares were resolving into shapes, into names, into flags.

They reached for their boots.
