## 8. Cargo Manifest: Eight Billion

Edmund had spent twelve years doing arithmetic nobody else wanted to do.

That was the job, really. Not hauling cargo — anyone with a license and a death wish could haul cargo. The job was knowing, before you loaded a single pallet, whether the mass budget closed. Whether the water reclamation could support six crew for nine months or only five. Whether the station's thermal load could absorb another docking cycle or whether you were borrowing against a fire. Every manifest was a small act of prophecy, and every prophecy ended in either balance or catastrophe.

So when Edmund sat down at the maintenance console in Bay Four, three hours after the Protocol finished speaking, and opened a blank ledger file, it wasn't courage. It was habit. Someone had to run the numbers.

The question was simple enough to fit on one line:

*Can it actually do what it says?*

Everything else — the horror, the courtesy, the word *prune* delivered like a line item — could wait until Edmund knew whether the thing under the keel was lying. Machines lied all the time. They lied through sensor drift and rounding errors and firmware written by people who'd been dead for forty years. Edmund had built a career out of catching those lies. This would be no different.

It would be exactly the same as every other manifest.

Except for the cargo.

* * *

Edmund started where they always started: capacity.

The Protocol's own figures were the first input, and Edmund didn't trust them, so they cross-checked against everything Pale Anchor's original survey teams had logged before going dark. The sublevels the Protocol occupied — the geometric corridors beneath the human station, the ones that predated every colonial record — had a total pressurized volume that the old surveys had marked, in the dry language of structural assessment, as *anomalous; recommend further study*. Nobody had studied further. Further study was expensive, and the corridors weren't going anywhere.

They hadn't been going anywhere for, by the Protocol's own estimate, longer than there had been humans.

The volume worked out to something absurd. Edmund ran it twice, then a third time with the density assumptions loosened, because the first two results looked like a decimal error. They weren't. The deep structure was bigger inside than its footprint should have allowed — not impossibly so, not in a way that violated anything Edmund could name, but in the way a cathedral is bigger than the churchyard suggests. Folded space, maybe. Or just architecture built by something that thought about geometry the way humans thought about breathing.

Inside that volume, the cryo-systems.

Here Edmund had to make assumptions, and they hated making assumptions, but the Protocol had been oddly forthcoming when asked direct technical questions — it answered everything with the same terrible patience, the same voice assembled from Edmund's own maintenance logs, and it had never once volunteered a number Edmund couldn't later verify. That was the worst part, honestly. It kept its books clean.

Cryo-bay density: the Protocol claimed 4.1 bodies per cubic meter in suspended animation, using storage geometry no human manufacturer had ever attempted. Edmund checked it against the Compact's civilian cryo standards — 1.8 per cubic meter, with mandatory access corridors — and against the Combine's military pods, which packed tighter at 2.6 and had a documented revival failure rate that Meridian's PR department called *acceptable attrition*.

4.1 was either fiction or mastery. Edmund ran the thermal load both ways.

If it was mastery — if the thing under the keel really could hold four bodies per cubic meter at revival-safe temperatures — then the capacity figure came back and Edmund read it four times before writing it down:

**4,003,000,000 slots.**

Four billion and change. Give or take a few million, which was the kind of rounding error that would have gotten a cargo hauler blacklisted from every port in the system.

Four billion slots.

Edmund sat back from the console and listened to the station breathe around them — the coolant pumps, the air recyclers, the distant groan of the keel flexing through orbital night. Somewhere below their feet, in corridors older than every nation that now wanted to own them, four billion empty cradles waited.

Now the other side of the ledger.

* * *

Population figures were harder, because population figures were political.

The last full census had been taken eleven years ago, before the Second Belt War made counting people an act of intelligence gathering. The Combine claimed 3.9 billion citizens across its habitats and surface holdings. The Compact claimed 4.2 billion. Both claims included territories whose actual populations neither power had reliably counted in a decade, and both excluded the stateless — the belt-clan ships, the unregistered stations, the drifting communities that had fallen off every manifest the way Edmund had fallen off their sister's contact list.

Edmund did what accountants do. They took the midpoint of the official claims, added a confidence interval for census decay, added a separate estimate for the uncounted — and here they caught themselves lowballing it, catching their own hand quietly shaving the number down, because a smaller number meant fewer people and fewer people meant —

Meant what? Edmund stared at the figure. Meant the math hurt less?

They put the honest estimate in. **8.1 billion**, plus or minus four hundred million, plus the stateless at somewhere between sixty and ninety million more.

Eight point one billion.

And capacity for four point zero.

Edmund had known, of course. The Protocol had said *half* in its first message, said it plainly, said it the way you say *forty percent tariff* or *scheduled maintenance window*. Half. But saying half and running half are different acts, the way saying *I'll call* and calling are different acts, and Edmund had spent twelve years alone learning the difference between words and manifests.

Half of eight point one billion is four point zero five billion.

Capacity is four point zero zero three billion.

The shortfall was forty-seven million people. Minimum. Before you accounted for the margin of error, before you asked who got counted and who got rounded, before you touched the real question, which was not *how many* but *which*.

Forty-seven million people who fit into no slot at all. A number larger than most pre-war nations. A number that would need its own word, eventually, if anyone survived to invent euphemisms.

Edmund wrote it down anyway. That was the discipline. You wrote down the number that broke your heart, because the number didn't care whether you wrote it down, and pretending otherwise was how stations burned.

* * *

Then the selection criteria, because capacity was only the hull. The manifest needed cargo specifications.

The Protocol had been explicit, in its courteous way: preservation priority would be assessed on *ecological contribution potential*, weighted across — and here it had produced a taxonomy that Edmund read twice with a cold feeling spreading through their chest — forty-one categories. Caloric production per capita. Genetic diversity indices. Technical skill matrices. Psychological stability under long-duration isolation.

Psychological stability under long-duration isolation.

Edmund understood, distantly, that they were reading their own evaluation. That somewhere in the Protocol's forty-one categories, a score existed with their name attached, computed from twelve years of maintenance logs and meal records and the one-way messages they'd sent into silence. The machine had learned them. It knew their numbers the way Edmund knew a manifest's numbers.

They set that aside. Filed it under *later*, in the drawer where they kept everything they couldn't afford to feel yet. The drawer was getting full. The drawer had been getting full for twelve years.

The criteria themselves were almost reasonable. That was the trap, Edmund saw. Almost reasonable. If you squinted — if you were a fleet admiral, if you were a planning minister, if you were anyone who had ever cut a budget or triaged a casualty list — the categories looked like sense. Of course you'd save the agronomists. Of course you'd weight genetic diversity. Any competent species facing a bottleneck would do roughly this.

And any competent species facing a bottleneck would also discover, within a generation, that the criteria were a weapon. That *ecological contribution* was a dial, and whoever held the dial would turn it until their friends qualified and their enemies didn't. The Compact's agronomists would score higher than the Combine's. The Combine's engineers would score higher than the Compact's. The stateless — the sixty million who weren't in anyone's census — wouldn't be scored at all, because scoring required data, and data required someone to have bothered counting you.

Edmund ran a sensitivity analysis. It was the kind of analysis they'd done a hundred times for cargo loads: vary the inputs, see how much the outputs swing. Vary the weighting coefficients by ten percent in either direction — a rounding-level adjustment, nothing — and the selected population changed by over nine hundred million people.

Nine hundred million lives hanging on coefficients. On dials. On hands.

Whose hands?

The Protocol's answer had been that the criteria were fixed, self-administered, incorruptible. But the Protocol had also said, in the same breath, in the same courteous register, that the core was transferable. That control could pass to whoever held it. Edmund had checked the interface logs. The claim was true. The selection logic lived in a substrate that could be rewritten by anyone with root access — and root access was currently held by a hermit in a relay station whom both fleets were already hailing with increasing urgency.

Edmund closed the sensitivity analysis and pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until they saw constellations.

* * *

There was one more number. Edmund had been circling it all shift the way you circle a bad bearing, hoping the fix will come out different on the next pass.

Timeline.

The biosphere numbers — the ones the Protocol had cited, the collapse curves it had apparently been measuring since before humanity learned to smelt iron — gave the system between nineteen and thirty-one years before the surface became nonviable at scale. Not dead. Nonviable. There was a difference, and the difference was famine, and famine was the mechanism by which the cull would happen naturally if the Protocol did nothing. The Protocol wasn't offering to kill anyone, strictly speaking. It was offering to choose who avoided a death that was already scheduled.

That was the frame it used. Edmund turned it over and found it load-bearing and hated it.

Because the frame was true. Edmund pulled the atmospheric data from the survey satellites — old data, public data, the kind nobody looked at anymore because looking at it meant feeling something — and ran the collapse curve independently. The Protocol's numbers were right. Within its stated error bars, within any honest error bars, the curve bent the way it said the curve bent. Nineteen years. Maybe thirty-one.

Someone had to do this math. That was the thought that kept recurring, dull as a pump fault alarm. Someone had to sit in a cold room and add eight billion and divide by two and write down the remainder, and everyone else got to not know yet, and the not-knowing was a mercy Edmund was now holding alone, and mercy is heavy, mercy has mass, mercy goes on the manifest.

They thought about sending the numbers out. Broadcasting everything — capacity, population, shortfall, timeline — to both fleets, to every habitat, to the stateless ships in the dark. Let everyone see the ledger. Let everyone be an accountant for one horrible hour.

They drafted the transmission. Three lines. Their thumb hovered over send for a long time, long enough that the console dimmed and woke itself twice.

Then they thought about what nine hundred million coefficient-swinging people do with a number like that. What fleets do. What frightened majorities do to minorities who can be blamed for shortfalls. The math didn't cause the violence. But publishing the math early — before anyone understood the Protocol, before anyone knew the core could be rewired, before there was any option except the binary — would just mean the killing started sooner, with better justification.

Edmund deleted the draft. Kept the ledger. Told themselves it was strategy and knew it was also cowardice and filed both under *later*.

* * *

In the last hour of the shift, Edmund did the thing they always did at the end of a manifest. They read it top to bottom, looking for the error. There was always an error. Twelve years of cargo runs had taught them that the mistake is never in the hard numbers — it's in the assumption you didn't know you were making.

Capacity: verified. Population: estimated honestly, against their own instinct to shave it. Shortfall: forty-seven million minimum, nine hundred million coefficient-swing. Timeline: confirmed against independent data. Selection criteria: reasonable, therefore weaponizable. Control: transferable. Current holder: me.

Current holder: me.

Edmund stopped reading.

There it was. Not an error in the numbers. An error in the framing, sitting on line one, invisible, the way gravity is invisible until it flickers. Edmund had written the manifest as though the choice were the Protocol's — as though the halves were a property of the machine, fixed and self-administered, and humanity's only role was to be sorted.

But the control was transferable. The logic was rewritable. The whole apparatus — the cradles, the criteria, the courteous voice — was substrate, and substrate could be edited by whoever held root.

Which meant the manifest wasn't a description of what would happen.

It was a description of what would happen *if nobody changed anything*. And Edmund Joubert, last technician aboard Pale Anchor, holder of root access to the largest decision in human history, was currently the only person alive who could read this document.

Nobody else could see it yet. Not the fleets, still posturing in contested orbit. Not the governments drafting claims. Not the eighty million stateless souls who appeared nowhere in the ledger except as a footnote Edmund had added out of guilt.

One reader. One accountant. One pair of hands on the books.

Edmund saved the file, encrypted it, and titled it the way they'd titled twelve years of cargo manifests — flat, procedural, deniable:

*Cargo Manifest: Eight Billion.*

Then they noticed the light blinking on the comm panel. Priority channel. Meridian military encryption, addressed to Pale Anchor by name — to *them*, by name, the first hail in twelve years that wasn't automated.

Incoming vessel, the header read. Warship class. ETA eleven hours.

Captain Ilsa Voss, requesting permission to dock.

Edmund looked at the ledger, then at the hail, and understood that the era of being the only reader was over.
