## 20. First Blood

The alarm came as a change in air pressure before it came as a sound.

Edmund had learned to read Pale Anchor the way sailors once read weather — through the skin, through the hairline cracks of habit. The station breathed on a schedule: intake valves cycling at 0400, thermal expansion ticking through the keel struts at dawn-shift, the low hum of the coolant pumps that had become, over twelve years, indistinguishable from silence itself. So when the breathing changed — a stutter in the intake cycle, a half-second where the air went thin and tasted of recycled metal — Edmund was already out of the bunk and pulling on a jumpsuit before the klaxon found its voice.

*Unauthorized approach. Docking Ring Two. Meridian hull signature.*

Then, eleven seconds later, as if the station had been waiting for someone to finish a sentence: *Unauthorized approach. Docking Ring Two. Halcyon hull signature.*

"Both of you," Edmund said aloud, to no one, to the station, to whatever was listening from beneath the keel. "You couldn't have scheduled it."

The comms had been screaming for six hours. Voss demanding boarding rights under some emergency provision Edmund had never heard of and suspected had been invented that morning. Admiral Reyes's fleet holding position at four hundred kilometers, close enough that their running lights made a second constellation outside the observation ports. And between them, Pale Anchor, rotating slowly, carrying its secret like a stone in its belly.

Edmund had spent those six hours doing what they did best: nothing visible. Checking seals. Running diagnostics on systems that didn't need them. Staying small. It was the deep-dwellers' first lesson, passed along by Amara with the gravity of scripture: *When the big ships argue, be furniture.* Amara's people had survived two generations down there by being furniture. Edmund had survived twelve years up here the same way.

But you cannot be furniture when both armies decide to walk through your living room.

* * *

The vents above Docking Ring Two were maintenance corridors barely wide enough for a person — designed for repair drones, actually, though the drones had been cannibalized for parts decades before Edmund arrived. Edmund crawled them now on elbows and knees, headlamp off, moving by memory and the ambient glow of status strips embedded in the duct walls every ten meters. Below, through the grated floor panels, the docking ring spread out in slices: the bay floor, the gantries, the customs partition that hadn't separated anything since the Combine abandoned the route twelve years ago.

Forty meters below, the first Meridian marines came through Airlock Four.

Edmund heard them before seeing them — the particular percussion of mag-boots on deck plating, eight boots moving in the synchronized rhythm of people who had drilled this a thousand times in simulators. Then the headlamps swept the dark bay, and Edmund pressed flat against the duct floor and watched through the grating as light crawled across the underside of the world.

They moved well. That was the first thing Edmund noticed, and hated noticing. Twelve years of solitude had not dulled the technician's eye; if anything it had sharpened it into something almost predatory, cataloguing everything, belonging to nothing. The marines stacked at the partition in clean geometry. A sergeant — compact woman, Meridian slate-grey armor with the pale blue chevron of the Third Orbital — signed hand signals that rippled down the line like wind through wheat. Within ninety seconds they held the customs partition, the gantry junction, and the environmental control alcove.

Professional. Quiet. Almost bloodless.

For about four minutes.

Because then Airlock Seven cycled, across the ring, and the Halcyon Compact marines came through in bone-white armor, and the mathematics of two armed forces occupying the same room began solving itself.

* * *

Edmund had watched wars the way everyone did now — through feeds, through commentary, through the sanitized grammar of casualty figures read by anchors who pronounced the numbers carefully, as if respect lived in diction. Forty thousand displaced. Six hundred dead. The halves of humanity, argued over in studios.

None of it prepared Edmund for the sound.

It wasn't the gunfire. Gunfire in vacuum-adjacent environments was strangely muted, suppressed by design — flechettes, mostly, because neither side wanted to puncture the station they were trying to seize. No, it was the voices. The shouting before contact. The specific human register of men and women yelling warnings at strangers they'd been ordered to kill, giving them one last chance, one last courtesy, *Halcyon personnel, drop your weapons, final warning* — and the answering shout back, defiant or frightened or sometimes, horribly, amused — and then the flat clatter of fire and the shouting stopped being words.

The first exchange lasted maybe twenty seconds. It happened at the gantry junction, directly below Edmund's position, close enough that Edmund could see faces through the grating — could see, in the strobing dark, a young Compact marine go down with his helmet cracked, could see him try to rise, could see the medic drag him behind a cargo pallet, could see whether his legs moved.

They moved. Then they didn't.

Edmund's hands were shaking. Edmund noticed this with the same detached precision that noticed everything else — *tremor, bilateral, adrenaline response, expected* — and hated the noticing, because the noticing was a kind of cowardice, a retreat into the language of diagnostics while human beings bled forty meters below.

*Get out,* said some older, wiser part of Edmund. *Crawl away. Go back to your bunk. Let them have the ring.*

But Edmund didn't move. Couldn't. Because beneath the fear was something worse: the understanding that these bodies — these exact bodies, these forty-some people currently shooting at each other in Edmund's home — were the leading edge of the arithmetic. Eight billion humans, halved. Half preserved, half pruned. Edmund had run those numbers in the quiet of the night cycle, had felt the guilt of the accountant, the abstract horror of large digits.

Digits didn't have medics. Digits didn't have a name tape on their chest plate, partially legible even upside down through a vent grate: *OKAFOR, J.*

Abstract halves became bodies. That was the whole lesson, delivered in eleven minutes, and Edmund was learning it pressed flat against cold metal while the war solved for X below.

* * *

The ceasefire attempt came at minute six, and it failed in a way that told Edmund everything about how the next phase would go.

A Meridian lieutenant — young, voice cracking upward on the vowels — keyed an external channel and broadcast into the open bay: "Compact forces, this is Lieutenant Deng, Meridian Third Orbital. This station is civilian infrastructure. We are both in violation of the Anchorage Accords right now. I'm proposing we withdraw to our respective airlocks and settle this at the negotiating table, where it belongs."

Silence. Long enough that Edmund dared hope.

Then a Compact voice, older, flatter: "Meridian knew. Your fleet didn't stumble onto this station. You've had telemetry on the Protocol signature for nine days. You came armed for occupation, not inspection."

"We came armed because *you* were coming—"

"You came for the core," the Compact voice said. "Everyone knows it. Everyone has always known it. So no, Lieutenant. No withdrawal. Not until we hold what we came to hold."

And Deng — and Edmund would think about this afterward, would turn it over during sleepless cycles like a stone with something fossilized inside it — Deng said, quietly, on the open channel: "I'm sorry. I really am. I have orders too."

The second exchange was longer than the first. It ate minutes seven, eight, and nine, and it spread. What had been a skirmish at the gantry junction became a fight for the whole ring — fire teams leapfrogging between cargo containers, a Compact squad trying to flank along the upper catwalk directly beneath Edmund's duct, forcing Edmund to flatten and hold breath as white armor passed close enough to touch through the grating. One of them was crying. Silently, the way trained people cry, jaw clenched, eyes streaming, weapon up, moving. Edmund watched a tear fall from inside a helmet and strike the catwalk grating and vanish, and thought: *there. There is the number. There is what eight billion divided by two looks like when you zoom all the way in.*

Minute ten. Someone — Edmund never learned which side — detonated a breaching charge against the customs partition instead of going around it, and the blast wave traveled up through the station's bones and into the ducts, and Edmund was lifted and slammed against the duct wall hard enough to lose the breath, the orientation, the certainty of which way was down. For three full seconds Edmund hung in the ringing dark, tasting copper, listening to the station groan, feeling through palms and knees the vibration of the only home they'd had for twelve years taking damage meant for other purposes.

Something in the walls had broken. Edmund could feel it — a wrongness in the harmonic, a note missing from the station's constant chord. Later there would be time to diagnose it. Later, if there was a later.

Minute eleven ended the way minute eleven was always going to end: not with victory but with exhaustion. Both sides reached the edge of their ammunition discipline simultaneously, or their nerve, or their orders — and the firing guttered, sputtered, stopped. In the ringing aftermath, the sounds that remained were worse than the guns. Medics calling for stretchers. Someone reciting a name over a channel, slow, formal, the way you do when you're confirming a death for the record. A marine — Meridian, slate-grey, sitting against a container with her helmet off and her hands pressed flat to the deck as if checking for a pulse in the station itself — vomiting, quietly, without shame.

Edmund counted. It was compulsive, irrepressible, the accountant's curse. Through the grate, in slices, in glimpses: bodies still, bodies moving badly, bodies being moved. The medics' tallies bleeding through on intercepted short-band. When the counting finished, the number was forty.

Forty, in eleven minutes.

At that rate — and Edmund's mind did the division before Edmund could stop it, cruel automatic arithmetic — the entire war for the system would take a little over two days to match what the Protocol proposed to do in a single afternoon. Humanity killing itself toward the same total, just slower, and with more steps.

* * *

Edmund stayed in the ducts for two hours after the ceasefire, because leaving meant being seen, and being seen meant becoming a piece on the board. Instead Edmund mapped what the battle had taught.

The Meridians held the customs partition and the eastern airlocks. The Compact held the western gantry and Airlock Seven. Between them lay thirty meters of no-man's-land strewn with cargo containers and the things Edmund refused to look at directly anymore. Neither side advanced. Both sides dug in. And both sides, Edmund noted with a dread that felt like ice water in the spine, had begun running power taps from the station grid — heavy draws, industrial draws, the signature of forces preparing to bring heavier equipment through the airlocks. Boarding craft. Breaching gear. Maybe worse.

Pale Anchor's grid wasn't built for it. Edmund knew the load tolerances better than anyone alive, and the numbers said the station could carry one army's appetite, barely. Two would brown out the life support loops within a day.

Which meant the station itself would soon become a third combatant, in the oldest way infrastructure goes to war: by failing.

Edmund began crawling. Away from the ring, deeper into the ductwork, toward the junction where the maintenance ways met the old freight elevator shafts — and beyond them, down, past the sealed sublevels, past the geometric corridors that predated every colonial record, down to where Amara Okonkwo's people kept their lamps lit and their children quiet.

There was a decision forming, and Edmund knew better than to pretend otherwise. The fleets would not share. Chapter after chapter of intercepted traffic proved it; eleven minutes of blood confirmed it. Which left the core — the Protocol's heart, the chamber where gravity pooled like water — unclaimed, contested, and waiting. Whoever reached it first would own the halves of everyone. Unless someone reached it first who wanted something else entirely.

Edmun didn't know yet what that something else was. But Edmund knew the way down.

At the duct junction, the headlamp finally clicked on, and in its cone Edmund saw the wall panel — an old status strip, scorched by the breach concussion — flickering through its damage routine. And beneath the standard fault codes, in a line of text no maintenance system should have generated, one new entry:

**KEEL ACCESS REQUESTED. SHALL I HOLD THE DOORS?**

The Protocol. Watching. Offering.

Asking.

Edmund stared at the words for a long moment, blood still loud in the ears, forty names hanging unspoken in the recycled air. Then, hands steadier than they had any right to be, Edmund reached out and touched the panel — and answered the only way a technician ever answers.

"Yes. Hold the doors. I'm coming down."
