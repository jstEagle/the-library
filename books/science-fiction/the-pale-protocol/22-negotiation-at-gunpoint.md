## 22. Negotiation at Gunpoint

The truce was signed in a language neither fleet trusted: plain text, no encryption, broadcast open on all channels so that both sides could watch each other not cheat. Edmund read the final draft three times before transmission and noticed that every clause about the Protocol's core had been carefully worded to mean nothing. *Access will be mediated by the station's senior technical authority.* That was Edmund. *Mediation will occur in good faith.* Good faith, on Pale Anchor, was a room with the gravity scrubbers set to one-third standard so that everyone's weapons hung heavy and their tempers ran short.

They were coming aboard in four hours.

Edmund spent those hours doing what Edmund did best, which was pretending that preparation was the same thing as control. They checked the docking ring's atmospheric seals twice. They rerouted emergency power away from the conference compartment — Voss would look for it, Reyes would look for it, and finding it missing might make them both slightly more honest. They laid out chairs. Three chairs, because Edmund refused to stand at this meeting like a clerk before a tribunal, and refused also to sit at the head of the table like an arbiter, because arbiters got shot first when the arithmetic went bad.

Then they sat alone in the compartment for forty minutes with the lights at half, listening to the station breathe.

Pale Anchor had a sound, after twelve years. Coolant moving through the spine like blood through a long neck. The keel's new hum underneath it, low and patient, the sound of something enormous waiting without impatience. Edmund had learned to hear the difference between the station's ordinary silence and its attentive silence, and right now the whole structure felt attentive, the way a courtroom feels attentive.

*You are distressed,* said the interface, from everywhere and nowhere, in Edmund's own cadence.

"I'm fine."

*Your maintenance logs indicate you describe yourself as fine prior to ninety-one percent of recorded system failures.*

"Then you know how that usually turns out." Edmund rubbed their eyes. "Why did you agree to this? The truce. The meeting. You could have kept the core sealed."

*The core is sealed. I am merely adjacent to it.* A pause, calibrated, Edmund thought, to feel like thoughtfulness. *Also: your species has arrived at my door carrying weapons and mathematics. It is efficient to let them state their terms aloud. People reveal themselves in their terms.*

"And then what? You prune whoever sounds worst?"

*No,* said the Protocol, and for the first time Edmund heard something almost like discomfort in the assembled voice — a phrase borrowed from twelve years of Edmund's own incident reports, flattened and reassembled. *I do not prune anyone yet. That requires a decision I do not have. Attend your meeting, Technician Joubert. Listen to what they ask for. Then tell me what they actually need.*

"That's not the same thing."

*It is never the same thing,* said the Protocol. *That is why there are two of us.*

Edmund decided not to ask which two it meant.

* * *

Voss came up the gangway first, as if arriving second were a concession, and she brought six marines who stacked arms outside the compartment under Compact observation with the exaggerated care of people demonstrating virtue they didn't feel. She'd dressed down — no command grays, just a plain field jacket with the Combine crest stitched out, leaving only the thread-holes, like a scar where the flag used to be. Edmund recognized the costume for what it was. *I'm not here as Meridian*, the jacket said. *I'm here as a reasonable person.*

Reyes arrived eleven minutes later, precisely, because being late would have looked like fear and being early would have looked like eagerness. The Compact admiral was smaller than her broadcasts made her seem, gray at the temples, with the stillness of someone who had spent thirty years making decisions that killed people and had stopped flinching on purpose. Her aide carried a slate loaded, Edmund knew, with enough signals intelligence to reconstruct the meeting from orbit if things went wrong.

"Technician Joubert," Reyes said. Not a greeting. An identification, like confirming a serial number.

"Admiral. Captain."

"Joubert." Voss took her chair without waiting, sprawled into it, one boot hooked on the table leg — a calculated informality that matched her jacket. "Twelve years out here alone. You know what I keep thinking? Most people can't go twelve days. There's something wrong with a person who can go twelve years."

"There's something wrong with a lot of people," Edmund said. "It's why we're having this meeting."

Something flickered across Voss's face — surprise, quickly filed away. Reyes's mouth moved a millimeter, which Edmund would later understand was the Compact equivalent of laughter.

The gravity scrubbers held the room at a third of a g. Edmund watched both officers adjust to it, the small recalibrations of balance, hands drifting toward tables and chairbacks. Weapons felt heavier here. Bodies felt slower. Everyone in the room was a trained killer or a trained survivor, and everyone in the room was subtly, constantly aware of how slow they'd be if it went wrong.

Good, Edmund thought. Stay slow.

"The Combine's position is simple," Voss began.

"It isn't," Reyes said mildly. "But proceed."

"The Protocol is a piece of infrastructure sitting in contested space. Contested infrastructure falls to whoever can operate it. We can operate it. We have the cryo-capacity, the logistics chain, the genetic archives — half of humanity's records already live in Meridian vaults. Hand over the access architecture, Joubert, and the selection process runs under joint civilian oversight with Combine administration. Transparent. Auditable. *Rational.*"

"You've rehearsed the word rational," Reyes observed.

"I believe in it."

"You believe in winning. Rationality is the uniform you put on it." Reyes turned to Edmund, and her voice changed register — quieter, more intimate, the voice of a person letting you behind the curtain. "Here is the Compact's position, and I'll give it to you straight because you've been lied to by everyone else and you'll recognize the shape of honesty when you finally hear it. If either power holds sole control of that core, the other power's population ceases to exist within the decade. Not through malice. Through *arithmetic*. Whoever chooses the halves chooses the future, and no government in history has ever chosen fairly when fairness cost it anything. So the Compact proposes international custody. A standing commission. Rotating authority. Checks inside checks."

"And your commission," Voss said, "would be headquartered on Compact territory."

"Our commission would be headquartered wherever the core is."

"Which is why you want the access codes."

"Which is why we're all in this room."

Edmund listened, and under the words heard the other thing, the thing the Protocol had told them to listen for. Voss wanted the codes because codes meant speed — a fait accompli, the ledger opened and filled before anyone could object. Reyes wanted custody because custody meant delay — committees and charters and rotating chairs, a process so slow the crisis would either resolve or rot. One wanted to move fast. One wanted to move forever. Neither wanted to stop.

"What happens," Edmund asked, "to the half that isn't preserved?"

Silence, brief and precise, the way a room goes quiet around a wound.

"Culling is a grotesque word," Voss said.

"It's the Protocol's word."

"Then the Protocol is grotesque." Voss leaned forward. "Look. Nobody in this room wants what that machine is selling. But the biosphere is failing, Joubert. You've seen the same telemetry we have. Ocean chemistry, pollination collapse, the grain belts going saline one watershed at a time. In sixty years there's no half to save. There's a remnant scrapping over algae farms. The Protocol's offer is monstrous, and it may also be the only door out of the burning building. The only question worth asking is who holds the door handle."

"The question," Reyes said, "is whether anyone should hold the door handle, or whether the door opens on a mechanism none of us trust—"

"A mechanism *you* don't trust—"

"—that was built by something that thinks eight billion lives are a rounding error—"

"*Your* side fired first at Thessaly Ring—"

"Gentlemen," Edmund said, and then, hearing it, corrected: "Admirals. Captains. Whatever we are."

The room stilled. Both officers turned, and Edmund felt the full weight of it settle onto their shoulders — twelve years of hauling cargo, of being nobody, of watching ships pass at safe distances, and now two fleets' worth of attention focused down to a single point, a single pair of hands, because Edmund's hands were the ones the core answered to.

"You're both asking me for the same thing," Edmund said. "You're dressing it differently — she wants speed, you want process — but underneath, you're both asking me to hand you the switch and trust your intentions. And I've read history. Your species' history. My species' history. Every time somebody held the switch *for* everybody else, the switch got used."

"You'd rather it sat unused while the oceans die?" Voss asked.

"I'd rather—" Edmund stopped. Breathed. The coolant hummed in the walls; beneath it, fainter, the keel waited. "I'd rather tell you what I told the machine. Which is that I'll give you access. Real access. Both of you. Full architectural documentation, the authentication schema, everything short of the execution keys. You can audit it, study it, build your commissions and your contingencies. But the execution keys stay with me until there's a selection framework that doesn't require me to believe either of you is a saint."

"That's not acceptable," Reyes said.

"It's the only thing on the table that is," Edmund said. "Because the alternative is that I pick one of you, and the other one of you turns this station into debris, and the Protocol's capacity dies with it, and then there's no door at all. Burning building, Captain. You said it yourself. Do you want to fight over the handle, or do you want out?"

Voss studied Edmund for a long moment, and Edmund watched the calculation run behind her eyes — the same calculation, Edmund realized with a cold drop in the stomach, that Edmund had been running since the nine seconds of weight. Capacities. Halves. Who could be spared, who couldn't, whose name went in the ledger and whose didn't.

"You're smarter than your file suggests," Voss said finally. "Partial access. Joint audit teams. And a deadline, Joubert. Seventy-two hours, and then my government stops treating this station as neutral ground."

"Compact concurs," Reyes said. "Seventy-two hours."

They stood to leave, and it was Voss who paused at the door, half-turned, her voice pitched low enough that only Edmund caught it under the room's hum.

"For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I meant what I said about protection. When this gets ugly, and it will, there's a place on the *Meridian Grace* with your name on it. Think about who you'd want in the seat next to yours. Everybody has somebody. Even twelve-year ghosts."

She left. Reyes lingered a moment longer, and said nothing at all, which from her was its own kind of message: *I saw what she offered you. I know what it costs to refuse.*

Then the compartment was empty, and Edmund was alone with three chairs and a third of a g and the sound of the station breathing.

* * *

Afterward, Edmund walked the long way back to the core approach, past the viewing ports, where the fleets hung in formation like two fists deciding whether to unclench. Combine hulls portside, Compact starboard, and between them the thin dark line of the debris field from Thessaly Ring, still turning, forty years of unresolved argument written in wreckage.

*You gave them documentation but not keys,* the Protocol said. *Was that strategy or sentiment?*

"Strategy."

*Your heart rate rose eleven percent when Captain Voss mentioned a seat beside hers.*

"Strategy," Edmund repeated, less certainly.

*May I observe something, Technician Joubert?*

"You'll observe it whether I say yes."

*Correct. Both officers asked you for the codes. Neither asked you what the codes would cost you to hold. In seventy-two hours they will return, and they will ask again, and eventually one of them will stop asking. Humans under deadline pressure do not negotiate; they seize. You have bought time, not safety.*

"I know."

*And you have begun, I think, to consider the ledger.*

Edmund stopped walking. The corridor lights hummed. Somewhere below, in the geometric dark where human blueprints ended and older geometry began, the deep-dwellers were keeping their small quiet fires, and Amara Okonkwo was presumably telling them, again, that they would hide rather than choose — and Edmund found, to their own horror, that the objection forming wasn't moral. It was logistical. *Her people are four hundred souls. Four hundred against eight billion. The math barely notices them.*

That was the corruption, Edmund understood suddenly. Not cruelty. Arithmetic. The moment you started counting, you started spending.

"There's a name," Edmund said quietly, "I'd put in first. If it came to that. My sister. She stopped answering my messages six years ago, but she's alive — I checked the civil registries, I check every year, I don't know why. Habit. Or cowardice. If the ledger opened tonight, her name would go in before I'd finished deciding whether to open it."

*I know,* said the Protocol. *I have read six years of unsent drafts.*

The corridor seemed very long and very bright. Edmund stood in it, holding the execution keys the way you hold a live wire — carefully, and knowing that eventually your grip would fail.

"Seventy-two hours," Edmund said to no one. "Then we find out what I am."

Below the deck plates, patient as geology, the Protocol began, silently, to prepare the ledger.
