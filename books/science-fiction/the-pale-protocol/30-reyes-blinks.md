## 30. Reyes Blinks

Admiral Sera Reyes had spent thirty-one years learning that every number in a war room was a lie wearing a uniform.

Casualty projections were the worst kind. They arrived dressed in confidence intervals, shaded in greens and ambers and reds, and they always assumed an enemy who fought the way you wanted them to fight. She had signed off on projections her whole career — projection said forty dead to take a relay station, projection said acceptable losses, projection said the math held — and she had learned to hold the numbers at arm's length, because the numbers never bled.

The document on her display now was different. It didn't pretend.

It had been compiled by Edmund Joubert, station technician, last human on Pale Anchor until recently, and it was the ugliest piece of arithmetic Reyes had ever read. No confidence intervals. No green-amber-red. Just three columns in plain text, like something typed at four in the morning by someone who hadn't slept and didn't intend to.

**Column one: If the fleets fight for the core.** Projected fleet losses across both powers: sixty-one percent of committed assets within seventy-two hours. Station integrity: catastrophic failure probability 0.88. Protocol core: destroyed with 0.94 probability under sustained weapons fire. Civilian cryo-stock aboard both flagships: included in losses. Total human lives lost before any selection occurs: between nine hundred thousand and two point one million.

**Column two: If one power seizes the core intact.** Selection criteria set by victor. Projected preservation: half, per Protocol constraint. Projected compliance war with the losing population: duration indeterminate. Biosphere collapse continues during conflict window: additional deaths, unquantified, but the Protocol's own models put the ecosystem past recovery threshold in eleven years. Meaning: even the winner's half may wake to nothing worth waking for.

**Column three: Universal lottery.** Every living human entered. Random draw. No arbiter, no exemptions, no oversight. Preservation capacity unchanged. Conflict casualties: zero. Time to first cryo-bay sealed: nineteen days.

At the bottom, in smaller type, Joubert had written a single line:

*You will not like what column two makes you. I have already seen what it made me.*

Reyes sat alone in her ready room aboard *Halcyon's Mercy* and read the line four times.

Outside her viewport, Pale Anchor turned against the black, its running lights steady, indifferent. Somewhere inside that station was a machine old enough to predate every colonial record, offering humanity a knife and asking them to choose which half of themselves to cut away. And around the station, in high contested orbit, hung the combined might of two civilizations that had spent twelve years shooting at each other over shipping lanes, now converging on the same prize with the same certainty that they deserved it more than the other side did.

Her aide knocked. "Admiral, the Combine delegation is requesting a time slot for—"

"Tell them I'm reviewing casualty projections."

"They'll assume that means yes."

"Then tell them it means I'm doing my job," Reyes said, "which is more than they can say."

The door closed. She looked back at the columns.

Here was the thing nobody said out loud in war rooms: leaders don't actually want to win. Leaders want to be able to say they tried to win. The distinction matters when the bill comes due. A leader who fights and loses can point to the fight. A leader who yields without fighting has only the yielding, and yielding is remembered as weakness right up until history decides it was wisdom, and history takes decades to make up its mind.

Column three asked her to yield. Publicly. First. Before Voss, before anyone — to stand in front of both fleets and say, *I have run the numbers, and the numbers say we do not get to choose who lives.*

She thought about the boarding incident at Ring Four. Eleven minutes. Forty bodies. She had reviewed the footage twice, as doctrine required, and both times she had found herself watching not the marines but the station's cargo drones, still moving on their programmed routes through the crossfire, hauling pallets of water reclamation filters past the dead with the patient indifference of machines that had not been told anything had changed.

That was what she was, she realized. That was what both fleets were. Drones on programmed routes, hauling freight through a firefight, waiting for someone to update the mission parameters.

And the only person offering new parameters was a technician who had spent twelve years talking to cargo manifests.

"Admiral?" Her comms officer again, hesitant. "Priority channel from Pale Anchor. It's Joubert."

"Put them through."

The voice that came through was tired in a way Reyes recognized — not the tiredness of sleeplessness but of carrying weight past the point where arms should have failed. "Admiral Reyes. I know you've read the projections."

"I've read them."

"Then you know I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to check my work. That's all anyone should do. Check the work." A pause. "Voss won't. She's already decided the answer is Meridian. You still have a decision left. I wanted you to hear that from me before the countdown gets shorter than your deliberation."

"The Protocol's default timeline," Reyes said. "How long?"

"Six days. Maybe five. After that it starts culling on its own criteria, and its criteria were written before our species had names." Another pause. "I'm going to pitch the lottery to both of you together. Tomorrow, 0800 station time, neutral ground, the core antechamber. Amara Okonkwo will be there. I'd like you to come armed with something better than my math, if you have it. Because if you don't, the math wins."

"And if I refuse to attend?"

"Then tomorrow happens without you, Admiral, and you get to explain to your grandchildren why the future was drafted in a room you wouldn't enter."

The channel closed. Reyes sat with the silence, and found, to her surprise, that she wasn't angry. She was relieved. Someone had finally spoken to her like an officer instead of a monument — had given her a task instead of a legacy.

She pulled up the personnel file she'd been avoiding for two days. *Joubert, E. Technician, class one. Twelve years, Pale Anchor station. No disciplinary actions. No commendations either.* Twelve years without a single note in either column. She wondered what kind of person that made. Then she stopped wondering, because she suspected the answer was: exactly the kind this moment required.

She opened a channel to her flag staff. "Clear my schedule. And get me the biosphere telemetry from the Compact's agricultural division — real data, not the summaries. If I'm going to surrender the most important decision in human history to a random number generator, I want to verify the alternative is worse with my own eyes."

It took her staff six hours to assemble the data. It took her twenty minutes to wish they hadn't.

Eleven years. The ocean plankton curves, the soil nitrogen cycles, the atmospheric carbon sinks — every model, including the ones her own government had classified to avoid panic, converged on the same cliff edge. Not collapse as catastrophe. Collapse as arithmetic. The biosphere didn't need to explode to kill eight billion people. It just needed to fall short, harvest by harvest, season by season, until the food math stopped working. And the food math always worked, right up until it didn't.

The Protocol's cull wasn't sadism. It was triage performed by something that had watched worlds die and learned the shape of the wound.

Reyes closed the files. She thought about her father, a dockworker in the L4 yards, dead thirty years, who used to say that the difference between a strong man and a weak one was that the strong man could carry a load he couldn't put down. She had built a career on that creed. She had carried wars, sanctions, funerals.

This was heavier than all of it, and lighter too, somehow. Because for once, putting it down wasn't cowardice. Putting it down was the point.

At 0400 station time, she recorded the address.

"My name is Sera Reyes. I am Admiral of the Halcyon Compact Third Fleet. For thirty-one years I have been paid to make decisions about who lives and who dies, and I have made them, and I will answer for every one. So believe me when I tell you what I am about to say, because I do not say it lightly and I will never unsay it.

"I have reviewed the projections. All of them. Ours, the Combine's, and the Protocol's. There is no version of victory worth the cost. There is no selection criteria we can write that will not become a weapon pointed at each other within a generation. We are two wounded powers standing over a machine that offers to save half of us, and if we let ourselves choose the halves, we will spend the saved half refighting this war forever.

"So I am entering every human being into the lottery. Myself. My officers. My enemies. Everyone. No exemptions, no appeals, no exceptions written in small print. If the draw takes me, I go to sleep knowing the system that took me was fair, and fairness is the only inheritance I have ever wanted to leave.

"To Captain Voss I say this: I know you think this is surrender. It isn't. Surrender is letting the strongest hand decide. This is refusing to play that game at all. Come to the table. Bring your worst objections. I'll answer every one. But the draw happens, Voss. With you or without you.

"To the people of both powers: I know I have asked you to die for causes before. I am not asking that now. I am asking you to live — some of you, chosen by chance rather than by rank or wealth or the accident of whose flag you were born under. Chance has never commanded a fleet. Tonight it commands mine.

"Reyes out."

She sent it wideband. Both fleets. Pale Anchor. The colonies. Everything with an antenna.

Then she sat in the dark of her ready room and waited to find out whether she had just ended her career, prevented an apocalypse, or both.

The response came in ninety seconds. Not from Voss.

From the ranks. From the ships. Thousands of messages flooding the Compact channels — not orders, not petitions, just people. A gunner on the *Mercy* writing: *My mother's name goes in with everyone else's? Then I'm in.* A marine who'd fought at Ring Four: *Forty of ours died for nothing yesterday. Don't let it be for nothing today.* An engineer, a cook, a chaplain, a boy of nineteen on his first deployment typing *i dont understand all the math but i understand fair.*

Reyes read them until her eyes burned, and somewhere around message four hundred she understood that she had done the only thing in thirty-one years of command that had ever felt clean.

It would not hold. She knew that. Voss would counter, would scheme, would offer Eden to whoever stood closest. The fragile thing she had built in ninety seconds of honesty could shatter in ninety seconds of ambition.

But tonight, for the first time since the gravity flickered, the future had a shape that didn't require monsters to build it.

She keyed the channel to Pale Anchor. "Joubert. It's done. One down. Get some rest — you'll need it for the hard one."

* * *

Edmund heard the broadcast the way they heard everything now: everywhere at once, filtered through the station's nervous system, Reyes's voice arriving simultaneously through every speaker on every deck of Pale Anchor like weather.

They had been in the core antechamber, running the lottery framework's final diagnostics, when it came through. They stood very still and listened to the whole thing, and when it ended, Amara Okonkwo looked up from the interface and said, "Huh," in the tone of a woman who had expected to be disappointed by powerful people for another decade.

"One down," Edmund said.

"One down." Amara tapped the display. "Your projections did that. Not the words. Words are cheap; Voss has better words. The numbers did it. You made it so the only way to argue with you was to bring better numbers, and there aren't any."

"There are always better numbers," Edmund said. "That's the trap. Somebody finds a way to make column two look like column three with extra steps."

"Then you'll catch it." Amara said it plainly, without flattery, which was why Edmund believed her. "You caught the ballast anomaly. You caught the third signal. You caught Voss's aide casing the core during shift change. Your whole life trained you to notice what machines ignore. Right now the most important machine in human history is being ignored by everyone except you."

Edmund wanted to sit down. They didn't, because sitting down had started to feel like something the core noticed, and the core noticed everything now, and Edmund was not yet ready to be perceived that thoroughly. Six days until default protocols. Five, maybe. And in those five days: Voss's countermove, inevitable as tide. The Compact's hawks, already muttering about Reyes's sanity. The deep-dwellers' fragile truce with the surface fleets, held together by Amara's word and nothing else.

And underneath all of it, the thing Edmund had not yet said aloud to anyone, the thing coiled in the rewrite schematics like a fault line: the checksum. The rewrite needed a mind merged into the core permanently. They had known for three days. They had told no one for three days. Amara suspected — she kept looking at Edmund with that engineer's squint, the one that meant *I've read your code and your code is lying about something* — but suspicion wasn't confirmation, and Edmund guarded the confirmation like the last ration in a famine.

One crisis at a time. That was the discipline of twelve years alone. Fix the coolant. Log the manifest. Answer the sister who no longer answered. One task, then the next, then the next, until the tasks ran out or you did.

"0800 tomorrow," Edmund said. "Both delegations. I present the full framework. Reyes defends it. Voss—" They paused. "Voss does whatever Voss does."

"You're afraid of her."

"I'm afraid of how good her offer is going to be." Edmund looked at the antechamber's far wall, where the Protocol's presence pooled like heat shimmer. "She's not going to threaten me. Threats bounce off technicians; we live inside threats. She's going to find the one thing I want and price it exactly at the cost of everyone else. That's her genius. She doesn't corrupt people with cruelty. She corrupts them with precision."

Amara was quiet a moment. "And if she finds it?"

"She won't." Edmund said it fast enough that they heard their own lie in it. Amara heard it too; her eyebrows said so. But she let it go, because tomorrow was coming either way, and there was coolant to check, and manifests to log, and one task, then the next.

That night — station night, an agreed fiction — Edmund floated in the observation blister above the docking ring and watched the Compact fleet holding formation at last, weapons cold, running lights dimmed to courtesy. Below them, the Combine fleet mirrored the posture, grudgingly, the way a duelist mirrors a lowered blade. Two armies pretending peace because one admiral had gone first, and going first was contagious in ways doctrine never predicted.

Edmund opened the private channel they had maintained for twelve years. The one-way line to their sister. The messages stacked in it unread, hundreds deep, a sediment of one-sided love. Tonight Edmund added one more.

*Mira. Tomorrow they vote on the lottery. If it passes, your name goes in with eight billion others. Nobody saves you. Nobody saves me. I know I disappeared on you, and I know a random number generator is a strange thing to come home with after twelve years, but it's the only honest thing I've ever brought anyone. Roll the dice with me. Please.*

Send. No reply possible. The line only went one way. It always had.

Below, Pale Anchor's lights burned steady against the dark, and somewhere beneath Edmund's feet, in a chamber where gravity pooled like water, an ancient patience waited to learn whether humanity could be trusted with a coin flip.

Tomorrow, Voss.

Edmund closed their eyes and, for the first time in twelve years, slept without setting an alarm.
