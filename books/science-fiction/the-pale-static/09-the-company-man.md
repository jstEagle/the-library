## 9. The Company Man

The drone-pod came out of the dark like a seed falling upward.

Wren watched it on the approach display from the moment it cleared the Vantablack's shadow—a slender teardrop of brushed alloy, running cold, no transponder singing anything but the blandest corporate handshake code. It had been launched from a relay buoy eleven light-hours out, which meant someone had known about this rendezvous before the Vantablack had finished hailing the Pale Static. Someone had been waiting for the beacon to wake up the way a man waits by a phone he already knows will ring.

He sat in the pod's single seat with his hands folded in his lap and his face arranged into an expression he had practiced for thirty years: pleasant, unhurried, faintly apologetic, as though he were about to ask to borrow a cup of sugar rather than erase three hundred people from history.

His name was Aurel Wren. His title, if anyone asked—and no one ever asked—was Continuity Liaison, Meridian Remediation Portfolio, Halcyon Consolidated Growth Trust. The title was designed to be forgettable. He had chosen it himself, from a list of forty, and had picked the one that sounded most like a filing error.

Through the small viewport, the Pale Static grew: a freighter gone gray and barnacled with nine years of unregistered solitude, her hull studded with sensor blisters like a face breaking out in cold sweat. She hung in the long shadow of a shattered moonlet, and beyond her, far off, the coordinates where Meridian should have been showed nothing at all. No colony lights. No debris field. No scorch mark where a fleet's guns had written their argument in plasma. Just clean vacuum, swept and tidy as a hotel corridor.

*They do good work*, Wren thought, not for the first time, and felt nothing whatsoever about it.

That was the trick of it. That was the whole profession, if he was honest with himself, and he was honest with himself exactly once a year, on the anniversary of the day he'd signed. You did not need to be a monster to do this work. You needed only to be *tidy*. Monsters left stains. Tidy men left invoices.

* * *

The pod docked with a sound like a held breath being released. Wren gathered his case—leather, real leather, a deliberate anachronism, because people trusted men who carried briefcases the way they trusted dogs who wagged tails—and stepped through the collar into the Pale Static's receiving bay.

The air hit him first. Nine years of closed-loop atmosphere, recycled through filters that had never once been replaced on schedule, and underneath it the sweet chemical ghost of cryo-fluid, the smell of sleeping bodies, the smell—though no one aboard would have phrased it this way—of a graveyard kept at minus one hundred and ninety degrees.

Three people waited for him. He had reviewed their files on the way in, or rather, he had reviewed what remained of their files after the remediation teams had passed through them like locusts through a field.

Captain Adaeze Osei stood at the center, and Wren felt the familiar lurch, the little vertigo he always felt around the rewound. She was perhaps ten years old. Small body, close-cropped hair, a child's smooth forehead. And above that child's face, eyes that had commanded a survey vessel for twenty years, eyes that had buried crewmates and signed off on fuel requisitions and argued with dockmasters, eyes that had watched something burn once and been told ever since that there was nothing there to watch.

To her left, a young woman—nineteen, maybe twenty, tall, with a jaw set like a bulkhead door. Ilsa Reyes-Vance, formerly senior xenolinguist, formerly dead, currently a stranger wearing her own younger face. Her file was the thinnest of the three. Remediation had gotten to her records early; she'd been flagged as primary witness material back when the operation was still called Operation and not yet called Housekeeping.

And to the right, hanging back half a step, a lean man in a maintenance jumpsuit with grease worked so deep into the knuckles it looked like tattooing. Sable Galloway. Officially: never existed. Unofficially, according to the last intact fragment of the Vantablack's manifest, recovered from a shielded backup the scrubbers had missed: navigator, Meridian Colony, presumed deceased, age thirty-four at time of death, cause of death listed as *orbital bombardment, civilian sector seven*, which was the corporate equivalent of writing *we did this* in a language only lawyers could read.

"Welcome aboard," Osei said. Her voice was wrong coming out of that mouth—low, gravelled, a contralto poured into a soprano's throat. "You're a long way from anywhere, Mr.—?"

"Wren. Aurel Wren." He extended his hand. The child-captain took it, and her grip was stronger than her wrist suggested, and her eyes did not blink enough. "Continuity Liaison. I'm afraid I've come about your salvage."

"We haven't declared any salvage," Ilsa said.

"No," Wren agreed pleasantly. "You haven't. That's rather the point."

* * *

They gave him a room because he asked for one, and he asked for one because rooms gave you leverage—you were a guest, guests got listened to, intruders got vented. The room was a converted storage locker with a fold-down bunk and a view of the reactor housing, and Wren found it charming in the way he found all honest things charming: briefly, and without wanting to keep them.

He unpacked the case. Inside, nested in foam: a slate loaded with documentation, a bottle of actual whisky from actual Scotland, distilled before the Trust had bought the distillery and renamed the casks, and a small black box the size of a cigarette pack that he hoped very much not to use.

Then he went to work, because the clock was working too. He could feel it in the ship—the faint subsonic shiver in the deck plates, the reactor breathing faster than reactors should breathe. Seventy hours, give or take, if the beacon's burn profile matched the projections. Less, if Galloway had been tinkering, and men who hid beacons in holds for nine years were always tinkering.

He found Galloway in the corridor outside the hold, doing something unnecessary to a conduit panel, which is what guilty men did instead of standing still.

"Mr. Galloway," Wren said.

The wrench stopped. "I don't know what you're talking about. My name is—" and here the man produced a name so hastily invented it still had the price tag on it, some alias keyed to a dead man's identity chip, and Wren let him finish the whole sentence before responding, because courtesy cost nothing and made people underestimate you.

"Of course," Wren said. "Forgive me. You simply have the look of a navigator. The hands. Navigators hold tools like they're apologizing to them." He smiled. "I wanted to thank you, actually. For maintaining the beacon. Nine years of scheduled diagnostics on unregistered hardware—that's devotion. The Trust values devotion. We build entire careers out of it."

Galloway's face did something complicated and then settled on blankness, which was itself an answer. "I don't know what you think is in that hold."

"I know precisely what's in that hold." Wren kept his voice at conversational volume, pitched under the hum of the recyclers. "Three hundred and twelve neural templates, Meridian-pattern, stored against template drift. One hundred and eighty-seven biological backups in active cryostasis. And one beacon, Halcyon patent 44-C, temporal shear emitter, rated for a single full-spectrum broadcast." He paused, savoring it slightly, the way a man savors reciting a poem he hates. "Plus one unit of contamination. Yourself."

Something moved behind Galloway's eyes—not fear, Wren noted with professional interest, but the thing fear wears as a coat when it has had nine years to get dressed.

"You're mistaken," Galloway said.

"Almost certainly," Wren agreed. "But the Trust pays me to be mistaken in useful directions. Walk with me?"

* * *

They walked. The Pale Static's corridors were narrow and dimmed to night-cycle amber, and everywhere Wren looked he saw the archaeology of a hidden life: ration wrappers tucked behind panels, a hand-drawn calendar scratched into a bulkhead with tally marks going back years, a child's toy—a real child's toy, a wooden bird—sitting on a shelf as though someone had placed it there to prove to themselves that soft things still existed.

"The way I understand events," Wren said, hands clasped behind his back, footsteps unhurried, "is this. Eleven years ago, the survey vessel Vantablack conducted a routine deep-field observation pass of the Meridian system. During that pass, the ship encountered an uncharted spatial anomaly. The anomaly exposed the crew to a temporal shear event. The crew aged backward. Their memories, remarkably, survived. The ship drifted silent for eleven years—power conservation, trauma response, pick your term—and has now returned." He glanced sideways at Galloway. "Is that more or less your understanding as well?"

"It's what happened to them," Galloway said carefully. "Yes."

"And Meridian?"

"There was no Meridian."

Wren stopped walking. He turned, and for the first time since docking he let his face go entirely neutral—not pleasant, not apologetic, just *open*, the way a door opens onto an empty room. It was, he had found, the most frightening expression available to a bureaucrat.

"Mr. Galloway—or whatever your name is—I am going to say a sentence now, and I would like you to listen to it the way you'd listen to weather. Not as a threat. As a forecast." He folded his hands. "Meridian does not exist. It has never existed. There is no colony charter, no settlement license, no shipping manifest, no census entry, no insurance bond, no grave registry. The star it orbited has no planets past the second orbit per every survey ever filed. Three hundred and twelve people were born, lived, loved, quarreled, planted gardens, and died screaming, and the universe—as far as its paperwork is concerned—has no memory of any of it. That is not a gap in the record. That is the record. Do you understand the difference? A gap can be filled. The record simply *is*."

Galloway's breath had gone shallow. "You're telling me you erased them."

"I'm telling you nothing so dramatic." Wren resumed walking, and after a moment Galloway followed, because people always followed; the corridor only went two ways and one of them was toward the airlock. "Erasure implies effort, struggle, a thing erased resisting. What we practice is closer to gardening. You don't fight the weed. You amend the soil so the weed was never suited to grow there in the first place. Records cross-reference records. Surveys cite surveys. Testimony cites testimony. Amend enough soil, and the garden grows the way you want it, and in fifty years even the gardener's grandchildren will believe the garden was always thus."

"And you?" Galloway's voice had roughened. "Do you believe it? Do you believe there was never a colony?"

Wren considered the question honestly, which was a luxury he permitted himself rarely and never in reports.

"I believe," he said, "that on the third of Frostmonth, eleven years ago, I sat in an office on Ceres Station and read a casualty projection with three hundred and twelve names on it, and that I signed a remediation order beneath it, and that my hand did not shake, and that afterward I went to lunch and enjoyed the lunch." He turned a corner without looking where he was going; he had memorized the ship's layout from a stolen schematic. "Whether that makes me a believer or merely a good employee is a question I've learned to stop asking. The lunch was genuinely good. That much I can verify."

* * *

He made his offer in the mess hall, formally, with witnesses, because offers made in corridors could be denied and offers made before a captain entered the log.

He laid the slate on the table between the tea bulbs and the salt tin. On its screen: a contract, clean and short, the best contracts always were.

"The Trust's position is simple," he told them—Osei in her too-small chair, Ilsa with her arms crossed, Galloway at the edge of the light like a man standing at the rim of a well. "The beacon is proprietary hardware deployed without authorization. Its archive contains corrupted data—sensor ghosts, template artifacts, noise misread as signal. The anomaly that affected the Vantablack's crew is under investigation by qualified parties. Your vessel is carrying stolen property in a decaying reactor configuration. We are prepared to resolve all of this generously."

"Generously," Osei repeated. Her child's voice made the word obscene.

"Generously," Wren confirmed. "The beacon is surrendered intact—hardware, archive, templates, everything. In exchange: full remediation of your crew's medical condition. The shear effect is reversible—we have the templates, we have the emitter, we have the physics. Every one of you walks away in your own body, your true age restored, your records immaculate. Pensions reinstated. Service time credited. The eleven lost years reclassified as paid leave." He let that sit. Paid leave. Eleven years of stolen flesh, invoiced as vacation. "And Mr. Galloway receives something extra. A new identity, fully documented, spotless. Or—if he prefers—the old one. Rebuilt. Restored. Sable Galloway, navigator, alive on paper for the first time in nine years. Age corrected. Record complete. A man again, in every database that matters."

The silence that followed had texture. Wren had heard silences like it before—in boardrooms, in the offices of men deciding whether to sign, in the quarters of witnesses deciding whether to remember. Silence was just decision making its bed.

"And the archive?" Ilsa said quietly. "The massacre record. The truth about what happened to Meridian. What happens to that?"

"The archive contains noise," Wren said. "It has always contained noise. Noise is deleted during standard maintenance. This is not concealment; it's hygiene." He smiled at her, warm as a heat lamp. "Ms. Reyes-Vance. I know what you remember. I want you to know that I know, and that knowing changes nothing, and that this is the most liberating piece of information anyone will ever give you. Memory is not evidence. Memory is weather inside a skull. The record is the ground you're standing on, and the record says there was never a colony, and in six months you will either believe that or be very tired of not believing it."

"You were there," Galloway said suddenly. "At the start. You're not just a liaison. You're—" He stopped, recalculating, and Wren watched him arrive at it, watched the arithmetic complete behind his eyes. "You're one of them. One of the rewound. You took the deal."

Wren looked down at his hands. They were a good hands—unlined, supple, the hands of a man of perhaps thirty-five. He had owned them for sixty-one years.

"I was there at the beginning," he said. "Not of Meridian. Of the program. I was the Trust's first field remediation officer, back when we still called things by their real names. I have personally amended the soil of four colonies and two research stations you've never heard of, because you were never meant to hear of them. And yes—I aged backward once myself, in an accident involving our own hardware, and yes, I signed, and yes, they restored me, and it was the easiest signature of my life, and I have never once regretted it, and that fact frightens me more than anything else I could tell you, which is why I only tell it to people who are about to become colleagues." He straightened the slate on the table by a degree, aligning its edge with the table's edge, because tidiness was the only prayer he had left. "I am not your enemy, Captain. I am your future. I'm what it looks like when you say yes."

Osei leaned forward. Ten years old, twenty years of command, and she looked at him the way commanders look at terrain they intend to cross.

"And if we say no?"

Wren sighed, gently, regretfully, a man closing a ledger.

"Then the beacon burns with the reactor, the archive burns with the beacon, the Vantablack's crew dies in a tragic unregistered salvage accident that no record will show anyone was present for, and I go home and enjoy a very good lunch." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and picked up his case. "You have until the reactor decides for you. I'd think quickly. Clocks are the one thing in this universe no one has ever managed to edit."

He was almost to the door when Galloway spoke, low, from the edge of the light.

"The wooden bird," Galloway said. "On the shelf in the corridor. Whose was it?"

Wren paused. He had passed it on his walk. He had noticed it, catalogued it, assigned it no significance, and moved on—the way he moved past everything, the way he had been built to move past everything, amendment after amendment until nothing stuck.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "I didn't ask."

And that, he thought, walking out into the amber-lit corridor, hearing behind him the reactor counting down its patient arithmetic, was the entire difference between himself and everyone on this ship, laid bare in a single question about a toy: they still believed the names mattered.

He checked his watch. Sixty-nine hours, forty minutes.

Somewhere in the hold, three hundred and twelve people slept through their own funeral, and the man who had dug their graves was learning, at last, what kind of man he intended to be.

Wren would need to move soon. But first—the lunch he'd replicated in his quarters was genuinely excellent, and tidiness, as always, could wait an hour.
