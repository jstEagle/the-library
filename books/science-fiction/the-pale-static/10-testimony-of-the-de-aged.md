## 10. Testimony of the De-Aged

Captain Amara Osei had commanded ships for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had never once held a briefing while sitting cross-legged on the deck because her legs were too short to reach the floor from a standard chair.

She did it now because dignity was a resource, and she was rationing hers. The observation lounge of the *Vantablack* had been built for adults—she had helped commission it, back when her hands could span the width of a console—and now every surface mocked her. The table came up to her sternum. The windows, which she remembered as framing a modest slice of stars, rose above her like cathedral glass. She sat on the deck with her eleven surviving crew arranged around her in a ragged crescent, and she felt less like a captain than like a schoolteacher leading story time, except every story ended the same way: fire, and running, and a sky coming apart.

They were children. That was the fact of them. Bodies between eight and twenty-two years old, wearing uniforms taken in at the waist with emergency cord and tape, faces smooth and unlined, hands small enough to disappear inside the maintenance gloves they'd all grown up using. And behind those young eyes sat the whole weight of their lives—the decades of service, the marriages, the losses, the slow accumulation of judgment that made a crew into something more than a payroll.

Osei looked at them and grieved twice over. Once for what they'd lost. Once for what they still had to do.

"Records first," she said. "Then memory. In that order, so nobody can accuse us of letting our feelings write over the data."

Ensign Okonkwo—young Okonkwo, twelve years old now, though he'd been forty-six when the pulse took them—had rigged a display from a salvaged tablet propped against a drinking bulb. He tapped it with a finger too small for the stylus he kept trying to use out of habit, and the archive bloomed across the screen in cold administrative text.

**MERIDIAN STATION — RECORD STATUS: NO SUCH ENTRY.**

Below it, the search log. Below that, the search log before that. Eleven years of queries, each one returning the same elegant nothing. Meridian had never been chartered. Never surveyed. Never settled. No colony registry, no shipping manifests, no casualty reports, no distress calls. The system itself appeared in the charts only as an unlabeled star with no planets worth naming—which was its own kind of lie, because Osei had stood on Meridian's second planet. She had felt real soil under real boots. She had watched the twin suns set over a settlement of four hundred souls, and one of those sunsets was the last thing she remembered before the white silence took her.

"No such entry," murmured Dr. Vashti, who at nineteen was the oldest-bodied among them and therefore, by some unspoken consensus, the one allowed to say bitter things aloud. "We were never born, apparently. Or we were never anywhere. I have a daughter. I have a granddaughter. Neither of them exists."

"They exist," said Okonkwo quietly. "The record doesn't. Those aren't the same thing anymore. We know that better than anyone."

Silence took the room. Outside the glass, the *Pale Static* hung against the dark like a splinter of older metal, its running lights dim, its hull scarred by nine years of solitary freight runs. Somewhere aboard that ship was a man named Sable Galloway who did not exist either—not in any archive, not in any manifest, not in the Vantablack's crew database where his name should have sat beside hers in the navigator's roster. Osei had checked. She had checked three times, then a fourth time drunk on recycled water and despair, and each time the database had looked back at her with the same blank courtesy: **NO SUCH ENTRY.**

She was beginning to understand that the blankness was not an absence. It was a presence. Something had reached into the universe's memory and performed surgery, and the scars were these clean little voids, these polite erasures, scattered wherever the truth had grown too inconvenient.

"All right," she said. "Memory. One at a time. Don't build on each other's accounts yet—I want the fragments raw, before they contaminate each other. Start anywhere. Start with a smell if you have to."

* * *

Dr. Vashti went first, because Vashti always went first; authority clung to her even in a teenager's body, even with acne starting along her jaw that she regarded with the detached fury of a woman reviewing an act of vandalism.

"I remember the morning of it," she said. "I remember the smell of the hydroponics bay—basil, always basil, Meridian grew too much basil and we teased the ag-techs about it for years. I remember the alert tone. Not the standard one. A corporate priority channel, encrypted, the kind you're not supposed to hear on a civilian station. And I remember—" Her voice snagged. The nineteen-year-old throat wasn't built for grief this old, and it cracked in a way her sixty-year-old throat never would have permitted. "I remember looking up through the dome and seeing ships where ships shouldn't be. Fleet hulls. Registry markings I recognized because I'd audited their supply contracts. They weren't there to inspect us."

"Take your time," Osei said, and hated how gentle her own voice sounded in a child's register.

"There isn't time to take. There never was." Vashti wiped her face with a sleeve two sizes too big. "The first volley hit the reactor ring. I remember the light going orange. I remember thinking, absurdly, about the basil."

Okonkwo next. His twelve-year-old voice piped high and clear, and the dissonance between it and what came out of it made Osei's skin crawl.

"I was on the comm relay. I got maybe ninety seconds of outbound transmission before they jammed us. Ninety seconds. I spent them wrong—I panicked, I burned forty of them trying to raise the colonial authority, which is exactly who wasn't coming. But in the last few seconds I broadcast everything in the local buffer. Sensor logs. Station schematics. The passenger and crew manifest." He looked up. "If any fragment of that transmission survived, somewhere, on any receiver pointed the right way at the right moment—then the record exists. It has to exist. Data doesn't die just because someone deletes the index."

"That," Osei said slowly, "is the single most useful thing anyone has said in nine years."

One by one, the others spoke. The engineer, Adeyemi, in a body of perhaps fifteen, remembered the sound—described it not as an explosion but as a *change in the station's breathing*, the pressure song of the life support shifting key, and how every adult on Meridian instinctively knew what that shift meant. The pilot, Reyes, aged down to ten, remembered holding a stranger's child in the evacuation corridor and the specific weight of her, the way the girl had stopped crying all at once, which Reyes had understood even then was worse than crying. The youngest survivor, Toma, eight years old now, who had been the station's cartographer and fifty-eight years old, spoke barely above a whisper and remembered only colors: the orange of the first strike, the blue-white of the secondary detonations, the gray of ash falling upward through breached decks because up was the only direction left that meant anything.

Eleven accounts. Fragmentary, overlapping, contradictory in trivia and unanimous in substance. Every one of them remembered Meridian burning. Every one of them remembered fleet hulls in the sky. Every one of them remembered that they had been real, that four hundred people had lived and worked and grown basil and argued about water rations on a station that, according to every archive in the reach, had never existed.

Osei let the silence settle after the last testimony, and in that silence she felt the vertigo open beneath her again—the same vertigo that had been waiting for her ever since the *Pale Static* answered their hail. Because here was the question she had been circling for days, the question that gnawed at three o'clock in every sleep cycle: *what if the records are right?*

What if the pulse that de-aged them had also corrupted them? What if their memories of Meridian were confabulation—elaborate, mutually reinforcing, seeded by shared trauma or by whatever process had rolled their bodies backward down its own timeline? What if she was a woman who had served on a ship that patrolled empty space, whose mind had constructed a massacre out of noise and fear, and eleven other minds had caught the construction like a contagion?

The archives said: *never happened.*

Her crew said: *we watched it happen.*

And between those two statements stood Captain Amara Osei, eight years old in the knees and sixty-one in the soul, responsible for making a call.

* * *

She called a recess she didn't need, just to walk. Her quarters were too far to be worth the trip—everything was too far now, doors and corridors scaled for the body she used to have—so she walked the ring corridor instead, one hand trailing the wall, feeling the vibration of the drive through the plating. Familiar vibration. She had known this ship's hum the way she'd once known a lover's heartbeat. The ship hadn't changed. Only her reach had.

Wren's drone-pod sat docked at the aft collar, and she gave it a wide berth without deciding to. The corporate liaison had arrived two days ago with his immaculate suit and his laminated calm, repeating his litany like a man saying grace: *Meridian was never settled. Your records are corrupted. Surrender the beacon intact and the Company will see you restored.* Restored. As if restoration were a thing the Company dispensed, rather than a thing it had stolen in the first place. As if the price of getting your years back was agreeing to remain a lie.

She passed the galley and heard laughter—actual laughter—and stopped. Inside, three of her crew, bodies ranging from ten to sixteen, were playing cards with a deck missing most of its face cards, substituting bottle caps. Reyes was losing spectacularly and accusing everyone else of cheating with the full rhetorical arsenal of a fifty-year-old chief pilot deployed through a child's larynx, and it was funny, it was genuinely funny, and for a moment the galley sounded like any ship Osei had ever served on, any port leave, any long haul between stars where the crew made its own weather.

That was when the decision finished making itself. Not through argument. Through bottle caps and bad card games and the sound of people who had been through hell together choosing, in the smallest available ways, to keep being a crew.

You could corrupt a memory. She believed that now; she had seen evidence of corruption with her own eyes, in mirrors, in the impossible arithmetic of her own reflection. Memory was tissue, and tissue could be edited.

But you could not corrupt *this*. Not loyalty forged across decades. Not the way Adeyemi automatically covered Toma's watch shifts because Toma's young body needed sleep the old mind resented needing. Not the way they moved around each other in narrow corridors, a choreography learned over years, still executing perfectly in bodies that had never rehearsed it. The records could erase a station. They could not erase the way Vashti put herself bodily between Wren's drones and Okonkwo during the last inspection, without thought, without hesitation—an instinct with thirty years of trust packed behind it like a charge.

Archives were written by whoever survived the writing of them. Crews were written by the living of it.

She turned and walked back to the observation lounge, and her crew assembled around her on the deck, small and patient and watching her with old eyes.

"Here is my finding," she said formally, because formality was another kind of rationed dignity, and she intended to spend it all. "The archives state that Meridian never existed. I find that statement to be false. I find it false not because your memories are perfect—they are not, none of ours are—but because there are eleven of you, and your fragments interlock. Vashti saw hulls; Okonkwo jammed a distress call at hulls; Adeyemi heard the reactor ring fail at the hour Vashti saw the first strike. Lies don't interlock. Fabrications drift apart under examination. Yours converge. That convergence is not something a temporal pulse could manufacture, and I'll tell you why: because I've read the engineering assessments of what that pulse does, and it edits tissue. It does not edit *meaning*. It cannot make eleven people invent the same basil."

A shaky breath went around the crescent. Toma, eight years old and ancient, smiled for the first time since boarding.

"So," Osei continued, "the official record of this vessel, effective immediately, logs Meridian Station as a confirmed settlement, destroyed by corporate fleet action, with approximately four hundred casualties, names pending recovery. Let them call me corrupted. Let them pull my certification. I am the captain of this ship, and this ship remembers."

Okonkwo raised his hand like a schoolboy, then seemed embarrassed by the gesture, then committed to it anyway. "Captain. If we log it—if we transmit anything, even a position report referencing Meridian—we're broadcasting a contradiction of the Company line. Wren will hear it. Whatever's behind Wren will hear it."

"I'm counting on it," Osei said. "A lie this well-maintained hates daylight. Our job is to be daylight."

She dismissed them, and they filed out—small feet, heavy tread, the strange double music of them—and she remained alone in the lounge with the stars and the distant splinter of the *Pale Static*, and she let herself feel, for exactly one minute by the chronometer, the full terror of what she had just done. Then the minute ended, because captains' minutes always end, and she got back to work.

It was Okonkwo who brought her the thread to pull. He came to her cabin an hour later, moving carefully in a body he still navigated like borrowed equipment, carrying the salvaged tablet against his chest like a hymnal.

"The ninety seconds," he said. "My transmission. I've been running propagation models on where the signal cone swept, given Meridian's position and the jamming window. Most of it dissipated into deep space. But the cone edge clipped one volume." He turned the tablet toward her. A star chart, sparse and functional, with a single highlighted track. "A commercial lane. Thin traffic. But not zero traffic."

Osei studied the track, and her stomach dropped with a sensation she hadn't felt since her first command—a falling feeling, the chart rearranging itself around a new truth.

The lane's thin traffic included, once every eleven months, a lone unregistered freighter running a route no scheduler would admit to. A freighter whose navigator had, nine years ago, begun maintaining an unregistered beacon in its hold.

A freighter that had been sitting in that cone's path the night Meridian burned—and had been close enough, positioned precisely enough, to catch the edge of a dying station's last transmission.

"Sable Galloway," Osei said slowly, tasting a name that officially did not exist. "He may be carrying more than a beacon."

"He may be carrying my ninety seconds," Okonkwo said. "Captain—we need to get back aboard that ship. Tonight."
