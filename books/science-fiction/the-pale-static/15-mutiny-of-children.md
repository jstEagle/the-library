## 15. Mutiny of Children

Captain Adaeze Osei had commanded the *Vantablack* for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once had to look up to meet a crew member's eyes.

Now she did it constantly. Her first officer stood at her shoulder in the corridor outside engineering, and he came up only to her elbow. His hands—hands that had threaded a survey drone through the ice canyons of Kessel-9 in a whiteout, hands that had held hers at a funeral on Meridian, though no record of any funeral existed anymore—those hands were small and soft as a boy's, knuckles still round with childhood. He was thirty-eight years old inside. The body was eleven.

They all were. That was the arithmetic of the *Vantablack*'s return: eleven years gone, eleven years unwound, every soul aboard restored to some earlier draft of themselves while their memories stayed stubbornly current. Osei herself had come back as a woman of twenty-six, which meant that somewhere in the beacon's stored templates was a version of her that predated her captaincy, and the machine had simply reached into her cells and set the clock back without asking.

She had made peace with it, mostly. You could make peace with almost anything if you treated it as procedure. De-aging was an engineering problem. Memory integrity was a medical one. The erasure of Meridian from every archive in the fleet—that was a legal matter, being handled by people whose names she didn't know, and she had learned not to ask.

What she had not made peace with was Wren.

The corporate liaison had arrived three days ago in a drone-pod that docked against the *Pale Static*'s hull like a tick finding skin. He was a neat man, gray at the temples, unhurried, with the particular calm of someone who has never once been surprised by bad news because he signed off on it in advance. He wanted the beacon surrendered intact. He said Meridian had never existed. He said it the way you'd say *the sky is blue*, and when Osei showed him the fragmentary memories of her crew—the burning orbital frames, the evacuation klaxons, the specific taste of recycled air on a colony that officially had never drawn breath—he had looked at her with something close to pity.

"Memory is not evidence, Captain," he'd said. "It's testimony. And testimony can be manufactured."

"Then manufacture me a reason my chief engineer remembers the serial number of a reactor that was never built."

He had smiled at that, faintly, and changed the subject, and Osei had understood then that they were past the stage where words would settle anything.

Now, standing in the corridor outside the *Pale Static*'s engineering deck, she watched Wren's security detail assemble in the docking bay below through the feed from a camera her de-aged comms officer had hidden in a cargo net three days ago. Six figures in matte-black exo-harnesses. Corporate markings stripped or painted over. They moved with the economy of professionals, checking weapons in silence, and beside them stood Wren himself, consulting a slate, his coat immaculate, his expression that of a man reviewing a delivery schedule.

"They're going for the beacon," said Ensign Okonkwo at her side. Eleven years old now, voice still carrying its adult cadence, flat and precise. "Bay Four. Direct route through the spine corridor. They'll breach the hold in—" he checked the clock drift between feeds, a habit drilled into him across two decades of service compressed into a body that hadn't finished growing—"four minutes, forty seconds."

Osei looked down the length of the corridor. It was narrow, lit in emergency amber because Sable Galloway had cut main power to everything but the beacon's systems, funneling every joule toward the transmission array. The air tasted of ozone and cold metal. Somewhere below them, seventy-two hours had become fewer than twelve; she could feel it in the way the deck hummed, a rising note under the soles of her boots, the reactor singing itself toward destruction.

Her crew waited along the walls. Nine of them. Nine adults wearing children.

There was Vasquez, who had been her pilot for six years and was now perhaps fourteen, sitting cross-legged on the deck plating with a cutting torch across her knees like a toy she'd been told not to play with. There was Halloway, her medic, who had returned to the *Vantablack* as a young man of seventeen and who had spent the intervening days quietly relearning the reach of his own arms, recalibrating every gesture, because muscle memory lived in the body and his body had forgotten. There was Chen, nine years old, who had been her quartermaster for longer than this child had been alive, and who was currently distributing improvised stun charges from a canvas bag with the solemnity of an altar server.

And there was Ilsa Voss, nineteen again, standing closest to the junction, a pry bar in one hand and an expression Osei recognized from a decade of shared disasters: the look of a woman doing arithmetic she already knew the answer to and hated.

"Captain." Ilsa's voice was low. "Wren's people are armed with live rounds. We have torches, pry bars, and whatever Chen stuffed in that bag. If we do this, we do it knowing what it is."

"I know what it is," Osei said.

She had spent three days telling herself there was another way. Petition the tribunal. Broadcast from the *Vantablack*'s own array. Wait for the fleet's answer to Sable's transmission, which even now was propagating outward at lightspeed, the massacre record landing in ten thousand receivers, undeniable, unrecallable. The truth was out. She had heard it confirmed twice: relay stations at Thessaly and Cold Harbor had acknowledged receipt, and their acknowledgments had not been jammed, because by the time Wren's superiors understood what was happening, the record already existed in too many places to burn.

So why fight? Why risk her crew—her *children*, her ancient, diminished, magnificent crew—for a machine that had already done its work?

Because the beacon wasn't finished. Because buried in its architecture was a restoration pulse, a final function that could bring back the sleeping backups in Bay Four—three hundred minds saved from Meridian's burning, waiting nine years in cold storage—and restore her own crew to their true ages, and pay for all of it with the life of the man who had built it. And Wren knew about the pulse. That was why he was here with soldiers instead of lawyers. The record was out, but the *resurrection* was still possible, and a company that could erase a massacre could certainly erase a resurrection. Kill the beacon before it fired, and the backups stayed dead forever, and the de-aged crew stayed children forever, and eventually everyone who remembered would age out of their testimony or be edited out of the archives, and Meridian would go back to never having existed, cleanly, permanently, with paperwork.

Osei had read enough history to know how it ended when good people waited for permission.

"Positions," she said.

* * *

They fought the way children fight, which is to say: badly, ferociously, and with a creativity that no training manual anticipates.

Wren's team came up the spine corridor in a wedge, exo-harnesses making each of them half again a grown man's mass, boots striking the deck in a rhythm designed to intimidate. Osei heard them coming before she saw them—the deliberate thunder of it, psychological warfare, letting the defenders hear death approach—and she crouched behind a coolant manifold with Halloway at her shoulder and thought, absurdly, of the day she'd commissioned the *Vantablack*, of the speech she'd given about the dignity of exploration, of how far she had traveled from that woman and yet how completely she was still her.

The wedge reached the junction. The overhead lights—what was left of them—died all at once, because Vasquez had spent the previous hour cutting the feeder line exactly where the schematic said it would be, and the corridor went black except for the amber glow bleeding up from engineering below.

In the dark, the wedge stopped being a wedge. Exo-harnesses are magnificent machines in open ground and coffins in a narrow corridor with no light, and Osei's crew knew every meter of this ship in a way no boarding party could, because they had spent three days learning it and nine years learning ships like it, and because children fit places soldiers don't. Chen went up the wall on handholds no adult would have bothered mapping and dropped a stun charge directly into the harness collar of the point man, and the man folded like a marionette with cut strings. Vasquez hit the second from behind with the cutting torch—not the flame, just the mass, a fourteen-year-old swinging four kilos of industrial tool with a lifetime of load-lifting technique her body could no longer honor, and Osei heard the girl make a small sound of protest as her shoulder reminded her what she'd lost—and the soldier staggered, and Halloway took his legs out from under him with a length of cable, and it was working, God help them, it was actually working—

Then the third soldier stopped trying to advance and started shooting.

The first rounds went high, suppression fire, sparks screaming off the ceiling. The second burst went low.

Osei saw it happen the way she would see it for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be, in whichever body. She saw Chen—her quartermaster, her steady hand, the man who had kept the *Vantablack* fed and supplied through two supply-chain collapses and who was now a small figure in an oversized jacket—step out of cover to drag Vasquez clear of the firing line, because that was the job, that had always been the job, keep the crew supplied, keep the crew moving, and the rounds took him across the chest and throat, and the small body came apart with a terrible ease, the ease of a body that had never been tested, never scarred, never hardened, a body nine years old taking bullets meant for a man of fifty.

The firing stopped. Everything stopped.

In the ringing dark, Osei crawled to him. She knew before she reached him. Everyone knew. Halloway was already there, hands pressed to wounds that his adult knowledge understood perfectly and his adolescent strength could do nothing about, and Chen looked up at her with eyes that were brown and clear and fifty-three years old, and he tried to speak, and blood came instead, and then he managed it anyway, one word, wet and small:

"*Inventory's*... short."

And then he was gone, and the corridor was full of the sound of children crying with old voices, which is a sound Osei would not wish on anything living.

* * *

They took engineering.

She could not afterward reconstruct the whole of it—grief does not file reports—but the shape of it was this: Wren's remaining soldiers reached Bay Four's threshold and found it welded shut from the inside, Vasquez having cut and sealed the inner hatch during the fighting, and found the secondary access flooded with vented coolant, Halloway's work, nonlethal but absolute, and found themselves pinned in a kill-box of darkness by defenders who no longer cared about survival because the worst thing that could happen to them had already happened, ten minutes ago, in a corridor, to a nine-year-old with gray hair's worth of memories.

Wren called for withdrawal at 0417. Osei heard it over the captured channel, his voice still level, still reasonable, adjusting, always adjusting, a man whose calm was not courage but inventory—assets deployed, assets lost, assets recoverable. He withdrew his people to the drone-pod and sealed it, and the *Pale Static*'s hull rang once with the pod's docking clamps releasing, and then he was gone, and Osei stood in the amber light of engineering among her surviving crew and felt nothing that resembled victory.

Chen's body lay in the corridor where they had carried him, covered with a thermal blanket because Halloway had insisted, because procedures mattered, because the dead deserved the dignity of protocol even when the universe had stopped extending any. Around him stood eight people who looked like they belonged in a schoolyard and stood like they belonged at a funeral, and Osei counted them twice, an old habit, accounting for her people, and both times the count came back wrong, would always come back wrong, one short, forever one short.

Vasquez sat against the bulkhead with her dislocated shoulder cradled, refusing reduction until the captain was seen to, insubordinate and correct. Ilsa Voss stood over Chen's body for a long time without speaking. When she finally turned, her face was wet and her voice was not.

"He died doing inventory," she said. "Fifty-three years of service and the last thing he thought about was the count."

"The count matters," Osei said.

"Yes," Ilsa said. "That's what I'm saying. That's the whole *point*." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, a child's gesture, furious at her own body for making it. "They erased three hundred thousand people and called it bookkeeping. We just lost one more because they're still doing the same bookkeeping. So we finish the count. All of it. Every name."

Osei looked at her crew. At Halloway, seventeen, holding a med kit he no longer had the fine motor control to fully use, weeping without noise. At Okonkwo, eleven, already running casualty analysis on a slate balanced on his knees because grief and duty had never learned to take turns in him. At the others, small and silent and unbroken.

Once, she would have given the speech. Once, she would have talked about duty and the long road and the meaning of sacrifice. But these people had crossed eleven years backward and buried their friend in a child's grave, and speeches would have been an insult.

"We hold the beacon," she said simply. "We hold it until it fires. Whatever it costs."

"And after?" asked Okonkwo, not looking up from his slate. "After the pulse. After the backups wake. After we're ourselves again—if we're ourselves again. What then?"

Osei looked down the corridor, toward Bay Four, where three hundred sleepers waited in the cold, and beyond them, out the viewport, toward the black where Wren's pod had vanished and where, sooner or later, something worse than Wren would come looking for what he'd failed to take.

"Then," she said, "we testify. All of us. In whatever bodies we've got."

The reactor's rising note climbed a half-tone beneath her feet, and somewhere below, a man named Sable Galloway was preparing to spend his life buying back theirs, and the clock on the engineering board read nine hours and change, and Osei—twenty-six years old, eleven years a captain, older than her own reflection—went to tell the dead man's backup that his count was about to be settled.
