## 16. Twelve Hours

The reactor's voice had changed.

Sable had lived with the Pale Static's heartbeat for nine years—long enough to know its moods the way a sailor knows weather by the ache in old bones. The steady thrum beneath the deckplates was gone now, replaced by something higher, thinner, a whine that sat just below the threshold of hearing and made his teeth itch. He stood in the reactor gallery with his palm flat against the coolant housing and felt it: not heat exactly, but *urgency*, the machine leaning into its own destruction like a runner committing to a fall.

Sixty-nine hours left. The countdown had eaten twelve of its seventy-two while he'd slept badly in the navigator's chair, dreaming of Meridian's sky full of fire.

He should have been afraid. Instead he felt something worse: clarity.

* * *

The beacon's schematics were spread across three screens in the hold, projected from the archive core he'd spent nearly a decade pretending wasn't there. He'd built this room into a shrine without meaning to—the cold-storage racks along the port wall with their three hundred sleeping backups, the workbench where he maintained the transmitter, the cot in the corner where he slept within earshot of the dead as if proximity were penance. Now the shrine had become an operating theater.

The countdown was simple, brutal engineering. The beacon had been designed for one full-power broadcast—a pulse that would shear through every receiver in range, carrying whatever archive it held. One pulse, then burnout. The reactor cycle that fed it couldn't be reversed once initiated; the fuel geometry made sure of that. Whoever built it had wanted a message that could never be unsent.

But Sable had spent last night reading deeper than the operational layer, down into the design philosophy, the engineer's marginalia, the abandoned branches of the code. And at 0400 ship-time, hands shaking around a cup of recycled coffee gone bitter, he'd found it.

A third option.

Not transmit *or* abort. Not the impossible arithmetic Ilsa had named two days ago, when they'd stood in this hold and she'd looked at him like he was both the wound and the bandage. There was another branch buried under fourteen layers of deprecated functions, commented out but never deleted, and when he'd compiled it in simulation the results had made him sit down on the deck because his legs stopped being interested in holding him up.

A restoration pulse. Full power, full spectrum—not a broadcast outward but a bloom inward, a temporal shear field shaped to wrap the cold-storage racks instead of the antenna array. Every backup in Bay Four, restored. Not copied into new bodies somewhere, not resurrected as data ghosts on a screen. *Restored.* Their stored neural templates used as anchors to rewind their bodies back along their own timelines—to pull them out of storage and set them on their feet, breathing, whole, aged exactly as they'd been when the first pulse took them.

Three hundred people. Alive again.

And the cost, written in the same plain engineer's hand as everything else: *Restoration requires a living template anchor. Anchor must be biologically compatible with all stored templates. Anchor will be consumed in the process.*

Consumed. He kept turning the word over, looking for wiggle room in it, and finding none. The anchor's own timeline would be spent to fund theirs—decades of biological age drawn out of him in a single pulse, poured into three hundred empty vessels until nothing remained. The simulation's projection of the anchor's post-pulse state was a flat line. Not death, exactly. Worse than death, maybe. A body emptied of its future, aged past the end of itself, a candle burned from both ends until the middle went too.

He read the line forty times. It didn't get softer.

*Biologically compatible with all stored templates.* Of course it meant him. He'd designed the compatibility profile himself, nine years ago, in the frantic hours before Meridian burned—he'd keyed the templates to his own genome because he'd been the only navigator on-site, because the system needed a reference point and he'd never imagined he'd be the only one left standing near it. His own caution, his own thoroughness, had built the lock he was now the only key for.

There was no one else. That was the thing he kept circling back to, the thing that felt less like tragedy than like design—as if some version of him, nine years younger, had already known how this would end and had arranged the room accordingly.

* * *

He worked through the morning in the particular silence of a man who has stopped lying to himself and hasn't yet decided what to do about it. The reactor's whine climbed a half-tone. Sixty-six hours. He ran the restoration simulation again, then again with variations, hunting for the flaw that would let him off the hook—the rounding error, the thermal limit, anything. The simulation was clean. Three hundred restorations, ninety-eight percent template fidelity, anchor consumption total.

Ninety-eight percent. Somewhere in his head, a voice that sounded like Ilsa said: *and the other two percent?* And he said back: *better than zero, which is what they have now.* And the voice said: *that's not why you're doing it,* and he didn't answer, because the voice was right.

Because here was the truth he'd been carrying since the night he fled Meridian, the truth he'd only ever admitted in the dark hours between watches: part of him had wanted to die there. Had stood in the beacon chamber with the fleet's torpedoes blooming against the colony shield and thought, with a calm that still frightened him to remember, *this is where I stop.* He hadn't done it. He'd triggered the pulse instead, saved the three hundred minds into backup, and run—and the running had been its own kind of dying, nine years of it, a slow consumption that asked less of him all at once and more of him every day.

Now the universe was offering him the exit he'd refused, dressed up as heroism. And the most sickening part—the part he couldn't say aloud, might never say aloud—was the relief. Underneath the fear, underneath the grief, some sealed compartment of him had opened at 0400 and breathed out: *finally. Finally it costs everything, so finally it counts.*

He knew what Ilsa would say to that. She'd already said versions of it, in the hold, in the corridor outside the galley, her nineteen-year-old face twisted with an anger too old for it: *You don't get to disappear. Disappearing is the one thing you're good at.* She'd said it like a diagnosis. He'd carried it around since like a stone in his boot.

But knowing she'd object and agreeing with her were different countries, and right now he was standing alone in the first one.

* * *

At 1300, Captain Osei came down to the hold.

She came alone, which he understood was deliberate—she'd learned in nine days of being eleven years old again that a delegation of children put people on edge, even children who moved like veterans and spoke like judges. She stood in the hatchway in a jumpsuit rolled three times at the cuffs and ankles, and she looked at the schematic screens for a long moment before she looked at him.

"You've found something," she said. "You've had the face for two days. The face you make when you've done the math and don't like the answer."

"I'm still checking the math."

"Captain Galloway." She used the rank deliberately; she'd started doing it after the mutiny, after Wren, after the child's body they'd buried in the torpedo bay with full honors because Ensign Okonkwo deserved full honors however small the coffin. "I commanded a survey vessel for six years before someone rewound me into this. I know what a man looks like when he's decided something and is waiting for permission he'll never ask for."

Sable turned off the third screen. The restoration code folded away into darkness.

"It doesn't matter," he said. "It's not viable."

"That's a lie," Osei said, without heat. "It's also beside the point. I didn't come down here for the math." She crossed the deck, small boots ringing on the plating, and stopped in front of the cold-storage racks—three hundred drawers of frosted glass, each labeled with a name in his own handwriting. She read them the way she must once have read casualty lists: top to bottom, unhurried, letting each name land.

"My daughter is in drawer sixty-one," she said.

Sable hadn't known that. Nine years of maintaining these racks and he'd never opened the manifest far enough to learn who was related to whom. It seemed suddenly like the single largest failure of his long cowardice—that he'd guarded these people like cargo and never once learned them like family.

"She was seven," Osei went on. "Amara. She liked to ride the tram cars on the ring road and press her face to the glass. If your machine can bring her back—" She stopped. Recalibrated. When she spoke again her voice was level in the way he'd learned to recognize as expensive. "Then I want you to tell me what it costs. All of it. Because I have watched men spend other people to buy clean consciences, and I have watched this crew bury one of our own in a box built for a tool, and I am done being lied to by adults."

"The anchor dies," Sable said. "The anchor is me. The templates are keyed to my biology—I did that myself, nine years ago. The pulse draws the restoration out of my timeline. All of it."

Osei was quiet for a while. In the silence the reactor's whine came faintly through the hull, and the refrigeration units hummed their patient chord, and somewhere above them the Pale Static creaked as it always did, like an old man shifting in sleep.

"And if you don't," she said finally, "the reactor blows in sixty-five hours, and the backups die with the ship, and the de-aged crew of the Vantablack stays de-aged, and Wren's employers win by default. Is that the shape of it?"

"That's the shape of it."

"Then we should tell Ilsa."

"No." The word came out faster and harder than he intended, and Osei's eyebrows went up—not surprised, he realized, but *noting*, filing the reaction where she filed everything. He made himself unclench his jaw. "Captain. If you tell her, she'll find a way to stop it. She'll tear the code apart looking for an alternative that doesn't exist, or she'll stand in front of the console, or—" He heard his own voice fraying and hated it. "She spent nine years thinking I died at Meridian. I'm not going to make her watch me die twice."

"You spent nine years watching *her* die every day," Osei said quietly. "In those drawers. You think she doesn't know what that costs? You think a woman who loved you can't do that arithmetic?" She stepped closer, and up close her eyes were wrong for her face in a way he'd never gotten used to—forty years of command behind a child's lashes. "Sable Galloway. I am going to tell you something I told my officers for six years, and I am going to tell it to you as a superior officer, because right now you are not behaving like one. A commander does not decide his own sacrifice in secret. A commander who dies in secret teaches his people that they were never trusted. Whatever you choose, you will choose it in front of us, or it will be worth nothing—not to the dead, and not to the living."

She turned and walked to the hatch. At the threshold she paused, one small hand on the frame.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "drawer sixty-one. Amara. If the price is you, I'd pay it. I want you to carry that with you, because I suspect you'll try very hard not to." And she was gone, her footsteps fading up the companionway, leaving him alone with three hundred names and a machine that wanted his life.

* * *

He didn't decide that afternoon. He ran simulations. He ate half a ration pack and forgot the other half. He stood in front of drawer sixty-one for a long time, reading a child's name in his own handwriting, trying to feel what Osei clearly felt and finding instead only the old familiar static—the guilt that had been his co-pilot for nine years, whispering that any ending was better than continuing to be who he'd been.

That was the danger. He could see it clearly now, the way you see a current only when you're already in it. The restoration pulse wasn't a plan. It was a permission slip. Every hour he spent with the code open, the sealed compartment breathed a little easier, and the man who'd fought to survive Meridian—the man who'd stolen a freighter and faked his death and endured nine years of cold-storage vigils rather than stop existing—got quieter and quieter, and the tired ghost in his place got louder: *let it end here, let it mean something, let it be over.*

Toward evening, the hold's comm chimed. Ilsa's voice, stripped of tone by the speaker, just words:

"Sable. Engineering says the reactor curve steepened at 1600. Revised estimate: fifty-one hours. We need to talk about the third option. Don't tell me there isn't one. Osei saw your screens."

He closed his eyes. Through the hull, the Pale Static groaned its long complaint, and in the frosted drawers the dead slept on, waiting for him to decide whether they were cargo or passengers, evidence or people, a burden or a debt.

Fifty-one hours. And somewhere above him, boots on the companionway, coming down fast.
