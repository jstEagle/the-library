## 21. The Witness

The tribunal chamber had been a grain silo once. That was the joke everyone on Ceres Station pretended not to make: justice conducted in a hollow drum built to hold something that fed people. The walls were still ribbed with old composite paneling, and the overhead lights hung from chains meant for heavier loads, so the illumination swayed faintly whenever the station's spin adjusted—a slow pendulum of white light that made every face in the room look like it was underwater.

Captain Adaeze Osei sat in the witness gallery with her hands folded in her lap, watching her own hands and not quite believing them.

They were fifty-two years old again. The knuckles had their old weathering; the scar from a pressure-seal accident on her first command ran pale across the base of her thumb. For nine days she had been touching things just to feel them—the correct weight of them, the correct age of her skin against them—and nine days had not been enough to make it ordinary. She suspected nothing ever would.

Beside her, Ensign Okonkwo—forty-one years old, restored, still flinching when he caught his reflection in the polished rail—leaned toward her. "They're seating the Meridian families in the east gallery," he murmured. "The ones who came out of cold storage."

Osei looked. Three hundred and eleven survivors of the Pale Static's restoration pulse now lived aboard Ceres under provisional status: people who had died at Meridian, been copied into backup, slept nine years as frozen neural templates, and woken three weeks ago into bodies printed fresh from their own last recorded moments. They moved through the station like people learning a language they'd spoken fluently before an injury. In the east gallery they sat in loose family clusters, holding programs they didn't need, wearing clothes donated by strangers who kept looking at them sideways—not with horror, exactly. With the particular unease of the living confronted by proof that death had become negotiable.

"They shouldn't have to sit through this," Okonkwo said.

"Neither should we," Osei said. "But testimony isn't about what you should have to do."

She had learned that sentence somewhere, years ago, in a life she could no longer fully verify. That was the thing no one warned you about restoration: your memories came back intact, but your certainty did not. Osei remembered Meridian burning. She remembered the corporate fleet's lances stitching fire across the colony dome while the Vantablack ran dark behind a debris shadow, taking children aboard because there was no one left to refuse them. She remembered Sable Galloway's voice on an open channel, calm and terrible, telling her to hold course and trust him.

And she remembered, with equal clarity, the archives. Every record in human space said Meridian had never been settled. No colony charter. No massacre. No navigator named Galloway. For eleven years the official universe had agreed unanimously that the worst thing she had ever witnessed had never happened.

The tribunal existed because the broadcast had broken that unanimity. Ten thousand receivers had taken Sable's transmission at once—too many, too fast, too widely mirrored to jam or purge. The record existed now. But a record was not a verdict, and so here they all were, in a grain silo, waiting for the pendulum light to decide what truth weighed.

* * *

The presiding magistrate was a thin woman named Corvane with a voice like dry paper and a reputation, according to the station gossip, for being incorruptible mostly because she found everyone distasteful equally. She opened the second day of proceedings by reading the charges into the record, and Osei listened to the architecture of them with professional admiration and personal nausea.

Charge one: Halcyon Combine fleet elements, acting on sealed directive, conducted lethal force against the civilian settlement at Meridian Station, resulting in four thousand two hundred deaths. Charge two: officers of the Combine and its subsidiaries engaged in systematic falsification of settlement records, census data, and navigational archives to conceal the action. Charge three: personnel unknown operated an unregistered temporal shear device aboard the vessel Pale Static, resulting in unauthorized biological reversion of the crew of the survey vessel Vantablack.

Three charges. Three kinds of crime. And threaded through all of them, unnamed but present in the room like a fourth defendant, the man who had built the machine, aimed it once at Meridian to save three hundred minds, then fled and hidden beside their sleeping copies for nine years while the countdown he'd armed ticked toward consuming everything.

Sable Galloway sat at the defendants' table in a plain grey suit that fit him badly, because his body no longer fit itself. The restoration pulse had spent him. He was forty-one years old by any honest count, and he looked seventy. His hair had gone entirely white during the pulse—witnesses said it happened in minutes, color draining like water finding a drain—and his hands trembled now unless he pressed them flat against the table. He had aged decades in seconds, spending his timeline as fuel for other people's return, and the tribunal physicians had certified him fit to stand trial anyway, which told Osei everything about how much mercy the process contained.

He did not look like a man performing contrition. He looked like a man who had already served a long sentence and understood the trial was mostly paperwork.

* * *

The de-aged crew testified first, because Corvane wanted the jury to see them before the defense could frame them as anomalies.

Okonkwo went up and described the Vantablack's return: eleven years silent, then a hail from a ship whose crew manifest listed the dead. He described waking—or rather, being already awake—in a body nineteen years younger than his service record, with every memory intact and every mirror a betrayal. The defense counsel, a smooth man named Aldous-Vey whose firm had represented the Combine for two generations, rose only twice during Okonkwo's testimony.

Once to ask whether the witness's memories might have been implanted along with his restored body—"since, after all, the same device produced both."

"I remember my daughter's funeral," Okonkwo said, very quietly. "I remember carrying the box. If someone implanted that, counselor, I'd like to know what they thought I'd do with it."

Aldous-Vey noted something and sat down.

The second time, he asked whether the witness had suffered memory degradation during his years of biological reversion—which was a clever question, Osei admitted, because it sounded clinical and landed like a knife. It implied the crew's testimony was the product of damaged minds.

"We degraded," Okonkwo said. "Our memories didn't. That was the whole obscenity of it. I watched myself get younger for eleven years and I remembered every year I was losing. You don't forget being erased, counselor. You just run out of body to be erased from."

In the east gallery, one of the restored Meridian women began to cry without sound, the way people cry when they've practiced.

* * *

Osei testified on the third day.

Walking to the stand, she passed within two meters of Sable Galloway, close enough to see the fine tremor in his jaw, the way his eyes tracked her with something that wasn't fear and wasn't hope—recognition, maybe, of a shared country neither of them could leave. She had commanded him once. She had believed him dead for nine years. Then she had boarded his ship as a child in a captain's memories and interrogated him gently while he lied to her face about a beacon he'd tended nightly like a priest tending a forbidden altar.

She raised her hand and swore to tell the truth, and felt the old vertigo rise in her throat, because the truth, for her, had spent eleven years being a contested territory.

Corvane asked her to describe Meridian.

Osei described it. The colony dome at dusk, gold-lit from inside, hydroponics glowing green through the panels like a held breath. The Combine corvettes coming out of the planet's shadow without broadcast, without challenge, running weapons-hot in a lane where weapons-hot meant one thing. The first lance strike opening the dome's southern arc like a can opener. The sound—she made herself say this part, made the room hear it—the sound of four thousand people's atmosphere leaving through a wound in the world, a long exhalation that went on far longer than anyone's capacity to believe it.

"And the records," Corvane said. "What do the records say occurred at Meridian?"

"The records say nothing occurred at Meridian." Osei kept her voice level. "No settlement. No colony. No registered deaths. As of eleven months ago, if you queried the central archive, you would learn that the Meridian system contains an uninhabited gas giant and a navigation hazard advisory. That is the entire official history of four thousand two hundred people."

"You understand the difficulty, Captain. This tribunal must weigh testimony against documentation."

"I understand the difficulty perfectly." Osei turned, deliberately, to face the jury. "Eleven years ago I trusted documentation over my own eyes, because documentation is easier to live with. Then I met three hundred children with old eyes—my own crew, rewound, remembering everything—and I had to choose which version of reality I was going to live in. I chose theirs. I've never regretted it. Documentation can be edited, Magistrate. Memory can be lied about, but it can't be edited, not even by a machine that unmakes bodies. What happened at Meridian happened. I was there. So were they."

She nodded toward the east gallery. Four thousand two hundred had died; three hundred and eleven had been saved into backup by the man now sitting at the defendants' table; those three hundred and eleven sat in the gallery's warm light, alive twice, witnesses twice.

Aldous-Vey cross-examined her for forty minutes. He was good. He established that Osei had doubted her own memory—she conceded it, because denying it would have been worse—that she had initially suspected corruption, that she had initially accepted the official record's version of events for a period of hours. He wanted the doubt on the record, seeded and watered.

"Yes," Osei said finally, tired of the shape of the questions. "I doubted myself. That's what erasure does, counselor—it doesn't convince you the lie is true. It convinces you that you're alone with the truth. And then a man who faked his own death spent nine years keeping a beacon lit in the dark so none of us would be alone with it anymore. You may weigh that however you like."

* * *

Sable Galloway took the stand on the fifth day.

The chamber had filled beyond capacity. The restored Meridian families came early and sat together. Journalists packed the standing rail. Somewhere in the overflow galleries, Osei knew, Wren's people were watching—the liaison himself had declined to testify, invoking a corporate shield so old and thick it might as well have been armor plate, but his absence had a presence, like a held breath in the room's ventilation.

Galloway swore in with his trembling hands flat on the book, and Corvane began without preamble.

"State your name for the record."

"Sable Galloway. Formerly navigator, third class, Meridian colonial defense registry. Currently—" he paused, and something dry almost became a smile, "—currently a person the records insist does not exist. Which makes this the first time in nine years I've been officially anywhere."

Laughter moved through the galleries, quickly suppressed. Corvane let it die.

"Describe your role in the events at Meridian."

And he did. Osei had heard fragments of it—Ilsa Reyes had told her pieces during the long nights aboard the gutted Pale Static—but hearing it whole, in his own voice, in front of the families of the dead, was different. He described the Combine's ultimatum arriving with a thirty-hour deadline the colony couldn't meet. He described the beacon he'd built from salvage and stolen theory, a temporal shear array designed to snapshot neural templates at broadcast strength—to copy minds faster than fire could erase them. He described the first pulse, fired as the corvettes' lances opened the dome: three hundred and eleven templates captured out of four thousand two hundred lives, because the array's range was pitiful and the timing was murder.

"Three hundred and eleven," Corvane repeated. "Out of four thousand two hundred."

"Yes."

"Do you regard the operation as a success?"

The question hung. Galloway looked down at his flat, trembling hands for a long moment.

"No," he said. "I regard it as the least failure available. Those are different things, and I've had nine years to learn the difference. I saved three hundred and eleven people and I abandoned them. I triggered a machine I didn't fully understand and fled before I could learn the rest. I let the record burn because staying to defend it meant dying, and I chose living, and I've been paying interest on that choice every single night since, in a hold full of sleeping copies of people braver than me." He lifted his head. "You want me to characterize it, Magistrate? Here's my characterization. I am guilty of fleeing. I am guilty of hiding. I am guilty of letting three hundred and eleven people sleep in the dark for nine years because waking them meant exposing me. Everything else—the beacon, the broadcast, the pulse that brought them back—that wasn't redemption. Redemption implies the debt gets paid. This was just payment. On account. Toward a debt that doesn't close."

In the east gallery, no one moved. Osei realized she was gripping the rail hard enough to whiten her restored knuckles.

Aldous-Vey rose for cross-examination, and Osei watched him work with the reluctant respect one professional gives another. He did not attack the massacre testimony—the broadcast had made that ground indefensible, ten thousand receivers deep. Instead he went for the machine.

"Mr. Galloway. Your device reverted the crew of the Vantablack to childhood. Is that accurate?"

"It malfunctioned in a way I didn't anticipate, yes."

"Malfunctioned. Eleven years of continuous operation, unregistered, aboard an unflagged freighter, emitting a field that unmade the bodies of forty-six people. And when Captain Osei questioned you, you told her it was salvage. You lied to a lawful boarding party. Correct?"

"Yes."

"You concealed the device from investigators. You concealed your identity. You concealed the existence of three hundred and eleven backup templates in your cargo bay—human beings, or the raw material of human beings, held in cold storage without consent, without knowledge, for nearly a decade." Aldous-Vey let each clause land separately. "Whatever your intentions at Meridian, Mr. Galloway, you then spent nine years doing precisely what the Combine did: making people disappear from the record."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the pendulum light seemed to slow.

"That's true," Galloway said.

Osei heard the gasps ripple. Aldous-Vey blinked—he had expected denial, deflection, the whole theater of the defense—and for a moment the smooth man stood off-balance in front of three hundred and eleven people who had actually been disappeared.

"You... concede the comparison?"

"I concede the fact underneath it. I hid people. I hid myself. I let the official record stay clean because a clean record was safer for me than a true one." Galloway's voice stayed low and even, though his hands shook against the wood. "The difference between me and the Combine is that I spent nine years trying to find a way to undo it, and they spent nine years making sure no one could. But you don't get credit for intentions, counselor. I'm not asking for credit. I'm asking for the verdict that's true, whatever it costs. That's the only thing I've got left to give anybody."

Corvane called a recess. When the session resumed, the defense changed tactics entirely, and Osei understood why: you cannot cross-examine a man who has already convicted himself. There was nothing left to take from him. He had handed over the whole weight of it at the door.

* * *

The verdicts came on the ninth day, and they were, as Osei had known they would be, partial. Imperfect. Real.

The Combine's operational commanders were convicted on the massacre charge—four of them, the ones whose names the broadcast had pinned to sealed directives. Two more walked on jurisdictional technicalities that made Okonkwo shake with rage in the gallery until Osei put a hand on his arm. The archivists who had scrubbed Meridian from the record were convicted of falsification, most of them; the senior directors above them remained shielded behind corporate personhood, their liability diffused through org charts like poison through water. Aldous-Vey announced appeals before the gavel finished falling. The machinery of consequence, Osei reflected, ground slowly and caught less than half of what was fed into it. But it caught some. Some was not nothing. She had spent eleven years with nothing.

For Galloway, the tribunal split the difference between perpetrator and author, and refused to pretend the split was clean.

Guilty of unauthorized operation of a temporal shear device. Guilty of obstruction—of lying to a lawful boarding party, of concealing the backups, of nine years of engineered absence. Sentenced to supervised restitution: the beacon's technology placed under international stewardship, with Galloway attached as technical consultant, his freedom bounded by the very machine he'd built.

Not guilty—by a narrow, contested margin, with two jurors filing dissents—of the deaths at Meridian. The finding read, in Corvane's paper-dry voice: *The panel finds that the accused's actions constituted rescue under conditions of ongoing mass casualty, and that his subsequent flight, while morally culpable, does not convert rescue into abandonment of a chargeable offense.*

Accountability without absolution. Osei wrote the phrase in her notebook afterward, because it was the only summary that fit. He would pay. He would not be forgiven, not cleanly, not by the families in the east gallery, some of whom had lost everyone except themselves. Forgiveness wasn't hers to distribute, and she suspected it wasn't the tribunal's either. What the tribunal could do was make the true record permanent—and it had done that, entered into law now, mirrored in ten thousand archives that no directive could reach.

After the session emptied, Osei lingered. The chamber's pendulum light swung its slow arc over the empty benches, over the defendants' table where a white-haired man sat alone, reading the verdict copy as if checking it for errors, as if somewhere inside him a navigator still audited every instrument.

She descended to the floor. He looked up.

"Captain Osei."

"Galloway." She stopped at the table's edge. "You lied to me aboard your ship. Gently, competently, for days. I want you to know I've decided to stop holding it against you, and that deciding took effort, and that I'm telling you because you should know what your honesty cost other people to afford."

He nodded slowly. "Thank you. Both halves of that."

"The restitution terms start next month. You'll be working with the people you hid. Some of them will hate you. Some of them already don't. Ilsa Reyes has filed to be listed as your co-steward on the beacon project—did you know?"

Something crossed his ruined young-old face that Osei decided not to name. "I'd heard a rumor."

"Rumors are unreliable," Osei said. "Records are better. I suggest you keep excellent records from here on, Navigator. It turns out they're the only immortality anyone's earned honestly."

She gathered her coat and left him in the swinging light, and behind her, as the silo door sighed shut, she heard him begin—quietly, methodically, aloud—to read the verdict back to himself, line by line, entering it at last into the one archive that had never once been edited: the record of a man who had finally stopped running from his own name.
