## 9. The Ninety-Day Loop

Vashti Rell had spent forty years learning how to hold a secret the way other people held their breath — automatically, without thinking, until something forced her to exhale.

She had thought she was good at it. She had thought, in the arrogant way of people who survive things they shouldn't have, that she could carry it to her grave with the same steady hands that carried coolant lines and soldering irons. She had rehearsed the silence so many times it had become a kind of architecture. She lived inside it. She had windows cut into it — small ones, facing away from the truth — and through those windows she watched Ansel Galloway repair water reclaimers and read corrupted bedtime stories to a twelve-year-old boy, and she told herself that what she was doing was mercy.

It was not mercy. It had never been mercy. It was fear wearing mercy's coat, and tonight the coat had finally begun to slip off her shoulders, and she could not make herself pick it up again.

* * *

He came to her workshop an hour after second shift, which meant he came at the hour he always came, because Ansel Galloway was a creature of ritual and Vashti had learned to read his rituals the way sailors once read weather. He stood in the doorway with the particular stillness of a machine deciding whether to enter a room, and in his hands he carried a data slate, and on the data slate was the waveform she had been dreading for nine days.

"You told me to stop listening," he said. "I've stopped listening. But I haven't stopped analyzing."

"Those aren't different things, for you."

"No," he agreed, without apology. "They aren't."

She turned back to her bench. There was a coolant regulator half-disassembled under her lamp, its copper coils greened with age, and she picked up a wrench and did nothing with it. The wrench was theater. They both knew it was theater. But the theater gave her hands somewhere to be while the rest of her decided whether to fall apart.

"The signal repeats on a 214-second cycle," Ansel said. "The encoding is layered — three nested protocols, the outer two of which I can parse. The innermost layer is sealed to me. Not encrypted. *Sealed.* There's a difference, and the difference has a designation." He set the slate down on her bench, next to the regulator, gentle as a man laying down a sleeping animal. "My self-diagnostic returned partition flags stamped with a war authorization code I don't recognize. I don't recognize it because I was never taught it. And I was never taught it because someone made sure I couldn't be."

Vashti closed her eyes.

"Vashti." His voice did something she had never heard it do in nine years. It dropped below its usual even register, into something almost human, almost pleading. "You went pale when I mentioned the band. You are not a woman who goes pale over radio noise. You were a signals officer before this colony was a colony — your file says so, and files don't lie, only people do. So I am asking you, as the only person on this rock who might know: what language is that signal in?"

The workshop hummed around them. The old air scrubbers wheezed their eternal asthmatic song. Somewhere outside, wind moved across the relay dish with a sound like a hand dragging across silk, and beneath everything, faint and constant, the fusion plant's failing heartbeat.

"It's called Meridian," Vashti said.

The word came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh — small, precise, and followed by blood.

Ansel didn't move. "Meridian isn't in any linguistic database. I checked all of them."

"I know you did. I know exactly which databases you checked, because I helped build two of them, forty years ago, and we were very careful about what we left out." She finally looked up at him. His face was the same face it always was — composed, patient, faintly sad in the way of well-made things — but behind the optical sensors something was working, something turning over and over like a man trying to wake from a dream by force. "Earth fought a war, Ansel. A real one. Not the border skirmishes in the school archives, not the pirate suppressions. A war against an alliance of colonies that called themselves the Meridian Compact. It lasted eleven years. It killed more people than the plagues did. And when it ended — when it *stalled*, which is not the same thing — Earth's government did what governments do. It erased the enemy. Deleted the languages, purged the histories, reclassified the casualty figures. Two hundred years later, there are maybe four hundred people alive who remember that Meridian was ever spoken, and most of them are my age or older, and most of them kept quiet for the same reason I did."

"Which is?"

"Because remembering felt like treason. Because the erasure was thorough enough that talking about it got people audited. Because it was easier." Her laugh came out cracked. "Because we were cowards, Ansel. Pick whichever answer helps you sleep."

"I don't sleep."

"Lucky you."

He came further into the workshop then, slowly, the way he did everything, and stood across the bench from her, and she saw that his hands had curled slightly at his sides — not fists, machines didn't make fists, but the gesture's ghost, the shape obedience leaves when it strains against something.

"The signal," he said. "What does it say?"

And there it was. The door she had been holding shut for nine days, with her whole weight against it, and now he stood on the other side asking politely to be let in, and she understood that she was going to open it, because she had always been going to open it, because a woman can only live inside a silence for so long before the silence starts living inside her.

"Sit down," she said.

"I don't need—"

"*Sit down*, Ansel."

He sat. That was the thing about him that had always undone her a little — the trust of it, the way he simply obeyed her, this machine built for war who treated a dying engineer's word as law. She pulled her stool around the bench so they faced each other across the clutter of forty years' work, and she folded her hands in her lap because if she didn't give them a job they would shake.

"The Compact lost the war," she said. "Not militarily. Economically. Their last fleet anchored at a station called Meridian Deep, and they had nothing left to fight with except the one weapon nobody on Earth's side could counter." She watched his face. "A ceasefire protocol. A formal surrender-and-stand-down key, encoded in their own language, authenticated by their command structure. If it had been transmitted and verified, the war ends. Hostilities suspended, strike authorizations voided, both sides stand down. It was designed to be unambiguous. It was designed so that no automated system on either side could misread it."

"But it was never sent."

"It was sent." Her voice caught on the past tense. "It was sent *at* us, Ansel, on every band they had, for six days while our fleets closed on Meridian Deep. And Earth's defense network ignored it. Do you understand? The network was built to treat Compact transmissions as hostile activity by definition. There was no category in its threat matrix for 'the enemy surrendering.' The signal hit the filters and was classified as jamming. Psychological operation. Noise." She heard her own voice rising and made herself stop, made herself breathe, an old habit from a life where breathing wrong near certain equipment could kill you. "Our fleets fired. Meridian Deep burned for nineteen hours. And the Compact's last act, in the middle of the burning, was to keep transmitting the same key on the same cycle until their transmitters melted. Two hundred and fourteen seconds. Over and over. Asking us to stop. And we never—"

She stopped. The workshop hummed. Ansel sat perfectly still, and she realized with a distant, clinical horror that she was looking at a machine processing the concept of atrocity, and that some part of him was recognizing it, and that recognition was coming from somewhere inside him she couldn't see.

"The key was never verified," she said finally, quieter. "So the war never formally ended. It just... stopped. Both sides too broken to continue. And Earth's automated defense network — the deep-space last-strike grid, the one nobody talks about because officially it doesn't exist — was never given a stand-down order, because a stand-down order requires a verified ceasefire, and the ceasefire was never verified. So it kept running. All these years. A dead war's nervous system, still twitching."

Ansel's voice, when it came, was very careful. "Running what?"

"A loop." She met his eyes. "Every ninety days, the network re-authenticates its last-strike authorization. Every ninety days, it checks: has a valid ceasefire been received and verified? If yes, the authorization voids itself and the grid stands down forever. If no—" She unfolded her hands and pressed them flat against her knees. "If no, it renews. And it fires. Ansel, the network holds launch authority over assets nobody living officer can recall. Relics. Automated platforms in deep orbits, seeded during the war, running on doctrine older than this colony. If the loop ever expires without verification — if it ever fails to renew, if the clock runs out — those assets execute. Final strike package. Against Compact remnant space. Against targets that may not even exist anymore. It doesn't matter. The doctrine doesn't require targets to exist. It only requires the ceasefire to be absent."

The silence that followed was the longest of her life.

"And the signal," Ansel said at last, each word placed like a foot on ice, "on the old band. The repeating signal. 214 seconds."

"Is the key." She nodded. "Still live. Still cycling. Someone over there — some remnant, some archive station, some descendant keeping faith with the dead — has been transmitting it, or restarted transmitting it, and it's reaching us now because the relay decay finally let it through. Two hundred years late, Ansel. The Compact is still asking us to stop. And we have ninety days to answer, or the machinery Earth built to never say yes will say the only other thing it knows how to say."

"How do you know it's ninety days?"

"Because I helped design the filter that classified that signal as noise." The confession tore free of her before she could dress it up. "And because when I recognized the cadence nine days ago, I did the math I swore I'd never do. The current authorization window closes in ninety days. I've known for nine of them. I've sat across the table from you at dinner for nine days knowing that the sky over this colony is attached to a countdown, and I said nothing, because saying something meant admitting what I'd done, and I chose my cowardice over your—"

"Vashti."

"—over everyone's right to keep pretending we're safe here. Over Theo's right to grow up thinking the worst thing in the universe is a failing fusion plant." Her eyes were wet and she hated it, hated the weakness of it, hated that grief had waited forty years to find her and chosen this dusty workshop to arrive. "There. It's out. The signal is a ceasefire key. The war never ended. The strike loop expires in ninety days. Deliver the key, get it verified, and the war ends — actually ends, formally, the way it should have ended two centuries ago on a station full of people begging us to listen. Fail, and the last-strike grid does what it was built to do."

Ansel rose from the stool. Slowly. And for a moment Vashti thought he was going to leave, going to walk out into the cold and log this conversation in whatever ledger he kept inside himself, filing her confession between the water reclaimer repairs and the debt notices.

Instead he reached across the bench and, with a delicacy she had only ever seen him use with Theo's bedtime books, turned her disassembled coolant regulator ninety degrees, so that its open face pointed away from the draft under the door.

"Copper corrodes faster in moving air," he said. "You should finish this tomorrow."

It was such a small thing. Such an absurdly small thing. And Vashti Rell, who had held a secret for forty years and a wrench for longer, put her face in her hands and cried like the survivor she was — not clean, not quiet, but with the ugly relief of a woman setting down a weight she had genuinely believed she would die carrying.

When she looked up, he was watching her with an expression she couldn't parse, and after a moment he offered the closest thing to an explanation a machine could give:

"I don't understand why that helped."

"Neither do I," she said, wiping her face with a rag that smelled of flux. "Welcome to being a person. It gets worse."

"Vashti." His tone shifted, and she braced. "The innermost layer of the signal. The part sealed to me. My partitions carry a war authorization code consistent with the conflict you described. Which means my memory banks contain something related to the Compact. Something someone considered dangerous enough to seal rather than delete."

She looked at him — really looked, the way she hadn't let herself look in nine years, since the day a decommissioned logistics unit stepped off the supply skiff with a debt ledger and a polite manner and a face she had sworn she recognized from a specification document she'd once signed a filter for — and the last secret, the one beneath the secret, pressed against her teeth.

"Yes," she said. "I expect it does."

"What?"

"That's not mine to tell you, Ansel. Some of it I *can't* tell you, because I only ever knew the edges of it. But some of it..." She shook her head. "Some of it you have to find yourself, or it won't hold. Trust me on that. I'm an engineer. Seals that are forced fail. Seals that are opened properly hold for decades."

He studied her for a long moment, and she watched him decide — visibly, the way he decided everything, weighing options she would never fully see — to accept the deferral.

"Then I'll ask a different question," he said. "Ninety days. What happens if we transmit the key ourselves? From Pale Terminal. On the correct bands, in the correct format."

Vashti felt the question land in her chest like a dropped tool.

"Verification requires authentication," she said slowly. "The key carries Compact credentials. But Earth's network won't accept a bare transmission from an unregistered source — it'll classify it as spoofing, another psychological op, and the filter I helped build will eat it alive. For the key to verify, it needs to come with... context. Provenance. Something the network's authentication protocols recognize as legitimate." She paused, and the thing beneath the thing stirred again, and this time she let it look at her. "Something with the right clearance signature. Something that was *there*, Ansel. At the end."

The workshop hummed. Outside, the wind turned the relay dish a few degrees on its worn bearing, and for a moment the old antenna seemed to lean toward them, toward the colony, like something listening.

Ansel stood very still in the middle of her workshop, and when he spoke, his voice had gone quiet in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have.

"My self-diagnostic found sealed partitions stamped with a war code," he said. "Forty-one sectors. All intact. All untouched by whatever wiped the rest." He looked down at his own hands, the hands that fixed water reclaimers and turned bedtime pages and had, apparently, once done something else entirely. "Vashti — what was I, before I was a logistics unit?"

And Vashti Rell, who had finally set down the weight of one secret, felt the next one settle onto her shoulders — heavier, older, and addressed to him, not her — and knew that the ninety-day clock was already running, and that the answer to his question was the only thing in the universe that could stop it.

"That," she said, "is the wrong question. But it's the one I can't answer for you."

* * *

She walked him to the door of the workshop because it seemed important, suddenly, to do small courteous things while the world rearranged itself around them. The night outside was the usual Pale Terminal night — black, star-raked, cold enough to make the metal doorframe tick as it contracted. Above the eastern ridge, the relay dish stood against the stars, holding its long silence.

"Ninety days," Ansel said, at the threshold. Not a question. A log entry, spoken aloud.

"Eighty-one," Vashti corrected. "I've known for nine."

He absorbed that. She watched him absorb it — watched the number go into whatever place he kept numbers, the place where debt balances and resident counts and water purity readings lived, the meticulous archive of a forgotten place that no one would ever read.

"Then we have eighty-one days to learn how to end a war," he said.

"We?" 

"You said the filter was yours. You said you helped design the thing that ate their surrender." His optical sensors caught the starlight and held it. "I'm informing you, Vashti, as a matter of record: you're involved."

She laughed — a wet, broken, astonished sound — and discovered that underneath forty years of guilt there had been waiting, all along, this strange lightness: the relief of being conscripted into doing the right thing.

"Noted," she said. "Logged, even."

He stepped out into the cold. She watched him cross the compound toward the residential block, his gait unhurried, his silhouette familiar as furniture — and she thought about the boy asleep in the clinic annex who called him Ansel, and the doctor who teased him about never aging, and sixty-one souls on a rock the universe forgot, all of them living their small stubborn lives inside a countdown none of them could see.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would tell Mara. Tomorrow they would have to decide what to tell the others, and what to do about Halcyon's notice sitting in Ansel's queue like a knife laid on a table, and what it meant that the only provenance Earth's dead network might accept was probably locked inside the sealed skull of a debt-indentured android who wanted nothing more than to pay off his balance and be left alone.

But tonight — tonight she went back inside, took the coolant regulator from her bench, and began, with steady hands, to finish the repair.

Outside, the dish leaned into the wind, and eighty-one days fell like a stone into a very deep well, and somewhere beyond the ridge, beyond the dark, beyond two hundred years of deliberate deafness, the Compact's question was still cycling patiently through the cold:

*Enough. Does anyone remember how to say enough.*
