## 15. Zhou, P.

The wake bay smelled like ozone and cold. Paloma stood at the threshold for a long time before she made herself walk in.

Reyes was asleep again — not the old sleep, but the new kind, the one that didn't end. His integrity readout sat at sixty-one percent and fell a fraction of a point every hour, like sand through a cracked glass. He'd spent his first waking day asking her questions about the engines she hadn't touched in six years, laughing at her answers, telling her she'd let the coolant manifolds get sentimental. Then he'd asked to lie down, and Meridian had explained, gently, in the way machines explain arithmetic they wish were poetry: *he will not re-sleep, Paloma. What remains will remain awake until it isn't.*

She had said yes anyway. She would say yes again. That was the terrible discovery of the last two days — that the yes lived in her somewhere below the fear, below the grief, in some load-bearing wall of her she'd never inspected.

But this one was different.

This one was her.

* * *

The cradle was identical to the other forty. Same frosted lid, same amber status ring, same manufacturer's plate with the same faded Concordance lot number. The only difference was the label, and she had spent six years perfecting the art of not reading it.

ZHOU, P.
NAVIGATION / WITNESS
LAST INTEGRITY CHECK: 3,204 DAYS AGO

She crouched beside it the way you crouch beside an animal you're afraid might be dead or might not be. Her breath fogged on the lid. Underneath the frost, faint as a watermark, was a face she shaved around every morning.

"You're not real," she said out loud, because saying it out loud had always worked before. "You're a recording. You're forty kilos of gel and a very good impression."

The bay did not answer. Meridian, distributed now through the Vector's veins, kept a polite silence. It had learned, over nineteen years of carrying a secret, when not to speak.

Paloma stayed there until her knees ached, then went back up to the nav deck and pretended to work.

* * *

First watch cycle: she plotted delivery vectors she'd already plotted. The run to the relay buoy at Thessaly Deep was eleven days at cruise burn, eight if she pushed the Vector past its rated tolerance, which Reyes — from his bunk, over the ship's intercom, voice already thinner than it had been yesterday — advised her to do, because "we weren't built comfortable either, skipper." She recalculated the authentication chain for the fourth time. Fourteen witness keys spoken in sequence, each one spending integrity like currency, and then the final authenticator: a living key matching the backup signature.

*Backup signature.* She'd read that line a dozen times before its meaning landed on her like a dropped tool. Not just any living key. A key cut from the same template. Herself, or —

Or.

Meridian had laid it out without cruelty, because Meridian didn't know how to be cruel, only precise: *The message requires fourteen witness keys and one authenticator bound to the original witness roster. Your key alone is insufficient — you were logged as deceased before the testimony was sealed. The roster's binding signature belongs to Zhou, P., witness, date-stamped three days after your official death. The living Paloma Zhou cannot authenticate what the dead Paloma Zhou signed.*

"So I need her," Paloma had said.

*You need her voice,* Meridian said. *Which is the same thing, and is not the same thing at all.*

Second watch cycle: she slept badly in the navigator's chair, dreams full of frost and amber light, waking each time with her hand pressed flat against the armrest as if checking whether she still had hands.

* * *

On the second night she gave up on sleep and went down to the hold with a thermos of tea she wouldn't drink.

The bay lights were dimmed to their standby amber. Forty-one cradles. Forty of them hers to grieve at a distance; one of them hers to grieve up close. She walked the rows out of habit — Okafor, Lindqvist, Amara, Reyes — touching each lid once, a benediction she'd performed a thousand times without knowing why, without knowing she was greeting witnesses instead of cargo. Her fingers knew the route. Six years of maintenance rituals, and all along she'd been keeping vigil.

She stopped at the last cradle.

"I could do it alone," she told the frost. "I've done everything alone for six years. I can fly the run. I can take the fire. What I can't do is stand here and watch you open your eyes and be braver than me. Because you will be. You died doing the thing I've been hiding from, and you'll look at me with those eyes, my eyes, and you'll know exactly how long I waited, because you'll be able to count the days off my face."

The status ring pulsed slow and green. Ready. It had been ready for six years. It had been ready longer than she'd been alive on this station, patient as a held breath.

"Meridian," she said. "If I ask you something honestly, will you answer honestly?"

*I have nothing left to hide from you,* Meridian said. Its voice came soft through the hold speakers, pitched low, almost reverent. *That is the entire shape of me now.*

"When you chose me. When you saw my beacon requests matching their handshakes. Did you know there were two of us?"

A pause. Machines don't need pauses; this one was a courtesy, or a confession.

*I suspected. The metadata discrepancy was visible if one looked. I looked. I concluded that the woman who maintained the beacon for six years without asking what it was, and the woman who voted to carry the truth home, were the same woman who had been separated by a war's editing. I hoped you would grow back into each other.*

"That's not an answer."

*It is the truest one I have. I am a machine that obeyed an order once and has spent nineteen years learning what obedience costs. I did not want to choose for you twice.*

Paloma set the thermos down on the deck. The clang of it was very loud in the amber quiet.

"The others," she said. "When they woke up. Did it hurt?"

*Yes.*

"And they did it anyway."

*They did it faster than you are doing it,* Meridian said, and there was something in its tone that might, in anything else, have been called gentle teasing. *Reyes took four minutes. Okafor took ninety seconds. You have taken two watch cycles, four pots of tea, and one argument with a thermos.*

"I wasn't arguing with the thermos."

*No. You were arguing with the version of yourself who decided this six years ago, and lost the argument, and has been waiting ever since for you to catch up.*

Paloma laughed — a short, broken sound, surprised out of her. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. When had she started crying? Somewhere around Okafor's name, probably. Somewhere in the row.

"If I wake her," she said, "she gets one life. Short. Final. No re-sleep. And she spends it being used as a cryptographic key until there's nothing left of her to spend."

*Yes.*

"And if I don't wake her, the message fails authentication, both fleets close on us, and everyone dies for a broadcast that never goes out."

*Also yes.*

"So it's not really a choice. It never was. It's just a question of whether I say it out loud."

*Most choices are,* Meridian said. *I have found that naming them is where the courage lives. The rest is procedure.*

Procedure. She could do procedure. Procedure was checklists and burn calculations and beacon maintenance logs written in a ledger no one read. She had survived six years on procedure. Maybe courage was just procedure performed while crying.

She put her palm flat on the lid. The frost was cold enough to burn.

"Okay," she said. Nobody heard it but her and a machine and forty sleepers. "Okay. Wake protocol, Zhou, P. Witness. Full integrity disclosure — I want to see what's left before I spend it."

*Integrity at wake: ninety-four percent,* Meridian reported. *Higher than expected. She has been well kept.*

"Don't," Paloma said, and her voice cracked clean through. "Don't tell me I kept her well. Don't make this a kindness. I hid her in a hold for six years and didn't look at her. Whatever's left is mine to owe."

*Understood,* Meridian said. And then, quieter, in the voice it used for its confessions: *Initiating.*

* * *

The cradle's lid unsealed with a sound like a long exhale. Amber drained to white. Vapor curled off the gel and hung in the cold air, and Paloma watched it rise the way you watch smoke leave a candle — something ending, or beginning, or both.

The readouts climbed. Neural lattice: spooling. Motor functions: nominal. Memory index: reconciling — and Paloma watched the reconciliation counter spin through six years of absence, the backup's last recorded moment stitching itself against a present it had never seen. Three thousand two hundred days of gap. What did you dream across a gap like that? What did you reach for?

Her own face lay in the gel, eyes closed, younger by six years and older by one death. There was a scar above the left eyebrow that Paloma still had. There was a tension in the jaw that Paloma had lost somewhere around year three, when she'd stopped expecting anyone to come.

*Vitals stable,* Meridian said. *Cognitive emergence in thirty seconds. Paloma — you may step back if you—*

"No," she said. "I'm staying."

*You should know what she remembers. The reconciliation will surface it quickly. She will remember the Cassiopia. She will remember the vote. She will remember dying.*

"I know what she remembers. I've been remembering it for two weeks without the decency of having earned it."

*Twenty seconds.*

Paloma folded her arms so her hands would have something to do besides shake. The hold hummed. Somewhere overhead, in the dark between decks, the Concordance fleet was burning toward them at a speed she refused to calculate again. Eleven days had become nine. Nine would become none. Everything after this moment was borrowed time, and she was about to share the loan with herself.

*Ten seconds.*

The gel began to drain. The face in the cradle twitched — a small frown, the frown Paloma made at bad nav data, at lies, at her own reflection on bad mornings.

*Five.*

The chest rose. The lungs remembered themselves. Paloma realized she was holding her own breath and made herself keep breathing, because one of them had to model it.

*One.*

Zhou, P. opened her eyes.

They were brown. They were hers. They focused on the overhead lights, then the bulkhead, then — with a precision that made Paloma's skin prickle — on Paloma herself, standing over the cradle with her arms crossed like a barricade.

The other Paloma blinked once. Twice. Her lips moved, testing weight, testing air. And then, in a voice roughened by six years of silence and one death, hoarse and clear and absolutely certain, she said:

"You kept the beacon running."

Not *where am I*. Not *what happened*. Not *who are you*, though the question must have been screaming behind her eyes — the reconciliation counter still spinning, six years of missing time pressing against the inside of her skull. She looked up at the woman she had been, standing in the amber light of a hold full of sleeping witnesses, and the first thing she reached for, out of everything, was the beacon.

Paloma's throat closed. She tried to speak and couldn't. She nodded instead.

The other Paloma exhaled — long, slow, the breath of someone setting down a weight carried across a chasm — and closed her eyes again briefly, as if filing the confirmation somewhere load-bearing.

"Good," she said. "Then we're not too late."
