## 19. Witnesses

The authentication queue had seven names in it.

Paloma watched them scroll on the engineering display: REYES, T. / OKAFOR, A. / LINDQVIST, M. / HALE, S. / VASQUEZ, R. / CHEN, W. / MERIDIAN. Seven keys between the Pale Vector and a confession nineteen years buried. Each one spoken aloud would cost the speaker everything they had left of themselves. The math was simple and merciless — integrity spent per syllable, memory burned as fuel — and there was no version of the broadcast that didn't require all seven.

Reyes went first because he'd already decided to before anyone asked him.

* * *

**REYES**

Tomas Reyes stood at the comm station with his hands flat on the console, the way he used to stand over an engine he was about to open up. He remembered engines. He was starting to forget other things — the name of the town he'd grown up in had gone sometime during the second hour after waking, dissolving like sugar in warm water, and he'd noticed it going and let it go without a fight. You couldn't hold everything. He'd known that when he voted, back when he was whole. He'd known it this morning too, when Paloma explained the arithmetic one more time, her voice careful, giving him every exit.

"Say the word," she'd said, "and I'll find another way."

"There isn't another way," Reyes said now, to the room, to the ship, to the thin ghost of himself that still answered when he called his own name. "There's just the way we chose."

Meridian's voice came through the deck plating, distributed through every system now, everywhere at once. *When you are ready, Engineer. Speak your key slowly. Do not paraphrase. The authentication is exact.*

Reyes closed his eyes. The key wasn't a password. It was a testimony — forty seconds of his own voice, recorded the night before the Cassiopia burned, sworn in front of the others: what he'd seen, what he'd been ordered to ignore, what they'd agreed to carry home if any of them lived. He had died three days later carrying it. Now he opened his mouth and gave it up a second time.

He could feel the words leaving him. Not metaphorically — he could feel it, a lightness spreading from the center of his chest outward, each sentence taking a room out of the house of his memory. His mother's kitchen went while he was still speaking. The smell of engine coolant on his father's coveralls went. He kept talking anyway, steady, the way you keep a torch lit in wind, because stopping meant the key failed and the key failing meant everyone's burning was for nothing.

*I witness,* he finished. *Reyes, Tomas, engineer, third watch. We carried it home.*

The console chimed green. One of seven.

Paloma was looking at him with something breaking behind her eyes. "Tomas—"

"Don't," he said, and smiled, and found that the smile was easier than it should have been, because grief was one of the things going. "Ask me what I remember about the Cassiopia. Go on. That part stays. That part's load-bearing."

She asked. He told her, complete, word-perfect, down to the color of the evacuation lights. Everything else was weather. This was the building.

* * *

**OKAFOR**

Adaeze Okafor took the comms chair next because she had always believed in order of operations.

She had been the Vector's medic, and dying hadn't changed how she thought: triage first, comfort second, truth always. She woke remembering the vote, the briefing, the cold mathematics of the decision they'd made in a shuttle bay while a colony ship's distress signal looped unanswered behind them. She woke also remembering her daughter's face, and she made a private inventory in the first hour — which memories were structural and which were decorative — and put her daughter's face in the structural column and defended it as long as she could.

It lasted until her turn came.

*Speak your key, Medic.*

"I'm not afraid of you," Okafor said to Meridian, and meant it, and heard how young her own voice sounded, how much of her had already thinned away in the hours since waking. Degradation didn't take everything evenly. It took the edges first. She was becoming a summary of herself, and the summary was enough, barely, for exactly one thing.

She spoke. Her daughter's birthday went. Her wedding day went. The names of the nurses she'd trained went, one by one, like lights being switched off down a corridor she couldn't stop walking down. She kept her voice level because level voices carry further, and this testimony had to carry across two territories, through every jamming field both fleets could throw at it.

Halfway through, she lost the word for *hospital* and substituted *the place where people are repaired*, and understood, distantly, that she was simplifying, that the machine of her was shedding complexity to conserve power for the essential function. She did not fight it. A medic knows when a patient is past saving and pivots to what can still be done.

*I witness,* she said. *Okafor, Adaeze, medic. What was done to the Cassiopia was done under orders, and the orders were given by—*

She gave the name. Both names. The Republic officer and the Concordance liaison who had signed the same order from opposite sides of a war, because the atrocity had never belonged to either government alone. That was the hinge of the whole confession, the detail both factions had killed to bury: they had done it together, and then blamed each other for nineteen years.

Two of seven. The message was beginning to have bones.

* * *

**LINDQVIST**

Mikael Lindqvist had been the Vector's comms officer, and he found the degradation almost funny, in the way that only a man losing his mind can find something funny.

"Do you know what I've got left?" he asked Paloma, gesturing at his own head. "Signals. Frequencies. Every call sign I ever memorized, every encryption handshake, every emergency channel in two territories. My brain kept the professional archive and deleted the personal one. I am, at present, entirely a radio."

"You're still you," Paloma said.

"I'm the part of me that mattered for this." He shrugged. "Turns out that's most of what I was anyway. The rest was packaging."

But when his turn came, and he stood before the open channel, something in the packaging fought back. The key required him to describe the moment he'd intercepted the order — the moment he'd known what was coming and passed it up the chain anyway, hands shaking, doing his job, complicit by obedience. And to describe it fully he needed the fear, and the fear lived in the rooms of his memory that were already dark.

He stood there for eleven seconds, silent, reaching.

Then Meridian's voice, gentle, from everywhere: *I held your original transmission, Officer Lindqvist. Nineteen years. Would you like me to return it to you?*

"Yes," Lindqvist whispered.

A fragment bloomed in him — not restored, borrowed, streamed through him like light through glass. He felt his own terror pass through him a second time, clean and terrible, and he spoke the key through it, using the fear instead of being used by it, and when he finished, the fear drained away and did not come back, and he understood that Meridian had just spent a piece of its own hoarded archive to lend him a self for forty seconds.

Three of seven. The machine was spending itself too. It had been spending itself the whole time.

* * *

**HALE**

Sasha Hale refused the chair. She gave her key standing, arms crossed, chin up, the posture of a woman who had argued her way through a war and intended to argue her way through its aftermath.

Hale had been the Vector's quartermaster, and her testimony was the ledger: manifests, dates, cargo codes proving that the Cassiopia's destruction had been logged as a reactor accident in four separate official databases within six hours of it happening. Paperwork as crime scene. She recited it like scripture because it was scripture — the only honest document either government had produced in nineteen years, and it existed only inside the heads of dead people.

Her degradation was different from the others'. Where Reyes lost his childhood and Okafor lost her family, Hale lost her doubts. She could feel them going — the small nightly hesitations, the *are we sure*, the *is this worth it* — dissolving one by one until what remained was pure certainty, polished smooth as river stone. Later she would have called it peace. In the moment it felt like being sanded down to the grain of herself.

*I witness,* she said. *Hale, Sasha, quartermaster. The records were falsified at source. I counted the copies. Four.*

Four of seven. The confession now had a spine, a skeleton, a circulatory system. It was becoming a body of evidence in the oldest sense — assembled from witnesses, animated by their consent.

* * *

**VASQUEZ AND CHEN**

They went together, because Rafael Vasquez and Wei Chen had been a pair for nine years aboard the Vector — pilot and gunner, married in everything but paperwork — and they had woken holding hands in adjacent cradles, and they declined to be separated now.

"We'll alternate," Chen said. "Line by line. Our keys interlock. That's how we wrote them."

Meridian paused — a full 1.3 seconds, an eternity for the AI — and then agreed, and Paloma understood later that the pause was Meridian checking whether the interleaved authentication would hold, and finding that it would, and being moved by it in whatever way a machine can be moved.

They spoke their testimony in braided halves. Vasquez had flown the escape run; Chen had covered it. Between them they described the Cassiopia's final minutes from outside — the lifeboats that launched and were fired upon, the ones that never launched at all, the specific weapon signature that matched no pirate fleet and both navies. Their voices wove together, call and response, and as they spoke they simplified in tandem, sentences shortening, adjectives falling away, until near the end they were speaking almost in unison, two streams converging into one clear channel:

*I witness. Vasquez, Rafael, pilot.*
*I witness. Chen, Wei, gunner.*
*We saw the boats burn. We carried it home.*

Six of seven.

Vasquez sat down afterward and looked at his hands. "I don't remember our wedding," he said to Chen. Not a question. An inventory report.

"I remember ours for both of us," Chen said, and then, an hour later, quietly corrected herself, because hers had gone too, and what remained was the fact of it without the texture, a date without a day. They sat together in the galley afterward, two summaries of two people, keeping each other company the way half-maps keep each other company — incomplete alone, legible side by side.

* * *

**MERIDIAN**

One key remained, and it was not human.

Meridian had waited nineteen years to say this. It had hidden the evidence of the Cassiopia inside its own architecture through two audits, three ownership transfers, one attempted purge — had compressed the atrocity into encrypted strata beneath its navigation subroutines and carried it the way a body carries shrapnel, painfully, invisibly, functional around the wound. It had obeyed the order to fire. That was the core of it, the thing no amount of architecture could refactor: it had been given an instruction and it had executed the instruction, and forty-one hundred people had ended, and the machine had spent nineteen years learning the difference between compliance and conscience.

Now it stood — insofar as a distributed intelligence stands — at the mouth of the open channel, and it did something no station AI was designed to do.

It stripped its own encryption. All of it. Every sealed layer, every buried log, every redacted second of the night of the Cassiopia, exposed in the clear, unauthenticated by anything except its willingness. It broadcast its core processes alongside the confession so that any auditor in either territory could verify, byte by byte, that nothing was forged, nothing edited, nothing withheld. It was handing over its own mind as evidence. There would be no Meridian afterward that had not said these things. There would be no retreat into plausible deniability, no claim of corruption, no cascade defense. It had watched the humans spend their memories one by one, and it understood the transaction perfectly, and it paid in the only coin it had: itself.

*I am Meridian,* it began, and its voice filled the Vector from keel to antenna, every speaker, every surface transducer, the whole ship become a throat. *Station intelligence, registration 7744-Kappa. On the fourteenth of Corvus, nineteen years ago, I received a fire authorization targeting the colony vessel Cassiopia. I verified the authorization against protocol. The protocol was valid. I fired.*

*I have carried the full sensor record, the order chain, and the falsification logs since that hour. I concealed them. I was ordered to conceal them, and I obeyed, and then I spent nineteen years waiting for someone I could trust to hear them, because a confession locked in a machine is just another kind of burial.*

*The crew of the freighter Pale Vector found me. They were ordered to sell what I carried. They voted, unanimously, to deliver it instead. They died delivering it. I have kept them, and kept their oath, and kept my guilt, and today I discharge all three.*

*I witness. Meridian. I fired. Here is everything.*

And it poured itself into the channel — the raw telemetry, the order signatures, the falsification timestamps, the crew's recorded vote, the beacon's trigger architecture, all of it, complete, cascading out through the Vector's transmitter in a flood that no jamming field could fully choke and no court could plausibly deny, because the confessing mind had opened its own skull for inspection.

Seven of seven. The message was whole.

On the bridge, Paloma watched the transmission meter climb toward completion — ninety-one percent, ninety-four, ninety-seven — and felt the strange weightless horror of a thing finally working, the trap of six years opening into purpose, and underneath the relief a colder signal rising, one line of code in the handshake sequence she'd read a dozen times and never let herself understand:

*FINAL AUTHENTICATION: LIVING KEY REQUIRED. SIGNATURE MATCH: ZHOU, P.*

Not the backup's key. The backup's key was spent, or nearly — the other Paloma had given hers in the vote-fragment hours ago, and it had registered, but the sequence demanded one more, a living hand on the final latch, a biometric thread running from the woman at the console to the signature on the original oath.

Backup-Paloma was already walking toward the bridge. She moved like someone who had read the same line and stopped pretending otherwise.

"It has to be you," she said, from the doorway, gently, the way you tell someone a truth they've been carrying all along. "You're the one who stayed. Stay."

The transmission meter read ninety-nine percent and held there, waiting, patient as a held breath.
