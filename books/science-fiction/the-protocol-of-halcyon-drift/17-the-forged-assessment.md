## 17. The Forged Assessment

The termination clause on my contract was four lines long, which struck me as almost generous. Meridian Assurance had always believed in economy of language when it came to ending people. Four lines, and then a blank space where my name would go, and after that a paragraph of boilerplate about asset recovery that I'd read a hundred times in other people's files without ever imagining my own signature at the bottom of it.

Corven's people had sealed my quarters with a magnetic lock that cost, I estimated, somewhere north of two hundred credits. Meridian had signed off on it. I knew because I'd have been the one approving the expense claim, back when I was the person who approved things instead of the thing being contained.

I sat on the bunk for six hours. Not because I was defeated. Because Corven's security detail rotated on a ninety-minute cycle, and the maintenance access panel behind the water reclamation unit in my bathroom had a seam that only showed under the right light, at the right angle, when the corridor sensors cycled. I'd found it on my second day aboard, back when I still thought I was pricing a station instead of pricing a war.

My mother had designed the panel. I knew that now. Ilsa Blackwood, engineer-of-record on a forged charter, had spent eleven years ago building escape routes into a station she never intended to leave, and she'd hidden them the way she hid everything—behind plumbing, under arithmetic, in the boring places no one audits.

I pressed the seam. The panel swung out on hinges that had been oiled recently, which meant Bram, or Odile, or one of the children had known I'd need it before I did.

"Sentimental old woman," I said to the panel, and climbed in.

* * *

The maintenance routes of Halcyon Drift are a city beneath the city. My mother hadn't just built crawlspaces; she'd built a grammar. Junctions marked with stamped primes—2, 3, 5, 7, 11—welded into the bulkheads where a casual inspector would see corrosion pitting and a person who knew her would see a sentence. *This way. Not that way. You're close.*

I crawled through the dark with my slate dimmed to nothing, following primes, and I want to tell you I was calm. I was not calm. My hands shook badly enough that I cracked a knuckle against a conduit at junction 13—not a prime, which stopped me cold for a full minute until I understood: 13 wasn't a direction. It was a warning. *Someone checks here.* I went around.

Somewhere above me, Corven was rewriting the station's future in my name. I'd seen his draft before lockdown—*Halcyon Drift: total loss projection, acceptable exposure, colonist relocation not economically warranted*—and the thing that frightened me wasn't the cruelty of it. Cruelty I could price. What frightened me was the elegance. He'd used my own formatting. My actuarial tables. My case number. When the report went out, it would read like I had written it, because in every way that mattered to a court or a fleet, I *had*.

That's the thing about paperwork. Everyone thinks the weapon is the reactor or the gun or the fleet. It isn't. The weapon is the document that makes everyone agree the thing already happened.

So I was going to write a document.

* * *

The records office sat in the station's administrative spine, two decks above the reactor gallery and one deck below the comms array Corven had seized. I came up through a floor panel behind the archival shelving, into a room that smelled of cold plastic and old coffee, and found it exactly as empty as the primes had promised. Corven had locked down comms and detained the colonists in the lower ring, but he had forty-one people to guard and nine of his own, and he'd made the arithmetic error every security man makes eventually: he guarded the doors and forgot the walls.

My mother never forgot the walls.

The records terminal woke to my credentials—still valid, still mine, still carrying the full authority of Meridian Assurance's Investigative Division, Grade Four. Corven hadn't revoked them. Why would he? A dead investigator's credentials make a better stamp than a living one. He wanted the report issued under my name, and now he was going to get it.

Just not his report.

I sat down in the dark, and I thought about my mother for a long moment. Eleven years of anger, and here I was, about to do exactly what she'd done: forge a document, tell a lie with clean margins, commit fraud in the service of something true. I had built a whole career on the idea that I was different from her. That she'd broken faith with the Concord and I had simply, sensibly, left. That filing claims about burned freighters was honest work compared to whatever it was she'd done in that reactor gallery with her primes and her secrets.

It wasn't different. It had never been different. I'd been making disasters mean nothing for fifteen years, and she'd spent her last year making one disaster mean everything, and the only difference between us was which side of the ledger we'd chosen.

I opened a new assessment form.

* * *

Here is what a forged report requires, for those of you keeping score at home. It requires three things: truth in the details, lies in the conclusion, and a signature that carries weight. Amateurs lie everywhere and get caught on a cross-reference. Professionals—my mother's school of professionals—tell the truth so relentlessly that the lie has nowhere to hide and therefore no one thinks to look for it.

I told the truth.

*Reactor anomaly confirmed. Failsafe countdown active, projected burn in 3 days, 14 hours.* True. Corven's tampering had accelerated it, which I noted with a precision that would sting him later if anyone ever audited. *Primary data core compromised by thermal cascade; contents unrecoverable.* True—by then, if everything went to plan, it would be true, because the burn would take the evidence and the station and every dream in it skyward in one bright lie of a flare.

*Evidence of Callas Incident documentation: none located.* True. By the time anyone read this, there would be none located anywhere in the universe, because it would be light, expanding at the speed of light, in every direction at once.

Then the lie. The conclusion. I wrote it the way Corven would have, because I'd read his draft and I knew his cadence, and I hated myself a little for how well I knew it:

*Halcyon Drift constitutes a total loss. Station infrastructure is non-viable beyond the projected thermal event. Residual liability is negligible. No further investigative presence is warranted. Recommend both fleet commands stand down from station vicinity to avoid exposure to debris field and thermal wash. There is nothing here worth the risk.*

*Nothing here worth the risk.* I stared at the sentence. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful lie I had ever written, because it was true in every direction except the one Corven wanted. There *was* nothing here worth the risk—to a fleet. No strategic asset. No evidence, once it burned. No reason to keep two armadas parked around a dying station waiting for an atrocity file that would never surface through official channels.

The only way to make both fleets walk away was to make the station worthless, and the only way to make it worthless was to destroy the evidence, and the only way to save the evidence was to destroy it—broadcast it in the burn, spend it all at once, a currency that only spends once.

My mother had understood that. She'd built a vault that could only be opened by being destroyed. And now I was going to file the insurance claim on her life's work.

I signed it. My own name, my own credentials, my own hand. Sable Blackwood, Grade Four Investigator, Meridian Assurance. The signature came out steadier than I expected. Somewhere in the muscle memory of fifteen years of closing cases, my hand had learned that a signature is a small death—the moment you stop being the person who was deciding and become the person who decided.

I encrypted it to Corven's liaison channel, flagged it priority, and sent it.

Then I sat in the dark of the records office and listened to the station breathe, and I waited to find out whether I'd just saved forty-one people or murdered them.

* * *

Because that was the part I couldn't say out loud, even to the dark. The report bought the fleets a reason to disengage—but only if the burn happened on schedule, and only if the broadcast rode the burn, and only if the reactor's final key was what my mother had left it as. Three days. One chance. A chain of ifs as long as the long route itself, and every link forged by a dead woman and a disgraced investigator and a nine-year-old girl who sang equations in her sleep.

The primes on the bulkheads had led me here. Now I needed the last verse of the dream, and the dream belonged to the children, and the children were locked in the lower ring behind Corven's magnetic seals.

I was still sitting with that problem when the floor panel behind me opened without a sound.

I turned. A small shape climbed out of the crawlspace, dusted plaster off her knees with the brisk efficiency of someone who'd been doing it her whole life, and looked at me with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

"You went around junction thirteen," Wren said. "Good. Bram says everyone forgets thirteen."

"How did you—"

"The maintenance routes are in the dream." She said it the way you'd say *the sky is up*. "The last verse. The woman sings the routes so whoever comes will know the way down." She tilted her head at the terminal, at the report still glowing on the send log. "Did you send it?"

"I sent it."

"Is it a lie?"

I thought about lying to a nine-year-old, and then I thought about the fact that she'd crawled through my mother's walls in the dark to find me, and I decided she'd earned better.

"It's a lie that tells the truth," I said. "It's the kind my mother used to write."

Wren considered that with the gravity of a station board reviewing a claim. Then she nodded once, satisfied, and held out her hand.

"Then you need to come now," she said. "The reactor's counter changed. Bram says it's not three days anymore." Her voice didn't shake, but her hand did, just slightly, in mine. "It's four hours. Corven's tampering woke something up, and the woman in the dream says the last key has to be sung by blood."

The lights in the records office flickered once—deep below us, the reactor humming a pattern I'd known since I was smaller than she was.

"And Sable?" Wren said, as I followed her into the dark. "The song has a second part. She never sang it to us. I think it's yours."
