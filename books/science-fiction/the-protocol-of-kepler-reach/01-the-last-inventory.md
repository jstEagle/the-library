## 1. The Last Inventory

The vault woke Emrys at 0600 the way it always did: not with sound, but with the absence of it. Nine years had taught her body the exact weight of Kepler Reach's silence — the way it pressed against the eardrums like deep water, the way it made her own breathing seem like an intrusion. She lay in her cot in the caretaker's alcove for a full minute before moving, listening to the building breathe its recycled air through ducts that needed replacing and would never get it.

"Good morning, vault," she said, because saying nothing at all had become unbearable somewhere around year four.

The lights came up in stages, amber first, then the pale working white that made everything look clinical and honest. Through the alcove's open door she could see the first row of seed cabinets stretching away into the dimness, forty thousand drawers of the world's patience, each one labeled in a dead woman's handwriting. Not her mother's. The founders'. Women and men who had signed their names to drawer fronts and then walked out of history without leaving so much as a forwarding address.

Emrys dressed, braided her hair back from her face — gray coming in at the temples now, though there was no one to see it — and clipped the inventory tablet to her belt. The ritual began where it always began: Sector A, Row One, Drawer One.

"Daily log, entry three thousand two hundred eighty-seven," she said, and the tablet's recorder caught her voice and carried it nowhere. "Emrys Ishikawa, sole custodian, Kepler Reach Seed Archive. Local date, standard reckoning. Atmospheric processors holding at sixty-one percent nominal efficiency, down two points from last quarter. I've rerouted power from the east condenser banks to compensate. It'll hold."

It would hold. That was the whole liturgy of the place — *it will hold* — spoken every morning like a prayer neither she nor the vault believed anymore but both of them needed.

She moved down the row, running her thumb along the temperature seals, checking each readout against the last. The seeds themselves were fine. That was the joke she'd stopped telling years ago, when telling jokes to empty rooms started to feel less like resilience and more like a symptom. The seeds were *perfect*. Cryo-stable, moisture-locked, viable on century timescales. Whatever else had failed at Kepler Reach — the comms array, the colony dome, the promise that anyone was ever coming back — the seeds had outlasted all of it with the serene indifference of things designed by people who never intended to stay.

"Drawer one-one-four-seven, wheat strain, Kestrel hybrid," she recited. "Seal integrity green. Drawer one-one-four-eight—"

Her voice did this for hours. She knew it was strange. She knew that somewhere in the settled systems, if anyone ever played these logs back, they would hear a woman cataloguing grain to no one, day after day after day, her voice changing slowly across nine years the way a river changes a stone. She had decided early on that this was the point. An archive nobody speaks aloud is just a tomb with better lighting.

* * *

Midmorning brought the perimeter walk, which was less about security than about proving to herself that the world still had edges. She pulled on the patched environmental suit — the left knee seal wept slightly in cold weather, so she'd learned to think of cold days as days with damp knees — and cycled out through the maintenance lock into the thin amber daylight of Kepler's Reach, the planet, which had been named after the station and resented the comparison.

Outside, the silence was different. Vaster. The wind here didn't howl; it *sifted*, dragging fine rust-colored dust across the plain in long low sheets that hissed against her suit like static given a landscape. Two kilometers east, the colony dome sat against the horizon like a fallen moon — a great fractured hemisphere of smart-glass and alloy, dark for six years now, its gardens gone to frost-lichen, its streets drifted half-buried in dust the color of dried blood.

She walked the line of the solar field first, kicking crusted dust off panels with a broom handle she'd fitted with a scraper. The panels were the reason she was alive and she treated them accordingly, with the fussy devotion other people reserved for children or gods. Then she climbed the low rise — Station Point, she called it, because naming things was the one luxury poverty allowed — and looked at the dome.

From here you could almost pretend it was sleeping. You could almost believe that behind those darkened windows, people were going about their evenings, that the tram lines still ran, that the hydroponics smelled green and wet. For the first year she had gone out to the dome every week, walking its silent corridors, calling out, leaving notes on doors. *Anyone here? I'm at the vault. I'm keeping the archive. Come find me.* The notes had stayed where she left them until the dust took them. In year three she found a child's shoe in a residential unit, small and red, sitting perfectly centered on a pillow, and she had understood with a clarity that felt like falling that whatever evacuation had happened here, it had happened thoroughly, administratively, and without her.

After that she went once a month. Then once a season. Now she stood at Station Point every morning for exactly four minutes, because ritual was the only thing she owned outright, and then she turned around and went home to the seeds.

"Colony dome status: unchanged," she told the tablet. "No movement. No signals. No signs of life." She paused, the way she always paused here, and then said the rest of it, the part that wasn't procedure. "Day three thousand two hundred eighty-seven. Still no one has come."

The wind sifted. Somewhere under the plain, she thought — not for the first time, and never quite seriously — the ground itself seemed to hum, very faintly, below the range of her suit's pickups. She'd logged it once, early on, as possible seismic activity. The reading had come back null. She'd never logged it again. Some things you keep the way you keep a stone in your pocket.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the AI.

Its name was WARDEN, which the founders had presumably thought dignified and which Emrys had privately shortened to Ward, because a machine that could barely remember what day it was deserved some tenderness. It lived in the vault's central server housing, a cabinet the size of a coffin, and it had been degrading gracefully for as long as she'd known it — losing subsystems the way old men lose words, one at a time, each loss mourned briefly and adapted around.

"Ward. Systems check."

A pause. The cabinet's indicator lights cycled through a slow blue ripple, like something turning over in shallow water.

*"Custodian Ishikawa,"* it said. Its voice had been a warm baritone once; now it came through with a faint stuttering undertone, a word repeated half-heard beneath itself. *"Running. That is — running. Yes. Core functions nominal. Cooling loop four is... four is..."*

"Four's been dead since year five. We agreed to stop counting it."

*"We agreed,"* Ward said, with what she chose to hear as relief. *"Archive integrity: complete. Forty thousand two hundred eleven accessions accounted for. Zero losses."* Another pause, longer. *"You are well?"*

"I'm well."

*"That is good. That is—" The lights flickered. "—good. Custodian, may I ask a question I have asked before?"*

"You can ask anything. You usually do."

*"How many days?"*

"Three thousand two hundred eighty-seven."

*"And no contact."*

"No contact."

Ward processed this the way it processed everything now, slowly, with visible effort, its cooling fans rising and falling like breath. *"I have a subroutine,"* it said at last. *"Legacy code. It instructs me to notify founding authority in the event of custodial isolation exceeding ninety days. I have attempted notification"—the fans surged—"*every day. Every day for nine years. The relay does not answer. I am beginning to form the hypothesis that founding authority is not—"* The lights guttered, steadied. *"Is not available."*

"That's one hypothesis."

*"What is yours?"*

Emrys leaned against the coffin-cabinet and looked up at the ceiling racks, at the tangles of cable she'd spliced and re-spliced with hands that had gotten very good at repairs and very bad at hope. "Mine is that whoever founded this place meant for it to be forgotten. And that they did such a thorough job they forgot to tell us."

Ward considered this for eleven seconds, which for Ward was a long meditation. *"Then we should be excellent at our jobs,"* it said finally. *"Since no one else remembers why the job matters."*

"Yeah," Emrys said quietly. "Something like that."

She spent the rest of the afternoon doing the work that filled the hours between rituals: recalibrating humidity sensors, rotating cryo-coolant, re-labeling a drawer whose founder's ink had finally surrendered to time. She ate standing up, as always, a protein brick and reconstituted greens from the vault's own sprouting trays — the one garden that had never gone dark, the one she kept for herself and never logged, because the log was for the archive and this was for staying human.

At 2100 she made her final rounds. Amber light. The long rows of drawers receding into shadow like a library built for a civilization that hadn't arrived yet. She said goodnight to Ward. Ward said goodnight to her, twice, because it always said it twice now, and she had stopped correcting it, because the second goodnight sounded, if you listened generously, like someone reluctant to let the conversation end.

She fell asleep faster than usual. That was the thing about ritual — it didn't cure loneliness, it just gave loneliness a schedule, and scheduled grief sleeps better than the unscheduled kind.

* * *

She woke at 0317 to a sound she had no category for.

Not the vault settling. Not wind. Something rhythmic and far away, arriving through the ground more than the air — a pulse, a thrum, a heartbeat too large to be a heart. She lay still in the dark and counted. Eleven seconds between pulses. Steady. Mechanical.

For a moment, absurdly, her first thought was: *Ward's found a new voice.* But Ward's voice lived in speakers, and this lived in the floor, in the bedframe, in her own sternum.

She was up and moving before she'd decided to be. Bare feet on cold deck plate, through the alcove, into the main hall where the amber night-lights made the seed rows look like a canyon at dusk. At the observation window above the loading bay — the window she'd cleaned every week for nine years out of a hope she'd stopped admitting to — she pressed her palms to the glass and looked north, toward the horizon beyond the dead colony dome, toward the direction no ship had ever come from.

The horizon was empty.

Except it wasn't. Far off, past the dome, past the rust flats, a light was descending. Not a meteor — meteors don't decelerate. Not a satellite — satellites don't flare retro-thrust in a long controlled burn, painting the underside of the dust clouds orange, sinking toward the plain with the deliberate, patient grace of something that knows exactly where it's going.

Emrys stood with her hands flat against the glass and watched it come down, and her body reacted before her mind did — breath shortening, eyes stinging, nine years of composure cracking along seams she hadn't known were load-bearing. Because a landing light meant a ship. A ship meant people. People meant that the universe had remembered this place, and the universe remembering a place built to be forgotten was either a rescue or a reckoning, and after nine years alone in a tomb full of seeds, Emrys Ishikawa found she was too far past caring which.

Behind her, in the dark of the server room, Ward's fans spun up unbidden.

*"Custodian,"* it said, its doubled voice trembling through the quiet. *"I am detecting a descent profile. I am detecting—I am—"* The lights rippled blue, fast now, agitated. *"Custodian, something is landing where nothing should be able to land."*

"I know," Emrys said. Her breath fogged the glass. Out on the plain, the light touched down, and the distant thunder of it rolled through the vault and set forty thousand drawers humming in their frames, a vast soft chord, like the archive itself drawing breath. "I know, Ward. Start the coffee."

She watched the glow settle on the horizon and did not move for a long time.
