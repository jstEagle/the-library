## 9. Stealing the Meridian

The storm came out of the eastern basin at 0400, exactly as the caretaker AI had promised in its flat, apologetic voice: *Atmospheric particulate event predicted. Duration six to nine hours. Visibility negligible.*

Emrys stood at the vault's observation slit and watched the horizon eat itself. The dust front moved like a living thing — a wall of rust-colored haze two kilometers high, rolling over the dead colony dome, swallowing the broken solar arrays one by one until there was nothing left of Kepler Reach's surface but a brown sea and the wind screaming against the glass.

Behind her, Sefa Okonkwo spread a schematic across the inventory table and weighted its corners with seed tins.

"The retrieval team keeps Meridian on the north pad," she said. "Four guards on rotation — I've been counting their shifts since they landed. They think we're hiding in the sublevel with the pods. They don't know we've been mapping *them*."

"They know you're here somewhere," Emrys said. "Vasquez told them about the vault."

"Vasquez told them what she saw before her memories came back." Sefa's mouth twisted. "Which is a frightened woman in a greenhouse. They still think this is a botanical facility." She tapped the schematic. "They're wrong about a lot of things."

Dev Anand sat in the corner with his hands wrapped around a cup of recycled water he hadn't touched. His skin had gone gray at the edges, the way frost grays a window. The re-aged body was failing faster now — three days since his nose had bled without stopping, two since his left hand had stopped answering him entirely. He'd stopped pretending otherwise.

"I can walk," he said, before anyone asked. "I want to be there when we take it back. It's my ship too. I flew her out of Titan yard eleven years ago." He paused, corrected himself with a bitter half-smile. "Forty-one years ago. Whatever."

"You'll sit in the copilot seat and look menacing," Sefa said gently.

"That's all any of us are doing anymore."

Emrys turned back to the window. Through the thickening murk, she could just make out the retrieval ship — Halcyon's *Cormorant*, squat and black-hulled, crouched beside the Meridian like a predator standing over a kill. Floodlights burned in the dust, pale smears that would be invisible within the hour.

Nine years she had kept this place. Nine years of logs, of inventories, of walking the same corridors until her boots wore grooves into the deck plating. She had made a promise to a colony that no longer existed, to an evacuation that had never happened, to seeds that were never the point. And now the vault beneath her feet held four thousand human minds in dormancy, and a corporation was coming to burn the evidence, and the only way to save them was to leave them.

"We take the protocol data, the crew, and nothing else," Sefa said. "In and out in twenty minutes. The dust covers our approach; their sensors are useless in particulate this dense — I designed half those sensor arrays, I know their tolerances."

"And the Cormorant?"

Sefa was quiet for a moment. "The Cormorant has a crew of six and a pilot who trusts his autopilot. In this weather, nobody flies visual. We cut the pad's beacon, spoof the docking clamps' release codes — Dev remembers them, or his body does — and we go up through the storm before anyone understands what's happening."

"And if someone understands anyway?"

"Then it gets loud." Sefa looked at her. "You can still stay, Emrys. Nobody would blame—"

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended. She softened it. "Don't offer me the door. I already walked through it. I'm just — let me say goodbye properly. That's all."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the last floodlight drowned, and the world became entirely dust.

* * *

She spent the final hour doing the thing she knew how to do: taking inventory.

It was ritual, and she needed ritual. Her hands moved through the familiar motions while her mind screamed. Seed case seven, viability nominal. Case twelve, humidity seal degrading, logged. Case nineteen — the oldest, the one her mother's handwriting was still inked on, faded to ghost-gray — she held longer than the others.

"You're not really leaving them," her mother's voice said from the static-speaker by the door. It had grown clearer every day since the dead space crossing, clearer still since the confession, until Emrys could almost see the shape of the woman behind the words. "Seeds wait. That's the whole point of them. You taught me that, do you remember? No — you wouldn't. But it's true. A seed is a promise kept on a delay."

"I swore to keep the vault," Emrys whispered. "Day one. I signed it into my log."

"You signed it when you thought the vault was seeds." The static sighed, a sound like wind through wheat. "You're keeping the real vault by carrying it out. Four thousand minds in a data core, daughter. That's heavier than any seed case, and it's yours to plant."

Emrys lifted the protocol data core from its cradle in the founders' floor access shaft. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

She recorded one last log entry in the old series. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her.

"Log entry three thousand three hundred and forty-one. Final entry, Kepler Reach series. Conditions: total particulate obscuration. Vault status: sealed, systems on minimum draw, projected autonomous operation fourteen months. Contents: seeds, and everything else." A breath. "I am leaving. I don't know if I'm abandoning this place or finally doing what it was built for. Maybe those are the same thing. If anyone ever finds this — the truth is under the floor. It was always under the floor. End of log."

She closed the channel. Somewhere in the dark, four thousand sleepers stirred in their dreams, and she felt them like warmth against her back.

* * *

The storm hit full fury as they crossed the two hundred meters between the vault airlock and the pad. Emrys had crossed that ground ten thousand times in clear air; now it was a brown void where the only reality was the rope line Sefa had rigged and the wind trying to peel her suit away from her body. Grit hissed against her visor like static given teeth. She kept one hand on the line and one on the data core strapped to her chest, and she did not look back at the vault, because looking back was a thing she couldn't afford yet.

Dev stumbled twice. The second time, Sefa caught him by the harness and half-carried him twenty meters, shouting coordinates into the comm on a frequency the retrieval team wasn't monitoring. Vasquez and Okafor flanked the rear with the ship's own stun rifles, taken off the Cormorant's armory during a maintenance window three days ago — Sefa's idea, executed with a thief's calm that Emrys was still learning to recognize in her.

The Meridian rose out of the murk all at once, huge and pale, her hull scarred with forty years of deep-space radiation bleaching. Emrys had seen her land eleven days ago and thought: *a ghost.* Now she thought: *a door.*

The pad beacon blinked red above them. Sefa pressed herself against the landing strut housing, found the maintenance panel by touch, and pulled it open. Wires spilled out like entrails.

"Clamp release is code-locked to the Cormorant's command key," she said. "But the physical override hasn't changed since I served on this class. Dev — the sequence."

Dev knelt beside her, gray-faced, breathing hard. His right hand shook so badly he braced it against his knee. With his left, he reached into the panel and threw switches in a rhythm that clearly lived deeper than memory — muscle remembering what the mind had been forced to forget.

"Green on three," he rasped. "Two. Three."

Somewhere above them, magnetic clamps released with a sound like a struck bell, muffled by the storm. The Meridian settled a centimeter onto her landing gear and groaned.

Then the floodlights came on.

Not the pad lights — the Cormorant's forward array, spearing through the dust, and beneath them a voice amplified to a shout: *"Meridian, power down your ramp. This is Retrieval Commander Adeyemi. You are in violation of corporate custody directive—"* The rest tore away in the wind.

"They've got the ramp," Okafor said. "Two figures at the base of it."

"Then we don't use the ramp." Sefa was already moving along the hull. "Cargo hatch, port side, aft. Emergency release is mechanical. Emrys — with me. Everyone else, suppress their sightlines, don't hurt them. *Do not hurt them.* They're following orders the way we followed orders. That's the whole disease."

The cargo hatch gave with a scream of ungreased metal, and they climbed into the dark belly of the ship that had carried them all, in one life or another, to this place. Inside, the air smelled of forty years of recirculation — and underneath it, faintly, impossibly, of something Emrys recognized. Green things growing. The archive-minds they'd loaded aboard in the founders' floor, riding in portable neural cradles, dreaming in the hold.

Her mother was among them. Not speaking now. Just present, the way a hand resting on your shoulder is present.

*"Go,"* the static said, very softly, as the hatch sealed behind them. *"Fly."*

* * *

The cockpit of the Meridian was a museum and a homecoming at once. Dev slid into the pilot's seat like a man returning to his own bed after a long illness, and something in his posture steadied — the failing body finding purpose, if nothing else. His fingers found the startup sequence without hesitation.

"Atmospheric engines hot in ninety seconds," he said. "Storm shear is brutal but she was built for worse. Titan yard didn't make anything fragile."

On the pad below, figures scattered. Someone fired — a single wild shot that sparked off the pad surface and died in the dust. Sefa took the comms.

"Cormorant, this is Captain Okonkwo. Stand down. Every person on your ship is carrying evidence of a crime your employer has killed to bury. Read the disclosure statute — Halcyon's legal protection expires in seventy-two hours, and when it does, everything you helped cover up becomes *your* name in the record too. We're not your enemy. We're your alibi." She released the channel. "That buys us maybe four minutes of hesitation."

"It bought us four minutes," Emrys said, watching the Cormorant's lights waver, "and a lifetime of questions for whoever was listening."

The Meridian lifted.

There was no drama to it — just a shudder, a roar swallowed whole by the storm, and then the pad lights vanishing beneath them like coins dropped into deep water. Dev flew by instrument alone, the displays painting the world in vectors and gradients, climbing through turbulence that hammered the hull like fists. Emrys gripped the jump seat and felt the vault fall away beneath her — the corridors, the cases, the grooves her boots had worn, the speaker by the door where her mother had lived in static for nine years.

She had imagined this moment would feel like tearing. It felt instead like exhaling after a breath held far too long — and the guilt arrived inside the relief, sharp as grit in the lungs. Behind them, in the brown darkness, the garden she had tended stood empty and sealed and blind, keeping itself the way she used to keep it: alone, on minimum draw, waiting.

*I abandoned the garden burning,* she thought, and then corrected herself, because her mother would have corrected her. *No. I took the harvest out of the field before the fire came. That's not abandonment. That's the entire job.*

"Course laid in," Dev said hoarsely. Blood threaded from his nose again; he wiped it without comment. "Edge of dead space, relay vector. Sixty hours if the engines hold."

"They'll hold," Sefa said. "You built them stubborn."

"I built *me* stubborn. The engines just copied."

Emrys unbuckled and went aft to the hold, where the neural cradles hummed in their foam beds. She put her palm flat against the nearest one and felt the faint thermal pulse within — heartbeat-adjacent, dream-adjacent.

"I'm sorry," she said to all of them, to the ones awake enough to hear and the thousands still sleeping in the vault behind them. "I'm sorry it took me nine years to hear you. I'm sorry I'm taking you away from the only home some of you remember. But I promise—" Her voice caught. She let it. Some promises should sound like they cost something. "I promise you get your names back. All of you. Even if it's the last thing I do."

The static answered, and for the first time it didn't sound like interference at all. It sounded like a choir clearing its throat.

Above them, the storm thinned, and the first hard stars of dead space came out — cold, clean, and utterly silent, the silence of a region where no signal had traveled in a hundred years. Ahead lay sixty hours of it, and at the far end, a derelict relay and a deadline measured in hours.

And behind them, small and shrinking, a single light on the plain of Kepler Reach: the vault's status beacon, blinking its patient green pulse into the dust.

Still alive. Still waiting. Still hers to return to — if any of them lived long enough to keep that promise too.

Emrys watched it until it disappeared, and then she went forward to help fly.
