## 14. The Relay at the Edge

The relay appeared first as a scar against the stars — a black line where the starfield stuttered, as if someone had dragged an eraser across the sky. Emrys saw it from the Meridian's observation blister and felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't known was clenched. Nine years of looking at the same dome, the same dust, the same four walls of her life, and now: a shape she had never seen before.

"That's it?" Sefa asked beside her. "That's the thing we crossed dead space for?"

The relay hung in orbit around a cinder of a moon, turning with the patience of something that had been forgotten on purpose. It was old — older than the colony, older than Halcyon Combine's charter, maybe older than any of the settlements it had once stitched together. A spine of dark alloy a kilometer long, bristling with antenna arrays like the ribs of some leviathan that had beached itself among the stars and been picked clean by time. Half its solar sails were torn to lace. The other half caught the distant sun and threw back a dull copper gleam, the only color in all that black.

"It was built during the First Expansion," Emrys said. "Before the corporate consolidations. Back when governments still pretended the frontier belonged to everyone." She had read about relays like this in the vault's surviving texts, back when she'd still believed the vault was what it claimed to be. "It doesn't broadcast anymore. It just listens. It's been listening for two hundred years to no one."

"Then we'll give it something worth hearing."

* * *

Docking took three hours because nothing aboard the Meridian worked the way it was supposed to anymore. The ship had been built for a crew of forty; they were six, counting Emrys, and half their systems ran on jury-rigged logic cores that Sefa's engineer, Priya Chandra, coaxed along with threats and tenderness in equal measure. The archived minds rode with them the way weather rides with a ship — invisible, everywhere, pressing at the edges of things.

Emrys felt them most strongly in the quiet hours. Since leaving Kepler Reach, since crossing into signal-dead space where no corporate interference could smother them, the static had become voices. Not all at once — that would have been mercy — but one at a time, surfacing like drowned things rising through dark water. A man who recited shipping manifests from a port Emrys had never visited. A woman who sang fragments of songs in three languages. A child who asked, over and over, gentle and terrible, *are we there yet?*

And beneath them all, steady as a heartbeat, her mother.

*You're doing well, little light.* The voice came while Emrys strapped herself into the docking harness, calm and close, as if Ilsa Ishikawa were sitting beside her instead of folded into a neural archive somewhere below decks. *Steady hands. Your father would have been proud of how you dock.*

*My father never saw me dock anything,* Emrys thought back, not sure if the minds could hear her thinking or only feel the shape of her attention.

*I saw. I always saw.*

She closed her eyes and let that sit inside her until the docking clamps caught with a shudder that traveled up through the deck plates and into her teeth.

* * *

The relay's interior was a cathedral that had been left to rot.

They entered through a maintenance lock whose seals cracked like old knuckles, and their helmet lamps swept across a space so vast that the beams simply gave up before reaching the far wall. Corridors wide enough to drive cargo haulers through. Ceilings lost in darkness. Dust — actual dust, undisturbed for generations — lay over everything in gray velvet drifts, and when they walked, their footprints bloomed behind them like wounds in the silence.

Emrys had expected dereliction. She hadn't expected beauty. Along the main transmission hall, someone — some crew, two centuries dead — had etched a frieze into the walls: human figures passing light hand to hand, station to station, world to world, all the way down the corridor and out of sight. The figures were crude, worn smooth by time, but the intention survived. *We were a chain once,* the frieze said. *Each of us holding up our small piece of the sky.*

"The transmitter core is this way," Priya said, checking her slate against schematics she'd reconstructed from fragments. "If the old survey data is right."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we're standing in a very large tomb with limited air." Priya smiled, and Emrys was learning to read these people — the returned, the re-aged, the walking evidence — and she knew that smile was how Priya held fear at arm's length. "Come on. History won't wait."

They found the core at the relay's heart: a chamber shaped like an egg stood on end, its walls lined with interface couches, each one cradling a harness of contact filaments gone brittle as autumn grass. At the center rose the transmitter itself — a column of dark crystal two meters tall, humming with the faintest residual charge, like a bell that remembered ringing.

Sefa ran her gloved hand above its surface without touching. "Priya. Can we power it?"

"With enough time? Yes. The solar array's shot, but the Meridian can feed it directly." Priya was already pulling panels, tracing conduits with the focused ferocity Emrys had come to admire. "Give me twelve hours and I'll have the transmitter ready to sing."

"Twelve hours until we can broadcast," Sefa said. "How long until Halcyon arrives?"

Nobody answered, which was answer enough. The fleet had been three days behind them when they'd made the crossing. Dead space had slowed everyone equally. Which meant the clock wasn't hours away. It was already ticking.

Emrys drifted along the row of interface couches, reading the faded stenciled labels. OPERATOR STATION 1. OPERATOR STATION 2. And then, smaller, on a plaque bolted to the central console, text in a language style so archaic it took her a moment to parse:

**DIRECT NEURAL INTERFACE REQUIRED FOR FULL-SPECTRUM TRANSMISSION. PARTIAL BROADCAST MAY BE ACHIEVED VIA CONSOLE ONLY. WARNING: DIRECT INTERFACE CARRIES RISK OF COGNITIVE DISSOLUTION. OPERATOR DISCRETION ADVISED.**

She read it twice. Then a third time, because the words refused to arrange themselves into meaning she could carry.

"Sefa," she said quietly. "Come look at this."

* * *

They gathered around the plaque like mourners around a headstone. Priya translated the archaic phrasing aloud, her voice flattening further with each sentence, until the chamber's silence pressed in around them.

"In plain terms," Priya finished, "the console alone can push a narrow-band signal. Text, maybe compressed audio. It would take weeks to transmit the full protocol and archive that way — and the signal would be weak enough for Halcyon to jam before it cleared the system." She looked up. "Full-spectrum broadcast — everything, all at once, unjammable, undeniable — requires a human mind in direct contact with the archive. The operator becomes the bridge. The archive pours through them into the transmitter."

"And the dissolution risk?" Dev Anand asked. He stood slightly apart from the others, as he had since his body had begun failing, leaning against the doorframe with the careful economy of a man conserving what strength remained. His voice was soft. "What does cognitive dissolution actually mean?"

Priya didn't answer. Neither did Sefa. It was the static that answered — rising through the chamber like cold water filling a hold, and Emrys felt her mother's presence gather close, felt the words form not in her ears but in the deep tissue of her mind:

*It means the bridge burns. It means the signal carries you away with it, piece by piece, memory by memory, until what's left is a voice with no one behind it. We have watched operators do this before, long ago, when this relay was young. They called it the honor post. None of them came back whole. Some did not come back at all.*

Emrys repeated it aloud, because the others deserved to hear it in a human voice, even though speaking it made it real in a way she wanted desperately to refuse.

The silence afterward had weight. Dust drifted through their lamplight. Somewhere far above, the ruined solar sails ticked against their moorings, patient as a clock.

"So we use the console," Dev said finally. "Narrow band. We send what we can."

"Weeks," Sefa said. "Halcyon will be here in days."

"Then we send the names first. Just the names. Let them—"

"They'll jam it, Dev. You know they will. A whisper gets buried. That's the entire architecture of what Halcyon built at Kepler Reach — burying whispers." Sefa turned away, pressed both palms flat against the transmitter's crystal column, and stood with her head bowed as though listening for something inside it. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — stripped down to bare structural elements, the way it went when she was deciding something irreversible. "There's another option."

"Don't," Priya said.

"Somebody interfaces. Somebody takes the archive's hand and walks it out through the transmitter. Full spectrum. Everything. The protocol, the names, nine years of logs, all of it, in one shout that no one on the other end can pretend they didn't hear."

"And that somebody dissolves," Dev said. "You heard the warning. You heard what the archive said."

"I heard." Sefa lifted her head. In the lamplight her face was very young and very old at once, both ages true. "I've been dead once already. Radiation sickness, slow and stupid, in a medbay that smelled like bleach. I remember every hour of it now — the memories came back whether I wanted them or not. Dying again, quickly, for something that matters—" She shrugged, a small motion that tried to make the enormous seem ordinary. "I've had worse Tuesdays."

"No," Emrys said.

Every face turned toward her. She hadn't meant to speak. The word had come out of her with a force that surprised her, and she let it stand there in the dust-hung air rather than take it back.

"Emrys." Sefa's voice gentled. "It has to be someone. And it should be someone who understands the archive, who's spent years learning to listen to it. That's you. I know that's you. But I'm the captain, and I'm already evidence, and my body is a corporate fabrication that was never meant to last. If anyone's expendable here, it's—"

"You are not expendable." Emrys heard her own voice crack and hated it and kept going anyway. "None of you are. That's the whole point. That's what Halcyon never understood — that's what the founders never understood. Minds aren't inventory. People aren't evidence. You don't get to decide who's the acceptable loss, because the moment you do, you're building another Kepler Reach."

"Then who?" Sefa spread her hands. "Someone volunteers, or the truth dies in this room, and Halcyon wins forever. Those are the options. I wish there were others. There aren't."

"There's me."

The words were out before Emrys had finished deciding to say them, and once they were out, she understood that she had decided long ago — maybe nine years ago, maybe the day she'd learned whose dreams signed themselves in the pods below the vault, maybe the day she'd first heard static resolve into her mother's voice saying her name. Every step of the long road here had been leading to this egg-shaped room, this crystal column, this single terrible door.

"I'm the archivist," she said. "It's my record. My logs. My mother. I'm the one who kept listening when there was no reason to listen, and the archive knows me — really knows me, deeper than any of you. I'm the strongest bridge you have. And I'm asking you — I'm telling you — let me do this."

"No." Sefa said it flatly, finally, the way bulkheads seal. "Absolutely not."

"You don't get to refuse for me."

"I'm the captain of this ship. On this mission, I absolutely do."

"Your mission ended eleven months ago, by your own count. Everything since then—" Emrys stopped, breathed. The anger was useful but it was also a fire burning fuel she might need later, and she banked it down to embers. "Sefa. Please. Listen to me. You died once. You told me about it — the bleach smell, the hours. You fought your way back from that. You have a second life that Halcyon stole and then tried to recall like defective equipment. Don't give it to them. Don't give it to this room. If someone has to burn as the bridge, it should be someone who chose this road from the beginning. Who stayed at Kepler Reach for nine years when everyone else left. Who listened." Her voice steadied. "I have been practicing for this my whole life without knowing it. Let me finish it."

The chamber held its breath. Even the static seemed to draw back, attentive, the way a family gathers outside a room where something irrevocable is being discussed.

Sefa looked at her for a long time. Emrys watched her run the calculations — the captain's arithmetic of lives and missions and acceptable losses — and watched her arrive, visibly, at the sum Emrys had already reached. Watched her hate it.

"If you do this," Sefa said slowly, "you won't just lose your nine years. That was the price of the broadcast at Kepler Reach — your recorded memories, spent as signal. This is different. Direct interface. Cognitive dissolution. You could lose everything. Your name. Your language. The shape of your own face."

"I know."

"You could come back as a voice with no one behind it."

"I know." Emrys touched the transmitter's crystal column, felt the faint hum against her fingertips, cold and alive at once. "But I also know what's in that archive. Four thousand minds who chose imprisonment over death so that someday, someone would tell the truth about them. One of them raised me. One of them sang to me through nine years of static and I didn't know it was her. If the price of giving them their names back is that I forget mine—" She swallowed. "Then it's a fair price. It's the cheapest thing I own."

"Emrys—"

"And you're not letting me go alone."

Sefa blinked. "What?"

"You heard me." Emrys turned to face her fully, and something in her had hardened now, set like resin, clear and permanent. "The plaque says direct interface. It doesn't say solitary interface. Two operators, linked couches, shared load. Priya — check the schematic. Tell me I'm wrong."

Priya was already moving, slate up, fingers flying. The pause stretched five seconds, ten. Then she looked up with an expression Emrys couldn't fully read — relief and grief wrestling to a draw.

"She's right. The original design used paired operators. Shared bandwidth, mutual anchoring. One mind alone carries the whole archive and tears. Two minds, linked, can distribute the flow — each holds the other together." Priya's voice dropped. "It halves the risk. It doesn't erase it. But it halves it."

"Then it's settled," Emrys said. "Two couches. Both of us."

"No." Sefa laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it. "You're impossible. You know that? Nine years alone on a rock and you come out of it more stubborn than anyone I served with in the fleet."

"I had good teachers." Emrys nodded toward the ceiling, toward the archive bays below decks where the Meridian carried its passengers of light. "Four thousand of them."

Sefa stared at her. And then, slowly, the fight went out of her posture — not surrender, Emrys realized, but something rarer: recognition. The look of a woman seeing clearly, perhaps for the first time, exactly who had been waiting for her at the edge of dead space.

"Both of us," Sefa said at last. "Together. And if it starts taking you — if I feel you going, if the link starts pulling you under — I pull us out. Mid-broadcast if I have to. Partial truth over no survivor. Swear it, Emrys. Swear it or I don't touch the couch."

Emrys wanted to argue. Every instinct she'd honed over nine years of solitary duty argued — finish the job, complete the record, see it through. But she looked at Sefa's face, at the young-old face of a woman who had already died once and clawed back, and she understood that this was the price of not being alone: compromise. Sharing the weight meant sharing the decision.

"I swear," she said. "Partial truth over no survivor."

"Then we have a deal." Sefa extended her hand, and Emrys took it, and the grip between them was brief and hard and said everything the words hadn't.

* * *

They spent the remaining hours preparing, and the preparation felt like a strange inversion of every ritual Emrys had ever known. For nine years she had maintained systems against decay — sealing cracks, replacing filters, keeping the garden alive a little longer. Now she helped Priya strip the interface couches of their brittle filaments and splice in fresh conductors salvaged from the Meridian's medical bay. Now she tested her own vitals monitors, calibrated the link harnesses, did all the careful work of preparing herself to be spent.

Dev Anand sat nearby through all of it, too weak to help, refusing to leave. As dusk-cycle dimmed the chamber lights, he spoke without opening his eyes.

"When I was dying the first time," he said, "in the evacuation, I made a list. Everyone I loved. I recited it to myself so I wouldn't forget them before the end." His breath rattled faintly. "This time I don't need a list. I'm looking at it."

Emrys knelt beside him and took his thin hand, and neither of them said anything else, because nothing needed saying.

Later, alone at the console while Sefa slept in shifts, Emrys opened a channel to the archive and let herself listen — really listen, the way she had in the vault, headphones on, static pouring through her like a river through a gate. The voices rose around her, thousands strong, and she felt them arranging themselves, settling into order, the way a congregation quiets before the processional.

Her mother came closest of all.

*Little light,* Ilsa Ishikawa said. *Are you afraid?*

"Yes," Emrys said aloud, to the empty chamber, because lying to her mother had never once worked. "Aren't you?"

*A little. But mostly I'm proud. Mostly I'm ready.* A pause, filled with the sound of four thousand minds breathing in the dark. *Emrys. Whatever happens tomorrow — whatever survives of you when the signal clears — know that the nine years were not a sacrifice. They were a gift. You gave us company in the dark. Tomorrow we give you back to the light.*

Emrys pressed her forehead against the cool crystal of the transmitter column and let the hum move through her skull like a second heartbeat.

Above them, at the system's edge, unseen but no longer unimaginable, the first running lights of the Halcyon fleet were burning their way inward through the dark.

Twelve hours, Priya had promised. Twelve hours until the transmitter could sing.

Eleven remained.
