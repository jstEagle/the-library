## 1. Contraband and Starlight

The blind spot had a name once — some customs clerk's typo, decades dead, that turned a forty-meter corridor of orbital space into a hole in the ledger. The clerks called it Corridor 9-C. Beatrix called it the Wound, and she flew into it the way other people went home.

"Freighter *Kestrel's Lament*, you are off your filed vector." The voice from Customs Control was young, bored, and running on the night shift's third cup of something synthetic. "Adjust heading or prepare for inspection."

Beatrix let the silence stretch just long enough to seem like an old woman fumbling her controls. Then she thumbed the comm. "*Lament* copies. Gyro drift on the number two thruster. Correcting."

She was not correcting. She was drifting, precisely and deliberately, eleven degrees off the corridor's centerline, into the seam where Station Meridian's sensor lattice met the customs array's coverage and neither one bothered to check the overlap because no clerk in thirty years had thought to reconcile the maps. The Wound was four hundred meters wide and nine kilometers long, and it was hers. She'd found it before she'd owned a ship, back when she'd been crewing ore haulers out of the Belt, and she had never told another living soul it existed. That was the whole trick of a blind spot: its value was its secrecy, and its secrecy was a thing you carried alone, like a stone in the chest.

The vials rode in the water reclamation tank behind her, nested in the filtration housing where the radiation profile of recycled graywater masked their neural signatures. Forty-one of them. Sealed memory, harvested clean, each one somebody's wedding day or last conversation with a dying parent or — in the case of the three vials wrapped separately at the bottom, packed in thermal foam — something considerably more expensive. Odile hadn't told her what those held. Odile never did. That was the courtesy between them: Beatrix moved what she wasn't told, and Odile paid as if she'd been told everything.

"*Lament*, your transponder is intermittent."

"It's an old ship," Beatrix said, and put a rasp in her voice that she didn't have to fake anymore. Thirty-eight years old, and her knees sounded like the airlock seals. "So am I."

A pause. Somewhere on the night shift, a clerk decided that an old freighter with a bad gyro and a worse transponder was not worth waking a supervisor over. "Proceed on corrected vector. Log the maintenance."

"Logging it now." She wasn't. She slid deeper into the Wound, and the customs array's attention slid off her hull like rain off waxed canvas, and she was invisible again, the way she liked it, the way she'd arranged her entire life to be.

Through the forward port, Sector Nine turned beneath her: the great slow wheel of the station, the dockyards strung along its spine like lanterns, and beyond all of it, far out past the shipping lanes where nothing official ever went, the beacon.

It hung in the dark like a splinter of bone. No registry listed it. No charter claimed it. It predated the settlement records, predated the faction accords, predated — so far as Beatrix could determine after eleven years of tending it — every document in the sector's archives. A needle of pale alloy three hundred meters long, crusted at its joints with sensor nodes that still answered her touch, older than anything else in the sky, and utterly silent. It did not transmit. It did not receive, so far as anyone knew. It simply endured, and Beatrix maintained it, and in eleven years she had never been able to answer the question that mattered: maintained *for whom?*

She'd stopped asking it out loud around year four.

"*Kestrel's Lament*," she said to the empty cabin, because the silence in a cockpit for nine hours was a physical weight and you learned to talk through it or go strange, "you are cleared through the Wound. Do not tell yourself where you've been."

Docking at Meridian took another hour, most of it spent being pleasant to a cargo inspector named Vashti who had a crush on Beatrix's forged identity and no idea she existed. Beatrix let the woman flirt with a fiction while the real cargo — the water tank, the filtration housing, the forty-one sealed lives inside it — passed under her signature without a flicker. Competence, Beatrix had learned, looked exactly like boredom. Nobody inspected a bored woman twice.

Odile's shop sat in the station's third ring, down a corridor where the gravity ran slightly light and the light itself ran slightly amber, so that everything looked like a memory already. The sign read ANTIQUITIES & APPRAISAL, which was true in the way that a knife is technically a tool.

"You're late." Odile Voss was small and silver-haired and had hands that never stopped moving, sorting, weighing, wrapping. Her eyes were the only young thing about her: quick, black, and always slightly amused, as though the universe were a joke she'd heard first. "I had a buyer circling since Tuesday."

"Your buyer can keep circling. Customs nearly woke up on my tail. Somebody recalibrated the array's outer ring."

"Someone always does. Someone always does it wrong." Odile held out her palm, and Beatrix set the access chip for the water tank on it. "Forty-one?"

"Forty-one. Three special, bottom of the case, foam-packed. Don't ask me what they are, because I don't know, and I've decided I sleep better that way."

"That's the wisest thing anyone's said in this shop all month." Odile's fingers danced across the chip, confirming. "Payment's in the usual split. Half escrowed, half—"

"Half in hard currency, yes. I remember the liturgy."

Odile glanced up then, and for a moment the amusement drained out of her face, leaving something older and more careful underneath. "You look tired, Crane."

"I'm always tired."

"No. Tired I know. This is different. You're wearing it like weather." She tapped the counter once, twice. "How long has it been since you took a run that wasn't contraband? Since you went somewhere for a reason that wasn't money?"

Beatrix considered lying, decided it would be wasted effort — Odile could smell a lie the way sharks smelled blood, at distance, through water — and said, "Eleven years."

"And how's the lighthouse?"

"The lighthouse is fine."

"The lighthouse is a piece of junk older than the station, and you spend half your life out there alone with it, and you call it fine." Odile shook her head and began counting currency into a case, her hands moving through the ritual. "You know what I see, when you come in here? I see a woman who's very good at being nobody. And I wonder sometimes what she was before she got good at it."

"Before doesn't exist," Beatrix said. "You know my file. Orphan of the Belt transit disasters. Raised by station charity. No family, no history, no debts. Clean as vacuum."

"Mm." Odile slid the case across. "Clean as vacuum. That's exactly what worries me about you, girl. Nothing in this universe is clean as vacuum. Things only look that way when they've been scrubbed."

Beatrix took the case and left before the conversation could become a question. That was the discipline. Questions were how smugglers died — not the customs kind, the internal kind, the ones that made you hesitate at the wrong moment, look over your shoulder one time too many. She had built herself a life with no questions in it: fly the Wound, deliver the vials, tend the beacon, sleep, repeat. A circle. Circles were safe. Circles came back to themselves.

She was halfway to the docking ring when she realized she couldn't remember why she'd started calling the beacon a lighthouse.

* * *

The flight out took six hours, and she spent them the way she spent all her flights: checking the vial cases' temperature readouts, running the thruster diagnostics she'd log and never file, eating a meal pouch standing up because sitting felt too much like stopping. The *Kestrel's Lament* was twenty-two years old and smelled of coolant and old tea, and Beatrix knew every rattle in her frame the way you know the creaks in a house you grew up in. Which was strange, when she let herself think about it, because she hadn't grown up anywhere. The orphanage records were sealed. The charity trustees who'd signed for her were dead. Her own childhood was a room with the furniture missing — she knew its dimensions by the shadows on the floor, and nothing else.

Everyone's memory had gaps. That was what she told herself. Trauma carved holes; poverty blurred things. Hers were just more thorough than most.

The beacon swelled in the viewport, bone-pale against the starfield. She brought the *Lament* alongside its midsection, matched rotation, and soft-docked at the maintenance collar she'd installed herself in her second year — the only new thing on the structure, the only thing that was hers.

Inside, the beacon breathed. That was the word she'd settled on, years ago, because there was no better one. Deep in the structure, some ancient system cycled on a rhythm just slower than a human pulse — a swell of warmth through the deck plates, a faint harmonic in the walls, a rise and fall of light along the spine conduits that no engineer she'd consulted could explain. Not power draw. Not cooling. Breath.

"Evening," she said to it, hanging her coat on the hook by the airlock. Eleven years of evenings. "Miss me?"

The beacon, naturally, said nothing. But the harmonic shifted — a quarter-tone, maybe less — and Beatrix chose, as she always chose, to believe it was greeting rather than coincidence. Belief was a tool like any other. It kept the loneliness from setting like concrete.

She worked. That was the mercy of the work: it filled the hands and the hours completely. She crawled the spine conduit and replaced two degraded node housings, machining the fittings herself because the beacon's threading matched no standard manufactured in the last century. She flushed particulate from the sensor arrays and logged their readings — steady, steady, steady, eleven years of steady — in the maintenance journal she kept in longhand, pen on paper, because paper felt more permanent than data and permanence was the whole point of tending something. She ate dinner sitting against the main trunk with her legs dangling over the access channel, watching the breath-light rise and fall along the walls, and for a little while the hollow place under her sternum was quiet.

It never stayed quiet. That was the honest truth of it, the truth she kept folded up small and didn't look at directly, the way you don't look at a sun. There was a space in her where a life should have been — parents, a history, a reason — and she had filled it with routine the way you fill a cavity with filling. It held. It held well enough. But some nights, out here with nothing but the beacon's slow breathing for company, she could feel the edges of it, the shape of everything missing, pressing against the routine like a face against glass.

"Why do I know your threading?" she asked the wall, once, quietly, the way you ask questions you've decided not to expect answers to. Her hands fit the beacon's fittings perfectly. Her penmanship in the journal matched, stroke for stroke, the faded annotations left by some earlier maintainer whose name wasn't recorded anywhere — the same slant, the same cramped efficiency, a resemblance she'd noticed in year two and never mentioned to anyone, including herself, in any way that counted as thinking about it.

The beacon breathed. The stars wheeled. Somewhere behind her, the maintenance console chimed — a soft, patient tone she'd heard perhaps five times in eleven years, always dismissed as a legacy system dreaming.

Tonight, for the first time, she looked at it.

The screen showed a signal receipt. Inbound transmission, source unregistered, origin point somewhere in deep space beyond the shipping lanes — a direction with nothing in it, nothing charted, nothing settled. The message was old, flagged by the beacon's own receiver as having arrived years ago and queued, patiently, for a maintainer who had never once checked the queue.

The text resolved slowly, character by character, in a protocol format that predated the station's founding:

**HALCYON RETURNING. ESTIMATE: SOON. PREPARE THE QUESTION.**

Beatrix sat very still. The breath-light rose along the walls and fell. Her heart, for once, was faster than the beacon's rhythm.

"Prepare the *what*?" she said.

The beacon breathed, and waited, and did not answer — but far out in the dark, at the edge of the long-range scopes, something was coming home.
