## 11. The First Erasure

The vial had been under her floor for longer than she had been herself.

Beatrix knelt in the crawlspace beneath the beacon station's hab module, her palm flat against a panel she had never consciously known existed but whose location her fingers found without searching. The metal was warm. Everything in Sector Nine was warm, fed by the beacon's slow arterial pulse of power, but this warmth felt different — felt *deliberate*, like a hand held over a candle flame for exactly as long as it could bear.

The panel gave with a click that sounded, in the dark, like a small bone breaking.

Inside, in a foam cradle molded to a single shape, lay a neural vial. Not one of Odile's trade goods with their standardized collars and cartel serials. This one was older — the glass ambered with radiation bloom, the collar hand-machined, the seal stamped with a symbol she recognized from the beacon's own hull: a circle bisected by a line, the two halves offset, neither quite touching.

The truce sigil. The one Ilsa had drawn in the dust of the Halcyon's quarantine deck with a child's finger.

Beatrix lifted it out. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Her hands were steady. She noticed that, and it frightened her more than shaking would have. Somewhere beneath the person she had become — the smuggler, the tender of a dead machine, the woman who ate alone and slept alone and called the loneliness *discipline* — there was a self who had knelt in this crawlspace with steady hands and buried this vial and then, presumably, walked away to have her mind scoured clean.

*You did this on purpose,* she thought. *You left yourself a letter.*

She climbed out of the crawlspace, sealed the panel, and carried the vial to the station's single chair, where she kept her playback rig — the same battered deck she used to verify contraband before running it past customs. She had replayed a thousand stolen lives through this machine. A bride's wedding day. A dockworker's last sight of his mother. A cartel accountant's memory of the first ledger entry, the one that had made Odile's hands go cold.

She had never once played a memory that belonged to her.

She checked the rig's isolation protocols twice. She told herself it was caution. She knew it was stalling. Outside the viewport, the beacon turned its slow rotation against the stars, and she thought: *when I know what's in here, I can't unknow it. Whoever I was, I'll have met her.*

She clamped the vial into the cradle. She pressed the contact pads to her temples. She said, out loud, to the empty room, in a voice that did not quite sound like hers: "All right. Show me."

* * *

The memory opened on a night sky that was not Sector Nine's.

She was standing on a balcony — no, a terrace, broad and open, on a station she did not recognize, high above a planet wrapped in cloud. The gravity was a fraction heavy. The air smelled of cut grass, which meant it was manufactured, which meant this place had money, which meant it was not a place she had ever been as the woman she was now. Below the terrace, lights: a city, or something shaped like one, all its windows the same warm amber.

And she was not alone.

The memory's owner — her — stood beside a tall man with grey in his black hair and the particular stillness of someone who had learned to hold his face very carefully. He wore no uniform, but everything about him said *uniform* anyway: the posture, the cropped hair, the way his eyes moved in disciplined sweeps across the terrace's approaches.

"Say it back to me," the man said. His voice was quiet. The memory carried it with terrible clarity, the way memories carry the sounds that matter.

"The waking order is a countdown," she heard herself say — her voice, but older in it somehow, or younger, harder at the edges, a voice that had not yet been sanded down by decades of solitude. "The beacon at Sector Nine maintains the interval. When it reaches zero, the default sequence triggers. Meridian's arks open first. Concord's stay frozen for the full interval after."

"And if either faction interferes with the beacon?"

"The truce fails. Both arks stay sealed. Everyone sleeps until the ships decay."

The man nodded slowly. He was looking at the cloud-wrapped planet as if he could see through it, down to the cryopods, the millions of them, the two great fleets of the frozen dead-not-dead. "You built a machine that decides which half of humanity wakes first," he said. "Do you understand what you've done?"

"I understand exactly what I've done." Her own voice, and there was no pride in it — or rather, there was pride, but it was the exhausted kind, the pride of someone who had carried something too far to set it down. "Both sides wanted a guarantee. Meridian wanted their people first so they'd control the docks, the stations, the recovery infrastructure. Concord wanted theirs first so they'd control the *narrative* — the story of who was rescued and who was kept waiting. Neither would sign a treaty that left the order to chance. So I gave them a machine instead of a chance. A clock. Neutral, tamper-evident, buried at a beacon nobody cares about, on a route nobody uses."

"And you're the only one who can maintain it."

"I'm the only one who *wants* to. That's the point, Anselm. The clock has to belong to no one."

The man — Anselm — turned to look at her then, and the memory's camera turned with him, and Beatrix saw her own face for the first time in the flesh of the memory. Younger. Sharper. The same eyes, though. The same eyes looking out of a face that had not yet learned to be alone.

"Then I'm sorry for what I'm about to ask you," Anselm said.

* * *

The memory jumped — the ragged cut of a spliced recollection, hours gone in an instant — and the terrace was dark now, the city below dimmed to embers, and the two of them were still standing in the same places as if neither had moved, as if the conversation had been a single held breath.

"They cheated," Anselm said. "Both of them. Within the week."

Beatrix, watching from inside her own buried skull, felt the words land in her chest like cold water.

"Meridian moved first," her past self said. Her voice was flat now, scraped clean of everything. "They seeded the beacon's maintenance schedule with a drift correction. Small. Legal, even, by the letter of the accord. It would have shaved four years off the interval. Their arks open four years early, and by the time Concord's people wake, Meridian owns every airlock in the system."

"And Concord?"

"Concord didn't touch the beacon. They went after the *architect*." A pause, and Beatrix felt her past self's mouth curve in something that was not a smile. "They offered me a fortune to introduce a counter-drift. I was supposed to be the neutral party. The clock that belongs to no one. And both sides looked at me and saw a door."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told Concord no. I told Meridian nothing — I let them believe their correction was still hidden." Her past self turned from the railing, and the memory's gaze swept the dark terrace, the sleeping city, the whole vast arrangement of a civilization holding its breath in frozen rows. "Here's what I learned tonight, Anselm. The waking order was never a technical problem. It's a moral one. And I built a machine to avoid having to make the moral choice myself — because I didn't trust either faction, and because, if I'm honest, I didn't trust *myself*. I didn't want to be the woman who decided whose mother wakes and whose mother keeps sleeping. So I hid behind a countdown."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to do something worse than choosing." Her past self's hands — Beatrix's hands, younger, unscarred — rose and touched the back of her own neck, where the playback jack sat. "I'm going to erase the choice. All of it. The architecture, the drift corrections, the terms, the names. Everything that would let either faction find the beacon's true function or bend it to their side. I'll wipe the design from my own mind, because I'm the last copy. I'll bury the record here, at the beacon, in a place only I would ever think to look — and then I'll forget where I buried it, because a memory I know I have is a memory that can be taken."

"That's not erasure," Anselm said quietly. "That's abdication."

"Yes." No flinch. No defense. "But it's also the only honest thing left. If I keep the knowledge, I'm a hostage. If I destroy the record, the clock runs blind and Meridian's drift wins. But if I *become* someone who doesn't know — someone with no faction, no stake, no past — then the choice stays open. Genuinely open. For the first time since the fleets launched." She was silent a moment. "Someone will have to make it eventually. Someone who isn't me. Someone who isn't *them*. Someone who can look at the question with clean eyes and answer it freely."

"You're describing a stranger."

"I'm describing *myself*," her past self said. "Just not this one."

Beatrix, clamped into the rig in the station chair, thirty-some years downstream of that night, felt something in her chest crack along a seam she had never known was there.

*She left it to me.* Not a message. Not a test. A *vacancy*. She had looked at the weight of the choice — the whole frozen weight of two civilizations — and instead of carrying it or dropping it, she had set it down inside her own body and walked out of herself, trusting that whoever stood up in her place would be lighter, cleaner, freer to choose than she had been.

*You made me the neutral party,* Beatrix thought at the memory, at the young sharp face on the dark terrace. *You made me the clock that belongs to no one. And you didn't even leave me a note.*

Except she had. She was looking at it. The whole memory was the note — and there was more.

* * *

The memory's final minutes were quiet, and quiet in the way that only the worst nights are quiet.

The playback rig's chamber dissolved into a small room Beatrix did not recognize: a clinic, or something like one, white and bare. She was lying back in a chair like the one she sat in now. A technician's hands moved at the edge of the frame, competent and impersonal, prepping the wipe.

Anselm stood over her. His careful face had finally failed him; he looked, in that moment, like a man watching a launch he believed would never come back.

"You could still destroy the beacon instead," he said. "Let the interval run out. Default order. Meridian wakes first and the galaxy learns to live with it."

"And four billion Concord sleepers learn to live with it too, or don't." Her past self's voice was already going soft at the edges — the wipe's pre-sequence, Beatrix realized, already threading through her, already smoothing her out. "No. The clock stays. The choice stays open. That's the whole point of me, Anselm. That's the last thing I'll ever build."

"Will you remember any of this?"

"A smuggler," she said, and there was wonder in her voice, genuine wonder, as if she could already feel the woman she was becoming arrive inside her. "I'll be a smuggler. I'll run memories past customs and keep to myself and tend a beacon I'll think is just a beacon. I'll be lonely." A breath. "Good. Loneliness is honest. It won't tell me what to choose."

The technician said something about beginning. Her past self looked up at Anselm one last time, and Beatrix saw — with a jolt that went through the rig's contact pads and into her skull like a struck bell — that Anselm was not just a colleague. The way he looked at her. The way she looked back. There were decades in that look, and a history, and a tenderness that had never been said aloud because neither of them had ever had the time.

"Take care of her," her past self said. "The one who wakes up. She won't know why she's sad. Don't tell her."

"I won't," Anselm said. "I'll just be sad with her."

The memory whited out.

* * *

Beatrix came back to herself in the station chair with her hands locked on the armrests and her breath coming in shallow, deliberate pulls, the way she breathed when the customs skimmers swept close and every muscle wanted to run.

The rig hummed. The vial, spent, sat in its cradle like an empty shell casing.

She sat there a long time. The beacon turned. Somewhere below, in the quarantine ring, forty children with adult memories slept in a sealed ship, and one of them — a girl of ten with a captain's gravity in her small body — had drawn the truce sigil in the dust and asked Beatrix which side she was on.

*I was neither side,* Beatrix thought. *I was the line between them. I still am.*

But the memory had given her more than her history. It had given her the shape of the trap. Her past self had erased the waking order — the *terms*, the drift corrections, the architecture — but she had said it herself: *someone will have to make it eventually.* And the beacon was accelerating. Ilsa had seen the numbers. The default sequence was coming whether anyone chose or not, and the default was Meridian's drift, Meridian's four stolen years, Meridian waking first while four billion strangers slept on.

Her past self had left the choice open. The cartel was about to close it for her.

And there was one more thing — the thing that made her sit forward slowly, the spent vial trembling now at last in her hand. Anselm. A man with grey in his black hair, on a terrace above a manufactured lawn, who had loved her enough to promise to be sad with her. A man who would be — if he still lived, if he had slept instead of aged — frozen in one of the two arks, waiting for someone to decide his morning.

*I erased myself to keep your choice safe,* she thought, to the woman on the terrace. *And you erased yourself to keep mine. We built a chain of strangers, and I'm the last link, and the clock is almost out of chain.*

She rose. She took the empty vial to the crawlspace, then stopped — and instead set it on the workbench in plain sight, where she would see it every day. A letter she had already read. A woman she had already met.

Then she keyed the station's short-range channel, the one frequency that could reach the quarantine ring without customs hearing, and said the words she had been avoiding for a week:

"Ilsa. It's Wren. I know what the beacon is. I know what I was. And we have a problem — because the people who built the drift are about to make sure no one ever gets to choose at all."

Static. A long breath of it.

Then a child's voice, awake at whatever hour it was, steady as a keel: "Tell me everything. And Wren — bring the vials. All of them."
