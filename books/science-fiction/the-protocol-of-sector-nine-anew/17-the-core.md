## 17. The Core

The beacon's spine was a shaft, and the shaft was cold in a way Tam had never felt cold before — not temperature, exactly, but *absence*, a hollowness that drank the heat from his skin through the suit's lining. He kept his hand on the guide rail and told himself, for the ninth time, that he had crawled through the Halcyon's plasma conduits when he was forty-two years old, that he had once fit his whole body into a coolant junction the size of a coffin and re-soldered a fusion tap by feel while the ship screamed around him.

That had been a different body. That body had hands that didn't shake.

"Talk to me, Tam." Ilsa's voice came through the suit channel, steady in the way she made it steady, which Tam knew was a labor, because he'd heard her voice at night in the quarantine bunks when she thought everyone was asleep, and it wasn't steady then. "How far to the next junction?"

"Forty meters," Tam said. "Then the shaft opens up." He checked the schematic floating in the corner of his visor — Beatrix had pulled it from the maintenance logs, the ones written in her own handwriting by a woman she didn't remember being. The schematic was old. Older than the Halcyon. Older than the colonies' founding charters. "Then it opens up into the core chamber, and everything past that point isn't on the schematic."

"Not on it," Beatrix said, "or *redacted*?"

"Redacted." Tam swallowed. The word sat wrong in his throat. "There's a block of the drawing that's just... filled in. Solid black. Like someone drew the room and then decided nobody should see it."

The channel was quiet for three seconds. In vacuum-suited silence, three seconds was a long time. Tam counted the seconds the way he used to count reactor pulses, an old habit from a life that had been taken away from him, or that he'd been taken away from — he still hadn't decided which way to say it.

"Of course it's redacted," Beatrix said finally. "I redacted it. The other me. The one who built this thing." A breath, ragged at the edges. "She didn't want anyone finding the core. She wanted them to find everything *except* the core."

"Except you," Ilsa said. "She wanted *you* to find it. Eventually. When you were ready."

"I wasn't ready. I'm still not ready. Let's keep moving."

* * *

They descended the last forty meters in single file, Beatrix first, Tam last, because Beatrix had argued — in her flat, stubborn smuggler's voice — that the person most likely to trigger something lethal should be the person the beacon was built around. Tam had argued back that the person most likely to recognize a lethal thing should be the engineer. He'd lost. He was starting to understand that you always lost arguments with Beatrix Crane, and that she let you think you'd won sometimes, and that this was its own kind of kindness.

The shaft ended.

Tam's helmet lamp swept across a space so large his mind refused it at first — refused to build the walls, refused to accept the ceiling, kept trying to interpret the chamber as open sky. It was a sphere, maybe two hundred meters across, and every surface of it was covered in the same pale material, and the pale material was *moving*, very slowly, in patterns like frost forming and unforming on glass. In the center, suspended on nothing, hung the interface.

It looked, Tam thought, like a chair that had grown instead of been built. A cradle of the same pale substance, curved and waiting, with a crown of filaments above it — thousands of them, hair-thin, drifting like kelp in a current that wasn't there. Below the cradle, the floor of the chamber was a single unbroken pane of dark glass, and beneath the glass, deep down, something glowed. A slow pulse. Amber. Once every four seconds.

Tam knew that rhythm. Everyone in Sector Nine knew that rhythm, if they'd ever stood under the beacon at night. The pulse everyone thought of as a heartbeat.

It wasn't a heartbeat. Now that he was this close, now that he could see it through two hundred meters of dark glass, Tam could see what it actually was. It was a *question mark*, drawn in light, over and over, and then erased, and then drawn again.

"It's been asking," he whispered. "The whole time. Everyone thought it was counting down and it's been *asking*."

"Save it," Beatrix said, but her voice had gone soft. She was staring at the cradle the way Tam had once stared at the Halcyon's drive core from the outside, the first day after they'd come back small — knowing it was the most important thing in the universe and knowing he couldn't touch it. "Schematic says the access ring is at the base of the cradle. Tam, with me. Ilsa, hold the shaft mouth. If anything comes down that tunnel that isn't us, you seal it and you don't open it for anyone. Understood?"

"Understood." A pause. "Beatrix."

"Yeah."

"Come back up. Both of you. That's not a request, it's a captain's order."

"You're ten years old, Captain Reyes."

"I'm forty-six years of service in a ten-year-old body, and I have sealed people behind blast doors before." Ilsa's voice didn't waver, but Tam heard the thing under it, the thing he'd been hearing from all the adults-in-children for months now: the sound of someone holding a door shut against their own fear. "Come back up."

* * *

The walk to the cradle took eleven minutes and cost Tam more than any crawl he'd ever done.

It wasn't the distance. It was the frost-patterns on the walls. As they got closer to the center, the patterns resolved, and Tam realized with a slow, cold drop in his stomach that they weren't patterns at all. They were *writing*. Millions of lines of it, in no alphabet he knew, scrolling across the pale surfaces in loops that repeated and varied and repeated. And threaded through the loops — he was sure of it, sure the way an engineer is sure when a tolerance is off by a hair — were fragments he *did* know. Fleet Standard. Old dialect. The founding-era script they used on the truce monuments.

The beacon's walls were covered in the truce. The whole truce. Every version of it, maybe. Every draft.

"She wrote it all down," Tam said. His voice came out small. "The architect. She wrote the whole bargain on the walls of the thing everyone thought was just a timer."

"Or she wrote every way it could fail," Beatrix said. She wasn't looking at the walls. She was looking at the cradle, and her stride had gotten shorter, and Tam realized she was afraid — actually afraid, not smuggler-cautious, not Wren-calculating, but the deep animal kind, the kind he'd seen in the ship's doctor the day the de-aging started and nobody knew if it would stop. "Tam. Status check. Out loud. It helps."

"Right." Tam ran the check because she asked, and because running checks was the one language in which he still felt like himself. "Suit integrity green. Power at ninety-one percent, draw's higher than it should be, the chamber's field is bleeding us. Air for six hours. Nerve response—" He flexed his fingers inside the gloves, watched them respond, watched them not shake. "Nerve response fine."

"Your heart rate's a hundred and forty."

"You can see that?"

"Your suit can see that. I can see your suit." Beatrix reached the base of the cradle and stopped. "Breathe, Tam. Four counts in, four out. I know it's an old trick."

"It's a good trick," Tam said, and did it, and hated that it worked.

The access ring was exactly where the schematic said, a band of darker material circling the cradle's base, and it was covered in sockets — dozens of them, small, hexagonal, sized for nothing human. Tam knelt and got his lamp close and felt his stomach drop for the second time in ten minutes.

"Beatrix. These aren't ports. Look at the spacing." He held his gloved hand up beside them. "They're spaced for *neural lace contacts*. Direct interface. No console, no keys, no codes." He looked up at her. "It doesn't want your hands. It wants your *head*."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"I've known since the maintenance logs. Why do you think I redacted— why do you think *she* redacted the chamber?" Beatrix crouched beside him, and in the flat white light of the lamps her face looked older than it was, carved and tired. "The beacon is keyed to a living mind. One mind. The architect's. It was built that way so no government, no fleet council, no cartel could ever trigger the waking order without her. She made herself the only key in the lock." A breath. "And then she erased herself. So the key is a woman who doesn't remember being one."

Tam stared at her. The frost-writing scrolled behind her, patient as tide.

"Then how do you open it?" he asked. "If you don't have her memories?"

"I don't know," Beatrix said. "I think — I think it doesn't need me to *remember*. I think it needs me to *choose*. But I'm guessing, Tam. I've been guessing since the dock, and I'm tired of telling children the truth, so there it is. I'm guessing."

* * *

They were still kneeling at the base of the cradle when the pulse changed.

Tam felt it before he heard it — a pressure shift in the chamber, a subsonic roll that came up through the floor and through his knees and into his teeth. The amber glow beneath the glass stuttered. The frost-writing on the walls stopped scrolling.

Every line of it. At once. Two hundred meters of ancient text, frozen mid-loop.

"Tam," Ilsa said in his ear. "What was that? The whole beacon just — the external frequency shifted. Kell's people on the ring are calling it in. The cartel ships are moving."

"Nothing yet," Tam said, and then the chamber corrected him.

The writing began again — but faster now, so fast the loops blurred, and the amber pulse beneath the glass quickened to match it. Four seconds. Three. Two. Tam's engineer's mind grabbed the numbers and did the arithmetic and hated the answer.

"It's accelerating," he said. "Beatrix, it's accelerating. That's the default sequence. If it reaches the end of the cycle without a valid input, it executes the standing order — the original waking order, the one from the founding, the one that—"

"That wakes my faction first," Beatrix said. "And freezes the children's generation for another century. Yes. I did the arithmetic too."

"How long?"

Beatrix didn't answer. Tam looked up and saw why. She was staring at the cradle, at the crown of hair-thin filaments above it, and the filaments had begun to move — drifting down, slowly, like a chandelier lowering itself, like the beacon was leaning toward her.

"How *long*, Beatrix," Tam said again, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, and he hated that too.

"By the pulse rate?" Beatrix swallowed. "Twenty minutes. Maybe less. It's not linear, it's compounding, so—"

"Less," Tam said. "It'll be less."

"Then we stop talking and we start working." Beatrix stood, and something in her changed — Tam had seen it before, in the quarantine ring, in the siege at the dock, the moment when the fear went somewhere else and left only the woman who moved through it. "Tam. The access ring. I need to know everything about it in ten minutes. Contact geometry, fail-safes, what happens if I sit down in that thing and it doesn't like me. If there's a way to do this that doesn't end with my brain fried, you find it. You're the best engineer I have ever met, and I have met engineers who were three times your age, and I am not saying that to be kind. I'm saying it because it's true and because right now it's the only thing standing between the two of us and twenty minutes."

Tam's throat was tight. He nodded, then remembered she couldn't see a nod through the helmet, and said, "Yes. On it."

He turned to the access ring and put his hands on it, and the cold came up through his gloves, and he began to work.

* * *

Here is what Tam found in the next six minutes, and he would remember every detail of it for the rest of his life, because it was the last six minutes of the world as it had been:

The access ring had nine fail-safes. Eight of them were mechanical and beautiful — pressure interlocks, neural-load governors, a kill-switch that would eject the occupant if their vitals collapsed. The ninth was not mechanical. The ninth was a single line of Fleet Standard etched into the underside of the cradle, in handwriting Tam recognized, because he'd spent a night in the quarantine bunks reading scans of the maintenance logs while Beatrix slept, and the handwriting on the cradle was the same handwriting as the logs, and the handwriting in the logs was Beatrix Crane's.

The line said: *I am sorry it has to be a mind. I could not make it anything smaller than a whole person.*

Tam read it three times. Then he called Beatrix over, and she read it, and she was silent for a long moment, and then she said, "She left me a note," in a voice Tam had never heard her use — not smuggler, not Wren, not the hard flat woman who ran vials past customs. Something younger than any of those. "Two hundred years, and she left me a note under my own chair."

"Beatrix," Tam said. "The governors will hold your vitals. The eject will work. But the interface load — the schematic numbers, the ones that aren't redacted — they're sized for an augmented lace. Fleet-grade. Yours is civilian, and old, and patched." He made himself say the rest. "It might hold. It might not. If it doesn't, the eject fires at brain-death thresholds, and the beacon still doesn't get its answer, and the default runs."

"Then I'd better not die," Beatrix said.

"That's your plan?"

"That's always been my plan. It's gotten me this far."

* * *

At T-minus eleven minutes — Tam was timing it now, calling it out every sixty seconds because Ilsa had asked him to and because the counting kept the terror in a box, kept it shaped like numbers instead of like the frost-writing and the lowering filaments and the amber question mark pulsing faster and faster beneath the glass — the chamber doors at the top of the shaft slammed open.

Tam spun, heart slamming, hand going to the cutting torch on his belt.

It was Ilsa. Alone, suited, sliding down the last stretch of the guide rail with a speed that would have gotten any of her crew a reprimand, and she hit the chamber floor running.

"Ilsa, the shaft—"

"Kell's holding the ring. The cartel breached the dock. They're coming down the primary spine, and it's a forty-minute climb, and I decided I wasn't going to spend those forty minutes wondering." She reached them, breathing hard, her child's face set in the expression Tam had followed through fire and vacuum and a war they'd inherited. "Report."

"Interface is neural-direct, keyed to the architect, she's the only key, default runs in ten minutes, and the seat might kill her," Tam said, all of it in one breath, engineer's report, clean.

Ilsa looked at Beatrix. Beatrix looked at the cradle. The filaments had drifted lower, close enough now that Tam could see each one ended in a contact the size of a snowflake.

"Then we're out of time to be careful," Ilsa said. "Beatrix. Last chance to walk away. We seal the chamber, we take the default to the council courts, we fight it with lawyers and truth and however many years we have. It might not work. But you'd live."

"And the waking order runs," Beatrix said. "My faction first. The generation that built the truce and then cheated on it, waking up to find the other half of humanity still frozen and a beacon full of children holding the keys. You've read the founding documents, Captain. You know what that world looks like. I do too. I just don't remember *how* I know." She stepped toward the cradle. "I didn't climb down here to walk away. I climbed down here to finish what a woman started two hundred years ago and was too afraid to finish herself. Sit down, Tam. Both of you. I need my engineers for about ninety seconds."

They helped her up into the cradle — Tam at her left side, Ilsa at her right, small hands and heavy keys, guiding her back against the pale substance that flexed to receive her like it had been waiting, like it had been *shaped* by the memory of her, which Tam supposed it had. The filaments descended. Beatrix flinched, one small flinch, and then went still.

"Contact sequence on my mark," Tam said, kneeling at the access ring, hands on the governors. "Ilsa, her vitals are on your board. The moment the load curve bends the wrong way, you call it, and I fire the eject, and we do not debate it. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Ilsa said.

"Beatrix?"

From inside the cradle, half-crowned in filaments, Beatrix Crane smiled — a real one, crooked and tired and unafraid, and Tam thought: *that's what forty looks like. That's what it's supposed to look like. Not the body. That.*

"Fire when ready, Mr. Tam," she said.

He closed the governors. The contacts touched her temples. The chamber went dark — every lamp, every glow, all of it swallowed at once except the amber pulse beneath the glass, which now beat once every single second, faster, faster, a question being asked with no time left to answer it.

And out of the dark, in a voice that was not Beatrix's and was not the beacon's and was somehow both — a voice like the frost-writing given breath — the core spoke its first words in two hundred years:

**"Architect. The truce ends. Who wakes first?"**

Tam's hands hovered over the eject, shaking, waiting for her answer — or for the load alarm.
