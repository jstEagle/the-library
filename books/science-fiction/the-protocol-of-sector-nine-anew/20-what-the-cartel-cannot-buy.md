## 20. What the Cartel Cannot Buy

Davor Kell had spent twenty-two years learning that everything could be bought. He'd learned it the way you learn anything in Customs: slowly, then all at once, then so thoroughly it stopped feeling like a lesson and started feeling like gravity. Inspectors bought their postings. Supervisors bought their silences. The Meridian cartel bought whole dock authorities the way other people bought produce — by weight, by season, by whatever was ripe.

He had believed it because believing it was easier. If everything had a price, then his own corruption was just arithmetic, and arithmetic carried no shame.

Then Beatrix Crane had stood in a quarantine airlock with a cartel's fortune on one side of her and forty children on the other, and chosen the side with no money in it at all.

And now, three days later, Kell sat in his office watching the arithmetic fail everywhere at once.

* * *

It began, as these things did, with a leak.

Not from Beatrix — Kell had expected it to come from her, and he'd been wrong, and being wrong about her had become a familiar and almost comfortable sensation. It came from inside Meridian itself. A junior archivist named Pell Osei, whose name Kell had never heard before that morning and would never forget after, had copied the cartel's internal ledger of purchased memories — including the beacon's original construction records, including the truce terms, including the waking order Meridian had paid eleven million credits to keep frozen — and pushed it onto every open channel in Sector Nine simultaneously.

No ransom. No negotiation. Just the raw data, tagged with a single line of metadata:

**THE BEACON IS NOT A CLOCK. IT IS A QUESTION. READ IT YOURSELF.**

By the time Kell's terminal finished syncing, three of his own inspectors were already reading it aloud in the corridor outside his office, voices overlapping like a congregation discovering scripture. The truce between the two cryosleep arks — the bargain that had held half of humanity in frozen suspension for two hundred years while the other half forgot them — had never been a countdown at all. The beacon asked. Someone answered. And the someone who had been answering, cycle after cycle, decade after decade, was a woman who kept erasing herself so the answer could stay honest.

Kell read the whole file twice. Then he read the part about himself — because there was a part about himself, buried in the cartel's purchase records, itemized with the cold precision of everything else they owned. *Inspector Davor Kell, Customs Division Nine. Asset acquired via memory brokerage, standard terms. Leverage profile: deceased dependent (female, age six). Retention status: active.*

They had written his grief down as an asset line. His daughter — not even his daughter, only the memory of her, the thirty seconds of her laughing in a rain of recycled water on a station he could no longer name — filed between a shipping manifest forgery and a bribe schedule.

He sat very still for a long time.

Then he did something he had never once done in twenty-two years of service. He opened his case files and told the truth.

* * *

The report went out at 0400 station time, unencrypted, addressed to the Oversight Board, the Sector Nine civil authority, and — because Kell no longer saw the point of half measures — to the public channel. Fourteen pages. Every payment he'd taken. Every warrant he'd quashed. Every smuggler he'd protected and every honest officer he'd undermined, listed by name and date, including the ones who had thought themselves his friends and would now learn otherwise. At the bottom, under the section labeled *Relevant Failures of Duty*, he wrote the sentence he'd been carrying since the night the Halcyon came home:

*"I was compromised by the Meridian cartel through purchased memory assets. I knowingly pursued the smuggler known as 'Wren' on falsified grounds while suppressing evidence of the beacon's true function. The evidence is attached. I resign."*

His supervisor called within the hour. Kell let it ring out.

His union representative called next. Kell let that ring out too.

The third call was from a number he didn't recognize, and on instinct — old instinct, corrupt instinct — he almost didn't answer. But something in him had shifted its weight, some load-bearing wall of cynicism finally giving way, and he picked up.

"This is Odile Marchand's advocate," said a voice like dry paper. "Madame Marchand is conscious. She has asked, specifically and repeatedly, for Inspector Kell."

Kell closed his eyes. The last time he'd seen Odile, she'd been bleeding out on the quarantine deck with a cartel bullet through her, having burned her own ledger rather than give up Beatrix Crane — and then given her up anyway, under duress, and hated herself for it with a completeness Kell recognized from the inside.

"Is she going to live?" he asked.

"The physicians say yes. Whether she intends to is another matter. She says she has a page to deliver, and she will not deliver it to anyone but you. She says—" the advocate paused, consulting notes, "—she says you are the only man in Sector Nine dishonest enough to be trusted with it."

That sounded like Odile.

"I'll come," Kell said. "But not today. Today I have to watch my career die in public, and I find I want to be present for it."

* * *

It died beautifully.

By noon, the Oversight Board had convened an emergency session and voted unanimously to strip him of his commission pending full investigation. By early afternoon, the civil authority had issued a statement expressing *profound concern* and *commitment to transparency*, which everyone understood to mean they were terrified. By evening, the story had metastasized past the beacon entirely. Every settlement, every ark-bound enclave, every orbital habitat with a receiver was arguing about the question the beacon asked — *who wakes first?* — and about the fact that for two centuries, one exhausted woman had been answering it alone in the dark.

Meridian tried to contain it. That was the tragedy of cartels, Kell thought, watching their statements scroll past: they were built for buying things, and there were some things that could not be bought back once given away. They issued denials. The denials were contradicted by their own leaked ledger. They offered rewards for information on the leaker. Three hundred people claimed to be Pell Osei within six hours, most of them joking, none of them prosecuted, because prosecuting truth-tellers had suddenly become bad business. Their stock in memory futures — the trade that had made them, the vials of yesterday sold to people starving for a past — collapsed by seventy percent in a single trading day, because it turned out that when people learned the largest memory broker in the sector had been sitting on the recorded confession of civilization's founding lie, they stopped trusting anyone's inventory.

A cartel, Kell reflected, was just a lie with logistics. And logistics could survive almost anything except the lie dying in public.

There was one more thing Meridian tried. It arrived at Kell's door at dusk, in the person of a courier with an unmarked case and an expression of professional neutrality. Inside the case: a single neural vial, sealed, tagged with a serial number Kell knew by heart. His daughter's memory. The original master copy — not the degraded duplicate he'd been leasing from the cartel for years, but the source itself, pristine, permanent, his.

The accompanying note read: *"A gesture of continued goodwill. Certain arrangements can be restored. Certain others need never have been disturbed."*

He understood the offer perfectly. Stand down. Recant the report. Testify that the leaked records were forged. And get back the thirty seconds of rain and laughter that Meridian had been renting him like a room.

Kell held the vial for a long time under his desk lamp, turning it, watching the memory-gel catch the light the way she used to catch the light, running through the spray with her arms out.

Then he walked to the nearest waste reclamation chute and dropped it in, and stood there listening until he heard the incinerator cycle, and felt — not peace, nothing so clean as peace — but the specific lightness of a man setting down a thing he'd mistaken for a spine.

Truth couldn't be stolen, he thought. Only rented. And he was done renting.

* * *

He went to see Odile on the fourth day.

She was propped up in a recovery cot in the charity ward, thin as a folded letter, one arm in a brace, her eyes already sharp enough to draw blood. She watched him cross the ward with the expression of a woman appraising damaged goods she'd once overpaid for.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I resigned publicly. It's the best I've looked in years."

"Mm." She patted the cot's edge. He sat. For a moment neither of them spoke; the ward hummed around them, all oxygen and regret.

"You gave me up," Kell said. "Under duress. And you burned the ledger first."

"I burned the *copy*. Never found the real final page, did they?" Her mouth twitched. "No. Of course they didn't. Because I'm still alive, and the page isn't where anyone thinks it is."

"What's on it?"

Odile reached beneath her pillow with her good hand and produced a folded sheet of flimsi, soft at the creases from years of hiding. She held it out, and when Kell took it, her grip tightened on the corner for one second longer than necessary — a fence's last instinct, the reflex of a lifetime spent knowing exactly what everything was worth.

"That," she said, "is the list of every memory Meridian ever bought from the beacon's original builders. Including the second half of Beatrix Crane's confession. The part she hasn't found yet. The part that changes what the beacon *is*."

Kell looked down at the page. Names, dates, lot numbers — and there, third from the top, an entry that made his breath go shallow: *Crane, B. — architect's confession, segment two — acquisition pre-dates current registry.*

"Why me?" he asked. "You have a dozen people closer to her than I am. People she doesn't hate."

"She doesn't hate you, Inspector. That's your tragedy and your qualification." Odile sank back against the pillow, exhausted by the transaction, but her eyes stayed on him. "And because I'm dying on a schedule now, and I wanted the last thing I ever fenced to go free of charge. Consider it my resignation. You'd know how that feels."

Kell folded the page into his coat, over his heart, where he supposed contraband belonged.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Deliver it." She closed her eyes. "And Davor — when you see her — tell her the ledger always balanced. Tell her I checked the math myself."

* * *

He found Beatrix at the beacon relay station two days later, or rather, she found him — stepped out of a maintenance corridor with her hands visible and her posture unreadable, the way smugglers greeted former inspectors, which was to say the way prey greeted predators who had recently changed diets.

"I heard you burned your career," she said.

"I heard you burned the cartel. Between us we've been thorough."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ground where one might build. He took out the flimsi page and held it between them without ceremony.

"From Odile. She's alive. She says the ledger balanced." He hesitated. "There's a second half to your confession in there. Segment two. I haven't read it. I want that noted somewhere official, if any official channels still take my calls — I haven't read it. It was never mine."

Beatrix took the page slowly, the way people take things that might be hot or holy. She scanned it, and Kell watched her face do several things in sequence — recognition, dread, and then a kind of terrible relief, like a woman finding the last piece of a map she'd hoped was lost.

"There's a choice-point," she said quietly. "The beacon was never meant to decide. It was meant to ask. My past self left the answer unmade. On purpose. For me." She looked up at him. "For whoever I became."

Kell nodded, and found that his throat had gone tight around something that wasn't quite words.

"They'll issue a warrant eventually," he said instead. "My resignation won't stop the Board forever. There are people above me who liked the old arrangement, who'll want Wren's head as a closing exhibit." He met her eyes. "I'm telling you this as a courtesy. As a debt. When the warrant comes, I won't be the one holding it. And if I can slow it down, I will. After that—"

"After that you should run," she finished. "Both of us. Smugglers and disgraced inspectors. We'd make a respectable crew."

"With the children?"

"With the children. Someone has to teach Captain Reyes proper evasion patterns, and apparently it takes a criminal to do it."

For a moment they stood in the relay station's dim light, two ruined professionals acknowledging the only currency that had survived the collapse — the truth, freely given, impossible to steal, appreciating daily.

Then Beatrix turned toward the beacon chamber, the flimsi page in her hand and Ilsa Reyes waiting somewhere beyond the bulkhead with a vote already cast, and Kell turned toward the dock, toward whatever a man became after he'd finally stopped selling himself by the pound.

Behind him, faint through the hull, the beacon's signal changed key — no longer counting down, but opening outward, a question spreading across the dark toward every sleeping pod in the system.

Who wakes first?

The answer, Kell understood now, was never going to belong to any of them alone.
