## 1. The Gardener's Almanac

The wind on Sector Nine came in from the western ridge at 0600, every morning, as if it had signed a contract. Casper Fairweather had logged it that way for eleven years: *0600, westerly, 14 knots, carries iron dust from the ridge scree.* The dust gave the dawn light a bruised, coppery quality, and Casper had long ago stopped thinking of it as weather and started thinking of it as a colleague—the only one who showed up on time.

They were in the long beds by first light, shears in hand, working the nitrogen-fixers. The alder-hybrids had gone leggy again at the northern row; they always did, reaching for a sun that arrived a half-hour later on that side of the dome. Casper pruned with the kind of attention most people reserved for prayer. Each cut was a small argument with the plant: *not there, not like that—there.* The shears made a sound like a whisper being agreed with.

"You're getting greedy," Casper told the row, because eleven years alone will make a conversationalist out of anyone, and because the agronomist who had trained them—hadn't there been an agronomist? there must have been—had said that talking to plants was superstition, and Casper had decided, in the absence of anyone to contradict, that superstition was a fine thing to keep. "I gave you that bed. I gave you the good drainage. And this is what you do."

The alder said nothing. It put out a new leaf, insolent and green.

Casper straightened, pressed a knuckle into the small of their back, and surveyed the garden. It was, by any honest measure, beautiful. Two hectares under glass and open sky, terraced in the old colonial pattern—food crops on the south slope, medicinals in the shaded hollow, the ornamental walk where Casper grew things no one would ever see blooming: moonwort, glass-lily, a climbing rose bred for a gravity it had never known. The dome's far curve caught the morning light and held it like a cupped hand. Somewhere under the mulch, ten thousand small lives turned the soil over, patient as monks.

It was a garden planted for the dead.

That was the phrase that came, unbidden, and Casper caught it the way you catch a wasp in a glass—quick, before it stings, and then a long moment of looking at it through the curve.

*No,* Casper thought. *A garden planted for colonists. For the hundred-year sleepers in the ships that hadn't come yet.* The terraforming charter was explicit. Sector Nine was a forward station: prepare the soil, prove the atmosphere, keep the beacon lit, and when the *Meridian* colony fleet arrived at the turn of the century, hand them a world ready to receive them. That was the work. That was the whole of the work.

Casper tipped the wasp-glass, and the thought flew off into the copper light.

They logged the morning: temperature, humidity, soil pH from the third bed, the alder pruning, a note about the irrigation line on the terrace that wept a little at every cycle and that Casper had decided, after seven years, was a feature rather than a fault. The log went into the station archive, timestamped, signed. Eleven years of logs, flawless. Casper had checked once, early on, out of boredom—read back through years of entries in a single long night, looking for the shape of their own history the way you'd read a stranger's diary.

The logs were perfect. That had bothered them for exactly as long as it took to fall asleep, and then it never bothered them again.

* * *

Midday was for walking the perimeter.

It wasn't required. Nothing on Sector Nine was required except the beacon and the garden, and even the garden would have forgiven neglect for a season. But Casper walked the perimeter the way other people went to church: the same route, the same pace, past the water reclamation sheds, past the seed vault with its triple seal, along the dome's northern arc where the glass was oldest and the light came through green as underwater.

At the north arc there was a stretch of ground—maybe six meters of it—where Casper never walked.

There was no reason. That was the honest truth of it, and Casper had made an honest life, so they admitted it plainly, at least to themselves: there was a stretch of ground they did not walk, and they did not know why, and after eleven years they had stopped asking. The soil there was fine. The weeds grew as weeds do. Once, in the fourth year, Casper had started to cross it and had stopped mid-stride with their heart going like a trapped bird, and had stood there sweating in the green light until the feeling passed, and had gone around, and had not tried again.

You learn to live around the missing parts. That was the whole art of it. Casper's memories of Earth were like a book with pages torn out—there was a harbor, there was a mother's voice saying something in a language that dissolved before it reached understanding, there was a storybook with a cover the color of deep water. The rest was smooth white absence, and Casper had learned to run a finger over the absences without snagging, the way you learn to ignore a tooth that aches only when you bite down on the left.

The storybook. That one snagged sometimes.

Casper stood at the edge of the unwalked ground, hands in pockets, and let the snag come. A word, floating up: *Vessic.* It wasn't a word for anything they could name—a place, a name, a language? It arrived with the smell of paper and a low adult voice reading aloud, and then it was gone, and Casper was left holding the shape of it like the dent a hand leaves in a bed.

"Huh," Casper said to the weeds, and walked around.

* * *

The afternoon went to the medicinals. The feverfew needed dividing; the comfrey had staged another attempt on the walkway and was repelled with prejudice. Casper worked with the door of the greenhouse open so the pollinators could come and go—the station kept a hive of engineered bees, docile and slightly too large, who had long ago stopped being livestock and become something like neighbors. One of them rode on Casper's shoulder for an hour, a warm ticking weight, and Casper narrated the work to it.

"Division like this—see, you want three crowns minimum, or you're just being cruel. And she'll sulk for a week. They always sulk. You'd sulk too if someone took a spade to your—"

The bee flew off, unimpressed. Casper finished the sentence anyway, to the feverfew.

This was the part of the day the charter hadn't prepared them for: not the work, but the silence around the work. Eleven years of a voice that only heard itself. Casper had gotten good at it—had developed, without noticing, a whole liturgy of small talk addressed to plants and bees and weather systems. But some evenings the silence came down like a change in pressure, and Casper would stand in the middle of the garden with dirt on their hands and feel the size of the dome and the size of the century and the size of the one small life moving between them, and the ache would arrive.

It wasn't loneliness exactly. Loneliness was a hunger, and Casper had learned to feed it. This was something else—an ache with a shape, like a missing tooth the tongue keeps finding. As if there were a chair somewhere that should have someone in it. As if a name were being said somewhere just below the threshold of hearing, and if Casper could only get quiet enough—

The comfrey needed tying back. Casper tied it back.

* * *

Evening: the log, the meal, the walk to the beacon.

The beacon stood on the ridge above the dome, a slender black spire with a single ring of light at its crown. It had no business being beautiful, but at dusk it was—silhouetted against the fading copper sky like a needle holding a thread that stitched the station to the stars. Casper climbed to it every night. It was the last item in the day's ledger and the one Casper never skipped, not through the fever year, not through the winter the irrigation failed, not once in eleven years.

The ritual was simple. Casper put a palm against the beacon's base, which was warm the way stone is warm after a sunny day, though it had no business holding heat, and said, "Still here."

The beacon pulsed once in answer—a soft bloom of light that rolled down the spire and out, silent, toward the dark.

That was the whole ceremony. Casper had no idea who the pulse was *for*. The charter said the beacon maintained navigation telemetry for the incoming fleet; the fleet was ninety years out; nobody was listening. But the pulse went out every night at 2100, and Casper said *still here* every night at 2059, and somewhere in the eleventh year Casper had realized that the ritual wasn't maintenance at all. It was a conversation. It was the only one they had where both parties spoke.

"Still here," Casper said.

The light bloomed. Held. Faded.

Casper stayed a while longer, as they always did, watching the first stars come out through the thinning dust. The ridge was cold. Below, the dome glowed faintly green, the garden breathing its slow night-breath, and Casper felt the day's contentment settle over them like a coat—real, worn-in, theirs.

And underneath it, as always, the ache. The missing pages. The unwalked ground. The word *Vessic* surfacing and sinking like something drowned.

Casper turned to go, and that was when the beacon spoke.

Not pulsed—*spoke.* The light at its crown stuttered, amber, then white, then amber, and a tone came through the spire's skin that Casper had never heard in eleven years: a rising three-note chime, patient, insistent, unmistakable.

An incoming signal.

Casper stood very still on the cold ridge, their breath hanging in the starlight, and felt the ache in their chest turn over like something waking up.

The beacon had never received anything before. In eleven years, not once.

It was listening now.
