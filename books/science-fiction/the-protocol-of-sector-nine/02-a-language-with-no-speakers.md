## 2. A Language With No Speakers

The signal came in on the third watch of the night, which was the hour Casper had privately named the listening hour, because that was when Sector Nine went quiet enough to hear itself breathe.

They were awake anyway. They were always awake for it, though they'd never once admitted that to the log.

Outside the viewport, the garden lay silvered under the light of a gas giant's reflected glow—rows of nitrogen-fixers with their feathery crowns folded for the night, the trellised squash vines hanging like sleeping animals, the long dark ribbon of the irrigation channel carrying meltwater from the polar tap toward beds that would not feed anyone for another hundred years. Somewhere past the eastern windbreak, the beacon stood on its single leg of durasteel, patient as a heron, blinking its slow red pulse into the dark: one flash every eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. Eleven years. Casper had never thought about whether those numbers meant anything until tonight, and even then only because the console woke them with a sound they had never heard before.

Not an alarm. Alarms on Sector Nine were designed by the same Bureau committees that designed everything else—flat, apologetic, impossible to panic about. This was different. This was a tone that rose and fell like someone clearing their throat before speaking, and it came from the beacon's receiver array, which in eleven years had logged exactly four things: two pulsar sweeps, one piece of orbital debris, and a comet catalogued before Casper was born.

Casper sat up in the narrow bunk, bare feet finding cold deck plating, and crossed the habitat module in nine steps. They knew the distance without counting. That was the kind of thing you learned about your own life after enough years alone: not its meaning, but its measurements.

The console displayed a waveform, long and layered, still arriving in pieces from somewhere beyond the system's heliopause. Signal strength: marginal. Origin: unregistered vector. Compression: none. And beneath the technical readouts, already decoded into raw audio because the beacon's software did that automatically, without asking permission—

A voice.

Casper pressed play before they could think better of it, and the habitat module filled with a language they knew.

*Vessic.*

They knew it the way you know a smell from childhood—whole, immediate, before understanding. The voice was a woman's, worn thin by distance or grief or both, and she was speaking quickly, urgently, the syllables rising at the ends of phrases like questions that expected no answers. Behind her voice there were other sounds: a low mechanical thrum, something rhythmic and metallic that might have been a hatch cycling, might have been knocking. And underneath all of it, so faint Casper had to hold their breath to catch it, a child was crying.

Casper stood in the cold blue light of the console with their arms wrapped around themselves, and understood every word.

That was the horror of it. Not the signal—a distress call, eleven years into a solitary posting, was strange but explicable; ships went missing, beacons failed, the universe was full of people calling out. The horror was that Casper Fairweather, who had been assigned to Sector Nine at age twenty-four with a standard linguistic package covering Trade Common, High Meridian, and the diplomatic pidgin used across the Reach, who had never been trained in any dead language, who had never *asked* to be trained in any dead language—

Casper listened to the woman speak and translated as naturally as breathing.

*We are remaining. We are remaining where we fell. If any hear this: what was done here must be kept. Keep the record. Keep—*

The phrase broke apart into static, then reassembled further down the band, repeating. A loop. The woman had been dead for decades, and her voice was still circling the dark like a moth against glass.

*Keep the record.*

Casper's mouth was dry. They said aloud, to the empty room, in Vessic: "Who are you?"

And their own voice answered them in Vessic, and that was worse. Because they hadn't decided to say it in Vessic. Their throat had simply known.

* * *

The storybook explanation arrived first, because explanations always arrive dressed in their most comfortable clothes.

There had been a book. Casper was almost sure of it. A childhood thing, back on Meridian Station—or had it been Ceres?—a slim illustrated volume their mother read aloud, about a girl who planted a garden on a dying world. The girl had sung songs in an old language, and the illustrations had shown flowers opening to the melody, and Casper had loved the book so fiercely they'd memorized the songs without knowing what they meant.

Except.

Casper pulled up their own file, the personnel record every station keeper carried in their wrist terminal like a second heartbeat. Birthplace: Meridian Station. Parents: listed, deceased, dates listed. Education: listed. Linguistic certifications: listed, three of them, none of them Vessic. And below that, the section Casper had skimmed a thousand times and never once read closely—the medical history, the inoculation schedule, the psychological evaluations, each entry stamped with the same grey seal:

*RECORD SEALED — BUREAU DIRECTIVE 7-C. ACCESS RESTRICTED.*

Eleven years. Eleven years of reading that line and thinking: bureaucracy. Eleven years of assuming that sealed records were like locked doors in a house you grew up in—present, ignorable, part of the architecture. Everyone's file had odd corners. Everyone's childhood had rooms the family didn't enter.

But now the door was rattling.

Casper sat down slowly on the floor of the habitat module, back against the hydroponics cabinet, and tried to remember the storybook. Tried to remember their mother's face. And found—this was the thing, this was the crack running through the middle of everything—found that they could remember the *book* with perfect clarity, the weight of it, the smell of the paper, the exact shade of green in the illustration of the garden, but the face above them as they were read to was a warm blur, a smudge of light where a person should be. They could remember the songs. They could not remember learning them.

You don't memorize a language from a picture book. You can love a language from a picture book. But the woman on the recording wasn't singing nursery rhymes. She was using the future tense of a subjunctive construction that Casper's mind parsed without hesitation, a grammatical form so specific that linguists had argued about whether Vessic even retained it, and Casper's brain handed them the translation the way it handed them their own name.

*What was done here must be kept.*

Here. The word snagged. Casper replayed the loop four times, five, leaning close to the speaker, and on the sixth pass they caught it—not in the voice but in the metadata, the carrier packet wrapped around the audio like paper around a stone. Transmission origin coordinates. The beacon had decoded them automatically, because the beacon was thorough even when Casper was afraid.

The coordinates resolved onto the station plot, and Casper stared at them for a long time.

Sector Nine's footprint was modest: the habitat ring, the greenhouse spans, the beacon pad, the garden itself laid out in its careful grid over six hectares of engineered soil. Casper knew every meter of it. They had dug every meter of it. They had turned that soil with their own hands for eleven years, season after season, composting and tilling and planting, and they knew its color the way sailors know the sea—grey-brown near the old construction pads, black and rich in the central beds, rust-red where the iron content ran high along the eastern terrace.

The coordinates put the signal's origin inside the footprint. Inside the garden. Eastern terrace. The rust-red soil.

Which was impossible, because the signal was coming from beyond the heliopause, from deep space, from a ship or a hulk or a ghost—and yet the beacon insisted, with the dumb patience of all machines, that the place the voice was calling from was forty meters from where Casper sat, buried under the tomatoes.

*We are remaining where we fell.*

Casper got up. They didn't decide to get up; their body simply stood, the way their throat had simply spoken Vessic, and they registered distantly that this was becoming a pattern—knowledge moving through them without passing through their consent. They pulled a jacket over their sleep clothes, took a hand lamp and a soil probe from the tool locker, and let themselves out into the night.

* * *

The garden at night was a different country. The gas giant's light made the rows look like the ribs of something enormous sleeping under a sheet, and the air smelled of wet loam and cut stems, the smell Casper had loved for eleven years, the smell they had told themselves was the whole reason they were here. They walked the main path fast, boots loud on the gravel, breath steaming, and the beacon pulsed overhead—eleven seconds, eleven seconds—as if counting them down.

The eastern terrace rose gently above the rest of the garden, terraced into three levels, planted with the deep-rooted perennials that liked the iron-rich ground. Casper stopped at the coordinates' center, which put them between two old quince trees they had pruned every autumn without ever asking why quince, why here, why these two trees set slightly apart from the others like a pair of mourners.

Their hands were shaking. They noticed it with a strange detachment, as if the hands belonged to the woman on the recording.

The soil probe went in easy—too easy. Eleven years of probing this terrace and Casper knew its resistance profile the way they knew their own pulse, and this was wrong. At forty centimeters the probe dropped through softness, through something yielding and fibrous, and came back up trailing threads of pale root woven tight around fragments of—

Casper knelt. The lamp threw their shadow huge across the terrace wall. With bare fingers—they didn't remember taking off the gloves—they brushed the soil away from the root mass, and the roots parted like curtains, and the light found bone.

Small bones. Woven into the root systems, cradled really, held the way soil holds anything it has been given long enough to keep. A fragment of rib. Something that might have been a small hand, the fingers curled, the roots grown through and between them so thoroughly that the tree and the child had become a single argument against letting go.

The sirens started then—not outside, not in the garden, but behind Casper's eyes, a memory arriving like rain from a clear sky: red light strobing down a corridor, the taste of copper, a woman's voice saying *keep the record, keep the record, don't let them—* and then white, and then nothing, and then eleven years of tomatoes.

Casper Fairweather knelt in the rust-red soil of the only home they could remember, holding a bone in one hand and a lifetime of lies in the other, and far overhead the beacon pulsed its patient red light—eleven seconds, eleven seconds—like a heart waiting to be asked what it had witnessed.

The loop was still playing on the wrist terminal. The woman's voice, thin and clear across the dark:

*If any hear this: dig.*
