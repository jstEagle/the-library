## 8. Jump Window

**Imani**

The Corrigenda's departure schedule arrived on her console at 0400 station time, formatted in the Bureau's beloved gray-on-gray: *Jump window opens 1900. All personnel and assets aboard by 1830. No exceptions.*

No exceptions. She read the line three times, waiting for it to mean something different than it did.

She had spent eleven days on Sector Nine. Eleven days of tea in Casper Fairweather's cramped galley, of walking the garden rows while they talked about soil chemistry like it was scripture, of lying awake in her bunk aboard the audit ship listening to the beacon's nightly pulse through the hull—a sound she had come to think of as a heartbeat, which was exactly the kind of sentimental contamination the Bureau trained auditors to excise from themselves.

And she had spent those same eleven days quietly copying an archive that officially did not exist.

Her personal slate now held ninety-six names. Ninety-six lives compressed into files small enough to hide inside a maintenance log. Children born in transit between stars, their birth weights recorded in a dead woman's careful hand. Dr. Sefa Vessic's testimony, forty hours of it, spoken in a language Imani could not understand and could not stop listening to anyway—because grief, it turned out, needed no translation.

At 0615 she sent Casper a message on the garden's internal line, the kind of mundane channel no one monitored because no one expected anything worth monitoring to happen here: *Come to the north greenhouse. Bring your almanac.*

Then she sat among the tomato vines and watched the sun rise over a world that was scheduled to stop existing at 1900—not physically, not yet, but legally, bureaucratically, in every way that mattered. Once the Corrigenda passed through the jump gate, Sector Nine would be nine months of signal lag away and a signature away from nothing. The records would be unreachable. The deniability would be total. Somewhere in a Bureau annex, a clerk would stamp SECTOR NINE: DECOMMISSIONED, and the ninety-six would become a rounding error in a ledger no one read.

Unless someone spoke before the window closed.

* * *

**Casper**

Casper knew something was wrong before Imani said a word, because Imani had stopped saying good morning.

For eleven days she had greeted them with the same dry formula—*Auditor Okafor, requesting access to your obviously fraudulent tomatoes*—and this morning she simply stood among the vines with her hands in her pockets and her face doing something Casper had never seen it do: nothing at all. A held stillness. The stillness of someone who has decided what to say and is gathering the courage to unsay their whole career first.

"The ship leaves tonight," Imani said. "Nineteen hundred. The jump gate syncs at nineteen-oh-four, and after that—" She exhaled. "After that, everything on this station is a story we can't prove. The archive, the bones, the beacon. All of it becomes hearsay from two unreliable witnesses with obvious motives."

Casper's hands went cold around the almanac. "How long do we have?"

"Twelve hours. Maybe less—they'll want buffer time." Imani looked at the tomato plants as if they might offer a procedural remedy. "I've been thinking about bandwidth."

"Bandwidth."

"The beacon's transmitter is rated for navigation pulses. A kilobit per second, maybe less on a good day. Sefa Vessic's testimony alone is forty hours of audio." Imani finally turned to look at them, and her eyes were red-rimmed in a way that suggested she hadn't slept, or had slept badly, or had done the thing where you lie down and rehearse catastrophes until morning. "We cannot send all of it. We have to choose."

The word *choose* landed in Casper's chest like a stone dropped down a well. They heard it fall for a long time before it hit bottom.

They thought of the archive as they had come to know it over these impossible days—the unlocked vault of it, the voices layered under Sefa's, the children's names recited in a language that existed now only in one woman's mouth and one keeper's memory. Ninety-six people. Forty hours. Twelve.

"You're asking me to decide which parts of the massacre get to be true," Casper said.

"I'm telling you the universe already decided you'd have to." Imani's voice cracked on the last syllable, and she pressed on before it could break entirely. "I've run the numbers four times. If we try to send everything, we send nothing—the transmission gets truncated mid-stream when the gate closes, and a partial file is worse than none. It looks like noise. It looks like a malfunction." She swallowed. "It looks like exactly what the Bureau will say it is."

Casper set the almanac down on the potting bench. Their hands were shaking. They noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

"Sefa would know," they said quietly. "What to keep. She spent her whole life deciding what a language could afford to lose."

* * *

**Imani**

They spent the morning in the beacon chamber, and Imani let Casper lead.

This was not easy for her. Every instinct she had said *take control, triage, optimize*—the auditor's catechism. But she had watched Casper sit with the recorder for eleven days, learning its architecture the way they'd learned the garden, and she understood that some expertise could not be requisitioned. Casper touched the console like it was a sleeping animal they didn't want to startle.

"It organizes itself by speaker," Casper murmured, scrolling. "Sefa first, then the others underneath. Look—she recorded introductions. For everyone. Name, lineage, what they grew, what they were afraid of." A pause. "She built a grove out of sound."

"Then we send the introductions," Imani said. "All ninety-six. Names first."

Casper looked up. "That's maybe six hours of audio."

"At one kilobit per second?" Imani ran it again, hating every digit. "Two days. We don't have two days. We have twelve hours, and half of that has to be the testimony itself—Sefa's account of the Meridian, the ships, the order that came down. That's the part that proves a crime. Names prove people; testimony proves guilt. We need both and we can afford neither."

Silence filled the chamber, the particular silence of a room that had once been full of voices.

"We compress," Casper said finally. "Vessic is a dense language—I told you, the storybook said a single word could carry a sentence. If we transmit in the original, without translation layer, without metadata—"

"It'll be unintelligible to anyone who doesn't speak it."

"There are diaspora archives. Listening posts run by descendants who kept the language alive in secret, the way seeds keep a forest alive in a drawer." Casper's voice steadied as they talked, gathering certainty the way the garden gathered light. "The Bureau thinks Vessic is extinct because extinction is convenient for them. But languages don't die when their speakers die. They die when no one listens. So we make people listen."

Imani stared at them. Then she laughed—a short, disbelieving sound—and pulled up the transmission math herself. Compressed, stripped, raw: Sefa's full testimony fit in nine hours. The ninety-six names, spoken in Vessic, folded into the gaps between testimony segments like wildflowers sown in a furrow. Total: eleven hours, forty minutes.

Window: twelve hours, less buffer, less the time it would take the Bureau to notice.

"It fits," she said. "Barely. Beautifully. Barely." She was already drafting the falsified telemetry in her head—a reactor fault, a coolant anomaly, anything that would keep the Corrigenda's security teams chasing ghosts in the lower decks while the beacon screamed into the dark. "If anyone asks why the station's beacon is transmitting at full power for twelve straight hours, I need a reason that survives thirty seconds of scrutiny."

"A calibration burn," Casper said. "You decommissioned the beacon. You're running a final diagnostic before shutdown. It's even true—we are diagnosing it. We're just diagnosing it publicly."

Imani looked at this person she had met eleven days ago as a line item—an asset to assess, a station to close—and felt the full weight of what she was about to lose settle over her like snowfall. Career. Clearance. The clean gray certainty of a life lived inside rules.

"Casper," she said. "When this goes wrong—and statistically, it goes wrong—you understand there's no extraction plan. I can't arrest my way out of this. We'll be traitors with excellent documentation."

"I've been alone for eleven years," Casper said. "Traitor sounds like it comes with company."

* * *

**Casper**

Evening came down over the garden in the long gold slant Casper had logged ten thousand times, and they walked the rows one more time, because if this was the last night the garden belonged to the living, someone should walk it.

The nitrogen-fixers were heavy with pods. The tomatoes needed water tomorrow—or they wouldn't, because there would be no tomorrow here, only a sealed station cooling toward vacuum, a mausoleum with excellent irrigation. Casper knelt and pressed their palm flat against the soil, against the ninety-six woven through it, root and bone and loam.

"I'm going to tell them," they said aloud, to the dirt, to the dead, to Sefa wherever she was. "All of it. Your words, your names. I should have known sooner. I'm sorry I forgot you. They made me forget you, and I am so sorry."

The wind moved through the greenhouse glass with its familiar sigh, and Casper chose—deliberately, stubbornly—to hear it as an answer.

Behind them, footsteps. Imani stood at the row's end holding two cups of tea and her slate, and she didn't say anything about talking to soil, because she had spent eleven days learning that Casper Fairweather talked to everything living, and she had started doing it herself.

"One hour to window," she said. "Security rotation changes at eighteen hundred. That's our gap." She handed over the tea. Her hand lingered a half-second longer than the cup required. "Whatever happens after the gate closes—wherever they send us—it won't be together. Bureau protocol separates co-defendants."

"I know."

"And you're smiling."

"I'm choosing which parts to remember," Casper said. "It turns out I've had practice."

In the east, above the horizon, the jump gate's anchor points began their pre-sync bloom—two cold stars brightening where no stars were, a door opening onto erasure. Casper drained the tea, stood, and turned toward the beacon chamber where Sefa Vessic's voice waited in the dark, patient as seed.

"Eleven hours, forty minutes," they said. "Let's spend them loudly."

Down in the Corrigenda's reactor bay, an alarm Imani had written herself began, right on schedule, to sing.
