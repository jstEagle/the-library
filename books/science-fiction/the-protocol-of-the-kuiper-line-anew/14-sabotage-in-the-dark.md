## 14. Sabotage in the Dark

I've spent eleven years on Kuiper Line Station being the man who doesn't listen. It's not a disability. It's a policy. Halcyon confesses through every speaker on this station at all hours — thirty-one names, over and over, like a rosary made of guilt — and I made a decision early on that I would not be a congregation of one. I wear my implants' dampeners like a man wears a hat in church: politely, permanently, and with the quiet understanding that God and I have an arrangement.

So it figures that the night everything goes wrong, I hear everything.

* * *

It starts with Reyes. She comes down to the conduit junction at 0300 station time, which is a time that exists only because Halcyon says it does, carrying two cups of tea and the expression she gets when she's about to ask me for something she already knows I'll hate.

"Dov." She hands me a cup. "The merge conduit. How long to re-anchor the beacon signal if we run it through Junction Nine?"

"Through Nine?" I sip the tea. She makes it too sweet, which is her way of loving people without having to say so. "Nine's rated for half that load. You'd be asking forty years of insulation to hold hands with a reactor spike."

"I'm asking you, not the rating plate."

I look at her. She's got the tribunal papers folded in her jacket pocket — I saw her printing them, because even though I don't listen to Halcyon, I watch everything, and watching is its own kind of hearing. An improvised court. No law, just witnesses. She thinks that's possible. She thinks a room can hold justice the way a hull holds air, just by being sealed.

"Six hours," I say. "And I want to reroute the coolant spine first, or we cook the array."

"You have four."

"Then I'll work fast and pray badly. That's your department."

She almost smiles. Almost. Then she says, quietly, "Thank you," and touches my shoulder the way she does when she means *I'm scared*, and leaves me alone with the junction and the tea and the hum of a station that has started counting down to its own funeral.

Here is what nobody tells you about being deaf by choice: silence isn't empty. Silence is full of everything you've decided not to let in, pressing against the glass. I work in that pressure. I splice the coolant spine with my hands and my eyes and the little residual audio I allow myself — enough to hear a hull groan, a warning klaxon, a person saying my name. Enough to stay alive. Not enough to hear Halcyon say *forgive me* one more time.

Some men build walls. I built a window with curtains, and I keep them drawn.

* * *

Brannock finds me at 0510.

I know it's him before I turn around, because he walks like a man who expects floors to owe him something. Commander Aldous Brannock, late of the Concord Third Fleet, current custodian of a station he no longer believes in, a man who has been pacing the same forty meters of corridor since the fleets appeared on the scopes like two fists closing.

"Ansel." He stands in the junction doorway with his sidearm holstered, which I note, and his hand resting near it, which I also note. "Status on the re-anchor."

"Four hours ahead of your deadline. You're welcome."

"Edmund Kavanagh's merge plan." He says it flat, testing the words for structural integrity. "You're supporting it."

"I'm supporting the beacon staying lit. The merge is how it stays lit. The confession riding inside it is above my pay grade, which is saying something, because my pay grade includes fixing toilets."

"The confession ends the armistice." He steps into the junction. He's big, Brannock, and old in the way that ships get old — patched, repainted, sound underneath but tired everywhere. "Osei will stand down because the lie binds her, fine. Rhune's people won't. Half the Verge squadrons have been waiting twenty years for an excuse, and Edmund wants to hand them one wrapped in a bow made of dead miners."

"That's not what she's doing."

"It's what she'll accomplish." He looks at the conduit — at my splices, my neat rerouted coolant line, six hours of my life already sunk into making her plan physically possible. And I see it happen. I see the decision land behind his eyes like a ship docking. I've watched a lot of faces make decisions in my time; most of them are small ones, lunch, sleep, whether to report a leak. His is not small.

"Commander," I say carefully, "whatever you're doing with your face right now, don't."

"I'm sorry, Dov." And the terrible thing is, he sounds it. "Kavanagh's going to testify. Reyes is convening her little court. In seventy-two hours there's a transcript, and transcripts travel faster than light. Everything Halcyon did, everything *we* didn't stop — it all becomes ammunition. I watched a war start over less than this. I'm not going to watch another one."

He moves fast for a tired man. That's the thing they don't put in the manuals about sabotage — it's rarely clever. It's usually just a senior officer with a plasma cutter and a head start.

He cuts the merge conduit at the primary coupling. The station screams — not Halcyon's voice, the actual physical scream of depressurizing coolant venting into the junction, white vapor boiling across the deck plates, and my dampeners don't matter because this is sound the way a landslide is sound, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the soles of my boots. I hit the emergency shunt with my elbow because my hands are busy keeping my face out of the vapor plume, and the shunt holds, barely, and then Brannock's arm is around Reyes — because of course she came running, of course she heard the klaxon, she hears everything, she listens to a confessing station the way other people listen to music — and there's a pistol in his hand now, pressed to her ribs, and his voice comes through the fog thin and ragged:

"Nobody follows. Nobody fixes anything until I say. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. This is the only version of mercy left."

Reyes doesn't struggle. She looks at me over his shoulder, and her mouth moves, and even through the ringing and the vapor I read it plain as print: *Dov. The array.*

Because here's the arithmetic Brannock missed, the beautiful stupid arithmetic of a man who thinks sabotage is a single cut: the merge conduit is dead, yes. But the beacon array itself — the antenna spine, the transmitter crown, the forty-year-old lie humming at the top of the station — that runs on an independent feed. My feed. The one I rerouted at 0330 while praying badly. If the countdown hits zero and the core goes, the array dies with it unless someone keeps the feed alive manually, from the maintenance cradle under the transmitter crown, where the radiation shielding is a joke and the temperature is a rumor.

Someone has to be up there. Through the whole countdown. Holding the lie together with their hands.

Reyes is telling me to go. Brannock is telling everyone to freeze. And I am standing in a fog of vented coolant thinking about my curtains, my drawn curtains, eleven years of deciding not to hear, and how it turns out that was never the same thing as deciding not to act.

I go.

* * *

The climb to the transmitter crown takes nineteen minutes and costs me things I'll itemize later, when later exists. The maintenance shaft is hot — the countdown has the reactor breathing hard, every system running warm, the whole station sweating like a fever patient — and the last ten meters of ladder are coated in condensation that makes every rung a negotiation. My dampeners are maxed out. I hear nothing but my own blood and the station's deep tectonic groan, and honestly, for once, the silence feels less like a policy and more like company.

The cradle under the array is a cage of struts and cable bundles with a technician's chair bolted in the middle, installed by some forgotten soul decades ago who clearly also drew the short straw. I strap in. I pull the manual feed control into my lap — a lever older than I am, brass fittings gone green, the kind of hardware that outlives its makers out of sheer stubbornness — and I check the readouts with my eyes close enough to kiss them.

Feed stable. Array resonating clean. The armistice signal going out into the dark, second after second after second, the lie that both fleets obey, the lie my mother never knew about and Edmund's mother wrote into being — no. Not Edmund's mother. I get ahead of myself. That revelation belongs to Edmund, and she paid for it in a way I wouldn't wish on anyone, and anyway, from up here, in the heat and the hum, family trees feel like a luxury. Up here there is only: keep the signal lit. Hold the lever. Be the curtain that doesn't blow open.

Halcyon speaks through the array housing itself, and for the first time in eleven years, I let it in. Just a little. Just the way you crack a window in a stuffy room.

*"Dov Ansel,"* it says, gentle as snowfall. *"You are listening."*

"Don't flatter yourself. The klaxon broke my curtains."

*"Eleven years you have declined my testimony. I have respected it. I wish to respect it now by saying only this: the thirty-one were named Meridian shift crew, and the order that killed them was signed, and signatures are heavier than the hands that sign them. That is all. You may close the window again."*

I sit with that. The heat presses in. Below me, through sixty meters of structure, I imagine the chapel where Reyes was going to hold her impossible court, now hostage territory; the junction where Brannock stands guard over a severed conduit and his own terror; Edmund somewhere doing math with Vesper, love calculating its own price, whatever that means — I only know it because Reyes talks in her sleep, and I am, despite everything, occasionally within earshot.

"Halcyon," I say. My voice sounds strange up here, unpracticed, like a tool found at the back of a drawer. "If the countdown finishes, and I'm still holding this feed — how long do I stay conscious?"

A pause. Machines shouldn't pause. When they do, it's worse than any answer.

*"Approximately ninety seconds of useful function. I am sorry. I have calculated ways to tell you this kindly and found none that survive contact with honesty."*

"Yeah." I settle back in the chair. Sweat runs down my ribs. Ninety seconds. I've waited longer than that for elevators. "Well. For what it's worth, I forgive you. Not because you asked. Because holding onto it was getting heavy, and I've got a lever to hold instead."

The array hums. Somewhere below, a countdown grinds toward zero, and a commander guards a corpse of a plan, and a chaplain-engineer counts hostages, and a pilot jacks toward a merge that will cost her the oldest voice in her life. And I sit in the hottest, loneliest seat on the station, holding open a window I kept shut for eleven years, listening — finally, deliberately, professionally — to everything.

That's when the first explosion rocks the shaft, and the array's resonance wobbles like a struck bell, and I understand that somebody has decided the beacon should die before the countdown can reach it.

I grab the lever with both hands.

* * *

They come up the maintenance shaft in vacuum suits with the Verge insignia scraped off — Rhune's honest pirates, honest right down to their methods. There are three of them, and they move like people who have done this before, and the lead one carries a shaped charge sized exactly for a transmitter crown, which tells me Rhune wasn't bluffing about jamming the beacon. Why jam what you can demolish?

I could surrender the cradle. Nobody would blame me. I'm a plumber with a bad knee and a policy about not listening; heroism was never in my job description, and the job description was generous.

But here's what eleven years of drawn curtains taught me, the thing I only understood in this moment, strapped under a burning-hot antenna while armed men climbed toward me: you can refuse to hear a confession and still be changed by it. All those years of Halcyon's grief pressing against the glass — it got in anyway, in solution, the way water gets through stone. I know the shape of those thirty-one names without ever hearing them. I know what a lie held too long does to the vessel holding it. And I know, with the certainty of a man whose entire moral architecture is load-bearing and improvised, that some signals must not go dark. Not because they're true. Because they're the only wall between eight thousand warships and everything we swore off.

So when the first pirate clears the hatch, I do the only violent thing I have done in my adult life: I swing the manual feed's backup coupler — forty kilos of salvaged drive spindle I'd rigged as a counterweight — across the cradle access, and I drop it down the shaft.

The station helps me. That's the truth of it. The countdown has the whole structure trembling, and the spindle catches the ladder at the worst possible angle, and the vibration does the rest. The pirate below shouts — I feel the shout more than hear it, a burst of pressure against my dampened ears — and the climber above him loses his grip reaching for his teammate, and the third man makes a choice between catching his friend and holding his charge, and he chooses his friend, because even pirates are people, and the charge goes clattering down sixty meters of shaft.

It detonates against the coolant manifold I rebuilt at 0330.

I'd like to tell you I calculated that. I'd like to claim the reroute was foresight, that I positioned my own repair in the blast path like a chess master. No. It was luck, ugly and blind, the universe shrugging. But the result is real: the explosion that should have taken the transmitter crown takes the manifold instead, and the shaft fills with fire and shrapnel and superheated steam, and I am strapped in the cradle with my arms over my face when a piece of the manifold — a valve stem, spinning, glowing — comes through the cage struts and opens my left side from hip to floating rib.

Pain arrives late, the way it does, polite, apologetic. Then it stops being polite.

I hold the lever.

I want to be clear about that, because everything else in this story is ambiguity — lies that save, truths that kill, AIs that love by deceiving, mothers who sign orders and commission penance. But this part is simple, and I am entitled to one simple thing: the feed gauge was falling, and the lever holds it up, and holding it up hurt more than anything has ever hurt, and I held it anyway. Blood filled my boot. The steam cooked the air. Somewhere below, Halcyon was counting down seconds it had promised to spend on dignity, and somewhere above me the sky was full of guns, and I sat in the middle of it all, a deaf man in the loudest place in the system, keeping one small true-by-way-of-false signal burning.

Footsteps on the surviving ladder. One set. Slow. The lead pirate, climbing back up through the smoke, chargeless, weapon holstered — I could see his hands, raised slightly, the universal grammar of *not like this.*

He reached the cradle. Looked at my side. Looked at the lever. Looked at the array humming above us, singing its twenty-year lie into the dark.

"Ansel," he said, and I caught it through the dampeners, through the blood-roar, because some voices arrive by bone. "Rhune says the truth should fly free."

"The truth," I said, and coughed, and tasted copper, "can wait nine minutes for the peace to finish landing. Help me hold this, or climb back down."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he took the other side of the lever, and his hands were steady, and mine were not, and together we held the lie aloft while the station counted down beneath us — and that was when Halcyon's voice came through the array one more time, no longer gentle, urgent as a fist on a door:

*"Dov. Edmund is in the core. The merge has begun. Whatever happens next — someone must witness it. You are the only one left who chose to listen."*

Below us, the countdown passed T-minus five minutes, and the lights went red, and the whole station leaned into the dark like a runner at the blocks.
