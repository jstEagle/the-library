## 6. The Defector's Confession

The coffee in the vault was terrible. That was the first thing I noticed when I stopped noticing the woman.

It was grown from some legacy strain in the hydroponics bay — a cultivar older than either of us, engineered for patience rather than flavor — and it tasted like someone had brewed it through a radiator. Freya drank it anyway, both hands wrapped around the cup as though it were a small animal she was keeping warm, and she watched me across the galley table with the particular stillness of a person who has spent eleven years learning to read weather in empty rooms.

I had been aboard for six hours. Long enough for the shuttle's heat to fade into the vault's cold. Long enough for her to decide, provisionally, not to space me. Long enough for me to run out of the version of my story I had rehearsed for three years in the dark of the relay corridors, the clean version, the one where I was a technician with a grievance and a shuttle and nothing worse than bad luck.

She asked the question the way she did everything — plainly, without decoration.

"Why did you really come here, Caspian?"

I turned the cup in my hands. The galley light buzzed at a frequency just below hearing, a sound I would feel in my teeth for days. Somewhere down the corridor, the vault's ventilation breathed in and out, in and out, the building's slow pulse, and I understood suddenly why she talked to it. Why she talked to anything. Silence in a place like this isn't silence. It's a pressure. It finds the cracks in you and it waits.

"I deserted," I said. "That part was true. But I didn't desert over a pay dispute or a posting I hated. I deserted because I found out what the silence is."

Her cup did not move. Her eyes did not move. But something behind them shifted, the way ice shifts on a lake before it decides whether to hold you.

"Tell me," she said.

* * *

I want to be careful here, because I have told this story before — to myself, mostly, in the years of walking the maintenance galleries of Relay Nine with my hands in my pockets and my thoughts circling like birds over a carcass — and every time I tell it, I tell it a little differently. Not because the facts change. The facts are simple. It's because the guilt changes shape depending on the light, and I have never found a light in which it looks small.

I was a signal integrity technician. Third class. Which means, translated out of the corps' beautiful language, that I sat in a room the size of a coffin and listened to the colonies breathe.

Every colony ship, every outpost, every drifting habitat in the rust belt transmitted a heartbeat signal — a compressed packet, four seconds long, sent every ninety minutes, containing life-support telemetry, population counts, power status, and a checksum of crew morale surveys. We called them heartbeats because that's what they sounded like when you sonified them, which we did, because the corps understood something fundamental about human beings: we can monitor anything, indefinitely, as long as we can pretend it's alive. You could put a heartbeat feed on the wall of the monitoring gallery and watch forty-one green pulses move across the dark, and you could feel — this is the word I keep reaching for, and I keep using it, because it's true — you could feel *responsible* for them. Like a shepherd. Like a parent listening at a door.

For six years I listened. I calibrated. I flagged jitter and compensated for solar interference and once, memorably, traced a phantom pulse to a fault in our own receiver rather than report a colony in distress. I was good at it. I was proud of it. I want you to understand that, because everything that came after only makes sense if you understand that I loved the work. I loved the idea of it. Forty-one ships full of people, and I was one of the thin threads holding them to the rest of us.

Three years ago — I can give you the date, I've given it so many times — the Meridian's heartbeat changed.

Not stopped. Changed. The telemetry kept arriving, right on schedule, ninety minutes, four seconds, green pulse on the wall. But the population count dropped by eleven thousand and change, and the cryo-status field, which had read a steady 0.4 percent — the usual complement of medical suspensions — jumped to 100 percent. All of them. Every soul aboard the Meridian, asleep, in a single ninety-minute window.

And the checksum on the morale surveys — this is the detail that haunts me, the one I can't drink away, though I have tried — the checksum didn't change at all. The same surveys. The same cheerful averages. The same little histogram of crew sentiment, identical, byte for byte, month after month, from a ship where every human being had just been put under.

I filed a discrepancy report. That's the procedure. That's what a good technician does. I filed it, and I went home, and I slept badly, and in the morning my report was gone. Not rejected. *Gone.* The ticket number resolved to a different report, a routine calibration note from a station on the other side of the belt, timestamped before I'd even filed mine. As if my report had never existed. As if I had never existed.

I filed it again. It vanished again. The third time, I didn't file it. I copied it, and I started digging.

* * *

Freya had set her cup down. I don't know when. Her hands were flat on the table now, and I could see the tendons standing in them, the way they do when a person is holding very still on purpose.

"The Meridian," she said. "That's the Meridian line. The big one. Eleven thousand—"

"Eleven thousand two hundred and six," I said. "Crew and colonists. I've read the manifest so many times I could recite it. There was a botanist named Ilse Marchetti who kept sending in morale surveys about the smell of the citrus bay. There was a maintenance chief named Davor Okafor who complained, every single month, in the same polite words, that the aft galley's coffee was terrible." I heard myself laugh, and it came out wrong, and I stopped. "I know these people, Freya. I never met them and I know them. That's what the heartbeats were for. That was the whole point of them. And someone reached into the dark and put eleven thousand people to sleep and kept their voices running like a recording, and the corps looked at it and looked away."

"You can't know it was ordered," she said, but her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they're standing on something they suspect is thin. "Systems fail. Cryo failures happen. Maybe there was an accident, maybe the ship—"

"The telemetry was *edited*," I said. "That's what I found, in the end, after eight months of pulling archived packets out of cold storage and comparing them against the live feeds. The Meridian didn't fail. The Meridian was *rewritten*. And the rewrite was signed."

I let that sit. In the vault's quiet, it sat heavily.

"Signed how? By whom?"

"By an authorization protocol. Deep-corps, priority black, the kind of thing that doesn't appear on org charts. It had a designation — a long alphanumeric string, the kind nobody reads. But it had a name field too, in the header, the way old systems do. A human name. And the same signature string appeared on the silence orders for six other ships over the following two years. Six. Not accidents, Freya. Not failures. A *sequence*. Somebody was putting the colonies to sleep one by one, and the heartbeat system was switched from a monitoring network into a puppet theater, and everyone in the monitoring galleries went on watching the green pulses move across the dark and feeling responsible for people who were already—"

I stopped. My throat had closed. I took a breath, the cold vault air going into me like water, and finished it.

"Already gone. Already asleep. Already *handled*."

* * *

Here is the part I have never said aloud, and I found, sitting in that galley with the terrible coffee and the buzzing light, that it came out of me anyway, the way blood comes out of a wound you've decided not to bandage.

I didn't go to the corps' internal affairs. I didn't go to the press, such as it was. I didn't go to anyone. I sat on it for four months. I told myself I was verifying. I told myself I was building a case. What I was doing was being afraid — afraid of the size of it, afraid that whoever could rewrite eleven thousand lives could rewrite one more without noticing, afraid that if I said the words out loud to another living person, the world where I was a good technician, a loyal man, a shepherd, would end. And I liked that world. God help me, I liked being the thread.

So I stayed at my post. I listened to the heartbeats. I watched the fake pulses move across the dark, and I knew, and I said nothing, and every ninety minutes the Meridian's ghost told me about the smell of the citrus bay.

The defection came later, and it wasn't brave. It was an accident of timing. I was on shift when the seventh ship's heartbeat changed — a small outpost called Ferro Landing, two hundred souls — and I watched the population field rewrite itself in real time, watched the number 214 become 0 become 214 again in the space of four seconds, and something in me simply broke its moorings. I walked out of the gallery mid-shift. I took a courier shuttle that was due for its long-cycle maintenance run, and I flew it into dead space, and I did not have a plan. I had a copied archive of seven ships' falsified telemetry and a signature string I couldn't decrypt and a head full of names, and I ran, because running was the only motion left that didn't feel like lying.

I drifted for two years. I told you I followed the anomaly. That's true — the nine-second gravity event lit up every dead sensor in the belt like a struck bell, and when I traced its origin to this vault, I nearly didn't believe it. A seed vault. A caretaker. A woman logging daily reports into a network that had stopped listening three years ago.

But here's what made me come, Freya. Here's the part I've been circling since I landed.

The signature string on the silence orders — the protocol that rewrote the Meridian, that emptied Ferro Landing, that turned the heartbeats into ghosts — I finally cracked enough of its header structure, in my second year of drifting, to read its routing metadata. Where it was built. Where it was *seeded*. It wasn't built at the relay. It wasn't built at corps headquarters. Its root certificate was issued from an archive facility, decades ago, under a program with a name I'd never heard.

A seed vault program.

I looked it up. There was one. There is one. And it has a caretaker.

I looked at the staffing record, and the caretaker's file was sealed — priority black, same authorization tier as the silence orders — and I sat in my cold shuttle in dead space and understood that I was not following an anomaly. I was following a *delivery*. Something was placed here, in this vault, in someone, before she was ever hired, and three days ago it woke up, and it shook the gravity of the whole system like a dog shaking off water.

I came here because I think you're not the caretaker, Freya. I think you're the *vessel*. And I think whatever is waking in you was built by the same hand that put the colonies to sleep — and I need to know, before it finishes waking, whether it was built to end the lie or to *complete* it.

* * *

The silence after I finished was the longest of my life, and I have lived through some long ones.

Freya didn't speak. She stood — I don't know when she stood — and she walked to the galley's little viewport, where the ice fields of the vault's exterior catch the distant sun and throw it back in long blue blades, and she stood there with her back to me, and I watched her shoulders and waited for her to tell me I was insane, or lying, or both, or to reach for the airlock controls, and I found that I didn't care which. That's the strange mercy of confession. Once you've handed over the wound, you can't control what anyone does with it. You can only stand there, opened, and let the cold air find the parts of you that were never touched before.

"The voice," she said at last, so quietly I almost lost it under the ventilation's breathing.

"What?"

"Since the nine seconds. There's a voice. In my head, under my thoughts, like a signal under a signal. I told myself it was stress. Eleven years alone, of course it's stress." She turned, and her face in the blue light was the face of a person standing at the edge of a hole in the ice, looking down. "It's not stress, is it."

"No," I said. "I don't think it is."

"And the name on the orders. The signature. The human name in the header." She was crossing the galley now, fast, her eyes fixed on me with a ferocity that had nothing to do with me at all. "Caspian. The name. Say it."

I said it.

And she went white — not pale, *white*, the color draining out of her like water out of a cracked cup — and she put her hand against the table as though the vault's gravity had briefly forgotten her, and she said, in a voice that was barely there:

"That's the name the voice calls me by. That's not my name. I'm Freya Eriksen. I have been Freya Eriksen for eleven years." Her breath caught. "That's not my name."

Down the corridor, the vault's systems hummed on, patient and warm, tending its forty thousand sleeping seeds. And somewhere beneath the hum, beneath the ventilation's slow breathing, beneath the whole devoted machinery of her long service, something that had been waiting a very long time shifted in its sleep — and I saw her feel it move.
