## 14. Sorel's Version of Events

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in old machines, and Administrator Edric Sorel had spent forty years learning to hear it the way other men heard music.

The outer relay was full of it now. The coolant pumps sang their one flat note behind the bulkheads; the antenna array ticked as it tracked satellites that no longer answered; and beneath all of it, the deep hum of the station's bones, the sound of a structure holding itself together out of habit. Sorel walked the corridors at the same hour every morning, not because anything needed walking, but because a man who stopped listening for what was wrong in his own house deserved whatever happened to him.

He heard them before he saw them — two sets of boots on the access ladder, uneven, exhausted, arguing in the low register that people used when they were afraid of being overheard by the dead.

So. They had come.

He had known they would, of course. That was the whole architecture of it. He had felt the first gravity event pass through the station like a shudder through a struck bell, nine seconds of weightlessness while his coffee climbed out of its cup and hung beside his head in trembling silver spheres, and he had thought, with something close to tenderness: *there she is.* The protocol, waking. The girl in the ice field, eleven years deep in her vigil, finally being called home.

He had watched their shuttle crawl across the dark on his scopes for six days. He had had time to prepare the tea.

* * *

Sorel believed in hospitality the way some men believed in scripture. When Freya Eriksen and Caspian Voss came through the airlock — frost still sublimating off their suits, eyes raw from dead space — he was standing in the relay's small galley with three cups steaming on the counter, and he let them see the cups before he said anything at all.

"Administrator Sorel," he said, and offered his hand to the woman first. "You must be the caretaker. I've listened to your logs for eleven years."

That was true, and he wanted it to be the first thing she learned about him. Truth, deployed correctly, was worth more than any lie. Her hand was cold and smaller than he'd expected, calloused in the specific pattern of someone who worked seals and valves by feel. She looked at him the way people look at water in a desert — wanting to drink, afraid of what might be living in it.

"You've been listening," she repeated.

"Every entry." He smiled, and made sure the smile reached his eyes, which took practice but was worth practicing. "Entry 2,847, when you found the frost bloom in Bay Nine and spent four hours photographing it because you said no one else would ever see it. I saw it, Ms. Eriksen. I want you to know that."

Something moved behind her face — a crack in the permafrost. He could work with cracks. Cracks were how the light got in, and also how you got inside a person without ever appearing to force a door.

The Voss man stood back, arms crossed, radiating the particular hostility of a man who has already decided you are his enemy and is waiting for you to confirm it. Sorel gave him a nod, warm but brief. You didn't waste warmth on men like that. You saved it for the ones who could still be reached.

"Come," he said. "Eat first. Then we'll talk. Dead space takes something out of a person. I've made the crossing enough times to know you shouldn't have conversations with what it leaves behind."

* * *

The galley table was real wood — walnut, salvaged from the first orbital habitats, older than any nation that still existed. Sorel had chosen this room for the meeting deliberately. Wood said *before*. Wood said *we were once a civilization that grew things*, and both of these people had spent their lives in service of growing things, in one way or another. Seeds. Signals. The same impulse, really: put something small and alive into the dark and trust it to take root.

Freya held her tea in both hands and did not drink. Voss ate everything he was given with the mechanical efficiency of a man refueling, watching Sorel over the rim of his bowl.

"Tell us about the colonies," Voss said finally. No preamble. Good. Sorel respected a man who came to the point, even if he intended to walk around it first.

"I will," Sorel said. "But let me tell you about myself first, because you've crossed all that darkness carrying a version of me, and I'd rather you have the right one."

He folded his hands on the walnut. Forty years of service. He let himself feel the weight of it, and let the feeling show.

"I was stationed at Meridian Control when the colony program began. I knew the ships. I knew the crews. I signed off on the provisioning manifests for the *Meridian* herself — I could tell you what year the coffee in her holds was roasted. So when I tell you what happened next, understand that it did not happen to abstractions. It happened to people whose names I knew."

Freya had gone very still. He kept his voice level, gentle, the voice of a doctor delivering a diagnosis he had delivered a thousand times and never grown comfortable delivering.

"The colonies failed, Ms. Eriksen. Not all at once. Not dramatically. They failed the way things fail out here — slowly, quietly, in the plumbing and the power curves and the genetics. Radiation accumulation. Closed-loop collapse. Fertility rates falling year over year until the demographers stopped publishing them. We watched it happen from inside for a decade. Every mitigation failed. Every workaround bought months, not years. And the end state was always going to be the same: billions of people dying in the dark, slowly, knowing it was happening, with nothing anyone could do."

He paused. Let the silence do its work.

"And here is the question that defined my life, and I suspect it will define yours: what do you tell them?"

Voss set down his bowl. "The truth."

"Yes," Sorel said warmly. "That's what you say when you're twenty-six years old and you've never watched a civilizational death begin. I said the same thing. I filed the reports. Do you know what happened? Panic. First the riots in the inner habitats — not because conditions worsened, Mr. Voss, nothing had worsened yet, but because *knowing* poisoned everything. Why maintain? Why plant orchards? Why have children? Hope is not a luxury out here. It is load-bearing. Remove it and the whole structure comes down faster than the failure ever would have arrived on its own. We proved that. God help us, we proved it with data."

He leaned forward slightly.

"So a decision was made. Not by monsters. By tired people who loved the species and hated themselves. The colonies would be told nothing. The ships would go quiet — managed quiet, orderly quiet — and the last functioning outposts, the vaults and relays, would be staffed by people who believed the silence was an accident, because belief is the only honest propaganda there is. A caretaker who knows she's tending a grave stops caring for it. A caretaker who thinks she's tending a garden—" he gestured, gently, at Freya, "—tends it for eleven years. Through frost blooms and failing seals and four thousand dawns. You kept the seeds alive, Ms. Eriksen. Every single one of them. Because no one told you it was hopeless. And because of you, when the science catches up — and it is catching up, the genome work alone justifies everything — those seeds are *viable*. Your ignorance was the most productive act of faith in human history."

"That's not ignorance," Freya said quietly. "That's abandonment."

"It's triage," Sorel said, without flinching. "I won't insult you by dressing it softer than that. Yes. We abandoned them to their own grief so that their grief wouldn't spread. Yes, the protocol exists to manage truth the way a dam manages water — not because water is evil, but because uncontrolled, it kills everything downstream. And yes, I have carried that dam on my back for forty years, alone, at the edge of everything, so that somewhere in the future there might be a spring worth flooding the valley for."

He sat back. Let them look at him. An old man, alone at a wooden table, offering them the only thing he had left: the burden, honestly named.

* * *

It was Freya he watched now, and Freya he spoke to, because Voss was already lost and they both knew it.

"You felt it, didn't you," Sorel said softly. "During the crossing. The doubt. The way it gets into the walls of you." He shook his head, sorrowful. "Doubt is the radiation exposure of the soul, Ms. Eriksen. Low dose, long duration, and it kills you so gradually you mistake the dying for maturity. I can take it out of you."

Her eyes came up. Sharp. Wary. But listening. God forgive him, listening.

"There's a facility," he said. "Inner system. Real gravity, real weather, real soil under real sky. The administration built it for exactly this contingency — for the day the protocol woke and its carriers came home. Restoration. Full diagnostic, full repair. And more than repair." He chose the next words the way he'd choose seeds for planting. "They can take the implanted material out cleanly. The memories that aren't yours, the protocols, the voice — gone. And the memories that *are* yours, the eleven years, the vault, the logs — restored, integrated, whole. You would wake up as yourself. Completely yourself. Perhaps for the first time, since apparently even your name was handled by someone else before you ever drew breath."

He saw the flinch. He pressed, gently, the way you press a wound to stop bleeding.

"You've been a tenant in your own head, Ms. Eriksen. Someone else holds the deed. I am offering you the eviction notice. Home. Wholeness. No more voices, no more protocols writing themselves into your sleep, no more nine-second falls. Just a woman who kept four thousand days of faithful watch, going where faithful watchers deserve to go." He spread his hands. "And Voss too, if he wants it. Amnesty. The corps forgets its defectors faster than you'd believe — forgetting is half of what we do."

The galley was silent except for the hum of the station's bones.

Freya looked down at her tea. And Sorel, watching her with forty years of practice, saw the thing he had hoped to see and dreaded seeing in equal measure: the longing. She *wanted* it. Of course she wanted it. Eleven years alone, and then dead space, and then a stranger's memories bleeding through her skull like ink through water — and here was an old man offering her a door out of her own haunted house.

Temptation required only that the bait be real. That was the cruelty and the elegance of mercy: it worked best when you meant it.

"I need time," she said.

"Of course." He rose, all warmth and courtesy. "The archive room is open to you — east wing, second corridor. Everything we have. I hide nothing in this station, Ms. Eriksen. Hiding is what got us here." A lie so smooth it had almost become true through repetition. "Rest. Eat. The station is yours."

* * *

After they had gone — Voss walking backward, watching him, Freya moving like a woman asleep on her feet — Sorel stood alone in the galley for a long time, looking at the three cups.

Two untouched. One drained to the dregs.

He washed them slowly, drying each one, and considered the shape of the next hours. The uplink lockdown was already staged — one authorization away. The shuttle's transponder would accept his override codes; he'd written them himself, years ago, for exactly this night. If the woman took the offer, all of this stayed holstered. If she didn't—

If she didn't, then he would do what he had always done. What the dam required.

He looked at his reflection in the darkened viewport: an old man, gray and neat, with kind eyes that had learned kindness the way a forger learns handwriting — well enough that even he sometimes couldn't tell the original from the copy.

In the east wing, faint through the deck plating, he could hear her footsteps. Moving toward the archive.

Moving toward the original orders.

Sorel closed his eyes, and began, quietly, to count the seconds.
