## 15. The Choice in the Cold Room

The archive was colder than the rest of the relay, and it was cold on purpose.

Freya felt it the moment the door sealed behind her — a dry, engineered chill, the kind that preserved paper and polymer and the fragile magnetic ghosts of old data cores. Sorel had given her access with a flourish of generosity, waving his hand across the reader like a man opening his own pantry. *Take whatever you need,* he'd said. *An honest servant has nothing to hide.*

She had smiled at him. Eleven years of smiling at an empty room had made the muscles easy.

Now she stood alone among the shelves.

The archive was smaller than she'd expected. After the vault — after four kilometers of racks and cryo-halls and seed corridors numbered like verses — this room felt almost intimate. Maybe forty meters square, lined floor to ceiling with storage cases, each one tagged in a handwriting style that had gone out of fashion decades ago. Dust hung in the air like something suspended mid-decision. The only light came from strips along the shelving edges, pale amber, the color of old honey, and it made the whole room look like the inside of a lantern.

Her breath fogged. She watched it drift and dissolve.

*You were built for this.* The thought arrived without permission, the way the woman's voice had been arriving for three days now, unspooling from somewhere beneath her own thoughts. She pressed it down. Not yet. First: proof.

Caspian was above, in the relay's operations ring, doing what he did best — making himself useful, charming the staff, mapping every lock and conduit with those quick gray eyes while pretending to study power schematics. He'd wanted to come down with her. She'd told him no. Not because she didn't trust him, but because whatever she found here, she needed to find it with no one watching. Some truths you have to meet alone or they don't take.

She pulled her gloves tighter and began at the first shelf.

* * *

The archive was organized by era, and the eras were organized by lie.

That was how it presented itself to her, anyway, shelf by shelf as she worked inward. The outermost cases held the public record: colony charters, launch manifests, the bright ceremonial documents of the Departure Years, all of it clean and hopeful and printed on stock meant to last five hundred years. She recognized some of the names. Everyone in the system recognized those names. Children learned them before they learned their own.

One shelf deeper, the tone shifted. Incident reports. Power budget disputes. A memo about coolant redundancy aboard *Meridian* that someone had annotated twice in red — once dismissively, once with a single underlined word: **again**. Freya stood very still in front of that one for a long moment, her breath clouding the protective film over the case, then moved on.

Deeper still, the shelves changed character entirely. No more official formatting. Hand-labeled cores. Dates that skipped years at a time. And a new classification code stamped on everything, small and unobtrusive, the kind of mark you only noticed if you were looking: **PRB**.

*Protocol of the Rust Belt.* Caspian had said the words like a curse in the shuttle, weeks ago, when he'd first explained what he believed lived inside her head. She hadn't wanted to believe him then. She could feel herself not wanting to believe him now, a whole-body resistance, like pushing against deep water.

She found the sealed case at the back wall, behind a false panel disguised as climate control housing. Her implant found it before her eyes did — a faint warmth behind her left ear, a soft insistence, like a hand resting on the back of her neck. Guiding her. She hated how grateful she felt for the guidance, and hated the gratitude, and hated most of all that whoever built her had anticipated even this: that she would hate being guided, and would follow anyway, because following was the deepest thing she knew how to be.

The panel gave with a click. Inside, nested in foam gone brittle with age, lay a single data core and a folded sheet of actual paper.

Paper. In this century. Whoever left this had wanted it *unhackable*, untouchable by any protocol including — she understood with a slow cold spreading through her chest — the protocol sleeping in her own skull.

She unfolded it with hands that shook only slightly, and read.

* * *

**ORDER 7-KESTREL**
**Classification: PRB / SOVEREIGN EYES ONLY**

*In the event of systemic colony failure — whether natural, induced, or declared — the caretaker asset designated FREYA-CLASS shall proceed to the outer relay and exercise final judgment regarding disclosure. This order confers no instruction as to which judgment is correct. The asset will know. That is what the asset is for.*

Signed:

**Dr. Annelise Kettering**
Director, Continuity Division

Below the signature, in different ink, added later, cramped and hurried:

*If you are reading this, Annelise, then it worked, or it didn't, and either way I'm sorry. You deserved to be a person first. — A.K.*

Freya read the page four times. Five. The amber light hummed. Somewhere far above her, the relay's ventilation cycled, a long mechanical sigh, and the dust rearranged itself around her like a congregation settling.

*FREYA-CLASS.*

Not Freya Eriksen, seed vault caretaker, hired eleven years ago from a shortlist she'd never seen. A class. A designation. A product line. She turned the phrase over and over and it cut differently each time — *asset*, *designated*, *the asset will know* — and underneath all of it, quiet as a heartbeat, the sentence that undid her completely:

*This order confers no instruction as to which judgment is correct.*

They hadn't programmed her answer. They'd programmed her *capacity to choose*. They had built a woman, planted her alone in the dark with four thousand mornings of devotion, and then written a document saying: whatever she decides, that was the design working as intended.

Her fury came up slowly, the way floodwater comes up a stairwell — level by level, drowning rooms she'd thought were sealed. Fury at Kettering. Fury at the Continuity Division, at Sorel with his warm voice and armed locks, at the entire architecture of people who had looked at the end of the world and decided the most important thing to preserve was *management*. But beneath the fury, colder and worse, ran something else: a terrible, grudging recognition.

Because they had been right about one thing. She *did* know.

She had known since the nine seconds of falling. Since the seeds drifted like snow and her first feeling — before dread, before fear — had been a wild, shameful wonder, the feeling of a system remembering what it was for. She had known in the dead space crossing, when she traded truths with Caspian in the dark and felt her own voice steady in a way it never had in eleven years of logging to no one. She had known standing in front of the *Meridian*'s hull, reading the crew manifest, whispering names into the black.

The choice had never been between truth and lie. It had been between the woman they built and the woman she'd spent eleven years becoming — and the horror, the unbearable, clarifying horror, was that those two women were not enemies. The caretaker who logged faithfully to silent skies and the asset designed to judge the silence were the same person, and always had been. Devotion and purpose. Prison and key.

"You deserved to be a person first."

Freya said it aloud, just to hear it in the cold room, and her breath fogged the words. Her eyes stung. She let them.

Then, carefully, she folded the paper along its original creases and slid it inside her jacket, against her skin, where no scanner would find it without asking her body's permission. The data core she took too. Whatever was on it, she would read every byte before she decided anything — but she already knew, with the flat certainty of a woman reading her own autopsy, that the core was secondary. The paper was the point. Someone had wanted there to be one human handprint in the middle of all this machinery. One apology, in ink, that no protocol could edit.

*You're not here to obey me,* the document said, in its careful bureaucratic way. *You're here to decide.*

And so the fury finished its work. It burned through the last of the girl who had believed she was hired, burned through the last comfortable fiction that she had stumbled into all of this, and what remained when the fire drew down was not ash. It was clarity — hard, cold, precise as the frost forming on the archive's oldest cases. She was not a caretaker who had discovered a conspiracy. She was the conspiracy's final instrument, awake at last, holding its own confession in her hands.

Which meant the choice was truly hers. Which meant, for the first time in her existence, she was free.

She laughed once — a short, startled sound that bounced off the shelving and came back to her sounding almost like weeping — and turned toward the door.

* * *

She was three steps from the exit when the implant spoke.

Not the woman's voice this time. Something else — a tone, low and structural, felt in the jaw more than heard, the way you feel a ship change course through the soles of your feet. The lights in the archive dimmed, held, and returned. Behind her, the false panel slid shut with a sound like a held breath released.

The door's status indicator, green since she'd entered, flicked to amber.

Freya stopped. Stood very still. Listened with her whole body, the way she'd learned to listen to the vault — not for sounds, but for changes in the quality of silence.

Above her, muffled through decks of rusted steel, something began to move. Not machinery. Footsteps — several sets, quick, coordinated, the particular rhythm of people who had just been given an order they'd been waiting to give. And threaded beneath them, thin as wire, the relay's internal comm channel opened on a frequency she shouldn't have been able to hear but could, now, clear as a bell rung inside her own skull:

*"Seal the lower decks. And find Voss. Now."*

Sorel's voice. Warm no longer.

Freya pressed her palm flat against the cold archive door and felt, beneath the metal, the faint tremor of the station reconfiguring itself around her — locks engaging deck by deck, a net drawing closed. They had let her into the archive. They had *wanted* her to find it, or wanted to know if she would. Either way, the test was over, and she had answered it by taking the paper against her skin like a second heartbeat.

She thought of Caspian upstairs, alone among strangers with armed locks. She thought of the shuttle on the ice, her only way out, her only way home to the vault. She thought of Order 7-Kestrel, and the woman who signed it, and the apology in different ink.

*The asset will know.*

She knew.

Freya Eriksen — FREYA-CLASS, caretaker, instrument, person — set her shoulder against the door, and pushed.
