## 13. Sabotage

The beacon told him before he did.

Silas had kept the habit for twenty years: every morning, before coffee, before the kettle even thought about boiling, he checked the reserve readout on his kitchen terminal the way other men checked weather. The numbers were a liturgy. Capacitor bank one at ninety-eight percent. Bank two at ninety-seven. Bank three holding steady through its slow, geological bleed. Twenty years of mornings, and the numbers had never once surprised him.

On the morning after Prentiss Vance's arrival, they did.

Bank one: eleven percent.

He stood in his kitchen with the steam of an untouched cup rising past his face and read it again, then again, as if repetition were a form of argument. Eleven percent. A number that should have taken four days to reach, dropping in a single night. He pulled the historical log with fingers that felt borrowed from someone else. The curve was beautiful and obscene—a smooth exponential decay starting at roughly 0300 local, ending at dawn, when whatever had been drinking from the reserves had apparently finished its meal and left the glass empty on the table.

Nobody drew power from the beacon's reserve banks. That was the entire point of them. They were not for use; they were for *holding*—a stored charge Odile Marchetti had designed into the system like a ballast, a deep reservoir that let the suppression field ride out solar weather and grid fluctuations without so much as a flicker. In twenty years of maintenance, Silas had drawn on them exactly twice, both times during lightning storms, both times logged, both times replenished within the hour.

Eleven percent meant the field was running thin. Running thin meant leaks. And leaks, Silas knew better than any man alive, meant bodies.

He dressed without tasting his coffee, took the long gravel road up to the beacon under a sky the color of unpolished tin, and let himself in through the maintenance hatch with hands that were almost steady.

* * *

Inside, the transmitter chamber smelled wrong.

Silas knew this room the way a priest knows his sanctuary—the particular mineral breath of the coolant lines, the ozone ghost that never quite left the air around the emitter housing, the faint animal warmth of the capacitors themselves, which hummed at a frequency you felt in your back teeth rather than heard. This morning the warmth was gone. The room felt like a church in winter. He put his palm flat against bank one's casing and it was cool as river stone, and something in his chest went cold to match it.

Then he saw the tap.

It was elegant work, he'd give whoever had done it that much. A shunt line spliced into the main draw conduit behind the inspection panel, routed down through the cable tray and out through a bore in the foundation that had been drilled—not cut, *drilled*, with a proper core bit—in clean silence sometime in the night. The splice used consortium-standard couplings. The insulation wrap was done in neat, overlapping turns by someone who cared about doing things right. Whoever had bled the beacon dry had not rushed, had not improvised, had not left so much as a stripped wire out of place.

They had simply decided the colony's shield was worth less than whatever they'd spent the charge on.

Silas crouched there in the cold hum of the dying field and made himself think it through, because thinking it through was the only discipline he had left. The tap required knowledge of the beacon's internals. There were perhaps six people on Vesper Hollow who had that knowledge, and two of them were dead, and one of them was drunk in a shipping container on the far side of the settlement, and one of them was himself.

Which left the auditor.

Vance had landed three days ago asking pointed questions about the beacon's power draw. Vance had clearance credentials that opened doors the colony council pretended didn't exist. And Vance had arrived—Silas had checked the port logs twice to be sure—one day before the drain began. The math was simple. The math was damning. The consortium wanted the beacon decommissioned for salvage; draining its reserves would accelerate the field's failure, manufacture an emergency, and hand Vance the justification he needed to condemn the whole installation as unstable. It was exactly the kind of quiet, deniable maneuver an insurance man would run.

Except.

Except that the splice work was too good for an auditor's quick job, and the bore through the foundation had been drilled from the *inside*, and there was a detail nagging at Silas like a splinter: the couplings were consortium-standard, yes, but the batch stamp on the insulation wrap was a colony supply code. Local stock. From the depot that only issued materials against council requisition forms.

He photographed everything anyway. Then he sealed the panel, disconnected the tap, and began the slow, ugly business of recharging what could be recharged—and all the while, in the back of his skull, a voice that sounded suspiciously like Odile Marchetti said: *You're looking for the thief in the wrong ledger, Quintana.*

* * *

He found Vance at the consortium guest quarters, eating breakfast alone with the methodical cheerlessness of a man who regarded food as fuel and meals as meetings.

"You drained my beacon," Silas said, without preamble, standing in the doorway with the rain just beginning outside.

Vance set down his fork. He didn't look startled. He looked, if anything, faintly relieved, the way a chess player looks when the opponent finally makes the expected move. "Good morning to you too, Mr. Quintana."

"Reserve banks one and two. Drained overnight. Someone spliced a tap into the draw conduit with consortium-grade hardware." Silas stepped inside and let the door hiss shut behind him. "You land, you start asking about the beacon's power consumption, and thirty hours later the beacon is bleeding out. I'm not a detective, Mr. Vance, but I can do arithmetic."

"Can you." Vance folded his hands on the table. Up close, in the flat morning light, Silas noticed the shadows under his eyes—the auditor hadn't been sleeping either. "Then do this sum. I filed a formal request yesterday to inspect the beacon's maintenance records. Denied. I requested access to the accident cluster files. Denied. I requested—politely, in writing—that the council confirm whether the beacon has an authorized power source, because my preliminary audit suggests it draws more energy than the entire settlement grid produces, from nowhere, and no one here will tell me why." His voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, tectonic. "I am an auditor, Quintana. I don't sabotage things. Sabotage creates liability. I document things. Documentation is how I kill things—slowly, legally, with paperwork. If I wanted your beacon dead, it would die in a hearing room, not in the dirt."

"The requisition stock on that tap came from the colony depot," Silas said. "Consortium people don't requisition locally."

"No," Vance agreed. "We don't." And then he looked at Silas for a long moment, and the auditor's mask thinned enough for Silas to see the calculation running behind it—not malice, but inventory. Weighing. Deciding how much truth this stubborn, exhausted claims man could be trusted to carry. "Who else knows the beacon's internals?"

"Me. Odile Marchetti. Three council members have oversight access on paper, though none of them have ever used it."

"Three council members." Vance repeated it softly, tasting it. "And which of those three has been arguing for the last two years that the settlement's infrastructure budget is insolvent? Which one chaired the committee that tried to sell the old survey equipment off-world?"

Silas felt the floor tilt slightly beneath his certainty. "Emeric Voss chairs the infrastructure committee."

"Mm." Vance picked up his fork, considered it, put it down again. "Here's what I know, Quintana, since we're apparently sharing. Two nights ago, someone accessed the depot after hours using a supervisor override. The override belongs to a council seat, not a name—I checked. And yesterday morning, before the sun was up, a hauler crew chief told me Voss asked him, very casually, over drinks, what salvage-grade capacitor stock might fetch per kilogram off-world." Vance's eyes came up. "Your beacon is dying, and the man who may have killed it wants to be paid for the corpse. But understand something before you go marching into that council chamber with your photographs. You have no proof. Neither do I. And a town that's spent twenty years not asking questions about its own dead will tear apart anyone who starts asking them now."

"They'll have to ask eventually," Silas said. "The field is failing. Whatever that reservoir was holding back—it's thinner than it's been in twenty years. If it goes, it won't be one farmer under a harvester. It'll be all of us, or worse."

"Worse," Vance echoed. He studied Silas with the expression of a man reading a claim file that refused to close. "You know what it hides. Don't you. You've known for years, and you've been maintaining it anyway, and signing the death certificates anyway." He shook his head slowly—not contempt, Silas realized, but something closer to recognition, the awful kinship of two men who had both built careers out of not saying things. "Get your proof, Quintana. Real proof. Because in about a week I'm filing a report that will end this colony's insurance coverage entirely, and when that happens, the only thing anyone will care about is who broke the beacon—and I promise you, they will not be fair about it."

* * *

The rain had thickened by the time Silas reached the depot, a grey sheeting that turned the settlement's lanes into rivers of mud and lamplight. He didn't go to the depot. Not yet. Proof first, accusation second—he'd learned that lesson across three hundred files, watching honest men ruin themselves on confident guesses.

Instead he went to the shipping container on the east edge of town where Odile Marchetti lived inside her bottles, and found her sitting on an overturned crate in the doorway, watching the rain, sober for possibly the first time since he'd known her. She'd heard about the drain. Of course she had. Half the settlement's grid telemetry ran through instruments she'd built, and the beacon's hunger spike would have shown up on her salvaged monitors like a scream.

"Show me the photos," she said, before he'd gotten his boots fully under the awning.

She scrolled through them on his slate in silence, her thumb moving with a precision that contradicted the tremor in her hands. At the image of the splice she stopped. Zoomed. Stopped breathing for a moment.

"That's my work," she said quietly. "Not my *technique*. My actual couplings. I donated a crate of spares to the depot fifteen years ago when the consortium audited our stores—they were going to write up the beacon as undocumented inventory, so I laundered the parts through the general supply." She looked up at him, and her eyes were terribly clear. "Anyone who tapped that line with those couplings either learned from me or learned from the manuals I wrote. Which narrows your list of suspects to people who've read my engineering documentation."

"Council oversight access comes with the full technical archive," Silas said. "All three of them."

"And Voss," Odile said, "sat in my survey prep classes for two years before the founding. He was mediocre, but he was thorough. He takes notes." She handed the slate back and stood, joints cracking, and looked out at the rain hammering the container yard. "You want to know the worst part, Quintana? He's not even stealing out of greed. Or not only. Voss genuinely believes the beacon is a waste—that the field is superstition, that the accidents are coincidence, that the money tied up in that transmitter could rebuild the water reclamation plant and double the settlement's arable yield. I've heard him say it at council sessions. He thinks he's being *practical*." Her mouth twisted. "Practical men are the most dangerous ones. Fanatics you can predict. Practical men will drain a lifeboat for scrap weight and sleep fine afterward."

"So we go to the council. Tonight. With the photos, the requisition trail, the depot override—"

"With what?" Odile turned on him, and there was twenty years of exile in her voice. "A disgraced engineer and an insurance clerk accusing the chairman of the infrastructure committee of sabotaging the colony's most expensive piece of equipment, right before a vote on whether to sell it? They'll laugh you out of the chamber. Worse—they'll believe Voss when he says *you* did it. Who else on this rock knows the beacon's guts like you do, Quintana? Who's been sneaking up there quarterly for two decades, logging hours nobody verifies?" She let that sit in the rain-noise between them. "That's how paranoia works. It doesn't need to be true. It just needs to be available."

Silas stood in the drumming rain and understood that she was right, and hated it. The town was already fracturing—he'd felt it for days, in the way conversations stopped when he approached, in the sidelong looks at the Reyes girl and her army of dreaming children, in the way people now crossed the street to avoid walking past the beacon road. Fear was loose in Vesper Hollow, and fear always went looking for a shape, and the shapes it liked best were familiar ones. The claims man who knew too much. The drunk who built the thing. The children who dreamed together. The outsider with the clipboard.

And underneath all of it, patient as groundwater, the real fracture: the field was failing, and everyone could feel it failing, the way animals feel weather coming, even the ones who insisted there was no such thing as weather.

"There's one more thing," Odile said. She disappeared into the container and came back with a battered data chit, holding it out in her open palm like something that might bite. "My monitoring array caught the tap's signature while it was happening. Power draw patterns are like fingerprints—every operator leaves a rhythm. Whoever ran that drain knew the system well enough to avoid the safety cutoffs, but they cycled the draw in a pattern. A habit pattern." Her jaw tightened. "It's the load-balancing sequence I taught my survey prep students. The one I only ever demonstrated twice, live, on the original beacon build. I've matched it against the class rosters."

She closed her fist around the chit.

"It's Voss," she said. "It's Emeric Voss, and I have his fingerprint, and tomorrow the council votes on selling the beacon he already gutted—and the question, Quintana, the only question that matters tonight, is whether we walk into that chamber with the truth, or whether the truth walks in ahead of us wearing his face."

Across the settlement, through the rain, the lights of the council hall were coming on one window at a time.
