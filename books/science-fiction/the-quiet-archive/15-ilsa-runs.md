## 15. Ilsa Runs

The evidence fit inside a lunch tin.

That was the joke of it, Ilsa thought as she sat on her bedroom floor in the blue hour before dawn, the tin open between her knees. Two years of dreams—hers and forty-one other children's—reduced to a data chip no bigger than her thumbnail, a folded map drawn in colored pencil on the back of a ration requisition form, and a single page torn from her notebook where she'd written down every name the voice had said.

Forty-one dreamers. One countdown. A relay station at the edge of dead space that none of them had ever seen but all of them could draw from memory, down to the rust streaks on its southern face.

And nobody with any power would look at it.

She'd given them three chances. First the school counselor, Ms. Adeyemi, who had smiled the way adults smile when they're deciding you're not sick, just dramatic, and prescribed rest and fewer late nights. Then Deputy Corso, who took her statement about the dreams with the flat patience of a man writing down nothing, and told her that if she kept spreading stories she'd be having a conversation with her parents and the magistrate both. And finally—last night—the council itself, because Ilsa Reyes did not know how to stop.

She'd stood in the council chamber for eleven minutes while Councilman Brack read her evidence summary aloud in a voice like he was reading a grocery list, and then he'd set it down and said, "Children dream, Miss Reyes. It's what they do." And the chamber had laughed—not cruelly, which was worse—and her mother had put a hand on her shoulder, and Ilsa had understood something with terrible clarity:

They weren't going to listen until it was too late to matter.

So now there was a lunch tin, and a window, and the long dark slope of Vesper Hollow's eastern ridge waiting outside it.

* * *

Taking the chip was easy. That was the part that shamed her later, how easy it was. The dreamers' collective archive lived on the school's shared terminal—a project space Ms. Adeyemi had given them so they'd feel heard, which meant it was protected by a password that was the teacher's own daughter's birthday, which Ilsa knew because Priya Adeyemi was a dreamer too and had told everyone her birthday at the autumn festival two years running.

Ilsa copied everything. Every recorded recitation of the countdown. Every sketch of the relay. The audio file from Tam Okafor, who dreamed loudest, his voice going ragged as he counted down numbers that didn't belong to any human numbering system—numbers that looped back on themselves like a rope biting its own tail. The spectrograms Odile Marchetti's old survey manuals had taught her to generate, showing the waveform underneath the words: a carrier signal, patient and enormous, rising out of dead space like a whale song heard through a hull.

Then, because she was who she was, she left a note.

*I'm sorry. You'll understand soon. Follow the beacon road.*

It wasn't enough and she knew it wasn't enough, but her hands were shaking and the sun was coming up over the smelter stacks and every minute she waited was a minute someone might decide to check the archive, or check on her, or decide—finally, uselessly—to take the dreams seriously by locking them away.

Her mother was still asleep. That was the mercy. Amara Reyes worked doubles at the water reclamation plant since Ilsa's father had gone into the ground, and she slept like a stone with a crack in it, and Ilsa stood in her doorway for one long breath looking at the shape of her under the blanket and memorizing it. The gray coming in early at her temples. The way her hand hung off the mattress edge, palm up, the way she'd slept since Ilsa was small enough to climb into bed during storms.

"I'm doing this so you don't have to bury me," Ilsa whispered, which was a stupid thing to say to a sleeping woman, and also the truest thing she'd ever said.

Then she went out the window with the lunch tin under her jacket, and she ran.

* * *

Vesper Hollow at dawn smelled like cold iron and yeast from the bakery vents, and Ilsa ran through it with her hood up and her lungs burning, taking the maintenance paths behind the hab-blocks where the drainage channels ran and nobody important ever looked. She knew these paths the way she knew the dream corridor—by heart, by repetition, by the particular stubbornness of a girl who'd spent fourteen years learning every unofficial inch of a town that officially pretended she was invisible.

The beacon road began past the last pressure fence, a strip of graded gravel winding up toward the dead moon's shadow-side, and along it, at intervals, the old survey markers stood like crooked teeth. Nobody walked the beacon road except Silas Quintana, quarterly, with his kit bag and his tired eyes. Everybody knew that. Everybody knew Silas went up alone to tend a transmitter that drew no power and served no purpose anyone could name, and everybody had agreed, in the unspoken way of small colonies, not to wonder about it out loud.

Ilsa wondered about it constantly. Wondering was practically her whole personality, according to Ms. Adeyemi.

Halfway up the first switchback she heard the hauler before she saw it—the deep electric whine of a consortium utility vehicle—and she flattened herself against a survey marker as it ground past below her, and through its window she saw Councilman Brack, riding shotgun, talking to the driver with his hands moving fast. Headed up-mountain. Toward the beacon.

Toward Odile.

Ilsa's stomach dropped through the gravel. She'd been so busy being angry at the council that she hadn't thought the obvious thought: if a fourteen-year-old could find the disgraced founding engineer living in a shipping container above the tree line, so could the men who'd voted to sell the beacon for salvage. The vote had been four days ago. Salvage crews would want their asset surveyed. And Odile Marchetti, drunk and half-legend, standing between them and the only person alive who understood what the beacon actually was—

Ilsa ran harder.

* * *

Odile's container sat on a shelf of rock above the old quarry, wired into a solar array that looked assembled from the parts of six other solar arrays, and it hummed. Everything about Odile hummed—the generator, the ancient radio scanner on her roof turning slow circles against the sky, the woman herself, who opened the door before Ilsa finished climbing the last meters, a wrench in one hand and a look on her face like weather.

"You've got Brack's people twenty minutes behind you," Odile said. "Get inside."

"How did you—"

"Scanner saw them leave the yard. Nobody drives the beacon road at dawn unless they want something." She stepped aside. "You're the Reyes girl. The dreamer."

"The loud one," Ilsa said, and ducked inside.

The container's interior was a museum of a life that had been dismantled and reassembled around guilt: survey charts yellowed under magnets, a cot, a wall of hand-labeled capacitors, a workbench buried in the guts of some device Ilsa recognized from her research with a jolt—the same architecture as the beacon, miniaturized, a suppression field you could hold in your hands. Odile poured coffee from a dented pot without asking and pushed the cup across the bench.

"Talk fast."

Ilsa talked fast. She laid it all out—the countdown, the ring of accident sites she and Silas Quintana had mapped together, the failing field, the jump window opening twice a century, the relay at the edge of dead space that forty-two children could draw blindfolded. She spread the colored-pencil map on the workbench and weighted its corners with capacitor housings. She played Tam Okafor's audio, and watched Odile Marchetti—who had built the cage, who had confessed to Silas in this very room about the thing she'd seen during first survey—go white to the lips when the voice came through the little speaker.

"That's not an echo," Odile said quietly, when it ended. "Twenty years ago it counted once. Now it counts in your heads." She looked at Ilsa with an expression Ilsa had never seen on an adult before: not dismissal, not worry-for-the-child. Recognition. "It knows you."

"It says my name," Ilsa said. "Every night now. Last night it said my mother's too."

Outside, faint but getting louder, the whine of the consortium hauler climbed the switchbacks.

"They're coming to decommission it," Ilsa said. "Or arrest you, or both. If they shut the beacon down before we answer the signal—" She couldn't finish it, because finishing it meant saying aloud what the dream showed her when the field failed completely: forty-two children asleep and never waking, a whole generation going dark like lamps in a row.

Odile was already moving. She swept the capacitors off the bench into a crate, pulled a floor panel, and started hauling out equipment Ilsa had only seen in diagrams—field calibrators, a signal decoder older than Ilsa's mother, a case of survey-grade batteries. Her hands shook, but they were sure-shakes, the tremor of a machine spinning up after decades idle.

"I made a bargain with myself twenty years ago," Odile said, not looking up. "Build the cage, keep the secret, die quiet. Guilt is a coward's penance, girl—it lets you suffer instead of act." She slammed the panel closed. "Your voice from dead space just gave me permission to stop."

"So you'll come? To the relay?"

"I'll come." Odile shrugged into a coat stiff with age, then paused, wrench still in hand, and gave Ilsa a long look. "But hear the cost, because nobody else will say it to your face. What you're doing—stealing evidence, running to me, forcing this—you can't run home after. Once we move, the council calls it theft, the consortium calls it piracy, and your mother learns what her daughter's been doing from a magistrate, not from you. There's no version of today where you stay a kid. Do you understand me?"

Ilsa looked at the map weighted down with capacitors. At the lunch tin in her jacket, holding two years of other children's nights. At the door, where the hauler's whine was close enough now to feel in the floor.

"I stopped being one the night the voice said my name," she said. "I'm just catching up."

Something moved across Odile's face—grief, maybe, or respect, or both wearing the same coat. She killed the interior lights and cracked the rear hatch of the container, revealing a maintenance tunnel cut into the quarry rock, older than either of them, sloping down toward the old survey roads.

"There's a second exit. Brack's people will knock politely first, and I intend to be impolite about answering." She shouldered the crate. "One problem remains, and it's the size of a ship. The relay is nine days' burn from here and neither of us can fly, and the only vessel in range rated for dead space is sitting in the consortium yard being fueled for salvage runs."

"The *Meridian's Grace*," Ilsa said.

Odile raised an eyebrow. "You've been dreaming the freight schedules too?"

"No." Ilsa allowed herself one thin, fierce smile—the first since the council chamber. "Silas has been watching it. He told me if it ever came to this, the Grace is the only ship on Vesper Hollow with a drive that won't shake apart past the marker buoys. He's been planning this longer than either of us. He just doesn't know it yet."

Behind them, fists hit the container's front hatch, and Councilman Brack's voice boomed through the metal, official and furious, demanding entry in the name of the colony.

Odile Marchetti grinned like a woman setting fire to twenty years of penance, pulled Ilsa into the dark of the tunnel, and sealed the hatch behind them.
