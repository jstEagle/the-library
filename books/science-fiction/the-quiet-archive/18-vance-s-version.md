## 18. Vance's Version

They let him out of the cargo bay on the third day, when the smell of his own unwashed fear had become a logistics problem rather than a punishment.

Prentiss Vance stood in the corridor of the *Meridian's Grace* with his back against the bulkhead and his hands visible, the way he'd learned in thirty years of audits that visible hands meant *I am not the threat in this room*. His suit was ruined—he'd slept in it, sweated through it in the recycled air of a cargo hold that smelled of coolant and old grain—and the creases in the fabric mapped his three days of confinement like a topographical survey of a very small, very bad country. Silas Quintana stood opposite him with a sidearm held low and easy, the way a man holds a tool he has used often and regrets using at all. Odile Marchetti was nowhere to be seen, which meant she was at the helm, which meant the ship was in the hands of a disgraced drunk, and somehow that was the least alarming item on Vance's ledger this morning.

"You could have kept me locked up," Vance said. His voice came out rougher than he intended, scraped raw from shouting at a door that had no ears. "Three days in a hold with a broken atmospheric recycler and no sanitation. That's a lawsuit, Quintana. That's *seventeen* lawsuits."

"File them when we're home." Silas didn't blink. "You get the galley, the observation blister, and the head. You get them under escort. You get them because Ilsa argued for it, and because I'm tired of hearing you kick the bulkhead."

"The child argued for my release."

"The child argued that a man who talks to himself for three days straight is a man with something to say." Silas's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "She was right. You've been rehearsing."

Vance straightened. Yes. He had been rehearsing. Three days in the dark with nothing but the hum of the recycler and the slow arithmetic of his own position, and he had done what he always did with a bad audit: he had found the numbers that told the truth. He had built the case in his head, section by section, exhibit by exhibit, and now it sat in him fully formed and airtight, and he was going to deliver it, because it was the only thing he had left to deliver.

"I want the observation blister," he said. "And I want you to listen. All of it. Then you can put me back in the hold if you like."

Silas studied him for a long moment. Then he jerked his chin down the corridor.

* * *

The observation blister of the *Meridian's Grace* was a bubble of old, scratched polycarbonate bolted to the hauler's flank, and through it dead space did what dead space always did: nothing, magnificently. No stars worth the name. No traffic. A darkness so complete it had texture, like velvet turned inside out, like the pupil of an eye dilated past all reason. Somewhere behind them—days behind them, weeks of fuel behind them—Vesper Hollow hung in its little pocket of borrowed light, and every child on it was dreaming the same dream, and the field that had kept them safe was burning out like a filament.

Vance put his hand flat against the cold of the viewport and began.

"Let me tell you what I know," he said. "Not what you think I know. What I *know*, from thirty years of reading other people's ledgers. I audit risk for a living, Quintana. I am the man the consortium sends when the numbers stop making sense, and the numbers on Vesper Hollow have not made sense for twenty years. Three hundred and eleven impossible accidents. Three hundred and eleven payouts, sealed, quiet, generous—generous enough that no widow ever hired a lawyer, generous enough that no journalist ever followed the money. Do you know what that costs? I ran it while I was sitting in your cargo hold. It's the single largest unexplained liability in the consortium's colonial portfolio. Someone has been paying to keep those accidents *unexplained*."

"Odile's beacon—"

"Is a line item." Vance turned from the dark. "The suppression field draws power from a grid the consortium funds under a geothermal subsidy code that doesn't exist. I've signed off on it every quarter for nine years. I never asked why, because the payments were clean and the accidents were quiet and that, in my business, is what a *good* portfolio looks like. I'm not proud of that. I'm telling you because you need to understand that I'm not here as your enemy. I'm here as the only man on this ship who has read the whole file."

Silas's jaw tightened. "Say your piece."

"Here's my piece." Vance held up one finger. "One: the field is failing. Marchetti admitted it. The children are proof—it's leaking into their sleep. Two: the field was built to hide the colony from *something*, and Marchetti built it because of something she saw on the first survey, and she was drunk for twenty years afterward, which is what people do when they've seen something their mind refuses to file. Three: you are now flying toward that something. Voluntarily. With a fourteen-year-old girl as your navigator, navigating by *dream*."

"That's a summary, not an argument."

"Here's the argument." A second finger. "In twenty years of leaks, the field has killed three hundred and eleven adults. Not one child. Not *one*, Quintana—until the field began to fail, and even then, the one child who should have died didn't. You told me that yourself, on the yard. A girl falls forty meters through a maintenance shaft and lands in a stack of packaging foam that was never supposed to be there. You called it an impossible survival. I've read the claim. The physics don't work. So ask yourself: if the thing out there wanted the children dead, why does it keep *missing*?"

Silas was quiet. The dark pressed against the blister, patient as a creditor.

"It's not trying to kill them," Vance said. "I agree. I've agreed since the yard. But that is *not the same* as it being safe. A thing that isn't hostile can still be a catastrophe. A lighthouse isn't hostile. An undertow isn't hostile. Gravity isn't hostile. You don't have to be prey to be destroyed—you only have to be *noticed*, by something so large that your entire colony is beneath its resolution. Marchetti didn't build a cage because she was a coward. She built it because she did the math on what was out there, and the math said *hide*, and she was right for twenty years. The field worked. Three hundred and eleven people is the price of twenty years of *working*. That's an actuarial rate I would sign off on tomorrow, and I have signed off on worse."

"And now it's failing."

"And now it's failing." Vance nodded slowly. "Which brings me to the part you're going to hate. Because here is what your plan actually is, Quintana. Stripped of the poetry. You are going to fly to an uncharted relay, open a channel to an unknown intelligence, and *tell it where we live*. You are going to take the one colony in human space that has survived twenty years in something's blind spot and you are going to walk it out into the open, deliberately, and you are going to do it because the alternative is a field failure that might—*might*—kill some children. Some. Not all. Marchetti's own numbers give the field another year of partial function. A year is time. A year is evacuation. A year is a consortium evacuation fleet, if I call it in—and I can call it in, Quintana, the moment we're back in range. Ships. Transports. Every child on Vesper Hollow moved somewhere the field's failure doesn't matter."

"There is nowhere the field's failure doesn't matter," Silas said. "You don't know what it's hiding us from."

"Neither do you." Vance's voice hardened, and he heard the auditor in it now, the flat, patient, immovable thing he'd spent thirty years becoming. "That's my point. *Neither of you knows.* You're gambling on a voice in a dream. I'm proposing the boring solution. The expensive solution. The solution with manifests and schedules and life-support tonnage, the solution that gets every child on that colony onto a ship and out of this region before your beacon dies, without ever once asking the dark for permission. You want to be brave. I want everyone to be alive. Those are not the same virtue."

"And if the voice is what you think it is?" Silas asked. "If it's a predator, or a scavenger, or just a fire that spreads to whatever it can see—then every evacuation ship you call in is a signal flare. You don't move three thousand people quietly, Vance. You move them with engine burns and comms traffic and distress beacons, and you light up the whole region like a festival. Your boring solution ends with the colony *announcing itself* anyway—except it announces itself while half-loaded, mid-panic, with no covenant and no answer prepared. If hiding is the strategy, evacuation is surrender with extra steps."

"Then we hide properly." Vance took a breath. This was the exhibit he'd saved. The one he'd turned over and over in the dark of the hold, the one that hurt to hold. "We go dark. All the way dark. The field is dying—fine. We don't need a field if there's nothing to suppress. We shut down the colony's emissions. Every transmitter, every engine, every heat source we can bury. We go quiet the way the first survey went quiet. Three thousand people, living dark, for as long as it takes to build a new field or a new answer or a new anything. It's brutal. It's a generation of poverty and silence. But everyone *lives*."

"And the children?" Silas said quietly. "They're already in the dream, Vance. They're already hearing it. You can't suppress a signal that's arriving inside their sleep. You can bury every transmitter on the colony and the voice will still be counting down in their heads, because it isn't coming through the air anymore. It's coming through *them*. What's your plan for that? Sedation? Do you know what you're proposing? You're proposing we keep three hundred children unconscious until the field is rebuilt—if it can be rebuilt—because waking up is too dangerous. That's not hiding. That's a ward. That's a ward with a whole town around it, pretending it's a home."

Vance had known this was coming. He'd rehearsed for it. He had an answer, a good one, actuarial and clean, and he heard himself give it and hated every syllable.

"I've priced worse," he said. "In my line of work, someone always asks me to put a number on a thing that shouldn't have one. A life, a limb, a childhood. I put numbers on them, and the numbers are never right, and the payouts go out anyway, because the alternative is paralysis. So yes. If the choice is a ward of sleeping children versus an open channel to something that erased the first survey's fifth crewman—*erased*, Quintana, not killed, there's no body, there's no wreckage, there's no line in any ledger, which is the only kind of death that genuinely frightens me—then I will put the children in the ward, and I will pay for the ward, and I will carry the number, because that is what I'm *for*. I'm the man who carries the number so nobody else has to look at it."

The blister was silent except for the ship's hum, that low patient tone that Ilsa had told him was the only voice she trusted out here. Vance looked at the dark and found, to his surprise, that his eyes were burning.

"You think I'm the villain of this," he said. "The consortium man. The suit. I've read my own character, Quintana—I audit that too. But here's what the suit knows that the dreamers don't. Every person who ever answered a signal like this one believed they were the exception. Every one. The first survey believed it. Marchetti's crew believed it. Four came back changed and one didn't come back at all, and the four who came back built a cage around an entire colony and never told a soul, and you want to know why? Not because they were evil. Because they *knew*. They knew what answering costs, and they decided three thousand strangers were worth more than the truth, and they lived with it until it drank Marchetti dry. That's the inheritance you're so eager to claim. Not wonder. *Guilt*, with a twenty-year head start."

"And if you're wrong?" Silas asked. "If the voice is what Ilsa says it is—patient, sad, waiting—then everything you're proposing is just a slower version of the same catastrophe, with more suffering on the way. And if you're right, and we answer, and it's a predator—then at least the colony dies knowing. I've spent twenty years filing lies about how people died, Vance. Three hundred files. I signed every one. Whatever else happens out here, I am not filing another one."

Vance looked at him for a long time. The old anger was there—the man had cost him his career in all but name, had stolen a consortium hauler with Vance locked in the hold, had put him three days in the dark—but beneath the anger was the thing Vance had come all this way to avoid admitting: the man was *right* about the files. Vance had read them. He'd signed the redactions himself.

"No," Vance said at last. "You're not. And that's the tragedy of you, Quintana. You're an honest man holding a dishonest hand of cards, and you're about to bet a colony on the bluff."

He turned back to the viewport. The dark looked back, total and unbothered, and for one vertiginous second Vance had the absurd, unshakable feeling that it was reading his ledger—that somewhere in all that nothing, something patient and vast was tallying his exhibits, weighing his numbers, and finding them, as it found all human arithmetic, adorable.

"One more thing," he said quietly. "You should know that I'm not arguing because I want to win. I'm arguing because someone on this ship has to be the one who's willing to say the unsayable, and I've been that man my whole life. So here it is, one last time, for the record." He straightened his ruined suit. "The colony should stay hidden. At any cost. Including the children. Including *my* cost, if it comes to that. And if you answer that signal, Quintana, and it goes wrong—and I am telling you, with thirty years of professional certainty, that it will go wrong—then every death that follows is a claim against *your* policy. Not mine. I'll be the one who argued. I'll be the one who was ignored. And I will testify to that, if there's anyone left to testify to."

"Then we understand each other," Silas said. He holstered the sidearm, which Vance realized with a small cold shock he had forgotten was there. "You're free to move about the ship. Odile will show you the galley. But you stay away from the helm, and you stay away from Ilsa."

"Ilsa," Vance repeated. "The navigator. The dreamer." He paused at the blister's hatch, and the question came out of him before he could stop it, small and human and utterly off-ledger. "In her dream. The voice. Does it—" He stopped. Started again, flatter. "Does it ever say names? Other than hers?"

Silas frowned. "Why?"

"Because in the hold," Vance said, "on the second night, when the recycler was screaming and I was talking to myself like a madman—the comms panel in there is dead, Quintana. Dead and disconnected. I checked it twice with the light off my watch face. And at 0300 by ship's time, it said my name."

The hum of the ship filled the silence. Out in the dark, nothing moved, and everything waited.

"It said *Prentiss*," he said. "And then it said a name I haven't let anyone say in eleven years. My daughter's."
