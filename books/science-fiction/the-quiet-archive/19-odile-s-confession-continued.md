## 19. Odile's Confession, Continued

The galley of the *Meridian's Grace* smelled of recycled air and burnt coffee, and Odile Marchetti had not slept in thirty-one hours when she finally said the thing she had spent twenty years not saying.

She had been circling it for two days. She could feel herself circling it the way a moth circles a bulb—drawn, repelled, drawn again—and Silas Quintana had sat across from her through every orbit with that ledger-keeper's patience of his, saying nothing, waiting the way you wait for a pressure valve to give. Ilsa Reyes was there too, curled in the corner bench with her knees up, pretending to read a maintenance manual she'd already finished, listening the way only fourteen-year-olds listen: completely, and pretending otherwise.

Odile turned her cup in her hands. The coffee was cold. She'd forgotten to drink it, which was unlike her; she'd spent twenty years never forgetting to drink anything.

"You want the story," she said. "The real one. Not the one in the inquiry file."

"The inquiry file says the survey team lost comms for nine days," Silas said. "It says they came back with corrupted telemetry and one crew member unaccounted for. It says the board found no fault."

"The board found no fault because the board didn't ask." She laughed, and it came out wrong, dry as static. "I asked. I asked for eleven years afterward, in my head, every night. That's why I drink, Quintana. Not because I'm sad. Because I'm *loud* inside, and alcohol turns the volume down just enough to sleep."

"Tell us," Ilsa said. Not a request. The girl had a way of speaking that made requests sound like coordinates.

Odile looked at her. This child who dreamed in countdowns. This child whose name the voice had spoken. And something in Odile's chest—the sealed compartment, the welded-shut hold where she kept the worst of it—groaned like a hull under load.

"All right," she said. "All right. Turn the lights down half. I can't tell this under full light. Sounds stupid, but—"

Silas reached over and dimmed the panel without a word. The galley went amber. Shadows pooled in the corners like ballast.

* * *

"There were five of us on the first deep survey," she began. "Twenty-two years ago. Before the colony charter was even ratified—we were the proof-of-concept run, mapping the Vesper system's outer shell, looking for jump anchors. Me, because I was young and good and knew it. Captain Edega Ruiz, who'd flown freight through three wars and treated the void like an old dog she mostly trusted. Tomas Okafor, our signals man—he could hear a carrier wave in his teeth, I swear it, he used to say the universe hummed if you were quiet enough. Priya Chandrasekaran, geologist, funniest person I have ever known. And Wren."

She stopped. The cup trembled against its saucer, a small bright ringing, and she set it down before it could shame her further.

"Wren Adeyemi," she said. "Our xenolinguist. Twenty-six years old. Laughed at his own jokes before he finished them. He used to sing while he worked—old songs from Earth, sea shanties, things his grandmother taught him. He said every ship should have a song so the dark knows what's inside it."

The recycler cycled. Somewhere aft, Vance's guarded footsteps crossed the cargo bay hatch, paused, moved on. Even locked away, the auditor listened. Odile supposed she couldn't blame him. She'd have listened too.

"We were forty days out, past the last charted anchor, in what the charts still call dead space because nothing lives there and nothing moves there and nothing is *supposed* to be there. We'd found nothing for six weeks. Beautiful nothing. I loved it out there—I know that sounds strange, but there's a kind of peace in a place that has never once been looked at. You feel like the first witness to creation.

"And then Tomas heard it."

* * *

"He heard it in his teeth first—that wasn't a joke, that was literal, he came to me at second shift holding his jaw and laughing, saying, 'Marchetti, either I need a dentist or we've got company.' We ran it through the array and there it was. A signal. Structured. Repeating. Coming from—nowhere. Not from a star, not from a rock, not from any point we could triangulate. It came from everywhere at once, the way heat comes off a road. Like the dark itself was talking.

"It was a counting pattern. Prime numbers first—you know, the classic hello, the mathematics handshake, the thing every first-contact protocol starts with because math is supposedly the one language everyone shares. Then it shifted. Sequences. Harmonics. And then—" Her hands made a shape in the air, cupping something invisible. "Then it started doing something none of us could parse. Something that felt less like language and more like *attention*. You know how it feels when someone looks at the back of your neck? The whole signal felt like that. Forty days of beautiful nothing, and then the nothing noticed us.

"Edega wanted to log it and run. Standard protocol. Non-responsive anomaly, record and withdraw. And I agreed. I want you to understand that—I agreed. I was twenty-nine years old and I was not brave, whatever the histories say about me now. It was Wren who argued to answer.

"He stood in the galley—this galley, or one shaped exactly like it, these haulers are all the same bones—and he said, 'It's been calling for longer than we've been flying. Nobody's ever answered it. That's not caution, that's cruelty.' He said the signal wasn't a threat display. He said threats don't repeat patiently for what the decay analysis suggested might be centuries. He said it sounded *lonely*.

"Priya voted with him. Tomas couldn't vote—he was half-deaf by then, the signal had done something to his inner ear, some resonance effect we never fully understood—but he wrote on his slate: IF IT IS LONELY, ANSWERING IS KIND. Two votes to answer, two against, captain's tiebreak.

"Edega looked at each of us for a long time. Then she said, 'One burst. Narrowband, low power, mathematical response only. Nothing personal. No names, no voices, no music.' She looked right at Wren when she said that last part. She knew him.

"One burst. That was the decision. One burst, and then we turn for home and let the boards argue about it for a decade.

"We sent it at 0400 ship-time. A prime sequence echoed back, plus a geometric progression, clean as a hymn. Four seconds of transmission. Low power. Narrowband. Everything by the book.

"The reply came back in ninety seconds.

"Ninety seconds, Quintana. Do you understand what that means? The nearest possible source was light-years outside any plausible range. For the answer to arrive that fast, the thing answering was either impossibly close—which our arrays swore it wasn't—or distance simply doesn't mean to it what it means to us. Either way, we had knocked on a door and the door had opened before the knock finished.

"And the reply wasn't math anymore."

* * *

Odile's mouth had gone dry. She reached for the coffee, remembered it was cold, drank it anyway. Bitterness was good. Bitterness was a rope back to the present tense.

"It was a voice," she said. "Not through the speakers—we checked, later, obsessively, for weeks—it wasn't acoustic, it wasn't electromagnetic in any band we could isolate. It arrived in the ship's systems the way water arrives in a basement. Through everything. Through the seams. Tomas heard it in his deaf ear. Priya heard it in her sleep. I heard it through the *hull*, through my boots, through the deck plates, a vibration that had no source. And Wren—

"Wren heard it clearly. Perfectly. In both ears, awake, standing in the corridor. He was the only one. Later we understood why, though understanding didn't help.

"It said one word. Or—one *name*. We never agreed on what we heard, that's the horror of it, five people in the same room and four different transcriptions. But it said something, and it said it gently, and it said it the way you say a word you've been rehearsing for a very long time. And then it stopped. Total silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"Edega ordered us home. No debate this time. We burned for the colony site at maximum safe thrust and nobody spoke above a whisper for six days.

"That's when the changes started."

* * *

"Tomas went first. His hearing never came back—in fact it got stranger, he started hearing things that weren't sound at all. He'd stand at a bulkhead and tell you what was on the other side. Cargo manifests. People's heartbeats. Once, on the trip home, he told Priya her mother had died, and gave the date, and he was right to the day. He wept for six hours after. Said the knowing wasn't his, said it was 'lent' to him, said he wanted to give it back and didn't know where the returns desk was.

"Priya changed quieter. She stopped sleeping—or rather, she slept the way the children sleep now, do you see? Deep, synchronized, dreaming in patterns. She'd wake and draw the same spiral over and over, pages of it, and when I asked her what it was she said, 'A map of being looked at.' She'd been a geologist her whole life. She'd never drawn anything abstract in her life before that signal.

"Edega changed least, which we attributed to her being Edega—granite, flint, a woman who'd buried two husbands and a war and come up still standing. But I found her once in the observation blister at 0300, and she was talking to the glass. To the dark beyond it. Politely. The way you talk to someone sitting across a table. When I touched her shoulder she came back to herself and looked at me with such naked terror that I never spoke of it, and neither did she, and that silence between us lasted until the day she died.

"And me?" Odile spread her hands, looked at them as if they belonged to a stranger. "I started dreaming in schematics. I woke up one morning and I understood the beacon design—*the beacon*, the suppression field, all of it—complete, whole, elegant, as if someone had left the blueprints in my head while I slept. I hadn't been able to solve the emission-damping problem for two years before the survey. Afterward it took me an afternoon. I built the cage out of a gift I received from the thing the cage was meant to hold shut. You can laugh at that. Sometimes I do. It's that or scream.

"But Wren."

Her voice did something then. It thinned. All the gravel and whiskey and twenty years of armor fell away from it at once, and what was underneath was just a woman in an amber-lit room, twenty-two years old in every way that mattered.

"Wren didn't change. That's what I need you to understand. Everyone thinks he changed, that the signal took him, that he came back wrong. He didn't come back wrong. He came back *clear*. He was more himself than any of us had ever been. He sang constantly now—not just working songs, new songs, songs none of us recognized, songs he said were 'requests.' He laughed more. He was kinder. He'd sit with Tomas for hours and translate the poor man's borrowed knowings into something bearable. He was luminous. He was the best version of himself I have ever seen anyone be.

"And on the last night before we made port, he came to me in the corridor and he said, 'Odile, it's not a monster. I need you to know that before you build your wall. Whatever you're going to build—and you're already building it, I can hear you designing it in your sleep—it isn't a cage around a monster.'

"I asked him what it was, then.

"He smiled at me. That smile. God help me, that smile. And he said: 'It's a keeper. It keeps the lights on for ships that sank a long time ago. And we answered, and now it knows our name, and it will wait for us the way keepers wait—for as long as it takes, past the end of patience, past the end of everything. Don't hide from it, Odile. Hiding hurts it. Hiding is the only cruelty left that works on it.'

"And then he walked out the airlock."

* * *

The galley was very quiet. The recycler held its breath. Even Ilsa had stopped pretending to read; the manual lay face-down on her knees and the girl's eyes were enormous and wet and fixed on Odile with an intensity that hurt to receive.

"No distress call," Odile said, because the story demanded the rest, all of it, every ugly gram. "No suit breach alarm. The outer door cycled clean, logged, authorized—his own authorization code, entered calmly, twice, the way you enter a code when you're sure. We were still under thrust. He went out the ventral lock at 0.3 g and he didn't tumble, didn't spin, didn't fight it. Tomas heard him—through the hull, through his borrowed ears—humming. Our ship's song. The one his grandmother taught him. Until the vacuum took even that, which doesn't take long.

"We turned back. Of course we turned back. Edega burned us around so hard the inertials screamed, and we searched for nineteen hours along his drift vector, and we found nothing, and then—"

She closed her eyes.

"—and then we found the suit. Just the suit. Folded. Neatly folded, gloves tucked inside, helmet resting on top, arranged on a chunk of ice the way you'd arrange clothes on a chair before bed. There was nothing in it. There was nothing *around* it for ten thousand kilometers. And pinned to the chest, wedged under the folded collar where a medal would go, was a slip of paper from the galley printer. Handwritten. His handwriting.

"It said: *KEEP THE LIGHT ON FOR ME.*

"So I did," Odile said. "That's the confession, Quintana. The whole wound, unpacked. The beacon isn't a shield I built out of fear. Well—it is, it absolutely is, I won't pretend the fear wasn't real, four of us came home haunted and the boards needed a story and 'anomalous sensor ghost' was the story they could live with. But underneath the fear, underneath the guilt, underneath the eleven years of drinking to make myself quiet enough to sleep—underneath all of it, that transmitter has been pointed at the dark for twenty years singing *keep the light on for me*, and I have told myself it was a suppression field, and I have signed the maintenance logs, and I have lied to myself so long and so well that I almost believed it.

"But the field is failing. And the children are dreaming. And the voice is counting down. And I know—I have always known—what happens when the count reaches zero. Not an attack. Not an invasion. Something worse and gentler than either.

"It comes to collect the answer. Twenty-two years late, patient past the end of patience, it comes to finish the conversation we started with four seconds of arithmetic. And it will knock on every door on Vesper Hollow, politely, the way you knock when you've traveled a very long way, and it will use the names of people it has never met, because it learned names from Wren, from the last thing he gave it—"

Her voice broke. She let it. Some breaks you don't patch; you let them show the structure underneath.

"—and the colony will open those doors, because who among us, hearing a beloved voice in the dark, does not open the door?"

Silas set his cold coffee down with great care, the way he handled evidence. "You said it comes to collect the answer. Collect it *how*?"

And Odile Marchetti, founding engineer, disgraced drunk, keeper of a twenty-year lie, looked at the girl in the corner bench—at Ilsa Reyes, whose name the voice had spoken, whose dreams had changed shape twice since they'd stolen this ship—and saw the answer arrive on the child's face before it arrived in her own mind.

Because Ilsa had gone white. Ilsa's hand had risen slowly to her own throat, to the spot where a collar would fold, and she whispered:

"It won't take the answer."

"*Ilsa,*" Silas said.

"It'll take a messenger." The girl's voice was steady and terrible. "That's what the dream showed me last night, after the names. Someone has to carry it back. Someone has to walk out the airlock singing." She swallowed. "And in the dream, Odile, the suit was already folded."

Before anyone could speak—before Silas could reach her, before Odile could deny it, before the confession could become a plan—the overhead comm panel crackled to life on its own. Dead frequency. No carrier. No source.

And through the static, faint and patient and sad, came the sound of humming.

An old song from Earth. A sea shanty. The *Meridian's Grace*'s song, so the dark would know what was inside it.

Behind them, through the cargo bay hatch, Prentiss Vance began to scream his daughter's name.
