## 20. Interference

The surge came at 0300 ship-time, which Silas had learned was the hour when dead space did its accounting.

He was awake because he was always awake at 0300 now. Twenty years of actuarial discipline had calcified into something his body refused to shed: the claims adjuster's vigilance, the sense that somewhere a file was open that needed closing. He sat in the Meridian's Grace's narrow galley with a cup of recycled coffee going cold in his hands, watching the bulkhead where the paint had bubbled from some ancient thermal event, and he listened to the ship breathe.

Then the ship stopped breathing.

It happened without sound at first—a pressure change in his inner ear, the particular wrongness of air that has decided to become something else. The overhead lights browned out to amber. The coffee in his cup trembled in concentric rings, like the surface of a pond before the stone arrives. And every hair on Silas's forearms stood up, not from cold but from charge, the way they used to stand up on his grandfather's farm in the minutes before a dry-season storm rolled off the hills.

"Odile," he said, and the intercom didn't answer him, because the intercom was already dead.

He made it four steps toward engineering before the sound arrived. It came through the hull itself, through the deck plates, through the soles of his boots and the bones of his feet—a groan so deep and so vast it wasn't a sound so much as a geological event, the ship's frame flexing around a current it had never been built to carry. Somewhere aft, a capacitor bank sang one high, pure note, the note a tuning fork makes just before it shatters. The lights went from amber to black. In the darkness Silas felt the static lift every loose hair on his head, felt the fillings in his teeth ache, and understood—with the strange, detached clarity that had served him through three hundred death files—that the suppression field behind them was dying, and it intended to die loudly.

The backup lights came up blood-red. Emergency power only. And beneath his boots, the hum that had become the ship's heartbeat over eleven days of empty dark simply... stopped.

Not all of it. The life support kept its low, patient thrum. But the long-range comms array—the great dish spine that ran the length of the hauler's dorsal ridge, the thing Odile had spent two days coaxing back to spec—the hum associated with it was gone, and in its place was a smell.

Ozone and burnt varnish. The smell of a lightning strike. The smell, Silas thought, of Vesper Hollow's harvester accidents, of the farmer Dario Meltz crushed under machinery that had been switched off for a decade, of every impossible file he'd ever closed. He had read about that smell in forensic reports for twenty years. Now he finally knew it firsthand, and knowing it firsthand told him everything the reports never could: this was what a leak smelled like. This was what the field's pressure valves smelled like, in the last half-second of the lives they took.

The field hadn't sent them a farewell. It had reached out with the last of its failing strength and touched the ship the way it had touched everything else it ever killed.

* * *

Odile Marchetti was already in the comms bay when he got there, and she was laughing.

It was not a good laugh. It was the laugh of a woman who had bet her remaining years on a single hand of cards and had just watched the dealer burn the deck. She stood ankle-deep in the access crawlway beneath the main relay housing, a hand-lamp between her teeth, both arms buried to the shoulder in a nest of cabling that had fused into a single black mass. The smell in here was thick enough to chew. Every diagnostic light on the board above her was either dark or the wrong color.

"Don't say it," she said around the lamp.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say *how bad*, and the answer is biblical." She spat the lamp into her free hand and looked up at him, and in the red emergency light her face was ten years older than it had been at dinner. "The surge came down the spine. Everything upstream of the isolators is slag. Long-range transmitter, the phased receiver, the signal processor—it's not damaged, Silas, it's *renamed*. That's not wiring anymore, it's sculpture."

"The short-range?"

"Short-range is fine. We can talk to ourselves all we want." She said it lightly, but her hands, when she pulled them from the cable nest, were shaking, and there was a burn across the backs of her knuckles she hadn't noticed yet. "The field gave us one last kiss on the way out. Twenty years of stored charge, discharging through whatever antenna geometry it could find, and we were the biggest antenna in the neighborhood." She sat back on her heels in the crawlway. "You understand what this means."

Silas understood. He had done the math before she finished the sentence, because math was the one prayer he knew by heart. "No calls home. No calls ahead. If the relay isn't exactly where the dreams say it is, no one will ever know what happened to us. And if it is where they say—if we answer it—no one back on Vesper Hollow will ever know why their children woke up."

"They'll know," Odile said. "The children will tell them. That's the whole point of the children." She climbed out of the crawlway with the stiff, deliberate movements of a woman managing pain she refused to name, and stood facing him in the red light. "I built the beacon to keep us quiet. The universe just informed me that quiet is over. One way or another, the colony gets heard. All that's left to decide is whether what hears it first is a friend or a scavenger."

"And we can't warn anyone which way we've chosen."

"No." She said it flatly, an engineer stating a load tolerance. Then, quieter: "We're alone, Silas. Properly alone. No consortium listening in, no council second-guessing, no auditor filing objections. Just us and the voice and about nine hundred hours of fuel." She almost smiled. "Do you know how long it's been since anybody on Vesper Hollow made a decision without someone else's permission? I don't. I genuinely don't remember."

* * *

They gathered in the galley because it was the largest room and the warmest, and because after eleven days in dead space the galley had become the ship's heart whether anyone planned it or not. Ilsa Reyes sat cross-legged on the counter with a maintenance manual open on her knees, though Silas had noticed she mostly used manuals as talismans—something to hold while she thought. Vance stood by the far bulkhead with his wrists still chafed from two days in the cargo bay, radiating the wounded dignity of a man who had been kidnapped and then, worse, been proven partially useful.

"Say it plainly," Vance said. "How bad."

"Biblical," said Odile.

"So we're blind. Deaf. Nine days from a relay station that may be a myth, carrying a message that may be a trap, and no way to tell anyone anything." Vance ticked the items off on his fingers, and Silas recognized the gesture—he did it himself, with claim files. The auditor's catechism. "I want to state for the record that this is precisely the scenario my risk assessments flagged."

"Your risk assessment recommended leaving the children in comas," Ilsa said, not looking up from her manual.

"My risk assessment recommended *not stealing a ship*."

"And look how well the alternative was going."

Silas raised a hand, and to his mild surprise, the room obeyed it. Twenty years of signing documents had given him a certain authority over rooms; he had never learned to use it until now, when authority was the only cargo left aboard. "Here's where we are," he said. "Fact one: the suppression field is gone. Whatever it was hiding us from—and Odile's story says scavengers, the dreams say something older—we are visible as of approximately eleven days ago. Fact two: we cannot call home, and home cannot call us. Fact three—" he paused, because fact three was the one he'd been turning over since the surge hit, the one that tasted like copper, "—fact three is that for the first time in twenty years, nobody is watching us. The consortium can't audit this run. The council can't vote it down. There is no one left to lie to."

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against the hull along with the dark.

"That's supposed to comfort us?" Vance asked.

"It's supposed to clarify things," Silas said. "Every bad decision I ever signed, I signed because someone was watching. Because the payout had to stay quiet, or the premium structure would collapse, or the council would panic, or the field would destabilize. There was always a reason the truth was too expensive. Out here—" he gestured at the bulkhead, at the black beyond it, at the whole soundless immensity pressing against the ship, "—the truth is the cheapest thing we have. It might be the only thing."

Ilsa closed her manual. "The dream changed last night," she said.

Of course it had. Silas felt the words land in his chest like a dropped weight. He had known—Ilsa's face at breakfast had told him—but hearing it aloud made it real, the way a preliminary report becomes a death when you sign it.

"Tell us," he said.

"It's not counting anymore. It's not even talking, really. It's..." She searched for it, frowning, fourteen years old and trying to translate something that predated language. "You know when you walk into a room where people were crying, before you see anyone? You just know. The air knows. Last night the dream was like that. Like walking into a room where something enormous has been waiting a very long time, and it heard us coming, and it's—" her voice caught, steadied, "—it's *relieved*. That's the word. Relieved. Like we're late, but we came."

Vance laughed, short and ugly. "A relieved monster. Wonderful."

"It's not a monster," Ilsa said, with the flat certainty of her age, and Silas noticed that none of them—not even Vance—quite met her eyes when she used that tone. The children had been right about the countdown. About the relay. About the corridor no chart showed. The girl had earned a kind of authority that no one had granted her and no one could revoke.

"Nothing about this stops," Odile said quietly. "That's what the surge means. The field is done hiding us, which means whatever's out there has had eleven days of clear sky pointed at our colony. Every hour we spend mourning our radio is an hour the clock runs without us." She pushed off the bulkhead. "I can rebuild the short-range into something with teeth. Maybe a thousand klicks of useful range—not enough to reach anyone, but enough to talk to the relay when we're close, if the relay wants to talk. Beyond that, we fly dark and we fly dumb and we trust a fourteen-year-old's homework."

"Trust the dream," Ilsa corrected. "Homework is just how I write it down."

* * *

Silas took the night watch alone, because someone had to, and because he had discovered that he no longer trusted himself to sleep.

The cockpit of the Meridian's Grace was a cathedral of glass and instrument glow. Ahead lay nothing—not emptiness, which implies a boundary, but nothing, the genuine article, a dark so complete that the navigation display's faint green wash seemed like a moral statement rather than a light source. Behind them, invisible at this distance, Vesper Hollow sat naked for the first time in twenty years, its children dreaming, its council arguing, its fields growing food for people who no longer knew what they were being protected from. Or whether they were being protected at all.

He thought about the files. He thought about them the way a man worries a broken tooth—Dario Meltz and the harvester, the Kessler twins and the decompression that shouldn't have been possible, the survey pilot whose skimmer fell out of a sky with no weather in it. Three hundred files. Three hundred payouts processed with weary competence, each one stamped CLOSED, each one a pressure valve on a machine he'd been maintaining with his own hands. He had told himself for twenty years that he was the colony's quiet caretaker, the man who made the terrible arithmetic bearable. Sitting alone in the dark with a dead radio at his back, he could finally admit the simpler truth: he had been the field's bookkeeper. Its accountant. Its accomplice with excellent penmanship.

And now the ledger was balanced by forces beyond his signature, and the only currency he had left was the truth, and there was no one left to sell it to.

The stars, at least, were honest. Dead space had no traffic lanes, no nav buoys, no consortium beacons blinking their cheerful actuarial reassurance. Just stars, unfiltered, in numbers that made a mockery of any census. Silas found one that didn't twinkle—a steady amber point, slightly reddened, sitting where Ilsa's charts said the deep corridor began—and he watched it for a long time. It occurred to him that the star might be dead. That its light might be a message from something that no longer existed, still traveling, still arriving, patient past all reason. He found, to his surprise, that the thought comforted him. Everything out here was a message from the gone. Why should the voice be any different?

At 0417, the short-range array—which Odile had stripped, rebuilt, and re-purposed into a listening post of frankly illegal sensitivity—made a sound.

Silas was on his feet before he'd decided to stand. The sound was not a voice. It was not a signal, not in any format the decoder recognized. It was a carrier wave, thin and steady, rising out of the direction of that amber star, and it carried on it a modulation so slow and so regular that it took him several full seconds to identify it.

A count. Not down. Up. One number per minute, climbing, unhurried, like footsteps on a stair.

He woke the others. They crowded into the cockpit in various states of undress and disbelief—Odile with a soldering iron still in her hand, Ilsa barefoot and wide awake as if she'd been waiting to be called, Vance last, hanging back at the doorway with his arms crossed and his face carefully arranged into skepticism that fooled no one.

"What is it?" Amara Reyes would have asked. What does it want? But these three, eleven days into the dark, asked the better question. It was Odile who asked it, staring at the waveform crawling across the screen:

"Is it answering us, or leading us?"

Ilsa stepped forward until the instrument glow lit her face from below, and Silas watched the girl listen—not with her ears, he understood suddenly, but with whatever channel the dream had opened in her and never entirely closed. Her expression went through fear, and wonder, and arrived somewhere he had no file for.

"It's not talking to us," she said. "It's talking to *everything*. That's the count from the dream, but forward. It's not calling us in." She turned, and her eyes were shining and terrified and absolutely certain. "It's opening the door. And Silas—there's more than one ship already heading for it. The dream's full of wakes. Full of old ships, coming out of the graveyard, all of them answering the same door."

Behind them, in the direction of home, the dark was silent. Ahead, the amber star held steady, and the count climbed—one, and then, a minute later, two—and Silas Quintana, alone in the truest sense he had ever been, put his hand on the throttle and did the only honest arithmetic left.

Three hundred files closed. One still open. And for the first time in twenty years, he intended to close it himself.
