## 22. Mutiny Math

The mess of the *Meridian's Grace* had been built for eight crew and a cargo master's optimism. Now it held four people, one of them a prisoner by courtesy, and the table between them was covered in Odile's printouts—actual paper, pulled from the hauler's ancient administrative printer because Odile distrusted screens the way other people distrusted snakes.

Silas stood at the head of the table with a mug of recycled coffee going cold in his hand and watched Prentiss Vance do arithmetic.

Vance did it beautifully. That was the thing Silas kept forgetting to guard against. The man had been locked in a cargo bay for six days, released only when the debris field made a second set of hands worth the risk, and he had emerged without rancor, without threats, without anything so crude as an ultimatum. He had asked for the ship's fuel figures, the relay's coordinates as Ilsa dreamed them, the window duration Odile had extracted from the structure's slow pulsing light. And now he moved a grease pencil across the paper in small, precise strokes, narrating nothing, asking for nothing, letting the numbers do what numbers always did better than arguments.

Silas had spent twenty years reading claim reports. He knew what it looked like when a man laid out a case so clean that disagreeing with it felt like disagreeing with gravity.

"Say it out loud," Odile said. She sat across from Vance with her arms folded, her gray hair tied back with a length of wire, her eyes red-rimmed from three days of holding the relay handshake protocols in her head. "I'm tired of watching you draw little lines."

Vance didn't look up. "Fuel-to-mass ratio gives us a burn budget. The corridor your dreamer navigates cuts transit time to the relay by maybe thirty percent against a ballistic approach—I'll grant that, it's generous of me, since we're trusting a fourteen-year-old's nightmares for course plotting." A line here, a bracket there. "Ninety-one hours to the relay at best burn. Ninety-one back, assuming identical conditions, which deep space does not promise. That's one hundred eighty-two hours. The window is ninety."

He set the pencil down with a small click, like a gavel.

"We cannot reach the relay, do whatever you intend to do there, and return before the window closes. Not with this ship. Not with any ship the colony has ever owned. The math doesn't care how brave we are."

The recycler hummed. Somewhere aft, the drive ticked through its thermal cycle, a sound Silas had come to think of as the ship breathing in its sleep. He looked at the numbers and felt the old familiar weight settle onto his shoulders—the weight of the three hundred files, the weight of every signature he'd put under a lie because the lie kept people alive. Vance had done his homework. Vance had probably done it in the cargo bay, in the dark, with nothing but a slate and six days of silence.

"So," Vance said, and finally looked up. His eyes were pale and patient and terribly reasonable. "We turn around. We go home. We spend the remaining time before the field fails reinforcing the beacon, rationing power, hardening the school shelters. We buy the children time instead of chasing a ghost's invitation across dead space."

"And when the field burns out?" Odile's voice was flat. "You've seen my projections. Weeks, not months. Maybe days, if the scavengers have already flagged our emissions spike from the capacitor drain."

"Then we die in our beds," Vance said, "instead of dying out here, having accomplished nothing, while the children wake up alone."

*While the children wake up alone.* Silas turned the phrase over and found the trap inside it, smooth and polished. As if turning back meant the children would wake up at all. As if the coma-dream holding every child under fifteen on Vesper Hollow would release them because their parents had chosen caution. Amara Reyes's last transmission—the one that had gotten through before the interference surge killed their long-range array—was still lodged in Silas's memory like a splinter: *They're sleeping deeper. Marta Voss stopped breathing for forty seconds this morning and came back on her own. Please hurry.*

Please hurry. From a woman who had once thrown him off her porch for asking questions about her neighbor's death.

"I want to be clear about something," Silas said. He set the coffee down. His hand was steady; twenty years of signing terrible documents had taught him that much control, at least. "You're not wrong about the math. You're wrong about what the math is for."

Vance tilted his head, inviting him to continue, courteous as an auditor at a deposition.

"You're treating the window like a deadline for a round trip," Silas said. "It isn't. It's a door. Nobody said we were coming back through it."

The silence that followed had texture. Odile went very still. Vance's face did something complicated and brief—a flicker, quickly smoothed, of the thing he kept buried under all that competence. Silas had seen it once before, in the cargo bay, when the voice had come through the dead comms and spoken a name: *Maren.* Vance's drowned daughter, eleven years gone, called up out of the dark by something patient and sad that remembered her.

"You're proposing," Vance said carefully, "that we jump. All of us. Through the window. To wherever it goes. Abandoning the colony entirely."

"I'm proposing that staying visible matters more than staying alive," Silas said. "I'm proposing that a covenant with whatever keeps that lighthouse is worth more than another twenty years of hiding, because hiding is what killed those people. Three hundred files, Prentiss. Every one of them a pressure valve on a machine built out of shame. You want to go home and reinforce the shame."

"I want to go home and save children," Vance said, and his voice cracked just slightly on *children*, and Silas understood that he was not only talking about the dreamers. "That's the whole of it. That's the entire content of my position. Everything else is decoration."

* * *

They argued for four hours.

Odile laid out the engineering case: the field's decay curve, the scavenger threat signatures she'd catalogued from the debris field, the way the relay's receiver stayed pointed at Vesper Hollow like an eye that refused to blink. Protection was possible—she'd found the covenant architecture nested in the relay's dream-language controls—but only if the children affirmed it, and only if the signal reached them, and the signal could only ride the window's carrier wave. No window, no covenant. No covenant, no protection. The logic closed like a jaw.

Vance countered with logistics, and his logistics were flawless. Fuel margins. Life support consumables. The failure statistics of a twenty-year-old hauler pushed past every design envelope. He did not raise his voice. He never raised his voice. He simply placed each fact on the table like a stone, and by the third hour the stones formed a wall, and behind the wall was home, and home was where the children were drowning in sleep.

And Silas countered with the only thing he had left, which was the truth of what he'd read in his own ledger. He told them about file two-seventy-four, the harvester farmer, and how the maintenance logs had been altered in his own hand. He told them about the ring pattern, the accidents arranged around the beacon like petals on a black flower. He told them that he had signed his name to silence for two decades and that the interest on that debt had come due in the bodies of sleeping children.

"I am not going back to stand in front of those parents," Silas said, "with nothing but a reinforced cage and an apology. I've delivered apologies before. They don't hold air."

"Then you're a coward running from a reckoning," Vance said quietly.

"Yes," Silas agreed, and watched the word land harder than any counterargument could have. "Probably. But Ilsa isn't running. And Odile isn't. And somewhere in that shared dream, three hundred children are holding a door open for us, and I will not be the man who let it close because the arithmetic frightened him."

* * *

It broke apart after the fourth hour, the way Silas had known it must—not with shouting but with a kind of formal sadness, like a partnership dissolving.

Vance stood, gathered his papers, squared their edges against the table. "I'll take the aft module," he said. "I'd ask that no one lock me in again. I've done my math and I'll keep doing it, and if the situation changes—if the fuel figures change, if the girl finds another corridor—I'll listen. But I won't help steer toward a cliff, and I won't pretend the cliff isn't there."

"No one's locking you in," Silas said. "We need your hands too much."

"That," Vance said, "is exactly what frightens me about you."

He paused at the hatch. For a moment the auditor's armor slipped and Silas saw the father underneath, the man who had heard his daughter's name spoken through a dead speaker and had not been able to hate the thing that spoke it.

"When Maren drowned," Vance said, to the hatchway rather than to any of them, "the search team wanted to stop at seventy-two hours. Standard protocol. The water was cold; survival past seventy-two was statistically negligible. I made them keep searching for nine more days." His knuckles whitened on the frame. "I know exactly what it costs to refuse the math. I paid it. So understand that when I tell you the math wins—it's not because I don't know how to fight it. It's because I know what fighting it leaves behind."

He went aft. The hatch sealed behind him with a soft pneumatic sigh, and the ship breathed on.

* * *

Odile stayed at the table, rearranging her papers into stacks that meant nothing, a woman buying time with her hands.

"He's right," she said at last. "About the round trip. About all of it."

"I know."

"The split course is the only play left, and you haven't said it yet because saying it makes it real." She looked up, and her eyes were clear for the first time in days, sober in a way that had nothing to do with drink. "Someone holds the window open at the relay. Someone runs back to the colony with the covenant signal. And someone flies decoy through the scavenger field, because the scavengers will strip anything that broadcasts, and the Meridian can't afford to be stripped."

Silas said nothing. The plan assembled itself in the silence between them, ugly and necessary, each piece demanding a body.

"Odile—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "I built the cage, Silas. Twenty years ago I welded the first plate of it, and everything that died inside the ring died because of my arithmetic. If anyone stays at that relay, in the cold, holding a door with their bare hands—it's the engineer. That's not sacrifice. That's bookkeeping."

She began gathering her papers, and then stopped, and her voice dropped to almost nothing.

"But whoever runs home has to get past the scavengers, and whoever draws them off—" She shook her head. "Don't decide tonight. Nothing good gets decided at hour forty of a crisis. Go sleep. Or go sit with the girl. She's in the observation blister again, watching the dark like it's going to introduce itself."

* * *

Ilsa Reyes sat cross-legged in the observation blister with her forehead nearly touching the glass, and the dark outside was total—no beacons, no traffic, no star bright enough to cast a shadow. Dead space lived up to its name. Only the faint gold smear of the debris field astern marked that anything had ever existed out here at all: centuries of ships that had answered signals, ground to dust and drifting.

Silas floated in the hatchway and watched her for a moment before speaking. Fourteen years old. The colony had dismissed her for years—difficult, dreamy, trouble—and now the entire expedition steered by the maps she carried behind her eyes.

"Vance wants to turn around," she said, without turning. Of course she knew. She always knew things she shouldn't. "My mom says adults only fight about two things: fear and guilt. Which one is winning?"

"Neither," Silas said. "Love is winning. It's just pointed in three different directions."

Ilsa considered that with the seriousness she gave everything. Then she turned, and in the dim emergency lighting her face was pale and absolutely certain, and Silas felt the floor of the conversation shift beneath him before she said a word.

"There's another corridor," she said. "I didn't tell you because I wasn't sure it was real until tonight. The dream changed an hour ago—the voice showed me again, closer this time. There's a path through the debris field, a channel the scavengers don't patrol. Old. Cleared long ago, like they're keeping it open on purpose." Her eyes were wide and unblinking. "Silas, it cuts the return trip to sixty hours. Sixty there, sixty back, if we leave within the day. The math works. The round trip works."

Behind them, down the spine of the ship, a hatch opened. Footsteps. Vance's voice, tight with something that was not skepticism anymore:

"Say that again. Slowly."
