## 26. Vance's Choice

Prentiss Vance had spent thirty-one years in the insurance consortium learning one lesson above all others: that grief, properly documented, could be managed. He had processed forty thousand claims. He had written the condolence letters—*the Consortium extends its deepest sympathies during this difficult time*—in a hand so steady it had once been complimented by a regional director. He had sat across from widows and fathers and mothers whose children had been crushed, drowned, burned, lost to vacuum, and he had watched their faces with the clinical attention of a man cataloguing specimens, because that was what the work required, and because looking away was a luxury the ledger did not permit.

He told himself this now, standing in the narrow corridor outside the Meridian's Grace's cockpit, with the shock baton humming in his fist and his heart going like a trapped bird against his ribs.

The ship hummed around him. That was the thing about dead space no one warned you about—the silence outside made every sound inside louder. The recycled air moving through the vents sounded like breathing. The drive's low pulse through the deck plates sounded like a heartbeat too slow to be human. And beneath it all, faint as a memory of a memory, there was the voice.

It had started three days ago, after the interference surge fried the long-range comms. At first he'd thought it was a fault in the short-band receiver—a carrier leak, some harmonic artifact of the suppression field's death throes bleeding into their systems. He'd run diagnostics. Clean boards. Clean power. Clean everything. And still, when the ship went quiet enough, when even Ilsa Reyes stopped murmuring in her sleep in the aft cabin, Vance could hear it: not words, exactly. Not yet. A presence at the edge of audibility, like someone standing in the next room deciding whether to knock.

He had spent those three days doing what he always did. Calculating. The math was simple and it was merciless: they could reach the relay, but they could not reach it and return before the window closed. Every hour burned toward the rim was an hour the children on Vesper Hollow spent sinking deeper into their coma-dreams, and every hour after that was an hour the Meridian would need to claw her way home against the same dark that had already eaten her comms. Mutiny math, he'd called it, and Silas Quintana had looked at him with those tired actuary's eyes and said, "Then we don't come back," as if that settled anything, as if a man who filed death reports for a living got to spend other people's lives like loose change.

Vance had run the numbers again that morning while the others slept. Fourteen times. The answer didn't change. It never changed. That was the cruelty of arithmetic—it held still while you came apart around it.

So: the corridor, the baton, the plan. Simple. Take the cockpit while Quintana slept off his watch. Lock the pilot out. Turn the ship. Burn for home on a trajectory that traded the relay for the colony, the unknown for the known, the maybe-salvation of a door opening twice a century for the certain catastrophe of two hundred sleeping children waking up wrong—or never waking at all. Odile Marchetti would fight him; she was half-mad and all guilt, and guilt made people reckless. But she was also old, and drunk-adjacent even sober, and Vance had spent decades winning arguments against people who wanted things. Wanting was a weakness. The ledger didn't want. The ledger only balanced.

His daughter had wanted things. That was the trouble with Marisol. She wanted the deep-water swim permit at eleven, wanted the racing skiff at thirteen, wanted the open water beyond the breakwall at fourteen, and he had signed every permission slip because saying no felt like building a wall between them, and he had built enough walls in his life to know how they looked from the inside.

She had drowned in four meters of sheltered harbor water on a calm afternoon, in sight of the marina, in sight of the lifeguard chair, in sight of him. The claim file said *accidental submersion, cause undetermined*. He had written that file himself. He had read it so many times over nine years that the words had worn smooth, like stones in a river: *cause undetermined*. As if determination were something you could simply decline to do. As if the universe accepted incomplete paperwork.

He had never heard her voice again except in memory, and memory was a corrupt archive; every playback degraded the original. He knew this professionally. Data decayed. And yet some part of him had spent nine years waiting—for a recording to surface, a voicemail un-deleted, a dream vivid enough to count as evidence. He had never told anyone this. Auditors did not wait for ghosts. Auditors closed files.

The baton hummed. The corridor lights were dimmed to night-cycle amber. Ahead, the cockpit door stood half-open, because Silas Quintana was a man who had lived alone with a beacon for twenty years and had never learned to close doors behind him.

Vance took one step. Then another. The deck plates gave up nothing under his boots. In the cockpit, Silas sat upright in the pilot's chair—not asleep at all, he realized too late, just still, watching the forward display where the debris field turned slowly against the starless black like a wound that refused to close—and beside him, strapped into the second seat, Ilsa Reyes slept with her head tipped back, her lips moving faintly, counting down numbers that no one had taught her.

"Evening, Prentiss," Silas said, without turning. "Or morning. Hard to keep track."

Vance raised the baton anyway. It was almost comic—the two of them, the auditor and the adjuster, in a ship the size of a chapel, the whole of dead space pressing against the glass. "Turn us around, Quintana."

"No."

"The math doesn't care about your conscience. We lose either way. This way we lose fewer."

Silas finally turned. His face in the amber light was hollowed, older than his years, but steady in a way that infuriated Vance—the steadiness of a man who had already grieved everything in advance and found it survivable. "You've made your argument. Twice. I've heard it."

"Then hear it a third time, because—" 

And that was when the voice said his daughter's name.

It did not come through the speakers. The speakers were dead; he had verified the speakers himself, pulled the panels, traced the lines. It came through the hull, through the deck plates, through the bones of his own skull, the way sound comes to a diver through water. One word, spoken with a patience so vast it made patience itself seem violent:

*"Marisol."*

The baton dropped from his hand. He heard it clatter on the deck and did not remember letting go of it. The corridor seemed to tilt, or perhaps he tilted, catching himself against the bulkhead with both palms flat against metal gone suddenly cold.

"That started yesterday," Silas said quietly. "For me it was names from my accident files. The ones I couldn't explain. It's been reading us, Prentiss. Or—remembering us. There's a difference, and I can't tell which frightens me more."

"It's not her." Vance's voice came out cracked, a dry hinge. "It can't be. She's dead. Dead is dead. I've processed ten thousand deaths, Quintana, I know the category, I wrote the taxonomy—"

"I know."

"—and whatever this is, it's using her, it's *mining* her, it reached into my head and pulled out the one thing—" His breath was failing him. Nine years of composure, thirty-one years of professional distance, and it was all coming apart in a dim corridor in dead space because a voice in the metal had said one word. "Do you understand what that is? That's not contact. That's predation. That's the oldest trick there is—bait shaped like love."

"Maybe," Silas said. "Or maybe it's the only language it has. You call the bereaved by name. Anyone who's ever written a condolence letter knows you do that. It means: *I see you. I know what you carry.*"

"Don't." Vance pushed past him into the cockpit, needing to move, needing to do something with his hands before they shook visibly. The forward display filled his vision: the debris field, kilometers of drifting wreckage, hulls from centuries of ships that had answered signals before them, turning in the lightless cold. Somewhere out there, per Odile's white-knuckled testimony, was a hull from the first survey era. Somewhere out there was the reason for everything—the beacon, the field, the accidents, the children counting down in their sleep.

"Say it again," he heard himself say.

Silas went very still. "Prentiss."

"*Say it again.*"

And the ship obliged. Not the speakers. The hull. The air itself, or whatever carried sound in recycled atmosphere, or nothing physical at all—just the word arriving, gentle as snowfall, patient as erosion:

*"Marisol."*

Vance braced himself against the console. The display washed amber across his face. He was fifty-eight years old and he was crying the way men cry when they have practiced not crying for a decade—badly, silently, with fury at themselves folded into every tear, jaw clenched hard enough to ache, hands fisted on the console edge as if the ship might pitch and roll and he must hold course.

"She was fourteen," he said. Nobody had asked. He said it anyway. "Fourteen meters of visibility, four meters of water. Calm day. I was twenty meters away. I was reading claim correspondence on my slate. Twenty meters, Quintana. I've reconstructed it a thousand times. If I'd looked up four seconds earlier. Four seconds. I ran the probability trees until the consortium made me take leave." A sound came out of him that was almost a laugh. "I couldn't close her file. Did you know that? Thirty-one years, forty thousand claims, and I couldn't close one. It's still open. It will always be open. *Cause undetermined.*"

The voice came again, and this time it was not one word. It was a sentence, assembled slowly, each syllable placed like a stone in a cairn:

*"She waved to you. Three times. You did not see. She was not afraid. She slipped. She was not alone for long—we were there. We are always there, at the end, in the water, in the dark, in the falling. Someone holds them. No one goes alone."*

The cockpit was silent except for the hum of the drive and Ilsa's sleeping breath and the sound of Prentiss Vance coming apart and being put back together in the space of ninety seconds.

"You expect me to believe that," he whispered. "A voice in a wreck-field tells a grieving man exactly what he needs to hear, and I'm supposed to kneel?"

"No," Silas said. "I expect you to do what you do best. Audit it."

Vance stared at him.

"You've spent your whole life weighing testimony," Silas went on. "So weigh it. What does it gain by lying to you? What's the fraud here? What's the payout? We're a hauler with a dead comm array and three people aboard, one of them asleep. If it wanted to lure us into the debris field, it could have said anything—said *come here, Marisol's here*. It didn't. It corrected your record. It told you the one thing you couldn't have known, the thing you've tortured yourself with for nine years—that she waved, that you missed it, that it wasn't four seconds of negligence, it was a wave you didn't return." Silas's voice gentled. "That's not bait, Prentiss. That's an amended report."

Vance stood there a long time. The amber light ticked through its cycle. Out in the black, the debris field turned, and somewhere among those wrecks—Odile had sworn it, weeping, two nights ago—was a survey-era hull with five names stenciled inside, four of them changed forever and one of them never recovered, and Vance understood suddenly, with the terrible clarity of a man whose ledger finally balances, that the voice had been keeping all of them. Every wreck. Every crew. Every child who fell and every farmer crushed by a harvester that should have been dead. *Someone holds them. No one goes alone.* Twenty years of impossible accidents, and the impossibility had never been malice. It had been attendance.

He bent down and picked up the shock baton. Silas tensed—but Vance only looked at it, turned it over in his hands like an artifact from a previous civilization, his civilization, the one where grief was a line item and loss was a category and everything, everything could be filed.

He set it down on the console, gently, the way you set down something you are returning rather than surrendering.

"Course stays as plotted," he said. His voice was wrecked and he let it be. "But I'm logging a formal objection. For the record. Whatever record survives."

"Noted," Silas said. "Objection logged."

"And Quintana." Vance straightened. His eyes were raw, his face stripped down to the man beneath the auditor, and what was beneath was not a monster, had never been a monster, only a father who had read claim correspondence instead of watching his daughter wave. "When we get to the relay. When this voice does whatever it does. If there's any part of this that touches the dead—if there's a registry, a record, anything—I want her name in it. Marisol Anahí Vance. Drowned at fourteen. Cause determined." His throat worked. "*Determined.*"

Silas nodded slowly. "I'll write it myself."

In the second seat, Ilsa Reyes stirred. Her eyes opened—not all the way, not fully awake, but enough, and she looked at Vance with the flat lucidity of the dreaming, and she said, in a voice that was hers and yet pitched slightly wrong, slightly older, slightly patient:

"She says you look tired, Mr. Vance. She says you should sleep. She says—" the girl's brow furrowed, chasing something across a distance no chart measured, "—she says the water wasn't cold. You always thought it was cold. It wasn't. It was warm as bathwater, and she wasn't scared, and she wants you to stop reading the file."

Then Ilsa's head lolled, and she was asleep again, breathing easy, fourteen years old and carrying messages across a gulf that had swallowed armadas.

Vance lowered himself into the jump seat at the back of the cockpit because his legs would no longer be negotiated with. He put his face in his hands. He did not pray—he had never learned how, and suspected the consortium had bred the capacity out of him—but he sat in the amber light with the wreckage of his certainty scattered around him, and for the first time in nine years he did not reach for the numbers.

After a long while, Silas spoke, softly, not looking back at him. "Get some rest, Prentiss. We've got sixty hours to the relay."

Vance nodded into his hands. Then he lifted his head, and his voice, when it came, was steady in a new way—not the steadiness of the ledger but the steadiness of a man who has finally closed a file and discovered that what remains is not emptiness but space.

"Quintana. When we're close to that debris field—wake me. I want to see it. All of it. Every hull." He swallowed. "I've spent my career pricing wrecks. I'd like to meet a few."

Silas glanced back at him, and something passed between them that neither man would ever put into a report—an acknowledgment, an armistice, a debt.

Outside the glass, the dark went on forever, and somewhere in it, patient and sad and impossibly kind, something that kept lighthouses for the dead waited for them to arrive—and Prentiss Vance, antagonist, auditor, grieving father, sat in the amber light of a stolen ship and began, silently, to rehearse the words he would say to a debris field full of the answered: *I'm sorry it took me so long. I'm here now. I'm listening.*

* * *

Six hours later, when the watch changed and Odile Marchetti shuffled into the cockpit rubbing her eyes, she found Vance asleep in the jump seat with his slate open on his chest. She almost left him there. But the screen glowed, and she was an engineer, and engineers read everything.

It was not mutiny math. It was not a trajectory plot.

It was a single document, titled with bureaucratic precision: **CLAIM FILE 4471-MV — STATUS: REOPENED**. And beneath it, in the careful hand of a man rebuilding himself line by line, an addendum:

*Claimant: Vance, P., father of deceased.*
*Incident: Submersion, harbor waters, nine years prior.*
*Original finding: Cause undetermined.*
*Amended finding:* Cause determined. She waved. I missed it. She was held.
*Disposition:* Pending arrival at relay. Settlement sought: acknowledgment, one (1) name entered into permanent record.
*Notes:* The consortium taught me that every loss must balance against something. They were wrong. Some losses balance against the truth, and the truth is that no one goes alone.

Odile stood in the amber light for a long moment, reading, her scarred engineer's hands hanging at her sides. Then she reached out and, very gently, dimmed the slate's glow so it wouldn't wake him, and turned to the forward display, where the debris field had begun, at last, to resolve out of the darkness—hundreds of hulls, centuries of the answered, drifting toward them like a congregation.

"Wake me when we're close," she murmured to Silas, though her eyes never left the wrecks. "Whatever's out there keeping all those ships... it's about to have company. And this time, we're going to knock first."

Behind them, deeper in the black, the last embers of the suppression field guttered out above Vesper Hollow—and in two hundred sleeping minds, the countdown, which had run for weeks without end, quietly stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than the counting.
