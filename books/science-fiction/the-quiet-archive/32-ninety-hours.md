## 32. Ninety Hours

The relay's heart was a room that should not have existed.

Ilsa felt it before she understood it—the way you feel a held breath in a dark house. The Meridian's boarding party had drifted through corridors of pale metal that predated the colony, predated the first survey, predated everything with a name, and at the center of it all was a chamber where the walls breathed light. Not the harsh flicker of human lamps. Something slower. Something that rose and fell like a sleeping animal's ribs.

"It's warm," she said, pressing her palm against the wall. The metal was blood-temperature. "Odile, why is it *warm*?"

Odile Marchetti stood in the middle of the chamber with her arms crossed and her face gone the gray of old ash. She had not spoken much since they docked. She had touched almost nothing. Ilsa was beginning to understand that Odile touched things only when she was sure of them, and here she was sure of nothing except that she was standing inside an answer she had spent twenty years building a wall against.

"Because it's alive," Odile said finally. "Or the closest word we have. The first survey called them keeper-stations. I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought I'd imagined them. Twenty years, and I told myself I'd imagined them."

The dream-vocabulary bloomed across the walls when Ilsa stepped closer. She had learned it the way you learn a language you've been speaking in your sleep your whole life—suddenly, completely, with the vertigo of realizing you always could. Shapes meant *open*. Spirals meant *listen*. A nested pattern of rings, like ripples in still water, meant *promise*.

And there, hovering at the chamber's center where the light gathered thickest, the relay had drawn the shape for *promise* over and over, hundreds of times, each one incomplete—each one waiting for something to finish it.

"It wants an answer," Ilsa said. "It's been waiting for an answer."

"It's been waiting a long time," Odile said quietly. "Look at the dust lines. Look at the wear on that threshold. This thing has been holding its breath since before humanity learned to jump."

Silas came through the corridor hatch with his ledger under his arm—he still carried it everywhere, that battered record of three hundred impossible deaths—and stopped when he saw the walls. Ilsa watched him read them the way he read accident reports: slowly, item by item, refusing to look away.

"Can it protect the colony?" he asked. "That's the question. Can it do what the beacon did, or better, before the field burns out?"

The relay answered before Odile could. The light shifted, and a shape unfolded across the far wall: Vesper Hollow—Ilsa knew it instantly, the twin continents, the shallow northern sea, the scar of the first landing—and around it, a lattice. A woven shell of light, dense and deliberate, nothing like the leaky, dying suppression field the beacon had been coughing out for two decades. This was not a hiding place. It was a *covenant*. The dream-language made it clear in a way English never could: not *invisible*, but *held*. Not *unseen*, but *claimed*. The voice in the dark was offering to stand between the colony and the scavengers, the way a lighthouse keeper stands between a ship and the rocks—not by moving the rocks, but by naming the shoals.

"It can do it," Ilsa breathed. "It can hold them. Forever, maybe. It's been holding other things forever—look, there—" She pointed at the fainter shapes woven through the lattice, other worlds, other colonies, other small bright lives tucked into the keeper's care. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

"Then do it," Silas said. "Now. Today."

And the relay showed them the price.

* * *

The chamber dimmed, and the shape of the covenant collapsed into a single line of light that ran from the relay outward—toward Vesper Hollow, forty days' burn at the Meridian's best speed—and then back again, and then the line pulsed, and pulsed, and above it the relay drew the shape for *window* with a countdown wrapped around it like a noose.

Ninety hours.

Ilsa did the math before anyone else could speak, because she had been doing impossible math in her head since she was nine years old, since the first time the dream came and she lay awake counting. Ninety hours. The Meridian's Grace could make the crossing to Vesper Hollow in forty days. Even if the children woke the instant the signal touched them—even if the affirmation took one second—even at the absolute theoretical limit of the ship's engines, with no deceleration, no margin, no mercy—the round trip was eighty days.

They were short by seventy-nine days and change.

"That's not a deadline," Odile said flatly. "That's a joke. That's the universe laughing at us."

But the relay was not laughing. The relay was patient, and the relay was sad—Ilsa had felt that in the dream for years, the vast gentle sorrow of a thing that had watched a thousand civilizations answer too late—and the countdown continued, steady as a heartbeat, indifferent to their despair.

"There has to be another way," Silas said. "There's always another way. There was always another way in every file I ever closed, if you dug deep enough."

"Sometimes there isn't." Odile's voice cracked on it. "Sometimes the math is just the math. I built a cage around four thousand people because I couldn't live with the math, and look what that cost. Three hundred of your files, Quintana. Three hundred."

Ilsa wasn't listening to them. She was standing close to the covenant-shape, close enough that its light lay on her skin like shallow water, and she was listening with the other part of herself—the part that had been counting down from the stars since she was small. And the other part of her noticed something the adults had missed, because adults always missed it, because adults believed that the universe spoke in equations instead of in names.

The countdown wasn't counting down to the window closing.

It was counting down to the window *opening*.

"Stop," she said. "Both of you. Stop. Look at the shape again. Look at what it's wrapped around."

They looked. Silas with his squint of weary suspicion, Odile with her engineer's frown, and neither of them saw it, because neither of them had spent a hundred nights inside the dream learning how the voice said things—how it never lied, but it never said things the flat human way either.

"The window isn't a door that closes," Ilsa said. "It's a door that *opens*. Twice a century, Odile said. But opening for what? For ships? The dream never showed ships. The dream showed—" She reached out and touched the covenant-lattice, and the relay shivered around her, and the light reorganized itself into something new: the colony again, but this time the children were visible in it, each one a small bright point, and the points were *connected*, threaded together like a loom, and the loom ran out through space to the relay like a cable of light.

"The children," she whispered. "It doesn't need the ship to come back. It needs the *signal* to reach them, and the signal isn't a radio wave. It's the dream. It's always been the dream. That's how it reached us across dead space with the field still up—that's why we dreamed it when no transmitter could have carried it. The dream doesn't care about distance the way light does."

Silas went very still. "You're saying the relay can send the covenant to them the way the voice sent the countdown."

"I'm saying I think it already tried." Ilsa's heart was pounding so hard she could hear it in her ears. "The coma. Amara's daughter, all of them, every child under fifteen asleep for six days—the dreamers' chorus. That wasn't the field failing. That was the relay *reaching for them* and not being able to finish, because the covenant needs an affirmation. A yes. It won't hold anyone who hasn't said yes. That's the shape—look at it—the promise-shape is *incomplete* on purpose. It's a question. It's been asking a question for centuries and nobody ever answered."

The chamber was silent except for the slow breathing of the walls.

"So we send the question," Silas said slowly. "The relay transmits, the children wake, they affirm, the covenant seals, and the colony is held. No round trip. No impossible math."

"And if it's not that simple?" Odile asked, and her voice was harsh with old scar tissue. "If the signal can't carry a question that complex through the interference the failing field is throwing off? If the children wake and they're fourteen years old and terrified and they say no? A covenant built on a child's frightened no is worse than no covenant at all. And if it fails mid-handshake—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. They had all seen the debris field on the way in. Centuries of ships. Centuries of colonies that had answered too late, or answered wrong, or never answered at all.

"Then we make it simple," Ilsa said. "We make it *so* simple. One word. We send them one word and they say it back. That's all an affirmation has to be, right? The relay doesn't need a speech. It needs a yes."

The relay's light rippled—something between agreement and amusement, if a structure older than the colony could be amused—and the countdown above them pulsed on. Eighty-nine hours now. Ilsa felt it in her teeth, in the backs of her eyes, a hope with a clock attached, which was the cruelest kind, because hope without a deadline can be carried gently, but hope with a deadline you have to *run* with, and running hope is heavy.

Eighty-nine hours to aim a question across forty days of dead space and get four thousand sleeping children to answer it.

* * *

They worked through the ship's night cycle, which didn't exist at the relay except as a decision they made. Odile jury-rigged an interface between the Meridian's systems and the relay's dream-language, translating her engineering into shapes the ancient station could read, and it was beautiful to watch—Ilsa stood in the corridor hatch for hours watching the disgraced engineer argue with a structure twice the age of human jump travel and slowly, grudgingly, win it over, the way you befriend a feral animal. The relay had protocols. The relay had *manners*, centuries deep, and Odile had manners too, the buried kind, the kind that survived twenty years in a shipping container with a bottle.

Silas sat with his ledger open on the deck and wrote.

Ilsa noticed it around the fourth hour. He was writing in the ledger—not reports, not claims, something else—and when she drifted close enough to see, the page was full of names. The names of the dead. All three hundred of them, and beside each name, in his small precise hand, a single line: what the covenant would have given them. *Mara Voss, harvester, year nine—would have lived.* *The Okafor twins, airlock failure, year twelve—would have lived.* He was building a case file for the universe, Ilsa realized. An itemized, unflinching account of exactly what the silence had cost, to be presented to the only court that had jurisdiction out here.

"Why?" she asked him quietly, when Odile was deep in the interface.

"Because if this works," Silas said, not looking up, "someone is going to want to forget. The consortium. The council. Maybe everyone. Forgetting is what people do with the impossible—it's easier than the alternative. So I'm writing it down so it can't be forgotten. That's the whole job. That was always the whole job." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "You were the first one who didn't forget. Fourteen years old and you wouldn't let the adults tell you the dream was nothing. I should have listened to you two years ago. I'm sorry it took me until the edge of dead space."

Ilsa didn't know what to say to that, so she said the true thing: "I'm scared."

"Good," Silas said. "Scared means you understand what's at stake. The council was never scared. That was the whole problem with the council."

* * *

The transmission plan came together in the eighty-fifth hour, and it was Ilsa's—not because she was the smartest person on the ship, though she was beginning to suspect she might be the only one who had been educated for this her entire life without knowing it, but because the relay would only take the question from someone who could speak it natively. Odile could build the throat. Silas could write the words. But the dream-language was Ilsa's mother tongue, and the relay knew it, and when she stood in the center of the chamber and shaped the question with her hands—*will you be held, will you say yes, will you let us keep you*—the whole station lit up like a struck bell.

The problem was the distance. The problem was always the distance. The relay could throw the signal, but dead space between here and Vesper Hollow was full of the failing field's interference, a wall of static and dying suppression, and Odile's models gave the question a forty percent chance of arriving coherent. Forty percent. Ilsa lay in her bunk in the Meridian that night—ship's night, chosen night—and stared at the ceiling and turned the number over and over. Four in ten. If you were a gambler you'd call those terrible odds. If you were a fourteen-year-old with four thousand sleeping siblings on the other side of the dark, you called them the only odds there were.

There was a knock, or the ship's equivalent—a tap at the hatch frame. Silas. He sat down across from her without being asked, the way he did, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the ship's hum filled the silence the way it always did, the companion-sound of the long dark.

"My mother is down there," Ilsa said finally. "Asleep with everyone else. She used to sing me the counting song when I was little—you know, the one every parent here knows, *one for the dark and two for the deep*—and I used to think she made it up. I used to think it was just a lullaby." Her throat tightened. "It was the dream. It's always been the dream. Our parents have been singing us fragments of it our whole lives and calling it a lullaby, because that's what you do with the impossible. You turn it into something soft enough to sleep to."

Silas was quiet for a long moment. "When we send the question," he said, "your mother might wake up mid-song. Mid-count. And she'll finish it, because that's what the song is—it's a promise someone started a long time ago and nobody ever finished." He tapped the ledger on his knee. "I've got three hundred names in here of people the silence killed. I'd like to write down one name of someone it didn't. Yours, maybe. First dreamer. First one to answer back."

"I haven't answered anything yet," Ilsa said. "The transmission goes out in five hours and it's a coin flip whether it even arrives."

"Then we flip the coin," Silas said, "and we stand behind it either way. That's all courage is, Ilsa. I learned it late. You're learning it early. I'm sorry about that too."

* * *

The eighty-ninth hour came, and they gathered in the chamber—Silas with his ledger, Odile with her hands buried in the interface to her elbows, cables running from the Meridian's core into the relay's ancient throat—and Ilsa stood at the center where the light pooled, and the countdown above them read like a held breath.

Eighty-nine hours, and the window would open, and the covenant would either seal or it wouldn't, and there was no second window, no extension, no mercy in the math.

"One more thing," Odile said suddenly, and her voice had gone strange. "Before we send it. The relay's showing me its power budget for the handshake. The transmission will take everything it has—everything. It'll be dark for six days after. Blind and mute. And if the covenant seals, the seal has to be confirmed back through this station, and if it's dark when the confirmation arrives—" She looked up at them, and Ilsa saw with a lurch that the old engineer was crying, had maybe been crying for an hour, silently, the way you cry when the math finally corners you. "Someone has to be here when it wakes up. Someone has to hold the handshake open from this side, manually, with their hands, for as long as it takes. And the life support in the core chamber is rated for one person for exactly the length of the handshake, and not one hour more."

The chamber breathed its slow light. The countdown pulsed.

"I built cages," Odile said. "Twenty years ago I built the best cage I knew how, and it cost three hundred lives and a colony's whole history of looking away. Let me build one true thing at the end of it."

"No," Silas said.

"Quintana—"

"*No.*" His voice cracked like ice. "We haven't even sent the question yet. We don't get to spend people before we've spent the question. Nobody dies at this station until the math makes them, and the math hasn't spoken yet."

But Ilsa had seen the relay's power diagrams over Odile's shoulder. She had done the numbers in the four seconds since Odile finished talking, because numbers were the one thing that never left her alone. And the numbers said Odile was right, and the numbers said the handshake was longer than the air, and the numbers said that somewhere in the next ninety hours, someone was going to have to do the cruelest arithmetic of all.

Above them, the countdown ticked past eighty-eight hours.

Ilsa raised her hands, and the light gathered, and she began to shape the question.
