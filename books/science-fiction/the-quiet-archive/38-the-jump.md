## 38. The Jump

The relay's core chamber was still warm when Silas sealed it.

That was the detail that would stay with him, he knew—not the math, not the countdown, not even Odile's last transmission, but the warmth of the metal under his palm as he pressed it flat against the hatch and felt, through two centimeters of alloy older than the colony itself, the faint residual heat of a woman who had walked into that room four minutes ago and was not walking out.

*Sealed from your side,* she had said, her voice coming through the intercom with the same dry steadiness she'd used to explain capacitor discharge rates in a shipping container that smelled of cheap grain alcohol and old grief. *Don't make it a ceremony, Quintana. I hate ceremonies.*

So he didn't. He turned the wheel until the dogs seated, until the pressure indicator went green, until there was nothing left to do with his hands, and then he stood there anyway, palms flat against the hull, listening to her breathe through the open channel because neither of them had thought to close it and neither of them wanted to be the one who did.

"Air's at nineteen percent," she said. "I've got maybe six hours if I don't exert myself. The handshake cycle needs continuous carrier modulation for three-forty. After that the relay holds it on its own." A pause. A small, wet laugh. "I always was good at making things run slightly longer than they should."

"Odile."

"Don't." Her voice softened but didn't waver. "Twenty years I carried what happened out here. Twenty years I drank myself stupid trying to forget the sound the fifth survey crew member made when the signal took her apart and put her back together wrong. You want to give me something? Give me this. Let my last act be an engineering solution instead of a confession. I've done enough confessing for one lifetime."

Behind him, in the relay's observation gallery, Ilsa Reyes stood with both hands pressed to the viewport, watching dead space turn slowly beneath them. She was fourteen years old and she had not slept properly in eleven days and she looked, in the pale light of the ancient structure's emergency strips, like something newly hatched—raw-eyed, unsteady, absolutely certain.

"The window," she said. "Silas. The window's opening."

He turned. Beyond the glass, where there had been nothing—a darkness so complete it registered less as absence than as pressure—the stars were moving.

Not drifting. Not parallax. *Moving.* They slid sideways in long, smooth rivers, as though someone had taken the sky by its edges and drawn it taut, and in the gaps between the streaming stars there opened a corridor of deeper black, a channel cut through the fabric of everything, running from the relay outward toward a point his instruments could name but his mind refused to picture. The jump window. Twice a century, the dreamers had said. A door that opens twice a hundred years, and it was opening now, and it would stay open for exactly as long as it stayed open, and no power in the universe they knew could hold it a second longer.

"Meridian's Grace is pre-flighted," Ilsa said, and her voice cracked on *pre-flighted*, because she was fourteen and she had just watched an old woman walk into a sealed room to die, and she was also fourteen and about to fly a stolen hauler through a hole in space, and Silas understood that those two facts were going to live inside her forever, side by side, the way they were going to live inside him. "Odile ran the numbers twice before she—" He heard her stop herself. "Before. The ship will make the transit. Barely. She said barely counts."

"Barely counts," Silas agreed. It was one of Odile's phrases. It had been one of his too, once, back when he still believed the universe rewarded precision. Now he understood it differently: *barely* was all anyone had ever been given. Barely enough air. Barely enough time. Barely enough truth, delivered twenty years too late to save anyone who'd died in the harvester fields and the pressure locks and the collapsed hab-sections whose files sat in his archive with his signature at the bottom of every redacted page.

He keyed the intercom one last time.

"Odile Marchetti. Founding engineer of Vesper Hollow. Builder of the beacon nobody ordered. Keeper of the worst secret in human space." His throat closed; he pushed through it. "For the record—for my records, which are the only honest ones left—I'm filing you as alive. Status: holding. Cause: choice. I'll amend the file when you tell me to. Don't tell me to."

Through the speaker, over the soft hum of the relay's carrier wave beginning to build, he heard her start to laugh, and then he heard her start to sing.

* * *

It was an old song. That was the first thing Ilsa said when they were strapped into the Meridian's Grace and pushing away from the relay on a burn that pressed them deep into their acceleration couches. Old. Older than the colony. Older, maybe, than anything else they'd ever heard.

"It's from the first survey," she said. "She sang it in the dream. All of us heard it—that last night, after the names started. It's the song the fifth crew member was humming when they found her suit. Odile never told anyone she remembered it. She only ever gave it to the dream."

Silas listened. Through the hauler's comm stack—which the surge had fried weeks ago and which the relay's carrier wave had, in some fashion none of them fully understood, resurrected—Odile's voice rode the signal out of the core chamber and across the gulf between the relay and their ship, thin and crackling and utterly alive. The melody was simple. Five notes rising, five notes falling, a shape like breathing. And underneath it, woven into the harmonics in a way that made the hair on Silas's arms stand up, the relay's own carrier tone hummed the same interval pattern, as though the ancient structure had learned the song and was singing harmony.

"She's broadcasting it on the covenant frequency," Ilsa said. She had the navigation board and her hands moved across it with a fluency that should have frightened him and instead filled him with a fierce, terrified pride. Dream-language controls, the relay had called them, and the girl the colony dismissed had spent eleven days learning to read them. "Every scavenger in the debris field is hearing it right now. Every automated wreck-hauler and signal-eater between here and Vesper Hollow. The covenant doesn't hide from them. It *names* them. It tells them: this system is kept. This system sings. Go around."

"And they'll go around?"

"The voice says they will." She glanced at him, and in the blue wash of the nav displays her eyes looked very old and very young at once. "The voice has never lied to us. It counted down for months and it never once lied."

The burn ended. Weightlessness returned. Through the forward ports, the corridor of stretched dark waited, and it was closer now, close enough that Silas could see structure in it—faint filaments of light along its edges, like the seam of a garment held up to the sun, like the glowing margin of a door left ajar in a house where everyone had gone to bed.

"Transit in ninety seconds," Ilsa read. "Window stability nominal. Whatever nominal means to a machine that thinks in dreams."

Silas pulled up the colony's telemetry one more time. He couldn't help it. It was the habit of twenty years—check the file, check it again, check it a third time—and this file mattered more than all three hundred others combined.

Vesper Hollow hung in the readout, forty light-hours behind them and receding, and for the first time since the beacon's construction, the colony was *visible*. The suppression field was dead—he'd watched it fail in real time from the Meridian's scopes, a slow dimming like a candle drowning in its own wax—but what replaced it was not vulnerability. Across the colony's entire electromagnetic profile, wrapped around every emission, every heat signature, every stray radio whisper of ten thousand lives, lay the covenant: a lattice of structured signal, patient and luminous, announcing to anything listening in the dark that these people were known, named, and protected. The children had affirmed it six hours ago, twelve thousand voices speaking in unison through a dying transmitter, and the relay had caught the final syllable with four seconds to spare, and somewhere in the debris field a salvage machine the size of a city had altered course by nine degrees and begun, very quietly, to go around.

"They can see us," Silas said. "Everything out there can see us."

"Yeah." Ilsa's voice was steady. "Isn't it better?"

He thought about the farmer crushed by a harvester switched off for a decade. About the pressure-lock technician whose suit had failed in atmosphere. About the child, years ago, whose fall should have killed her and hadn't—the first miracle in a ledger full of deaths. Twenty years of impossible accidents, and every one of them had been the field's pressure valve, the suppression bleeding off what it couldn't contain, and he had signed each report *cause undetermined*, and the consortium had paid each claim without question, and the town had buried its dead and called it bad luck, and the whole rotten architecture of silence had rested on the shoulders of one drunk engineer and one tired adjuster who'd stopped asking why.

"Yes," he said. "It's better. God help me, it's better."

"Transit in thirty seconds," Ilsa said. "Strap in tight. Odile says the shear on the far side feels like being born."

"Did she say that before or after she volunteered to suffocate?"

"After." Ilsa smiled, and it broke his heart, and he let it. "She said it laughing."

Ten seconds. The corridor filled the forward view now, a river of absolute black bordered in light, and Silas felt the hauler's frame begin to sing—a low resonance in the deck plates, in the couch frame, in his own sternum, the same five-note motif rising and falling, rising and falling, as though the ship itself had caught the song.

Five seconds.

On the open channel, Odile's voice climbed toward the melody's peak, and Silas realized with a jolt that she wasn't singing alone anymore. Underneath her, layered and multiplied, came other voices—dozens, hundreds, a chorus riding the carrier wave from somewhere impossibly far away. The children. Still dreaming, or dreaming again, or simply listening across the dark the way they'd listened all along. Twelve thousand voices on Vesper Hollow, singing a lost survey crew's song back to the woman who'd carried it alone for twenty years.

"Oh," Ilsa whispered. "Oh, they're singing *with* her."

Transit.

* * *

Being born, Odile had said. She'd undersold it.

There was no sensation of motion. There was a sensation of *unfolding*—of the Meridian's Grace being gently, patiently turned inside out and then right way round again, of every atom in Silas's body being asked a question and answering correctly. Light behaved strangely for a duration he could not afterward measure; the cabin filled with a radiance that had no source and cast no shadows, and in that light he saw—Ilsa saw it too, she described it later in words that made the archivists weep—the corridor walls not as emptiness but as *depth*: strata upon strata of faded signals, the compressed echoes of every voice that had ever passed this way. Ships' logs. Distress calls. Lullabies. The accumulated speech of centuries of travelers, pressed into the dark like flowers into a book.

And among them, distinct, warm, unmistakably hers, Odile's voice continued—no longer from the relay behind them but from everywhere, from the medium itself, woven into the corridor's fabric. Singing. Holding the note. Keeping the handshake alive with the last breath her body had to give, so that the covenant would bind not just to the relay but to the colony, to the children, to the future, all the way through.

Then it was over. The light withdrew. Stars snapped back into their proper places—different stars, unfamiliar constellations, a sky no chart aboard had ever mapped—and the Meridian's Grace drifted in ordinary space on the far side of the window, and behind them the corridor began, smoothly and without drama, to close.

Silas watched it seal. He watched the light contract to a seam, the seam to a line, the line to nothing, and where the door had been there was only the old familiar dark, and he understood that he was looking at the exact spot where Odile Marchetti had spent her final hours keeping a promise, and that no instrument aboard would ever point him back to it.

The comm stack crackled. Carrier wave, still live, still carrying.

"—and that's the chorus resolved," Odile's voice said, mid-sentence, conversational, as though she'd been chatting the whole time. "Handshake is locked. Covenant propagation complete. Relay's holding it autonomously now, which means—" a breath, thinner than before, "—which means the engineering problem is solved, and all that's left is the human one, and thank God that part was never mine."

"Odile." Silas's voice came out rough. "Status report. That's an order."

"Nineteen minutes of air left, by my count, and my count's been good for forty years." No fear in it. Almost amusement. "Long enough. Listen to me, both of you. The far side charts are in the nav buffer—Ilsa, you'll find them under the dream-notation, third layer. Follow the bright thread. It runs all the way to settled space; the voice marked it for you. Three weeks, maybe four, and you're home."

"Home," Ilsa repeated. The word seemed to surprise her.

"You're going to need to tell them everything," Odile went on. "Quintana—you especially. No more redactions. You write it all down, every death, every cover-up, every name, and you make them read it out loud. That's your penance and your gift and I expect both delivered in full." A pause. "And Reyes. Girl. You keep the new beacon lit. First apprentice keeper in history—make sure they write that down too, and spell my name right in the dedication. M-A-R-C-H-E-T-T-I. Two T's."

"I promise," Ilsa said, crying without any shame at all. "I promise, I promise."

"Good kid." The carrier wave hummed. Somewhere in its harmonics, faintly, the five notes rose again. "Quintana. Last thing. When you get home—when you see the sun come up over the harvest fields, and nobody dies in them—"

Her voice caught. For the first time, the engineer's steadiness slipped, and what showed underneath was not terror but something gentler and far harder to bear: relief. Twenty years of guilt, letting go.

"—think of me. That's all. Just think of me once, in the morning, when it's beautiful."

The channel stayed open. Neither of them closed it. They flew on through unmapped stars with the line held open for nineteen minutes, saying nothing, needing to say nothing, while a dying woman hummed an old song into the dark, softer and softer, until the hum became the carrier wave and the carrier wave became the silence between stars, and even then Silas did not key it off. He let it ride, empty and open, all the way to the edge of charted space.

Ahead, faint but unmistakable, a new signal pulsed on the bright thread: a navigation marker, placed there by something patient and sad and kind, pointing the way home.

And behind them, in the direction of a door that would not open again for fifty years, the dark was quiet—and for the first time in the history of Vesper Hollow, the quiet was not hiding anything at all.
