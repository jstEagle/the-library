## 3. Crew of the Long Patience

The Long Patience had a heartbeat, and Odile Vann could hear it through her boots.

It wasn't a healthy heartbeat. It was the arrhythmic thump of a fusion drive that had been rebuilt four times by three different crews, each of whom had left their own opinions welded into its housing like scar tissue. She lay on her back in the drive bay with a stethoscope pressed to the coolant manifold — an actual brass stethoscope, salvaged from a hospital ship that had been scrapped before she was born — and she listened the way other people prayed.

*Thump. Thump. Hiss. Thump.*

That hiss was new. That hiss was going to ruin her evening.

She slid out from under the manifold, leaving a smear of grease down the front of her coveralls that matched the eleven other smears already there, and sat up in the amber work-light. The bay smelled the way it always did: hot metal and ozone and the faint sweetness of the solvent Sefa insisted on using to clean her hands, which was technically a degreaser and legally a controlled substance in two jurisdictions. Home, in other words. The only home Odile had ever chosen.

She'd been aboard the Patience for six years now. Before that there had been the Meridian Institute of Applied Thermodynamics, where she'd spent nine years learning to make engines sing, and before *that* there had been a mining station called Corvus Deep, where she'd spent her childhood learning what happened when someone else's engine didn't. When the pressure seals failed on Corvus Deep, the official inquiry took fourteen months to conclude that no one was at fault. Fourteen months, while the survivors lived in temporary housing and the dead went unburied because the ice they were stored in belonged to a company that had gone bankrupt. Odile was eleven years old when she learned that institutions do not grieve. They file reports.

So she'd made herself into something that didn't need an institution. She made engines need *her*, specifically, individually, irreplaceably — and then she found a ship small enough and broken enough that no one would ever take it away from her, or take her away from it.

The Long Patience was a Meridian-class heavy hauler, forty years old, originally built to move water ice between the outer colonies back when the war still had rules. She'd been decommissioned, repossessed, burned in a customs raid, sold at auction twice, and finally purchased for less than the cost of her own fuel by a man with a dead man's face who asked very few questions and paid wages on time. Odile had taken one look at the drive signature — a stutter in the second harmonic that would have made any fleet engineer weep — and signed on before Zephyrine Iversen finished explaining the pay scale.

Six years later, the stutter was still there. She'd learned to live with it. Some scars you don't fix; you just keep them company.

The hiss, though. The hiss she would hunt tonight.

* * *

She found Sefa in the cockpit, which is where Sefa lived the way barnacles live on hulls — permanently, defensively, with surprising strength.

Sefa Okonkwo-Demir was fifty-one years old and had flown everything with wings, thrusters, or an argumentative flight computer. She was a compact woman built like a tool designed for one purpose, with iron-gray hair cropped close to the skull and hands that were never entirely clean of hydraulic fluid no matter how much she scrubbed. She sat in the pilot's chair with her feet up on the console — a violation of seventeen regulations, none of which applied out here — running approach simulations against a debris field that existed only in her head.

"You're making that face," Sefa said without turning around.

"What face?"

"The face where you've heard something die and you haven't told it yet."

"Coolant manifold, secondary loop." Odile leaned in the hatchway. "There's a hiss."

"There's always a hiss."

"This one has a *rhythm*. Rhythm means resonance. Resonance means a crack that's growing. Crack grows, loop fails, loop fails and we lose secondary cooling during a burn, and then you get to find out how good a pilot you are while the drive compartment turns into a sauna."

"I know exactly how good a pilot I am," Sefa said. "I'm excellent. It's everyone else who's mediocre." She swung her feet down and finally looked over, and her dark eyes did the thing they did — the quick flicker toward the hatch, the reflexive check of sightlines, the old habit of a woman who had spent eleven years flying medical evacuations out of contested corridors during the worst years of the war. "How long to fix?"

"Four hours if I can isolate the section. Six if I have to pull the panel."

"Zeph booked us a departure window."

"Zeph books windows. I book realities." Odile crossed her arms. "Tell him six."

"Tell him yourself. He's doing paperwork."

Odile winced. Paperwork meant the estate notices again. Once a quarter, like clockwork, the Iversen estate's frozen accounts generated another legal notice demanding biometric confirmation of identity from Elias Iversen, deceased, care of his own estate, which was both a bureaucratic absurdity and, Odile suspected, a wound that never quite scabbed over. Their captain would sit in the mess with those notices spread out in front of him like a man reading his own autopsy, and he would sign them, every one, in careful handwriting, as though neatness could make the lie true.

"He hates those," Odile said.

"Everyone hates something," Sefa said. "Hate's cheap. What matters is what you fly toward anyway." She stood, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and clapped Odile on the shoulder with a hand that weighed about as much as a wrench. "Go fix your hiss. I'll tell Bram dinner's at nineteen hundred, and if he brings another one of his footnotes to the table I'm serving them separately."

* * *

Bram was in the cargo hold, because Bram was always in the cargo hold these days, sitting cross-legged on a crate of agricultural filters with a battered reader terminal balanced on his knees and a mug of tea going cold beside him.

Bram Tulloch had been, in another life, a Senior Conservator of the Colonial Records Authority — third-highest clearance in the archive service, custodian of eleven million documents, a man whose signature carried legal weight on four worlds. Then he had refused, on principle, to certify the destruction of a records cache that a fleet admiral wanted erased, and the archive service had discovered that principles are expensive. They stripped his credentials, blacklisted his name, and let him keep nothing but his memory, which was considerable, and his habits, which were incorrigible.

He was sixty-three, tall and stooped like a question mark, with white hair he cut himself badly and reading glasses held together with wire. He wore cardigans in deep space. He quoted regulations the way other people quoted scripture. And he treated every document that crossed the Patience's hold — manifests, contracts, customs declarations, the junk mail of interstellar commerce — as though it might be the thread that unraveled something important.

Which, lately, some of them were.

"Bram," Odile said, dropping onto the crate beside him. "Dinner at nineteen hundred. Sefa says leave the footnotes at the door."

"The footnotes are the best part of me." He peered at her over the wire glasses. "Manifold?"

"Hiss. Secondary loop. You heard it too?"

"I hear everything on this ship. It's the only conversation available sometimes." He tapped the reader. "Speaking of which. Our captain's quarterly correspondence arrived. Would you like to see what a dead man's mail looks like?"

"No."

"'Demand for biometric attestation,'" he read anyway, gently, the way you'd read a eulogy. "'Submit within thirty days to confirm continuity of legal identity or forfeit claim.' Signed by a clerk who has never met either of them." He lowered the terminal. "You know what strikes me, Odile? Ten years, and the estate still can't decide whether he's a person or a filing error."

"That's why he doesn't open them until night," Odile said. "When he thinks we're asleep."

"I know. I hear everything on this ship." Bram folded his glasses. "Do you ever wonder what he was, before? Before the inheritance?"

"Every day. He won't say. There's ten years of him before us and it's all sealed rooms." She shrugged. "But I figure — Corvus Deep taught me you don't dig at people's wreckage unless they invite you. You just make sure the seals hold."

Bram looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind the glasses — recognition, maybe, of a fellow keeper of things unspoken. "The seals hold," he agreed. "Nineteen hundred, then. I'll bring the tea."

* * *

At nineteen hundred, the mess of the Long Patience held all four of them around a table that had been bolted down twice and dented more times than anyone could count, under a light fixture that buzzed at a frequency Odile had long ago stopped hearing and Sefa claimed she could navigate by.

Dinner was protein noodles with actual vegetables — hydroponic basil, grown in a tray Odile kept in the engineering bay because growing things felt like a form of argument against the void. Sefa ate standing up, halfway, before Bram shamed her into sitting. Zephyrine arrived last, carrying the smell of the office and the particular set of shoulders he wore after signing things.

He was a big man, their captain, broad through the chest, with Elias Iversen's face — a strong jaw, dark hair going gray early at the temples, eyes a deep brown that people who had known the original said were wrong somehow, warmer, or sadder, or simply occupied by someone else. He moved carefully, like a man aware his body was evidence. He smiled rarely, but when he did, it rearranged the whole room.

"Estate notice?" Sefa asked, too bluntly, because Sefa's kindness had always come out sideways.

"Quarterly ritual." He sat. "Thirty days to prove I'm not myself."

"One day," Odile said, "the courts will have to invent a whole new category of stupid just to describe that."

"They already have. It's called 'clone law.'" He twirled noodles onto his fork without appetite. "Anyway. New contract came through. Kestrel Verge run, refrigerated freight, decent pay, boring route. We leave in three days."

"Boring routes are my favorite kind of lie," Sefa said. "But fine. Three days." She pointed her fork at Odile. "Your hiss?"

"Fixed. Mostly. I want to watch it through one full burn cycle."

"And my footnotes?" Bram asked hopefully.

"Were delicious," Zephyrine said, and for a moment — just a moment — the tiredness fell off his face and he was simply a man eating dinner with his family, and the mess was warm, and the basil smelled green, and the ship hummed around them like something alive that had decided, against all odds, to keep beating.

Later, when the others had drifted off — Sefa to the cockpit, Bram to his crates, Zephyrine to whatever sealed room he kept inside himself — Odile stayed at the table with her cold tea and listened to the ship.

Thump. Thump. Hiss.

Still there. Faint. Patient.

She'd deal with it tomorrow. Tonight she just sat with it, the way you sit with anything you love that isn't well — and thought, not for the first time, that the Long Patience was the truest name anyone had ever given anything. A ship called patience, crewed by people who were waiting — for absolution, for a name, for a record restored, for a home that hadn't burned. None of them had said it aloud yet. Families rarely announce themselves. They just keep setting the table.

She was rinsing the mugs when the comm chimed: an incoming message, priority-flagged, routed through three relay hops from somewhere in the Kestrel Verge.

From Meridian Station.

Addressed to: E. Iversen, or heir.

Odile stared at it for a long moment. Then she saved it, unread, and went to find the captain.
