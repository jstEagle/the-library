## 6. A Contract With a Dead Man's Face

The message came in on a private channel at 0300 station time, which was the hour when honest people were asleep and dishonest ones were working, and Zephyrine Iversen had long since stopped pretending to be either.

He was awake because the Long Patience's coolant loop was making a sound like a man clearing his throat before saying something regrettable, and Odile had threatened — cheerfully, the way she did everything — to weld his cabin door shut if he didn't log the anomaly properly this time. So he sat in the cramped office off the galley with a mug of recycled tea going bitter beside him, watching the channel request pulse amber against the dark of Meridian Station's outer viewport, and he thought: *nobody offers a cargo contract at three in the morning unless the cargo is trouble.*

He accepted anyway. That was the thing about inheriting a dead man's debts: you learned that trouble paid better than honesty, and honesty, in his experience, mostly paid in legal notices.

The face that resolved above his desk was unremarkable in the way that expensive tailoring makes faces unremarkable — smoothed, symmetrical, a face designed not to be remembered. Gray suit, gray eyes, a voice like a filing cabinet closing.

"Mister Iversen."

"That's me." It was and it wasn't, and he'd stopped flinching at the sentence around year four.

"My principals have reviewed your ship's record. Reliable. Discreet. Unremarkable." A pause that was almost a compliment. "We require transport for sensitive medical cargo. Origin Meridian Station, destination disclosed after departure. Single transit, no intermediate stops. The fee is two hundred thousand credits, half up front, half on delivery."

Zephyrine kept his expression where it always lived these days — somewhere between tired and polite. "Two hundred thousand is a lot of credits for medical cargo."

"It is valuable cargo."

"What kind of valuable?"

"Pediatric." The word came out smooth as lubricant. "Specialized pediatric cases requiring urgent transfer. The station quarantine has become... administratively burdensome. Our clients wish the children moved somewhere they can receive proper care."

Something cold walked up Zephyrine's spine and sat down at the base of his skull.

*Children.* He thought of the gossip that had been circulating through the dockside cantinas for two weeks now — the school wing sealed, fleet medics at every junction, parents turned away with printed explanations. He thought of Marisol Okonkwo's mother, who loaded cargo containers three berths down and had started sleeping in her skiff rather than go home to an apartment that felt too quiet without her daughter's dreams being talked about over breakfast. He thought of the word *quarantine*, and how quarantine was a word that meant *we are protecting them* right up until it meant *we are keeping them*.

"Forty children," Zephyrine said quietly. "That's the number I've heard."

The gray face didn't move. "The manifest will be provided upon acceptance."

"You want me to haul forty quarantined kids out of their own colony, in secret, at night, for people who won't show me a manifest." He leaned back until the old chair creaked its familiar complaint. "Mister — I'm sorry, I don't think I caught a name."

"No, you didn't."

Fair enough. Honest, even, in its way. "Here's my problem," Zephyrine said. "I run cargo. Grain, machine parts, frozen fish, once an entire decommissioned chapel, which is a story I'll tell you if you pay me enough. I don't run people. Especially not people who can't say yes for themselves. You want to move those children, you do it through their parents, through the colonial authority, through channels that leave paperwork a mother can read."

"The fee is negotiable."

"It's not about the fee."

"Everything," said the gray man, with the mild certainty of someone quoting scripture, "is about the fee. Eventually. Everyone has a number, Mister Iversen. Yours is simply higher than most, given your circumstances."

And there it was. The collar-tug. The little hook beneath his sternum that every mention of *his circumstances* set swinging. *Your circumstances*: the estate frozen in probate, the debts compounding like frost, the name he wore like a coat cut for a taller man. Ten years of being legally Elias Iversen and personally nobody at all, and this stranger had done his homework and knew exactly which bruise to press.

Zephyrine looked at the tea. Looked at the viewport, where Meridian's ice shelf glittered under the docking floodlights, beautiful and indifferent, hiding whatever it hid.

"No," he said.

The gray man tilted his head a degree, as if recalibrating an instrument. "May I ask why? Not sentiment, surely. Your record suggests—"

"My record suggests I'm broke, discreet, and unremarkable. All true. But I knew a man who took contracts like yours. Clean ones. Sensitive ones. Cargo that needed to be *unremarkable*." Zephyrine heard his own voice go flat and distant, the way it did when he talked about Elias, which was rarely, and never comfortably. "He told me once — or I read it in his notes, it's hard to keep straight whose memories are whose anymore — that the worst cargoes always come with the best fees, because the fee is what buys your silence when you finally understand what you carried."

Silence on the channel. Long enough that Zephyrine counted the coolant loop's throat-clearing twice.

Then the gray man smiled. It was a small smile, precise as a scalpel incision, and it changed nothing about his face and everything about the temperature of the room.

"A pity," he said. "My principals had hoped to avoid complications. They remember your original fondly."

The words landed wrong. Not the content — Zephyrine had spent a decade hearing people talk about Elias in the past tense, sometimes tenderly, usually carelessly. What landed wrong was the *fondly*. The warmth of it. The familiarity. As though this man had known him. As though—

"Who are your principals?" Zephyrine asked, and his voice came out quieter than he intended.

"Administrative elements within the Fleet Medical Directorate."

"Fleet Medical doesn't run black contracts through brokers with no names. Fleet Medical files forms. In triplicate. I've read enough of them to know."

"Directorates contain many departments," the gray man said pleasantly. "Some of them file forms. Some of them handle matters that would be... destabilizing, were they handled openly. Your original understood the distinction. He worked adjacent to such matters for years. Until, of course—" the smile widened by a millimeter, "—he didn't."

The channel died.

Zephyrine sat very still in the dark office for a long moment, the amber afterimage of the call fading from his vision, the tea forgotten, the coolant loop clearing its throat again and again like a nervous witness.

*Until, of course, he didn't.*

He should let it go. That was the sane move, the survival move, the move ten years of hauling other people's freight had taught him: see nothing, log nothing, deliver the grain, pay the interest, live to be humiliated by another biometric checkpoint next month. The Long Patience had a crew of three who trusted him to keep them boring. Boring was oxygen. Boring was life.

But.

He pulled up the case files.

He'd inherited them along with everything else — Elias's personal archive, encrypted, half-corrupted, a dead man's obsessive hoard of documents that Bram had spent years coaxing open one corrupted sector at a time. Most of it was expense reports and survey data and letters to a sister who'd stopped writing back. But there was a folder Bram called *the interesting ones*: fragments of correspondence, redacted memos, a single photograph of a ceremony Elias had attended eleven years ago, back when he'd still been employed by institutions instead of haunted by them.

Zephyrine opened the photograph and enlarged it until the pixels went soft and grainy, and there — on the lapel of a fleet administrator standing third from the left, a man whose face the archive had tagged only as *REDACTED — MEDICAL DIRECTORATE LIAISON* — was a pin.

A small silver pin. A stylized caduceus wrapped not around a staff but around a closed eye.

He'd seen that pin before. Twice. Once in the photograph, filed away and mostly forgotten. And once — six months ago, in a customs dispute that had gone nowhere, in a corridor on Tollgate Nine, on the lapel of a gray-suited man whose face he hadn't been able to remember afterward no matter how hard he tried.

*A face designed not to be remembered.*

Zephyrine's hands were steady. He noticed that about himself distantly, the way you notice weather in another country. He opened the personnel fragment Bram had recovered last spring — three lines of legible text out of four hundred corrupted ones — and read them again:

*...liaison attached to Founding-Era Records Review... clearance exceeds directorate charter... projects designated ECHO-CLASS are not to appear in any index...*

ECHO-CLASS.

Elias had underlined it. In the margin, in handwriting Zephyrine knew better than his own — it was, after all, his hand too, younger, more certain — Elias had written a single word:

*Why?*

*

He found Odile in the engine room at 0410, lying on her back beneath the coolant exchanger with a wrench in one hand and a diagnostic slate balanced on her sternum, humming something tuneless and defiant.

"Coolant loop," she said, before he could speak. "Bearing four's got scoring. I'll have it swapped by noon. Also you look like a man who's seen a ghost, and since you technically live with one, that's saying something."

"Somebody just offered me two hundred thousand credits to smuggle forty children off Meridian."

The humming stopped. Odile slid out from under the exchanger and sat up, and her face did the thing it always did — went from joking to granite in the space of a breath, all the warmth packed away somewhere load-bearing. "Say that again slower."

"Gray suit. No name. Called it sensitive medical cargo. Pediatric cases. Said the quarantine was 'administratively burdensome.'" Zephyrine leaned against the bulkhead. "I said no."

"Good."

"And then he said his principals remembered Elias fondly."

Odile was quiet for a moment. She set the wrench down with exaggerated care, the way she did when she wanted her hands to have something to do besides shake. "Fondly. Like they knew him."

"Like they worked with him." Zephyrine held up his slate, the photograph glowing on it, the little silver pin circled in red he'd drawn with a shaking finger he couldn't remember shaking. "This insignia. It's in his case files. Medical Directorate liaison, Founding-Era Records Review, clearance above the charter. And now the same office wants forty dreaming children moved off-station in the dark, no manifest, no parents, no paper trail."

Odile stood. She was a small woman and she made herself smaller still when she was thinking, shoulders curled in, like a ship running dark. "You're thinking it's connected. The dreams. The quarantine. Him."

"I'm thinking I refused a contract and a stranger quoted my dead original's employment history at me like a threat." Zephyrine laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow, an echo in an empty hold. "Ten years, Odile. Ten years I've been carrying his name around like luggage somebody else packed. Debts, warrants, probate hearings, checkpoints where they make me prove I'm not myself. And in all that time, nobody — *nobody* — has ever cared who Elias Iversen was. To the fleet he's a closed file. To the courts he's an outstanding balance. To me he's..."

He stopped. Odile waited. She was good at waiting; it was half her job.

"To me he's a face in the mirror that isn't mine," Zephyrine finished. "And tonight some gray man with a pin on his lapel looked at that face and smiled like he recognized it. Like he was *glad* to see it. And I need you to tell me I'm imagining the connection, because if I'm not imagining it, then the murder we inherited wasn't random, and the dreams aren't a malfunction, and those children aren't sick — they're *receiving* something. Something worth killing a scientist over ten years ago and sealing a school wing over now."

Odile looked at the photograph for a long time. The engine room hummed around them, the great tired heart of the Long Patience doing its imperfect best.

"I can't tell you that," she said finally. "I can't tell you you're imagining it. But I can tell you what I'd do in your place, and what I'd do is wake up Bram, because that man knows more about what the fleet buries than the fleet does, and because right now you've got the look of a man about to do something stupid alone, and Iversen—" she tapped his chest with one grease-blackened finger, gently, "—you don't get to do stupid alone anymore. That's what the family's for."

*Family.* The word still caught somewhere in him, like a key turning in a lock rusted shut, opening onto a room he kept expecting to be empty and never was.

"Bram's asleep," Zephyrine said.

"Then he's about to have a very educational nightmare," Odile said, and handed him the wrench to hold while she killed the lights.

Above them, through the engine room's single viewport, Meridian's ice shelf turned slowly in the station's shadow — white, ancient, patient — and somewhere beneath it, though neither of them knew it yet, something grown and not built waited in the dark with a dead man's voice, holding its coordinates like a prayer, repeating them to forty sleeping children:

*tell them it was staged.*

*tell them it was staged.*

*tell them.*
