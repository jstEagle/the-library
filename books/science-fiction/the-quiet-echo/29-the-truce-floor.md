## 29. The Truce Floor

The summit station at Halcyon Gate had been built to impress, and it still did, the way a mausoleum impresses — with scale, with marble that had never known weather, with ceilings high enough to make every person beneath them feel briefly like an insect that had wandered indoors. Zephyrine came up out of the civilian ring through a service corridor that smelled of recycled air and floor polish, carrying a refrigerated case of "seed stock" in one hand and a forged maintenance chit in the other, and stepped onto the truce floor.

It was not a floor so much as a wound dressed in ceremony.

The great rotunda had been partitioned down its center by a wall of translucent smartglass, fleet blue on one side, Concordance green on the other, the two halves mirroring each other exactly — same horseshoe of delegate platforms, same banks of aides and translators, same hovering displays scrolling casualty figures that both sides had stopped believing years ago but still updated hourly, because the numbers were the only language left they shared. Between the two halves ran a narrow neutral corridor, ten meters wide, lit from above by a band of white light that someone had decided symbolized hope. It looked more like a surgical incision.

Zephyrine stood at the edge of it, in a borrowed technician's jumpsuit two sizes too big, and watched the war try to end itself and fail.

He had imagined this moment for three systems' worth of running. In his imagination there had always been more drama to it — alarms, barricades, his own voice ringing off the marble. Instead there was this: a thousand people in good clothes talking past each other in a room designed to make them feel small, and him among them, anonymous, unremarkable, holding a box of ice that contained the reason for everything.

*You have no standing here,* he told himself, and the words had teeth. *No credentials. No delegation. No name anyone in this room would recognize — except one, and that one is dead.*

He moved along the perimeter, unhurried, the way Odile had drilled into him during the convoy run: *If you walk like you're lost, you're a problem. If you walk like you're bored, you're furniture.* He was furniture. He was a man delivering seed stock to hydroponics, and if he happened to pause near the public gallery rail, well, even furniture got tired legs.

From the gallery he could see most of the fleet side. The delegation sat beneath the fleet's old sigil — the seven-pointed star that Elias's lattice said had been sewn over a lie before the ink on it dried. Their lead negotiator, a silver-haired woman whose name Zephyrine didn't know, was speaking now, her voice piped faintly through the gallery speakers: something about security guarantees, about verification regimes, about trust that could not be asked for but must be built. Across the glass, the Concordance delegation listened with the particular stillness of people who have heard the speech before and know how it ends.

On the great display above the rotunda, the countdown burned in patient amber:

**TRUCE EXPIRES: 04:11:52**

Four hours. Give or take a minute for the relays.

Zephyrine's hands were steady. That surprised him. Somewhere between Tollgate Nine and the convoy checkpoint, his body had apparently concluded that terror was a luxury it could no longer afford, and had filed it away with the rest of the things he'd lost — the *Kestrel's Loom*, his cargo license under any name at all, the modest legal fiction that he was a person rather than a dispute. What remained was a strange, scoured calm, the kind he'd once seen in Sefa after a burn gone wrong: not bravery, just the absence of anything left to flinch with.

He set the case down beside a pillar, crouched as if checking its seals, and let himself look — really look — at the room where the last ten years of his life had been decided without him.

* * *

The deadlock, when you watched it long enough, resolved into a rhythm. That was the horror of it. It wasn't chaos; it was choreography.

Every ninety minutes or so, one side would table a proposal. The other side would receive it, deliberate visibly, and reject it — not because the proposal was unacceptable, Zephyrine began to suspect, but because rejecting it filled the time. The mediators from the Neutral Compact moved between the delegations like water finding channels, wearing expressions of professional grief. Aides passed paper. Translators murmured into collars. And the countdown burned down, indifferent, taking the ceasefire with it minute by minute while everyone in the room performed the ritual of peace with the enthusiasm of mourners at a funeral they'd already catered.

"They've been at the same clause for six hours."

The voice came from beside him — an older woman in gallery seating, civilian clothes with a Compact observer's pin, speaking without looking at him, the way strangers talk in queues. "Same clause since I arrived yesterday. Paragraph nine. Verification of forward garrison withdrawals." She shook her head slowly. "I've sat through four summits. This is the quietest one. Do you know what I mean? Nobody's shouting. Nobody wants to be the one who walked out first. So they'll sit here until the clock runs out and then everyone will say the other side killed it."

"Do you think it can still work?" Zephyrine asked. He kept his voice flat, curious, a technician making conversation.

She finally looked at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed with exhaustion. "Work? Son, I watched the draft text this morning. There are forty-one unresolved articles. You don't resolve forty-one articles in four hours. You resolve them in four months, or you bury them." She turned back to the glass. "But nobody down there wants to be holding the knife when the clock hits zero. So we all sit here and watch it happen, very politely."

Zephyrine looked at the case at his feet. Inside it, wrapped in coolant gel and disguised as agricultural stock, lay a core sample of grown neural tissue that knew why paragraph nine would never close — because the garrisons both sides were so afraid of had been pointed at an enemy the fleet itself had manufactured, and the men who'd done the manufacturing had spent thirty years making sure no verification regime would ever look behind the founding lie.

*I have the answer,* he thought. *I am standing twenty meters from the answer, and I am furniture.*

"Excuse me," he said to the woman, and picked up the case.

* * *

Getting closer was insultingly easy. That was the thing no one told you about power: at the summit of it, the security thinned, because everyone inside the inner rings had already been vetted to death, and the system's imagination did not extend to a man in a maintenance jumpsuit carrying a box.

He followed a food-service lift to the mezzanine level, showed his forged chit to a compact drone that scanned it, found it adequately boring, and waved him through. From the mezzanine he could see down into the neutral corridor itself — the ten-meter strip of white light where the mediators walked their endless shuttles. He could see faces now. He could see the fleet delegation's military liaison, a hard-eyed colonel with Rennick's insignia on his shoulder board, leaning to whisper something to the silver-haired negotiator, and he could see the negotiator's face change — a flicker, quickly mastered, of a woman being told the ground was moving.

Rennick was here. Or her hand was. Zephyrine felt it the way you feel weather coming — pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the air handlers. Bram had been taken to Halcyon Gate; Odile had confirmed the transfer manifest before they'd split up at the dock. Which meant Rennick's operation was centered here, which meant the lattice's burial was scheduled here, timed — he understood it suddenly, with cold clarity — to the same clock burning overhead. The truce wouldn't just lapse. It would lapse *clean*, with the truth sealed under ice and under interrogation cells, and the war would resume on terms the hardliners had chosen.

Unless.

He crouched again by the case, pretending to adjust a seal, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since Meridian.

To speak on the truce floor, you needed credentials. To submit evidence, you needed a sponsoring delegation. To be heard at all, you needed to exist — a registered party, a recognized interest, a name in the system. He had none of it. His legal identity had died with the *Kestrel's Loom*'s registration; his genetic identity was classified contraband; his face belonged to a murdered man whose estate was frozen pending resolution of a case no court wanted to open. If he stood up in that rotunda and said *I have proof the founding atrocity was staged*, he would be detained before he finished the sentence — and the lattice would go into an evidence locker, and the locker would go into a vault, and the vault would outlive them all.

There were, he counted, perhaps sixty people in this building who could carry his message to the floor. Every one of them was either committed to the deadlock, afraid of the deadlock ending, or bought.

And below it all, quieter than the arithmetic, the other truth he'd been carrying since the hold of the *Long Patience*: even if he had the floor, even if they gave him a podium and an hour — would they believe him? A clone. A smuggler. A dead man's echo, arriving at the last hour with a box of ice and a story that unraveled thirty years of history. The truth was not enough. He had learned that on Meridian, watching Voss's face. Truth needed a throat, and every throat in this building belonged to someone with reasons to keep it closed.

His hands were still steady. He noticed that, and hated it a little. Steady hands, useless ones.

"Sir?"

A steward. Young, harried, holding a datapad. "That unit needs a destination code if it's staying on the mezzanine. Hydroponics is deck six."

"Of course," Zephyrine said. "I'm waiting for a pickup. Courier's late."

The steward glanced at the case, at the chit on his chest, and — this was the mercy of hierarchy — decided not to care. "Pickups have to log at the freight desk. Don't leave it unattended."

"I won't," Zephyrine said, and meant it more than the man could know.

* * *

He found a recessed alcove near the gallery rail, half-hidden behind a display column cycling through archival footage of the war's first week — he made himself watch it, frame by frame, the atrocity footage the whole galaxy had seen, the burning transport, the seven hundred dead that had lit the fuse. The lattice's surface layer held the raw telemetry of that same hour. The two did not match. They had never matched. He had watched the comparison eleven times in the hold with Marisol asleep against the coolant lines, and every time it landed in his chest like a dropped weight: *they filmed it twice*. Once for history. Once for themselves.

An aide hurried past the alcove, speaking into her collar: "—no, the Secretary won't take the fleet's supplemental until paragraph nine moves. Tell them paragraph nine or nothing." A pause. "Then tell them we'll be having a very short evening."

Zephyrine closed his eyes.

Ten years ago — eleven, depending on how you counted the vat time — Elias Iversen had stood somewhere in this same system with the same knowledge and no better options, and had chosen not the summit, not the press, not the courts, but ice. Had chosen to grow the truth into living tissue where no subpoena could reach it, and had paid for that choice with his life. And now his echo stood in the palace where the decision was actually made, holding the answer, and discovered that the palace had no door marked *truth*. It had doors marked *procedure*, *standing*, *verification*, *sovereignty* — forty-one locked doors, and four hours, and him.

He thought of Marisol, asleep in the civilian ring under Voss's watch, dreaming the white corridor. He thought of Bram in a cell somewhere beneath his feet, smiling at interrogators. He thought of Odile's hands in the drive housing, Sefa's jaw set against the dark, the whole improbable family scattered across this station on the strength of his conviction — and for the first time since Meridian, conviction felt like arrogance. He had told them the message had to reach this room. He had not fully believed, until he stood in it, how thoroughly the room was built to refuse messages. It was a machine for converting urgency into delay. It ate four hours the way the war ate systems.

The countdown ticked past 03:00:00. Somewhere below, the silver-haired negotiator called a recess that fooled no one.

* * *

He was still in the alcove when the lights changed.

Not all of them — just the ambient strips along the mezzanine, dipping from warm white to a cooler tone, and the displays flickering in a way displays shouldn't. A half-second stutter. Then smooth again, seamless, the kind of glitch a tired eye forgives. But Zephyrine had spent a decade around Odile's machinery, and Odile had taught him the difference between a fault and a hand on the switch.

Across the rotunda, the fleet delegation's section dimmed fractionally further — their own local grid, isolated, controlled. And in the hush of the recess, he saw the colonel with Rennick's shoulder board step out of the delegation horseshoe and speak quietly into his own collar, and he saw two marines in dress grays appear at the rotunda's main entrance who had not been there five minutes ago, and then two more at the gallery stairs.

Not yet an occupation. A rehearsal for one. Men learning the sightlines before the order came.

Zephyrine's mouth went dry. *When the truce lapses, they seal the ring. Sefa said it, Rennick's own doctrine says it — cut comms, lock the civilians out, control the narrative in the first hour.* The plan he and the crew had built assumed they'd have the night to find another way in. The plan had assumed the enemy would wait for the clock.

The colonel's eyes swept the mezzanine, slow and professional, and for one suspended second they crossed Zephyrine's — a maintenance tech with a freight case, nothing, furniture — and moved on.

Zephyrine exhaled, picked up the case, and did the only thing left that powerlessness allowed: he went to find his family, and to tell them the room had just run out of patience faster than the clock.

Behind him, the countdown burned on — **02:47:19** — and beneath the marble, deeper than any of them yet knew, the station's sleeping thousands had already begun, softly and in their thousands, to dream of a white corridor and a door.
