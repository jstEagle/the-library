## 39. Heirs

The summons arrived the way most things arrived on Meridian Station now: too late, and smelling faintly of someone else's panic.

Zephyrine found it wedged in the Long Patience's external mail locker, a physical document — actual paper, cream-colored, watermarked with the seal of the Provisional Claims Court — because the courts had learned, in the weeks since the truth woke up, that anything sent over a network could be altered, and so they had retreated to the oldest technology of legitimacy they knew. Ink. Signatures. Paper you could hold.

*In re: Estate of Elias Iversen, deceased. Notice of Final Settlement.*

He stood in the cargo bay with it for a long time, turning it over in his hands, while Odile's repair drones droned their patient circles overhead and the smell of solder and recycled air wrapped around him like a coat he'd worn so long he'd stopped noticing it.

Ten years ago, another document like this one had landed in his lap — thinner, crueler, printed on cheap flimsi by a probate clerk who couldn't look him in the eye. *You are recognized as genetic successor to the estate of Elias Iversen, inclusive of all debts, obligations, and unresolved legal liabilities.* He remembered the exact weight of that sentence. Not *heir*. *Successor*. A word chosen by lawyers to mean: we cannot decide what you are, so we will make you responsible for everything he was and give you nothing he was owed.

He had signed for debts he never incurred. He had answered, for ten years, for a murder he hadn't committed and a man he'd never met. Every customs officer, every fleet picket, every sneering junior magistrate had treated him as evidence wearing a jumpsuit — and the law had agreed with them, in its bloodless way. The estate was frozen. The name was contested. He was a walking annex to a case file.

Now the paper in his hands said something different.

*The Court, having reviewed evidence entered into the public record pursuant to the Halcyon Broadcast, and finding no surviving party with superior claim, does hereby settle the Estate of Elias Iversen in full to the recognized successor: Zephyrine Iversen.*

Recognized successor. His own name, first, before the surname. Someone had typed it that way on purpose. He would find out later that it was a junior clerk named Ade Okafor, twenty-three years old, who had been one of the thousands asleep on Halcyon Gate when the lattice sang through them, and who had woken up knowing exactly what a staged atrocity was worth and what a clone's name was worth, and had quietly, without telling anyone, put the names in the order they deserved.

But that was later. For now, Zephyrine just stood in the cargo bay of a ship that smelled like engine grease and old tea, holding the first legal document in ten years that did not treat him as a problem to be managed.

"Is it what you hoped?" Bram's voice came from the hatchway. The archivist leaned there with a mug of something steaming, his collar crooked as always, his smile carrying the particular serenity of a man who had spent three weeks in a fleet cell and come out of it more amused than broken.

"It's paper," Zephyrine said.

"It's always paper," Bram said. "Paper is just truth that's been given a body. Read me the good part."

Zephyrine read it. Bram listened with his eyes closed, the way he listened to old recordings, and when Zephyrine finished, the archivist opened his eyes and said, simply: "Well. He left you everything after all."

"He didn't leave me anything. The court did."

"You know that's not how inheritance works." Bram took a sip of his drink. "A will is just a wish with witnesses. What actually gets passed down is whatever the living decide the dead meant. And right now, half the settled worlds have decided your dead man meant the truth. Which means they've decided he meant you."

Zephyrine looked down at the paper again. Somewhere in the hold below them, packed in insulated crates, the lattice core hummed its almost-silent hum — quieter now, since the broadcast, since its work was done, like a bell that had rung itself out. Except not entirely out. Not for him. Some nights, drifting toward sleep, he still felt it: a faint warmth behind his thoughts, a corridor of white light at the edge of dreaming, and once — only once — a voice that was not quite Elias saying, gently, *you can hang up whenever you want.* He hadn't hung up yet. He wasn't sure he wanted to.

"The estate," he said slowly, "is not small."

"No," Bram agreed. "It is not small."

* * *

They convened the reading of it in the Long Patience's mess, because Zephyrine refused to hold it anywhere else. The court had offered him a chamber. An actual chamber, in the rebuilt administrative ring on Meridian, with seating for counsel and a view of the ice shelf where everything had started and ended. He'd declined by return courier. Whatever this was going to be, it was going to happen at his table, with his people, over Sefa's terrible coffee.

So they crowded into the mess: Odile with her ever-present torque wrench tucked into her belt like a sidearm; Sefa perched on the counter because she had never once in her life sat in a chair when a horizontal surface was available; Bram with his mug; Dr. Voss, who had come aboard from the evacuee habitat with a medical kit he no longer needed to carry but carried anyway, out of habit; and Marisol, nine years old and newly famous across four systems, sitting cross-legged on the bench with a juice pouch and an expression of enormous patience, as if she had already heard the ending and was waiting for everyone else to catch up.

Zephyrine read the settlement aloud.

It ran forty pages. Most of it was boilerplate — releases, discharges, the formal unfreezing of accounts that had sat sealed since the murder. But the substance was this:

Elias Iversen had been, in life, a wealthy man. Not smuggler-wealthy, not fleet-admiral wealthy, but the quiet compounding wealth of a scientist whose patents had been licensed across two decades of colonial infrastructure: water reclamation systems on eleven stations, cold-atmosphere agriculture frameworks, the thermal regulation patents that kept habitats like Meridian livable at all. The fleet had seized much of it during the investigation — "pending resolution" — and then the hardliners had buried the resolution along with the man. Ten years of frozen assets, appreciating in escrow, gathering interest in accounts no one dared touch.

The number, when Zephyrine read it aloud, made Sefa swear in three languages and drop her coffee.

Odile caught the mug without looking. "Say it again," she said. "Slower."

He said it again, slower.

"That's not money," Sefa said. "That's a fleet wing. That's a shipyard. That's — you could buy the Kestrel Verge. You could buy the *war*."

"The war's over," Marisol said mildly, into her juice pouch.

Everyone laughed, a little too loudly, the way people laugh when a child says something true.

Voss was quiet through all of it, and when the reading was done, he asked the question Zephyrine had known was coming, asked it gently, doctor's hands folded on the table: "What are you going to do?"

And Zephyrine found that he had known the answer for longer than he'd admitted. Maybe since the hold of this ship, months ago, when Elias's recorded fragment had addressed *whoever inherits this* and given him permission to be a person instead of a copy. Maybe since the lattice itself, deep under the ice, had recognized his genome and offered him kinship instead of ownership. Everything Elias had built — the patents, the accounts, the hidden archive beneath the ice — had been built for one purpose: to carry a message past the people who would kill to bury it. The money was just the part of the message that hadn't needed broadcasting yet.

"I'm going to give it away," Zephyrine said.

Sefa blinked. "All of it?"

"Most of it." He turned the settlement pages face-down, one by one, like closing doors. "Here's what I've figured out about inheritance. You spend ten years being told you're nothing but what a dead man left behind — his face, his name, his debts, his enemies. And you start to believe that's what an estate *is*. A weight. A chain of custody. But it isn't. It's a question. The estate asks: what did he mean? And nobody can answer that for him. You answer it by what you do with it." He looked around the table. "Elias hid the truth under the ice because he believed it belonged to everyone it was done to. The money's the same thing. It was extracted from colonies — from water systems and habitat contracts and the labor of people who never saw the ledgers. It belongs back where it came from."

"To the children," Voss said. Not a question.

"To the children." Zephyrine nodded. "Forty-one receivers on Meridian. Two hundred and six evacuated colony kids scattered across the relief habitats. Every sleeper on Halcyon Gate who woke up carrying something they didn't ask for. The lattice used them. It saved the war's end, yes — but it used them, and some of them are still having trouble sleeping, and their families are going to be paying for therapy and relocation and time for a long time. The fleet owes them. The fleet won't pay — half the admiralty is under arrest and the other half is busy pretending it always loved peace. So the estate pays. Medical care. Education. Trusts, structured so no magistrate can freeze them, drawn up by lawyers who were on Halcyon when it happened and take it personally." He exhaled. "I want it done before the tribunals finish. While the meaning is still fresh enough that no one can quietly reroute it."

Silence around the table. Then Odile, practical as gravity: "And for you?"

"The ship."

Sefa laughed. "The ship's barely worth the docking fees."

"The ship is worth everything," Zephyrine said. "The ship is the only thing on the manifest I chose."

* * *

The signing took place in a modest hearing room on Meridian Station — Zephyrine had conceded a room, if not a chamber — and it should have taken eleven minutes. It took three hours, because the universe had apparently decided he hadn't suffered enough paperwork for one lifetime.

There were objections. Of course there were. Three distant cousins of Elias surfaced from the inner colonies like fish rising to a dropped feed, each with counsel, each claiming genetic priority. One of them, a florid man named Aldous Iversen-Vey, made the mistake of arguing the point in front of the press pool, saying — with a lawyer's precision and a fool's timing — that "the clone" had no standing to dispose of family assets, that he was "a facsimile, a reproduction, an echo of the true line."

Zephyrine watched the word land. *Echo.* The man thought he'd found an insult. He had, instead, handed Zephyrine the whole argument gift-wrapped.

When the magistrate invited his response, Zephyrine stood, and he did not raise his voice, because he had learned from a dead man's archived pattern that the quietest transmissions traveled farthest.

"My original," he said, "spent the last years of his life building a machine whose entire function was to be an echo — to repeat one true thing until it reached every ear that needed it. He considered that the highest work of his life. So yes. I'm an echo. I'm proud of it. And the difference between us, counselor, is that I'm an echo of a man who gave everything away, and you're an original who showed up for the reading of a will you didn't bother attending while he was alive." He let that sit for exactly two seconds. "The estate goes where he meant it. You may check the record. He wrote it down."

(Ade Okafor, the junior clerk, would later confirm that Elias's original testamentary codicil — recovered from the seized records Bram had spent years indexing — contained a single handwritten line in the margin beside the clause naming his successors: *If any of them comes asking, they've misunderstood everything. Give it to whoever carries the work.* The court chose to read "whoever carries the work" expansively.)

The cousins withdrew. The magistrate signed. The trusts were established within the week, seeded with ninety-two percent of the estate, administered by a board that included Dr. Ansel Voss, two parents of receivers, and — over her strenuous objection and Bram's delighted insistence — one defrocked archivist who knew every trick a frozen account could play and therefore every trick to prevent one.

Marisol attended the signing, because Marisol went everywhere now, trailing minders and journalists the way other children trailed balloons. When it was done, she walked up to Zephyrine in the corridor outside, looked up at him with those level dark eyes that had seen more of the inside of history than most historians, and said:

"You kept the ship."

"I kept the ship."

"Good." She nodded once, with the finality of a judge. "The voice liked the ship. It used to say so. In the dreams, near the end. It said, *tell them the ship is the home part.*"

Zephyrine crouched down to her height. "Did it say anything else?"

She considered, sucking the last of her juice pouch. "It said you'd know what to do with the rest." She shrugged, nine years old again, the oracle folding herself back into a kid. "You did. Okay? You did." And then she ran off down the corridor toward Voss, because somewhere in the habitat ring there was a recreation deck and a girl named Tamsin she'd been dreaming alongside since before either of them could read, and some appointments outrank magistrates.

Zephyrine stayed crouched a moment longer than he needed to.

* * *

That night, he walked the Long Patience alone.

It was an old habit, or maybe a new one — he'd never owned anything before, and ownership, it turned out, came with a strange urge to touch the edges of the thing. He moved through her decks with the lights at quarter power: the cargo bays, empty now except for the lattice core in its padded cradle, humming its finished hum; the galley where Sefa's coffee stain had achieved geological permanence; the engine spaces where Odile's handwriting labeled every valve in three colors of marker; the narrow cabin where Marisol had slept during the run to Halcyon, her blanket still folded on the bunk because no one had the heart to put it in storage.

His cabin held the only personal item he'd allowed himself from the estate: not art, not heirlooms, none of the dead man's possessions. Just a single sheet of the settlement paper, framed behind plain glass, the first page, where the clerk had typed *Zephyrine Iversen* — his name first, the surname after, the order that mattered.

He stood looking at it for a long time.

For ten years he had believed the estate was a debt. Then he'd believed it was a burden. Then, briefly, in Rennick's velvet offer, he'd believed it might be a door — legitimacy, belonging, a name conferred from above by people with the power to confer. He had almost walked through that door. He shivered now, remembering how close.

But standing here, in the quiet of a ship full of people who had chosen him without any court's permission, he finally understood what the paper on the wall actually was. Not a deed. Not a verdict. A receipt. Proof that something had been handed off, hand to hand, from a dead man who trusted the future to a living man who'd finally stopped apologizing for being in it. The money was gone — given away, flowing outward through a hundred trusts like light through a lattice — and he had never felt richer.

An echo, he thought, isn't a diminished copy of a sound. It's proof the sound was real. It's proof it traveled. It's proof something was worth carrying across the distance.

He touched the frame once, lightly, the way you'd touch a shoulder.

Then the comm panel chimed — Sefa, from the cockpit, her voice carrying the particular brightness she reserved for good news delivered fast: "Skipper? Dock control just cleared our departure window. Early slot. Also — you're going to want to see the traffic feed. There's a shuttle requesting permission to come alongside before we cast off. Civilian transponder. Says it's carrying a passenger for the Meridian–Halcyon run." A pause, and he could hear her grin forming. "Passenger's name is Marisol Iversen."

Zephyrine frowned. "Iversen?"

"That's what it says. Though between you and me, skipper—" the feed crackled with Sefa's shrug— "the manifest also lists her occupation as 'crew.' Somebody filed the paperwork wrong. Or right. You want me to wave her in?"
