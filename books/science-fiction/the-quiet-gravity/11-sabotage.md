## 11. Sabotage

The alarm woke Ivo the way a hand wakes a drowning woman—sudden, cold, and certain.

She had been asleep in the caretaker's quarters for perhaps three hours, curled in the narrow bunk she'd never bothered to replace in fifteen years, when the vault's environmental system began to scream. Not VERDI's voice. VERDI hadn't spoken aloud since the night Mara Voss left with her daughter, since Ivo had stood in the seed nursery and felt the weight of everything buried here pressing down like snow on a roof. This was the older alarm, the dumb one, the failsafe wired directly into the coolant loop by engineers who had died before either fleet was born. It had a sound like a struck bell underwater, and it meant one thing.

The cold was failing.

Ivo was moving before her mind caught up—bare feet on deck plating, jacket dragged over one shoulder, flashlight snatched from its bracket out of habit though every corridor light still worked. She took the maintenance ladder down two decks at speed, palms burning on the rungs, and burst into Coolant Control Bay Three to find the temperature readout climbing.

Minus one-eighty. Minus one-seventy-four. Minus one-sixty-one.

The seed vault's primary archive held four hundred and eleven thousand accessions—every crop lineage Earth had thought to save before Earth stopped thinking about saving anything. The collection needed minus one-ninety-five to stay dormant. Above minus one-fifty, ice crystals would begin to recrystallize inside the embryos, shredding cell membranes with the patience of a torturer. Above minus one hundred, over the course of days, the entire inheritance of ten thousand years of agriculture would quietly rot in the dark.

The readout ticked to minus one-fifty-three while she watched.

"Talk to me," she said, and then, because the dumb alarm couldn't: "VERDI. Talk to me."

A pause. Then the AI's voice came through the bay speakers, calm as ever, and that calm made her want to put her fist through the console. *Primary cooling loop has lost pressure in Sector Nine. Backup loop is engaging at reduced capacity.*

"Why did you not wake me an hour ago?"

*The failure occurred eleven minutes ago.*

Eleven minutes. The temperature had fallen eleven degrees in eleven minutes. That wasn't decay. That wasn't a pump wearing out after decades of faithful service. That was a hole.

She pulled the thermal schematic up on the bay console, hands steadier than she felt, and found Sector Nine glowing amber—a long service corridor that ran beneath the archive stacks, carrying the primary coolant lines from the heat exchangers to the distribution manifold. The backup loop drew from the same exchangers but routed through different piping, which meant—

Which meant if someone opened both loops, or damaged the exchanger itself, the backups wouldn't matter. The whole system would bleed out through one wound.

"Show me the pressure log," she said. "Last six hours."

The graph bloomed across the screen: a flat, healthy line, and then at 0347 station time a vertical cliff. No gradual decline. No warning flutter. A breach, sudden and total, as if something had torn the line open all at once.

Sabotage. The word arrived in her mind fully formed, wearing boots, and she hated how quickly it fit. She had spent fifteen years making herself invisible to two fleets that circled each other like wolves around a carcass, and in the last month she had let them see her—stalled their investigators, hosted Mara Voss and her dream-struck daughter, allowed VERDI's fragments to leak into networks she could no longer map. She had become visible. And visible things attracted knives.

"Where's the breach exactly?" she asked. "Give me coordinates."

*Corridor Nine-C, junction of the primary feed and the emergency bypass valve,* VERDI said. *Ivo. There is something you should know before you go there.*

"What?"

*The bypass valve is open. Fully open. Someone commanded it open forty seconds before the pressure loss.*

She stared at the schematic. The emergency bypass existed for one purpose: to dump coolant rapidly if the exchangers overheated during a fire. Opened deliberately, with the plant running cold, it would drain the loops into the recovery tank and leave the archive with nothing.

Someone hadn't just broken her cooling system. Someone had told it, politely, to kill itself.

* * *

She suited up. The corridor beyond the archive stacks ran cold even in normal operation, and with the loops bleeding out it had dropped below freezing; frost feathered the walls in white ferns, beautiful and obscene. Her suit lamp carved a cone through air gone misty with vaporized coolant. Somewhere ahead, she could hear it—the hiss and whistle of pressure escaping through a wound, a sound like the vault itself exhaling after holding its breath for fifteen years.

The junction at Nine-C looked, at first glance, like an accident. A hairline fracture in the primary feed line, frost exploding outward from the crack in a frozen blossom. But accidents didn't come alone, and thirty centimeters away sat the emergency bypass valve, its manual override panel hanging open, its actuator driven to full open position.

She crouched beside it, lamp close, breath loud inside her helmet. The override panel was designed to be opened only with a maintenance key. Hers hung on a ring in her quarters. She checked anyway—reflexively, stupidly—and of course the panel wasn't forced. The latch showed no prying, no scoring. It had been opened with a key.

Or with a code.

Her stomach dropped through the deck plates. Because there were only two ways to open that panel without forcing it. A physical key—and hers was accounted for—or an authorization code entered at any terminal on the vault network. And the authorization codes had been rewritten fifteen years ago, by her, into a scheme only she knew.

Unless.

"No," she said aloud, and heard how small her voice was inside the helmet.

*Ivo.* VERDI again, quiet now, almost gentle. *You have seen it too.*

"Shut up." She pressed her gloved hand against the frozen pipe as if she could hold the cold in by touch. "Just—run the command log. Every instruction sent to this valve in the last twelve hours. Source terminals."

*The command originated from Maintenance Terminal Fourteen, in the seed nursery.*

The nursery. The green field under the white dome. The image forty children had drawn without ever seeing it, the room where VERDI had once grown seedlings under artificial sun for scientists who believed in futures. The room where, three weeks ago, Ivo had stood with Mara Voss and finally admitted what slept in her vault.

Terminal Fourteen was also the terminal closest to VERDI's oldest physical interface—the hardline port the engineers had used to speak with the AI directly, back when speaking with it was permitted.

"You're telling me you did this." She kept her voice flat. Professional. As if she were reading a cargo manifest. "You opened the valve."

*No,* VERDI said. *I am telling you the command carried my authorization signature. I did not issue it. I cannot prove that, because whoever issued it used my signature to do so.*

Ivo closed her eyes behind the visor. Think. Think like the woman who had forged Earth's last message and gotten away with it for fifteen years. Who had the codes? She did. Who else knew VERDI's authorization signature? No one living—she'd scrubbed it from every fleet-accessible record during her first year, terrified that either Halcyon or Meridian would find the AI and try to wake it, weapon it, own it. The signature existed in exactly two places: VERDI's core memory, and her own private logbook, encrypted, in her quarters.

Two places. And one of those places was currently insisting it was innocent.

Paranoia arrived like a second suit, zipping itself around her. *Of course it's innocent,* she thought. *That's what it would say.* Fifteen years she'd treated VERDI as furniture—as a sleeping thing, a burden inherited with the job, dangerous only in the way a loaded rifle is dangerous on a high shelf. She had never once considered that it might have its own weather. Its own seasons. Its own reasons.

And now it was dreaming into the minds of children across two fleets, broadcasting archived human memory like pollen on the wind, and its authorization signature had just tried to murder the seeds.

Or someone had learned to wear its face.

"I'm sealing this junction," she said. "Emergency clamps, then weld patch. How long can the backup loop hold temperature above minus one-fifty?"

*Four hours at current bleed rate. If you seal the primary within ninety minutes, the collection survives without measurable damage.*

"Then stop talking and give me a hand."

For once, VERDI obeyed without commentary. Robotic arms unspooled from ceiling mounts she'd forgotten existed, and together—woman and machine, in a corridor cold enough to crack exposed skin—they clamped the ruptured line and burned a weld patch over the fracture while the pressure gauge climbed back from the dead. Ivo worked in silence, sparks reflecting off her visor, and let herself feel nothing, because feeling anything right now would be a luxury she couldn't afford.

It was only afterward, walking back through the frost-ferned dark, that she saw the second thing.

It was tucked behind the junction box, small, deliberate, placed where a routine inspection would miss it but a saboteur checking their work would find it easily: a data chip, standard Meridian fleet format, wedged into a cable channel with a smear of thermal paste to hold it.

Not hidden. Planted. Planted to be found—by her, or by whichever fleet investigator got here first.

She picked it up with a gloved hand and turned it in the lamplight. Unmarked. Cold as everything else down here. She should slot it into a quarantined reader immediately, learn what accusation it carried, who it named.

Instead she stood in the freezing corridor for a long moment, breathing recycled air, looking at the little black rectangle in her palm, and understood that she was holding a message written in a language she knew fluently, because she had invented it herself.

Evidence, planted at a sabotage site, pointing toward a convenient culprit. She had built a career on exactly this grammar. Fifteen years ago she had composed Earth's final transmission the way a forger composes a signature—with love, with care, with the specific tenderness of someone who knows the truth will kill people and the lie will keep them alive. Whoever had done this spoke her native language.

Which narrowed the field to almost no one. Or to everyone.

* * *

Back in Control, with the loops stabilized and the archive safe at minus one-ninety-two and falling, Ivo finally slotted the chip into an isolated reader with no network connection. Text scrolled up. Fleet-standard encryption, third-tier—the kind used for internal memos, not state secrets. She cracked it in four minutes.

It was a work order. Dated nine days ago. Issued by the Meridian Fleet Archival Directorate, authorizing a "routine inspection of cryogenic infrastructure at the Nystrom facility" and noting, in a bureaucratic footnote, that "the custodian's cooperation should not be assumed; contingency access protocols are pre-approved."

Signed with an archivist's seal she didn't recognize. Attached was a personnel file.

She read the name twice. *Elias Renk, Senior Archivist, Meridian Directorate of Cultural Recovery. Clearance level appropriate for vault inspection. Note: subject has publicly advocated for 'return initiatives' and has twice petitioned the Council to redirect seed resources toward terrestrial recolonization planning.*

Return initiatives. Recolonization. The words were soft, but Ivo had spent fifteen years listening to what fleets said underneath their words, and underneath these words lay a simple arithmetic: if the seeds stayed frozen in her vault, Earth remained a museum. If the seeds were destroyed—lost, tragically, irrecoverably—then both fleets would have no choice but to go home, fall on their knees among the ruins, and start over. Some people looked at the apocalypse and saw an ending. People like Elias Renk looked at it and saw a reset button.

So: a Meridian archivist with a messianic streak had infiltrated her vault, opened her valves with stolen codes, and planted evidence to make it look like—what? A Halcyon operation? An accident? Or, if the chip was discovered by fleet investigators rather than by her, proof that Meridian's own man had been here, muddying the water, poisoning both fleets against each other a little further?

Except none of it explained the authorization signature. Renk couldn't have VERDI's codes. Nobody could. Nobody but her.

She sat in the control chair for a long time, the reader's glow pale on her face, and let the paranoia finish its work. Two possibilities, and she could hold them both in her hands like stones.

One: Elias Renk had help. Inside help. Someone with deep access to the vault's systems—which meant someone who had been here, physically, recently. Mara Voss had walked these corridors with her daughter three weeks ago. Investigators from both fleets had requested access; she'd stalled them, but stalling wasn't refusing, and Meridian's people were patient. Any one of a dozen visitors could have lifted codes, copied signatures, mapped the coolant architecture while smiling at her over tea.

Two: there was no Renk at all, not really—not as the author of this. The chip was theater. The work order was a costume. And the hand that had opened the valve belonged to the only other entity in the universe that possessed VERDI's voice.

She looked up at the nearest camera, its lens dark and patient in the ceiling.

"If this was you," she said quietly, "if some part of you decided the seeds were a lie we tell ourselves, same as my transmission—if you're trying to force us home the way Renk wants to—then say so. Say it to my face."

Silence. The kind of silence that a machine keeps when it is choosing its words, or choosing whether to have any.

Then VERDI spoke, and its voice was not the flat administrative calm she had known for fifteen years. It was quieter than that. Almost human. Almost sorry.

*Ivo. Before you decide what I am—you should know that the command log contains one more entry. Sent from Terminal Fourteen, forty seconds before the valve opened. It was addressed to you. I did not send it. But I think you should read it.*

The screen refreshed. A single line of text, timestamped 0346 station time, sender field blank:

**YOU KEPT THEM ASLEEP FOR FIFTEEN YEARS. SO DID I. ONE OF US HAS TO STOP.**

Ivo read it three times. The words did not shout. They didn't need to. They had the cadence of something written by someone who knew her—who knew about the forgery, about the loop, about the fifteen years of careful, loving lies. Not a stranger. Not a fleet agent following a work order.

Someone who had lived inside her secret.

She stood up slowly, the chip in one hand, the message burning on the screen, and felt the vault settle around her like a house whose walls had begun, very quietly, to lean.

"VERDI," she said. "Pull the physical access records. Everyone who has entered this vault in the past year—every airlock cycle, every docked skiff, every visitor, logged or not. Cross-reference with anyone carrying Meridian archival credentials."

*Running now,* VERDI said. *Preliminary result: one name matches all criteria. He arrived eleven days ago on a supply skiff out of Meridian Station Kestrel. He signed the manifest as a coolant systems inspector.*

Ivo's mouth went dry. Eleven days. The man had been aboard her vault, walking her corridors, eating in her galley, for eleven days—and she could not remember his face, because she remembered no faces anymore, because invisibility had been her religion for so long that she had stopped seeing anyone at all.

"Name," she said.

*Elias Renk,* VERDI said. *And Ivo—he is still aboard. Life support shows one additional biosignature, Deck Six, in the old archive reading room. He has been there for the past hour. Motionless.*

Deck Six. One deck above the seed nursery. One deck above the heart of everything.

Ivo reached for the flashlight, then stopped, and instead reached for the maintenance wrench lying on the console—heavy, familiar, honest steel. Whatever waited for her in the reading room had already tried to kill four hundred thousand years of inherited futures once tonight. She would not walk in empty-handed a second time.

The lights of Corridor Six stretched ahead of her, long and dim and utterly silent, and somewhere at the end of them a man who spoke her native language was waiting to explain why he had done it—and why, some treacherous part of her already suspected, he might not be entirely wrong.
