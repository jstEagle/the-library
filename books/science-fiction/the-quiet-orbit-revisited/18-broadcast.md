## 18. Broadcast

The archive hummed under her hands like a sleeping animal.

Oscar stood in the relay's core chamber with Sefa's cracked datapad jacked into the main trunk, the black box's contents laid out in columns of light around her: timestamps, voiceprints, order authorizations, the whole architecture of a murder dressed up as logistics. Quintana's last hour played on a loop she couldn't stop watching—the locked cabin, Andrade's voice through the door, the second heartbeat that had turned out to be her own original's pulse hammering against a bulkhead mic.

Behind her, the relay shuddered.

"That's the third one," Brannoc said over comms from the docking ring. "They're cutting through the outer hatch. Thermal lances, by the sound."

"How long?"

"Minutes. Plural, but not many of them."

Sefa was still in the medbay two decks down, stitched and gray-faced, insisting over the channel that she could walk if someone would just let her walk. Ilo had refused. Ilo never refused anything, and hearing the refusal in his voice told Oscar exactly how bad the debris field had been, how close they'd come to not making it here at all.

And now the strike team was here too, because of course it was. The trap had always been open. The welcome had always been a noose.

She looked at the archive again. At the delete command sitting one line above the transmit command, both of them waiting, patient as loaded guns.

* * *

The choice had seemed abstract for three years. That was the thing nobody told you about decisions like this—they stay theoretical right up until the moment they aren't, and then there's no time to become the person who can make them.

Destroy it, and the truce holds. Eleven days left on the clock, and Adeyemi had spelled it out at the rendezvous: the hardliners needed a casus belli, but they didn't have one yet. The Quintana case was legally closed. The relay was a ghost story. If this archive burned, the war machine went back to grinding slowly, and maybe—maybe—cooler heads kept it grinding forever. Millions lived who would otherwise die. That was the arithmetic. She'd done it a hundred times in the transit, in the medbay watch hours, staring at Sefa's bandages.

Transmit it, and every engineered person in contested space stopped being property overnight. Every clone grown in a fleet crèche, every replacement built to tie off a loose end, every asset with a registry number instead of a name—they'd all have proof. Not hope. Proof. That the program existed, that it killed its originals, that it made people specifically so it could unmake them later. The hardliners would call it forgery. The truce would collapse anyway. But the collapse would happen in daylight, with the truth on every channel, and some fraction of the fleet—Adeyemi's fraction—would refuse to fire.

Millions might die. Thousands might finally be born.

There was no line between those numbers she could stand on. There never had been. That was why the fleet had wanted her destroyed or owned: not because she knew something, but because she *was* something—a walking argument that personhood could be manufactured, and manufactured things could be recalled.

The hull rang again, closer.

"Oscar." Brannoc's voice, steady the way he got steady only when things were worst. "Whatever you're going to do. Do it soon."

* * *

She thought about the manifest. Three years ago, a routine customs scan, and Vess Andrade's name sitting on her own cargo list like a hand reaching out of a grave. The ship that never flew. The bait that worked, because she'd come, because they'd known she'd come, because you cannot build a person out of someone else's memories without leaving them a homing instinct toward their own death.

She thought about Andrade in the core chamber below, the body grown around a conviction, asking her with genuine, terrible confusion: *Did I do it? Was it me?* He hadn't remembered killing anyone because he hadn't. Neither of them had. The murder had been performed by professionals and laundered through copies, and the copies were the evidence, and the evidence was supposed to be erased.

She thought about Ilo burying him in the debris field an hour ago, saying words over a man made in a vat, meaning every one of them.

She thought about her own name. *Oscar.* Not Quintana. A name she'd picked out of a dead registry herself, forged along with everything else—and somehow, over three years of hauling scrap and eating bad protein with these three idiots, it had stopped being forged. It had become hers the way nothing the fleet ever grew became theirs: by being lived in.

The strike team wasn't coming to destroy the archive. That was the part Adeyemi's warning had made clear. They were coming to destroy the *witnesses*. The archive they'd take, sanitize, reissue. Her, they'd burn with the relay and call it an equipment malfunction, and the other clone—the successful one, the one raised on purpose instead of by accident—would sign off on the report with Quintana's own handwriting, because that was what she'd been grown for. To be the eraser. To be the version of Oscar who survived by belonging to them.

The outer hatch gave way. Oscar heard it through the deck, a groan of metal surrendering.

* * *

"Brannoc," she said. "Get everyone to the ship. Now."

"We're not leaving you."

"You are. That's an order from the captain whose name isn't even real." She keyed the channel wide so Ilo and Sefa could hear. "I need you gone, because if this goes wrong, someone has to be alive to say what happened here. Not the fleet's version. Yours."

Silence on the channel. Then Sefa, hoarse: "You broadcast that thing, and every bounty board from Meridian to the Rim lights up with your face."

"I know."

"And you'll be alone in it."

"I've been alone in it for three years. You just made it livable." She swallowed. "Go."

A long pause. Then Brannoc: "We'll be in the ring. Engines hot. If you're not aboard in four minutes, we're coming in after you, and I don't care how many soldiers you've got between us."

That was the found family, right there—not the warmth, the stubbornness. She cut the channel before it could break her.

* * *

Boots in the corridor now. She could hear them through the relay's old bones, disciplined, unhurried. They knew the building was theirs. They knew she was a cornered animal, and cornered animals didn't get negotiated with.

Her finger hovered over the commands.

Delete. Transmit. Delete. Transmit.

Here was the thing she finally understood, standing in the light of her own origin story: there was no version of this where she stayed safe. Destroying the archive didn't save her—it just saved *them*, saved the truce, saved the machinery that had already scheduled her disposal. The quiet life was over the moment her face appeared on that manifest. The only question left was whether the next three years would be spent running from the truth or carrying it.

And if she carried it—if she put it on every channel in contested space—then somewhere in a fleet crèche, some other engineered person would hear it and know they weren't broken, weren't anomalies, weren't property. They'd know there was a name they could pick. An orbit they could choose.

It wasn't peace versus war. It was her survival versus everyone else's chance at one.

The door at the end of the core chamber opened.

* * *

She came in alone.

Of course she did. Strike teams breached; principals walked. Oscar's double crossed the chamber with Quintana's stride, Quintana's posture, wearing a fleet field jacket tailored to a body Oscar knew intimately because it was hanging off her own shoulders. Same face. Same hands. The difference was in the eyes, and it took Oscar a heartbeat to find it: no fear. None anywhere in her. Three years of hiding had carved fear into Oscar so deep it was structural. This woman had never hidden a day in her life. She'd never had to. She'd been the one they kept.

"Step away from the console," the clone said. Her voice was Quintana's voice, pitched calm. "This doesn't have to go the way they planned it. That's not a threat. It's an offer."

"There's only one way they planned it."

"They planned for you to resist. Resisting is fine. Resisting is expected." She stopped six meters away, close enough to talk, far enough to be shot at. "What they didn't plan for is me deciding I don't like the plan. So. Walk me through what's actually in that box, and maybe we both walk out."

"You read the case file. You know what's in it."

"I know what they told me is in it." Something flickered there, fast, gone. "I've spent my whole life being told what's in things."

Boots on the stairs behind her. Two soldiers in the doorway, weapons up, and Oscar saw the math change in her double's posture—a fractional stiffening, a soldier's awareness that the audience had arrived and the private conversation was over.

"Ma'am," one of them said. "Orders are to secure the archive and neutralize the asset."

"I heard the orders." She didn't turn around. "Give me a minute."

"Ma'am—"

"*A minute.*"

They held. And Oscar understood, with a clarity that felt almost like falling, that this was the hinge. Not the console, not the archive, not the fleet factions circling overhead. This. Whether the person the conspirators built to be their perfect instrument had enough of Quintana in her—enough of whatever Quintana had been before they started carving—to hesitate.

So Oscar did the only thing she could. She talked.

"You know what my first memory is?" she said, fingers resting on the console, not pressing anything. "Not mine. His. Quintana's. A kitchen on Ceres Station, real gravity, his daughter burning toast. I don't know if it's installed or inherited or invented, and I spent three years trying to figure it out, and I never will. That's what they did to us. They made sure we can't trust our own pasts." She watched her double's face. Nothing moved in it, but nothing moved in it *carefully*. "But you know what I figured out? It doesn't matter where the memory comes from. It matters what you do while you're having it."

"Save the speech."

"It's not a speech. It's the only thing I own." She lifted her chin toward the archive light. "In there is proof that the people who made us murdered the man we're copied from, and built replacements to clean up after, and scheduled both of us for disposal the moment we stopped being useful. You're the useful one. For now. Ask yourself what happens when the next loose end has your face."

"I know what my function is."

"Do you? Because I met Andrade. Downstairs, before we buried him. Grown around a conviction he didn't commit, kept alive as a living confession, and when I asked him about the murder he looked at me like a child asked to explain a crime from a dream." Her voice cracked and she let it. "That's the retirement plan. That's what 'function' means to them. You're not their successor. You're their spare part, and spares get used up."

The soldiers shifted. Somewhere above, Brannoc's engines cycled up—four minutes burning down.

"So here's your minute," Oscar said. "I'm going to press this. Everything goes out—every channel, every registry, every crèche file the fleet ever sealed. The truce dies. The war starts, maybe. Or maybe it doesn't, because half the fleet just found out they've been commanded by murderers, and Adeyemi's people refuse the order, and it fractures instead of ignites. I don't know which. Nobody knows which. That's the price, and I'm paying it, because the alternative is that every person like us stays a number forever." She looked at her own face across six meters of humming dark. "You can shoot me first. You'd be doing what you were grown for. Or you can stand there and let me do the one thing neither of us was ever allowed to do."

"Which is?"

"Choose."

* * *

Later—years later, in the telling—Oscar would try to describe the silence that followed, and fail. It lasted perhaps three seconds. It contained the hum of the archive, the boots of men waiting for an order, the distant thunder of Brannoc keeping a promise, and the entire weight of two lives built from the same stolen template, diverging in real time.

Her double looked at the console. At the transmit command, glowing, patient.

Then at her own hands, as if checking them for someone else's fingerprints.

"Ma'am," the soldier said again, tighter now. "The asset is attempting to access the archive. We are authorized to use lethal force."

"I know what you're authorized to do." Her double's voice had changed. Quieter. Stripped of the fleet polish down to something rawer, older, more like the voice in Oscar's inherited memories. "Stand down."

"Ma'am—"

"*Stand down.* That's not a request from your officer. It's a request from—" She stopped. Started again. "From someone who'd like to find out what her name is."

Oscar pressed transmit.

* * *

The archive went out like a dam breaking.

Every channel. Fleet command, fleet common, the civilian bands, the truce monitors, the scavver nets, the colony relays—Sefa's crack had widened the black box into a broadcast array, and the truth of Oscar Quintana's murder poured into contested space in raw, unsealed, undeniable form: the order authorizations, the voiceprints, the clone program manifests, the disposal schedules with her registry number on them and, three lines down, another number that belonged to the woman standing across the room.

The soldiers lunged. Her double moved first—not toward Oscar, but between them, one arm out, and whether it was tactics or something else, it bought the seconds that mattered. Oscar ran. Through the side passage Sefa had mapped, past the chamber where Andrade's empty cot still held the shape of a man the fleet had grown to be guilty, up the ringing stairwell while alarms she'd triggered bloomed red behind her.

The docking ring. Brannoc's face in the hatchway, furious and relieved in equal measure. Ilo hauling her inside by the collar. Sefa, upright against all medical sense, already shouting course corrections.

The ship tore free of the relay as the first breaching charges blew in their wake, and through the viewport Oscar saw the derelict fall away, burning now, its terrible welcome finally closed.

On the console beside her, the transmission log ticked upward. Delivered. Delivered. Delivered. Ten thousand channels. A million receivers. Somewhere out there, the truce was expiring into the loudest silence in history, and hardliners were reaching for firing solutions, and Adeyemi was—she had to believe—reaching for something else.

Beside her, uninvited, a new signal blinked onto the local band. One ship, detached from the strike team, matching their heading at a respectful distance.

Text only, routed through a scrubbed relay:

**They'll call it mutiny. Good. I never liked the uniform. Where are we going?**

Oscar stared at it. *We.*

She looked at her crew—her bleeding, stubborn, impossible crew—then at the open dark ahead, and typed back the only answer that had ever been true:

**Away. Then wherever we decide.**

The fleet fell astern. The truth flew ahead of them, faster than any ship, and there was no taking it back, and she had never felt lighter in her life.
