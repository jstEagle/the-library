## 5. Ilsa's Testimony

*They tell you falling is like flying. They are wrong. Falling is like being unmade by arithmetic.*

Ilsa Voss had kept a journal since she was six years old — paper, at first, in a house on a moon that no longer had a name anyone used, then encrypted files, then the shard itself, which was not designed for journals but for manifests and shipping ledgers and the boring, holy bookkeeping of ordinary life. She had always believed that records were a form of prayer. You wrote down what happened so that the universe could not pretend it hadn't.

She was thinking about this, or something adjacent to it, in the crawlspace behind the coolant lines of the *Kestrel's Debt*, where the fever had her and would not let go, and where the ship's engines hummed through the hull like a lullaby sung to someone else. She was thinking: *write it down. Write it down before the fever takes the words apart.*

So she did. Here is what she wrote, or said, or dreamed she said, into the recorder Gideon Joubert held steady in their scarred hands:

---

**Entry one. The morning of the nine seconds.**

Meridian Station, ring four, residential. Population four hundred and eleven. I know the number because I counted them every quarter-cycle for the census ledger. Four hundred and eleven names, four hundred and eleven air allotments, four hundred and eleven small lives I was paid almost nothing to keep track of.

I remember the morning because it was ordinary. That is the thing no one believes. There was no warning siren. No strange reading on any console. Mrs. Adeyemi in 4-C was arguing with her son about his homework — I could hear it through my door, the way sound carries in old stations, everything thin as paper between one life and the next. The boy's name was Tomas. He was twelve. He hated mathematics.

At 06:14 station time, gravity stopped.

Not failed. *Stopped.* Like a hand lifting off a drum. Everything that was resting became restless all at once. My tea rose out of its cup in a brown ribbon and hung there, turning slowly, and I remember thinking — this is the detail I keep returning to, the one that makes me feel insane — I remember thinking, *how beautiful,* before I understood what beauty costs.

Then the screaming started, and the beauty was over forever.

Four hundred and eleven people. Loose objects first — cups, tools, a child's toy drone, someone's dentures — then the people themselves, drifting up from beds and chairs and corridors, tumbling into each other in the long dark throat of the central axis. The station wasn't built for freefall. Nothing rotates anymore; rotation is expensive; the Concord says gravity plating is cheaper than spin, and we believed them because believing them was easier. So when the plating died, there was nothing to hold us but walls, and walls only help if you can reach them.

Old people drowned in their own saliva. I learned that in the first minute. Fluids pool in your lungs when there's no down for them to drain toward. Mr. Okafor from hydroponics suffocated forty meters from me and I could not reach him. I tried. I pushed off a bulkhead and went the wrong way and watched him turn blue-green in the emergency lighting, and his hands were open, reaching for a floor that wasn't there.

The children screamed longest. Then they stopped screaming, some of them, because they'd learned to hold their breath and float still, waiting for the grown-ups to fix it, and the grown-ups could not fix it, and the silence afterward was worse than the noise. Silence in freefall has a texture. It feels like being inside a held breath that isn't yours.

Nine seconds. That's what the instruments would say later, if there were later instruments. Nine seconds of dead gravity across the whole system, and then it came back like a slammed door, and everything that had been floating fell at once.

Everything fell at once.

I was in a doorway. Doorways are mercy in a gravity failure; you can wedge yourself into the frame and let the structure take the impact. I wedged myself in and I heard four hundred bodies hit decks and walls and each other, and the sound — I have no word for the sound. It was not one sound. It was four hundred small sounds happening simultaneously, and the human ear cannot separate them, so the mind does something worse: it makes them one sound, one enormous wet applause, and then it makes you understand that you are listening to your neighbors dying, all of them, in the time it takes to say their names.

Tomas Adeyemi fell from the third-level atrium. His mother caught part of him. Not enough of him.

I climbed out of my doorway into a corridor full of broken geometry. People folded into shapes people don't make. The tea was still on the ceiling somewhere, or had come down with everything else, spattered across the dead like a brown constellation. I found Mrs. Adeyemi holding what she could hold and making a sound I will hear until I die, which may be soon, and I knelt in the wreckage of my census district and I began, God forgive me, I began to count.

Because counting is what I do. Because someone had to write it down.

---

*The fever comes back here. The recorder catches her breathing for ninety seconds, and once, very quietly:* "Tomas. Tomas. Tomas."

---

**Entry two. The lights.**

They came at 07:02. Forty-one minutes after the fall. White lights, hard boots, the particular silence of soldiers moving through a space where everyone is either dead or too broken to run.

I want to be precise about who they were, because precision is the whole point of me. Gray armor, no insignia I recognized, Concord-pattern rifles set to wide dispersal. A woman with a slate and a voice like a filing cabinet told us — the fourteen of us still breathing, huddled in the ring-four commons among our neighbors — that we were being evacuated for our safety. She said the word *safety* three times. I counted that too. Counting again. It's how I pray.

They sorted us. That's the only honest word. They walked down the line of survivors and looked at us the way you look at cargo, deciding which holds are sealed and which are compromised. A medic checked pupils, asked questions. Can you stand. Do you know what happened. Did you see anything unusual before the event. *Unusual.* As if nine seconds of universal freefall were a weather pattern, a quirk, a thing the universe occasionally does and no one need take personally.

Four of us they tagged green. Ten they tagged amber. I never learned what amber meant. The ambers were walked toward the docking arm first, and the gray armor closed around them, and I heard — I will swear this on anything you ask me to swear on — I heard the airlock cycle twice after they went through, and no ship departed the arm until hours later, and when the manifest updated, ten names had been moved from *survivor* to *unaccounted.*

Unaccounted. As if the universe had misplaced them. As if they were socks.

The greens — me, a dockhand named Petrov, an engineer whose name I never got because he stopped answering on day two, and a girl of nineteen named Suri who kept asking about her brother in ring five — the greens were loaded onto a Concord transport under the pretext of medical evacuation. On the transport, a man without a rank sat across from me and explained, kindly, the way you explain things to the simple, that the station had suffered a catastrophic structural cascade. That there had been no gravity anomaly. That any records suggesting otherwise were sensor corruption. That the station's population had been — and here he paused, checking his slate, enjoying the word — *minimal.*

Minimal. Four hundred and eleven. Minimal.

He asked if I understood. I said yes. I understood perfectly. I understood that I was looking at the shape of the erasure, and that the shape had a face, and that the face was patient, and that patience is what power looks like when it isn't bothering to be afraid.

Suri cried quietly the whole flight. When we landed, they took her one way and me another, and I heard her ask about her brother one more time, and the answer was a hand on her shoulder guiding her through a door, and the door closed, and Suri's name went on my list too.

My list. I should explain my list.

I am an archivist. Third class, Meridian civil records. For eleven years I maintained the station's population manifest — births, deaths, transfers, the whole slow tide of us. When the soldiers came, when the white lights came, while they were sorting the living from the witnesses, I did the only thing I know how to do. I pulled the master manifest onto my personal shard and I copied it, all of it, four hundred and eleven entries with birthdates and blood types and next-of-kin and the little free-text notes the system allowed — *likes mathematics, hates it actually; sings in the shower; afraid of the dark, working on it* — and I compressed it and encrypted it and hid it in the checksum fields of a seed-stock shipping ledger, because nobody audits seed ledgers, nobody has ever audited a seed ledger in the history of agriculture.

Then I put the shard in my boot, and I smiled at the man without a rank, and I told him I felt much better now.

They kept me for six days in a place with no windows and excellent food. On the seventh day they decided I was compliant, forgettable, and low-value, and they shipped me out on a cargo contract to a station that officially does not exist. People are like that, the powerful. They mistake quietness for emptiness. They look at a small middle-aged archivist with dust-colored hair and they see furniture.

Furniture keeps lists.

---

*Here the fever bends the record. The next passage is spoken half-asleep, and Gideon Joubert, holding the recorder, does not move for the entirety of it.*

---

**Entry three. What I need you to carry.**

Listen. Whoever is listening. The engineer with the kind hands, maybe, or the pilot with the angry eyes, or the captain — the captain who signed nothing, I heard about that through the ductwork, sound carries on ships the way it carried on the station, everything thin as paper between one life and the next.

Listen. The shard is not just a manifest. I know what Sefa found when she cracked the outer shell — coordinates, yes, a place, an old vault, pre-Concord, a network they never touched because it grew before they did. Go there. But understand what you're carrying. Understand that it isn't data.

It's Tomas Adeyemi, age twelve, who hated mathematics and was starting, secretly, to love it, and his mother knew, and his mother is entry two-eighty-eight and she died holding him and the record shows cause of death: structural collapse, and the record is a lie, and the lie is the weapon, the lie is the second weapon, the one they'll use again —

It's Mr. Okafor, who grew tomatoes in hydroponics and gave them away free to anyone with children, who suffocated forty meters from me reaching for a floor that wasn't there —

It's Suri, nineteen, who asked about her brother until a door closed —

Four hundred and eleven entries. I counted them onto that shard the way you count beads on a string, and every bead is a person the Concord has already deleted, and they will delete more, they are going to do it again, bigger, I saw the schedules, I copied what I could see before they wiped the terminals, there's a date, there's a second test, it's written in the deep fields of the shard where only an archivist would think to hide a calendar —

And I am dying. I know the math of my own body; I've done the census; the numbers don't reconcile. So I need the promise. I need it more than morphine, more than water, more than the fever breaking.

Promise me you won't bury my testimony with me. Everyone buries testimony with the dead — that's the whole trick, that's how erasure works, grief gets folded in with the body and lowered into the ground and covered over, and everyone agrees to grieve quietly, and quiet is what they want, quiet is the protocol —

Promise me the shard goes on. Promise me the names get read out loud somewhere, someday, by someone who never met them, so that four hundred and eleven people were *here*, were real, drank tea and argued about homework and grew tomatoes and were murdered in nine seconds by a weapon their empire tested on them like they were ballistics gel.

Promise me, Captain. Gideon. Whatever you call yourself.

Swear it on the seeds. You haul seeds; I've seen the crates; I know what you're really doing out here, hauling futures around a dead system. Swear on the futures, then. Swear that mine doesn't end in a crawlspace with a corrupted shard and a fever and no witness.

Say it. Say it out loud so the recorder catches it. Records are a form of prayer, and I need one more prayer answered before —

---

*The recording ends there. In the medbay alcove, Ilsa Voss sleeps with her hands curled around the shard as if it were warm, though it is room temperature, though it has always been room temperature. Gideon Joubert sits with the recorder in their lap for a long time, replaying the last thirty seconds, and then plays them again.*

*In the galley, Sefa has the shard's deep fields open on a workbench screen, and she has gone very still, and she calls Oyelaran's name once, quietly, the way you call someone when the floor has just become a different kind of floor.*

*"You need to see the date," she says. "You need to see how close it is."*
