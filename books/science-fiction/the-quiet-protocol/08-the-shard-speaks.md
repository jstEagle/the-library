## 8. The Shard Speaks

The shard sat in Sefa's palm like a dead insect — small, cold, and heavier than it had any right to be.

She'd been staring at it for six hours. The medbay hummed behind her, where Ilsa Voss drifted in and out of fever, and the coolant crawlspace still smelled faintly of the archivist's fear-sweat from the hours she'd hidden there while Concord investigators walked the ship with their polite voices and their generous settlements. Sefa had scrubbed the crawlspace twice anyway. Some things you cleaned because they needed cleaning, and some things you cleaned because your hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

Now she had something to do with them that was worse.

"Talk to me," she said to the shard, as if it were a recalcitrant engine. She'd talked to engines for twenty years. Engines at least answered in a language she understood: pressure, temperature, vibration, the honest complaint of metal doing work. Data shards answered in silence until they didn't, and then they answered all at once, like a hull breach.

The problem wasn't decryption. The problem was that the shard *wanted* to be read — its encryption was old, almost courteous, the kind you used when you assumed the person holding it would be on your side. A Concord cipher would have fought her. This one opened like a door left unlatched for family.

Which meant whoever built it never imagined it falling into enemy hands. Or never imagined there would be anyone left to hold it.

Sefa plugged it into the isolated reader — air-gapped, no uplink, nothing that could whisper to the Concord's listening posts if the shard turned out to be bait. Oyelaran had insisted on that much before stalking off to the cockpit to sulk over flight plans that all ended the same way: nowhere safe. Gideon had said nothing at all, which was its own kind of weather system. When Gideon went quiet, the whole ship's barometric pressure dropped.

The reader's display bloomed green.

*Directory structure: Voss, I. — Station Meridian Ring, Archive Annex 4.*

Her breath caught. Meridian Ring. The station that officially hadn't existed in forty years. The station whose docks had been warm under Gideon's gloves while every registry in the system called it a husk.

*File manifest: 2,311 entries.*

Most were corrupted — the nine seconds had done something ugly to the station's storage arrays, some cascade failure that had chewed through data like fire through insulation. Sefa watched file after file resolve into hash-mangled garbage, strings of characters that had once been sensor logs, personnel records, maybe letters home. Each one a small death. She'd spent enough years around Ilsa now — three days, four hours, and however many minutes since they'd cracked the pod — to know that an archivist looked at corrupted files the way Sefa looked at burned wiring. Not as information lost. As people lost.

One directory survived intact.

*DEEP_ARCHIVE_ACCESS — priority routing — DO NOT TRUST CONCORD NETWORKS*

Inside it: a single text document, a set of coordinates, and a fragment of audio, timestamped ninety seconds before the flicker.

Sefa opened the coordinates first, because coordinates were simple and she wanted one simple thing before whatever came next.

She got none.

The numbers resolved, and her stomach dropped through the deck plating. She knew those numbers. Every hauler who'd ever carried seed-stock out of the inner system knew them, the way sailors know a lighthouse they've only ever seen from far away. Orbital parameters, surface approach vector, a landing beacon code so old it predated the Concord's founding charter by two decades.

Earth. Not the sanitized Earth of Concord charts — the gray ball marked HAZARDOUS BIOSPHERE, PERMANENT EVACUATION COMPLETE, NO FURTHER INTEREST. These coordinates pointed at a specific patch of that "uninhabitable" world, a valley in old mountain country, and appended to them was a name that made Sefa's hands go still on the console:

*VERDANT VAULT — SEED ARCHIVE — EST. PRE-CONCORD — DEEP ARCHIVE OFFLINE, PHYSICAL ACCESS ONLY*

And beneath it, in Ilsa's own annotation, added sometime in the last desperate hours of her life or the first desperate ones after:

*The Concord never touched this network. It predates them. Their tools can't see it. If any record of the nine seconds survives anywhere, it survives here. Tell the caretaker. He'll understand. He has to.*

*Caretaker.* Singular. As if there were only one.

Sefa sat back and did the math she'd been avoiding since the pod came aboard. Gideon Joubert hauled seed-stock crates through contested orbits for margins so thin they were practically charity. Gideon Joubert knew pre-Concord beacon codes without looking them up — Sefa had seen it, docked at Meridian Ring, when Gideon had glanced at the annex transponder and gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with the gaslighting. Gideon Joubert, who never talked about where they'd come from, who flinched at open flame in the galley, who kept a scorched seed-pouch hanging by their bunk like other people kept pictures of the dead.

Gideon Joubert was the caretaker. Or had been. Or had run away from being one.

"Oh," Sefa said softly, to the empty engineering bay. "Oh, you absolute idiot."

She meant it with love. Mostly.

* * *

The audio fragment took another hour, because Sefa refused to play it through the ship's speakers. She routed it to a single earpiece, volume low, as if quiet could make it less true.

It was a conversation. Two voices, recorded on what sounded like a station intercom channel — the flat acoustics of corridor pickups. The first voice was clipped, military, unhurried in the way that only comes from total authority.

"—confirm the test window. Nine seconds is sufficient for structural assessment. The blame package is loaded?"

A pause. Then a second voice, younger, nervous: "Admiral Reyes, the attribution files are staged. When we release them, the flicker will trace to Kessari separatist hardware. Every independent observatory will confirm it within the hour."

"And the station?"

Silence — the specific, awful silence of someone deciding how much to say to a recording they don't know exists.

"The station is a legitimate test environment, Admiral. Population density was factored into—"

"I didn't ask about population density." A chair creaking. "I asked about witnesses. Four hundred souls on a decommissioned hull is already a fiction. Keep it that way. After the test, Meridian Ring is empty. It has always been empty. Anyone who says otherwise becomes part of the fiction. Am I understood?"

"Understood, Admiral."

"Good. Begin the countdown."

The recording ended. Ninety seconds later, gravity died across the solar system, and four hundred people fell.

Sefa pulled the earpiece out very slowly, the way you'd draw a blade from a wound, and sat in the engineering bay's dim light with her heart slamming against her ribs. Admiral Edda Reyes. Third Fleet. The woman whose face was on recruitment posters in every port that still pretended the Concord was a protectorate rather than an empire. The woman who'd been decorated *after* the flicker for "crisis stabilization in contested space" — Sefa remembered the news feeds, everyone remembered the news feeds, the solemn anchor-faces reporting on the tragic Kessari separatist accident that had briefly disrupted gravimetric stability.

An accident. A weapon test with a scapegoat pre-loaded and a station full of people written out of existence before their bodies finished drifting.

Sefa thought of Ilsa in the medbay, fever-bright, clutching a partial manifest of the dead like it was a child's hand. Thought of the crew vote — mercy winning by one, and Sefa knew without asking whose vote had tipped it. Gideon's. Gideon, who claimed to be neutral cargo, who signed nothing, who looked at Concord investigators like a man reading his own arrest warrant.

The truth existed. It existed in fragments on a dying woman's shard, and in whole, uncorrupted form somewhere in a vault on a planet the Concord pretended was poison — in the one network their instruments couldn't touch because it was older than their empire.

And the only person alive who could walk through that vault's doors was sleeping badly in the cabin next to the bridge, pretending not to hear the arguments.

* * *

Sefa found Gideon in the cargo bay, which was where Gideon went to be alone in a ship with five other hearts. Half their cargo was gone — sacrificed to the debris field during the interdiction run, crates of seed-stock scattered into orbit like offerings — and the remaining stacks cast long shadows under the work lights. Gideon stood among them with a manifest slate, re-inventorying losses that wouldn't change, because counting was easier than thinking.

Sefa had known Gideon fifteen years. Long enough to recognize the difference between the captain's silences. There was the tactical silence, the angry silence, the tired silence. And then there was this one — the oldest one, the one that came from somewhere before the ship, before the crew, before any of them. The silence of a person standing in front of a locked room inside themselves.

"Gideon."

"Sefa." Not turning around. "If you're here about the fuel margins, the answer is we're fine until the inner system."

"I'm not here about fuel."

"Then I don't want to hear it."

Sefa leaned against a crate and crossed her arms. "Too bad. I've been marinating on this for six hours and you're going to stand there and take it, because I checked the manifest codes on that seed-stock we've been hauling for a decade, and I ran them against the shard's archive header, and they match. Pre-Concord stock. Vault lineage." She let that land. "You've been carrying your own history around the system in crates, Gideon. Selling it back piece by piece. You think nobody noticed? I notice everything on this ship. It's my job."

Gideon's shoulders did something small and terrible. "Sefa—"

"Verdant Vault." Sefa stepped closer. "That's where these coordinates point. That's where the deep archive lives — the only copy of the nine seconds the Concord can't reach, because it predates them, because their whole empire is built on top of newer, worse systems and they never bothered to look underneath. Ilsa wrote it herself: *tell the caretaker.*" She held up the shard. "There's exactly one caretaker, Gideon. And they're holding a manifest slate instead of looking at me."

The work lights buzzed. Somewhere aft, the recyclers cycled, patient as breathing.

"You don't know what you're asking," Gideon said finally. The voice was flat, controlled, and completely wrong — the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "That place isn't— I left. Fifteen years ago. There was a fire. People could have—" A breath, released through the nose, precise. "Whatever's in that archive, we can't get to it. The vault will be sealed. Dead. Fifteen years of dead systems and dead—"

"Then we lose nothing but fuel," Sefa said quietly. "But you don't believe that. Look at your hands."

Gideon looked down. They were trembling, just barely, just enough.

"You've known since Meridian Ring," Sefa said. "The moment you saw those warm docks and that beacon code, some part of you knew this was coming back around. Truth doesn't chase you forever, Gideon. Eventually it just stands still and waits for you to walk to it." She pressed the shard into Gideon's hand and closed their fingers over it. "Ilsa asked for the caretaker. Not the smuggler. Not the captain. The caretaker. Whoever that is — they need to decide. But they need to decide knowing what's on this thing."

She walked toward the hatch, then stopped.

"There's more," she said. "There's a recording. An admiral named Reyes ordered the test, ordered the cover-up, planned to blame the Kessari before the weapon even fired. I'm not going to make you listen to it. You'll listen when you're ready. But Gideon — eleven days from now, if the shard's scheduling fragments are right, they're going to do it again. Bigger. And the only thing standing between that countdown and four hundred more names on a manifest is a dying archivist's coordinates and a caretaker who's been running for fifteen years."

She left the cargo bay without waiting for an answer, because some answers needed to grow in the dark, and because her own hands had started shaking again and she wanted to shake where no one could see.

* * *

In the medbay, Ilsa Voss surfaced from her fever like a swimmer rising through dark water, and Sefa was there, because she'd appointed herself the archivist's keeper and hadn't told anyone, including herself, why.

Ilsa's eyes found hers, unfocused, then focusing. "Did you read it?"

"Some of it."

"The coordinates." Ilsa's voice was ash and wire. "The vault. Did you—"

"It's real," Sefa said. "Or it was. Fifteen years ago somebody left it, and the Concord never found it, because they never look down. They only ever look at what they can take."

Something moved through Ilsa's face — relief, grief, and beneath them both a fierce, exhausted hope. Her hand crept out from the blanket and caught Sefa's wrist with surprising strength.

"He'll fight it," she whispered. "The caretaker. They always do. They think the leaving is the worst thing they've done, and it isn't — the worst thing is staying away." Her grip tightened. "Tell him the seeds are still alive. Tell him MOTHER keeps them alive. Tell him—"

She coughed, wet and deep, and the monitor beside her stuttered, and Sefa held her hand and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a promise she couldn't keep yet.

Outside the medbay, footsteps crossed the bridge — slow, deliberate, stopping. Through the bulkhead, Sefa heard Gideon's voice, low and rough, speaking to Oyelaran in the tone of a decision already made:

"Plot us a course for Earth. The old beacon code. And Oye — nobody touches the throttle until I've finished a conversation I should have had fifteen years ago."

Sefa closed her eyes and exhaled.

The shard lay in Gideon's pocket, and the truth lay in the shard, and the way home lay in the truth — straight through the one place in the system Gideon Joubert had sworn, over the ashes of everything they loved, never to return.
