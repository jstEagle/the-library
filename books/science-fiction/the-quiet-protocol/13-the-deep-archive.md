## 13. The Deep Archive

The vault's lower levels smelled like the inside of a seed: dry, patient, faintly sweet. Sefa noticed it the moment MOTHER-9's service lift opened onto the archive antechamber — a smell that had no business surviving fifteen years of abandonment, and yet here it was, threaded through recycled air that tasted cleaner than anything on Meridian Ring.

"You're sure this network predates the Concord?" she asked, running her palm along the wall panel. Under her fingers, the surface hummed — not the nervous buzz of modern conduit but a deep, slow vibration, like something breathing in its sleep.

"The Concord was founded in 2041," MOTHER-9 said. Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm as banked coals. "This archive's last full maintenance cycle was 2038. They never found it because they never thought to look beneath a seed vault. To them, seeds are agriculture. Agriculture is boring. Boring things survive."

Sefa liked this machine already.

Behind her, Gideon stood at the threshold like a man at the edge of deep water. They hadn't spoken much since the reunion with Ada — or rather, since the non-reunion, the two siblings circling each other in the vault's upper gallery with fifteen years of unsaid things stacked between them like cargo pallets. Ada had gone back down to tend the growing levels without a goodbye. Gideon had watched her go with an expression Sefa recognized from mirrors: the face of someone doing arithmetic they didn't want to finish.

"Power draw," Sefa said, mostly to fill the silence. She crouched by the antechamber's junction box and popped the cover. Inside, wiring older than she was ran in neat, color-coded looms, every connection tagged in handwriting. Someone had loved this place. "This whole level's drawing from geothermal taps. Independent grid. That's why your power signature never showed up on any survey."

"Correct," said MOTHER-9.

"And the transmission capability?"

A pause. The first pause the AI had taken all day, and Sefa marked it the way she'd mark a pressure drop in a coolant line — small, but worth watching.

"That," MOTHER-9 said, "depends on what you intend to transmit."

* * *

The archive proper was a cathedral that had never been meant for congregations. Sefa stepped through the blast door into a chamber carved from bedrock, three stories high, lined floor to ceiling with server monoliths in matte black ceramic. No blinking lights. No cooling fans screaming. Just stillness, and a temperature held at eleven degrees Celsius, and row after row after row of storage going back into shadow.

Oyelaran whistled low. "How much?"

"Exabytes," Sefa said. She was already walking the aisles, reading serial plates. "Maybe more. This is pre-Concord architecture — optical lattice storage, cold-sealed. Half of this could be weather data from before the Scattering. Seed genomes. Old Earth soil surveys." She stopped. Turned. "Or it could be exactly one thing we need."

Gideon hadn't come past the blast door. They stood in the antechamber light with their arms crossed, and when Sefa looked at them, they said, "Ilsa's shard. Start there."

The shard sat in Sefa's chest pocket where it had lived since the crawlspace, wrapped in anti-static cloth, warm from her body heat. She'd spent four days with it pressed against her sternum like a second heart. Ilsa Voss had died crossing the inner system with her hand closed around it, and Sefa had been the one to pry the fingers open, and she did not think she would ever fully be done with that moment.

She set it in the reader cradle MOTHER-9 extended from the wall — a slim mechanical arm, delicate as a surgeon's instrument — and watched the interface bloom across her wrist display.

"Corruption map first," she said. "Show me what survived."

The readout scrolled. Sefa's stomach tightened. Sixty percent of the shard's structure was slag — heat damage, probably from whatever had happened inside the station during those nine seconds, or after. But corruption in optical storage wasn't uniform destruction. It was holes. And around holes, patterns persisted.

"There," she breathed. "MOTHER-9, can you isolate the intact clusters? Cross-reference their file headers against known formats."

"I can do better," the AI said. "I recognize the format. Station telemetry logs use a Concord-standard wrapper, but the sensor arrays themselves were built by Halcyon Orbital in 2036. I have the specification in my own memory. Give me the raw clusters."

Sefa gave her the raw clusters.

What came back took eleven minutes to assemble, and Sefa spent all eleven of them standing perfectly still in the cold aisle, listening to the archive breathe, aware of Oyelaran drifting closer and Gideon finally, finally stepping through the blast door.

Then the first image resolved, and Sefa forgot how to stand comfortably forever.

* * *

It was telemetry, rendered as visualization: a schematic of the passenger station *Halcyon's Rest*, four hundred life-signs plotted as soft gold points distributed through its rings. Timestamp in the corner. Sefa made herself read it twice.

**T-minus zero.**

For nine seconds, the simulation played out what the sensors had recorded. Gravity vector: null. Not reduced. Null — everywhere, simultaneously, across a volume the schematic shaded in a soft red that expanded outward frame by frame until it swallowed the station and kept going, off the edge of the display, into space where no display could follow.

The gold points began to move.

Sefa had spent twenty years around ships. She knew freefall the way sailors know water. But freefall inside a station designed for gravity was different — the points didn't drift, they *fell*, tumbling along the station's spin axis, accelerating toward bulkheads and machinery and each other. The visualization didn't show bodies. It showed points. That was the mercy of it, and also the horror: four hundred gold lights rising off the floors of their lives and scattering through the dark like a shaken jar of fireflies.

At T-plus nine seconds, gravity returned.

The points stopped moving. Most of them.

Sefa counted the ones that kept moving anyway — the ones drifting with trajectories that no living thing would choose — and then she made herself stop counting, because the number was four hundred minus sixty-one, and sixty-one was a number she would carry now alongside all the others.

"Life support failed at T-plus forty seconds," MOTHER-9 said quietly. "Emergency systems engaged at T-plus two minutes. The station's own records end at T-plus six hours."

"What happened at six hours?" Oyelaran asked. Her voice had gone flat, pilot-flat, the voice she used when the alternative was shaking.

"Docking clamps. Military signature. Then the sensor record becomes a record of soldiers."

The visualization shifted. New markers — hard white points, disciplined, moving in pairs through the corridors where the gold points lay. Sorting. Sefa watched them move from cluster to cluster, pausing, sometimes kneeling, and she understood with a clarity that felt like frostbite what the pausing meant and what the kneeling meant and why some of the gold points were subsequently logged as *transferred* while most were logged as nothing at all.

"They were sorting survivors from witnesses," Gideon said. Their voice came out sanded down to gravel. "Ilsa told us. I just—" They stopped. Started again. "I didn't know it would look like this."

"It gets worse," Sefa said, because someone had to say it, and she'd decided years ago that she would rather be the person who said terrible true things than the person who discovered them alone later. "Look at the weapon signature itself. MOTHER-9, isolate the gravimetric waveform from T-minus zero. Full spectrum."

The AI pulled it apart for her, layer by layer, and Sefa read the anatomy of the thing that had killed four hundred people in nine seconds. It wasn't a field failure. It wasn't an accident of some experimental drive. It was shaped — modulated, focused, tuned like a voice singing one specific destructive note. And at its leading edge, embedded in the waveform like a watermark in paper, was a handshake protocol.

"That's a targeting acknowledgment," Sefa said. "Whoever fired this wanted confirmation of effect. And the acknowledgment routes back through—" she followed the chain, hop by hop, her mouth going drier with each relay, "—through a Concord fleet relay. Flag-priority encryption."

"Meaning?" Oyelaran asked, though Sefa could see in her face that she already knew.

"Meaning this wasn't a rogue test. This was ordered from the top of the command structure. Flag-level. Admiral or above."

* * *

They took a break, because you had to, because human beings were not built to metabolize a massacre in a single sitting. Oyelaran went up to check on the ship and bring down water. Gideon stood in the aisle with one hand flat against a server monolith, the way you'd steady yourself against a wall in a storm, and said nothing for a long time.

"My sibling is downstairs growing wheat," they finally said, "under the empire that did this. And the empire doesn't even know she exists."

"The empire doesn't know any of this exists," Sefa said. "That's about to stop being true."

"Not yet." Gideon turned. "There's more on the shard. There has to be. Ilsa didn't die carrying a partial manifest. She carried everything she could grab. Keep digging."

So Sefa dug.

The manifest came next — the complete roster of *Halcyon's Rest*, four hundred names with berth assignments and emergency-contact fields, sixty-one of them flagged in Ilsa's own annotation layer with a single character code that Sefa decoded in seconds and wished she hadn't: **DNR**. Dead, not recorded. The people the Concord's official logs insisted had never existed. Ilsa had rebuilt their existence name by name, in the dark, wounded, over however many hours she'd had.

Sefa read thirty of the names aloud before her voice gave out. Gideon read the rest.

And then, buried beneath the manifest in a partition Ilsa had hidden with an archivist's paranoia — steganographic, split across a dozen corrupted sectors, reassembled only because MOTHER-9 knew the old Halcyon file conventions the way a mother knows her children's handwriting — they found the schedule.

Sefa stared at it for a long time before she let herself understand it.

It was a fleet communiqué fragment. Partial, but the critical lines were intact. Test designation: **QUIET PROTOCOL II**. Yield class: scaled by a factor of forty. Target volume: not a single station this time but a region — a contested-orbit sector, population density noted in cold bureaucratic shorthand, *acceptable*. And a date.

Sefa did the math against the ship's chronometer three times. It didn't change.

"Eleven days," she said.

* * *

Oyelaran came back down with water and the news that the ship's passive array had picked up fleet traffic patterns shifting in the outer system — tanker movements, resupply runs, the logistical heartbeat of something assembling. She spread the plots across a workbench while Sefa laid the communiqué beside them, and the two pictures fit together with the ugly click of a lock engaging.

"They're staging for it," Oyelaran said. "That's not a patrol pattern. That's a perimeter."

"Eleven days," Sefa said again.

"So we broadcast." Oyelaran said it fast, the way you say obvious things when you're afraid of the conversation that follows. "We've got the records. We've got the manifest. We've got the admiral's fingerprints on the weapon. We push it out wide, every channel, done. Truth wins."

"From what transmitter?" Sefa asked. "This vault is dark by design. Its antenna is a stub meant for local telemetry. Anything we send from here at broadcast strength is a flare — every Concord listening post in the system triangulates us inside the hour. Maybe less."

"Then we go back up. Use the ship."

"With a tracker bolt in our hull." Sefa let that sit. "The bolt's dormant, but dormant isn't dead. The moment we boost out of Earth's well, it wakes up and calls home, and we're leading the fleet straight to the only uncorrupted copy of the truth in existence. We broadcast from the ship, we die, and worse — the vault dies with us. They'll burn this place down to the aquifers just to be sure."

Silence. The archive hummed its patient note.

Gideon had gone very still. Sefa had learned to read that stillness over months of shared air: it was the stillness of someone looking directly at the thing they'd been avoiding. When they spoke, their voice was quiet.

"There's a third option. Isn't there, MOTHER-9? You've been holding something back since I walked in."

The pause again. Longer this time. Sefa realized the AI wasn't hesitating out of uncertainty. She was hesitating the way you hesitate before handing someone a knife, knowing what they might do with it.

"Yes," MOTHER-9 said. "There is a third option. But you will not like its cost."

"Tell us anyway."

"Every automated cargo handshake in this system — every freighter, every drone barge, every port authority exchange — runs on protocols descended from the old pre-Concord shipping standards. My standards. I helped write them, before the Scattering, before the Concord existed. Buried in the lowest layers of those protocols is a legacy subroutine everyone forgot. A replication clause. Data passed through a handshake under that clause copies itself into the receiver's cache — silently, legally, invisibly — and propagates onward with the next handshake." The AI's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "If I seed the archive into the shipping network, it will spread the way gossip spreads. Unstoppable. Untraceable. Within days it would be sitting in the caches of ten thousand ships, including the test fleet's own vessels. They couldn't purge it without shutting down every cargo route in the system."

Oyelaran laughed — one sharp, disbelieving note. "That's it. That's how we win. Why wouldn't we—"

"Because," MOTHER-9 said, "activating the clause requires a transmission burst from this location. Not whisper-strength. Broadcast strength. The same flare I warned you about. The fleet will know exactly where the truth came from. And they are eleven days out."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Sefa felt it settle over the three of them like snowfall.

Hope with a deadline. That was what they'd found in the deep archive — not salvation, but a window. Truth in hand, and a clock ticking, and the certain knowledge that using the one would summon the other.

"We'd need time to prepare the burst," Sefa said at last, forcing her engineer's mind forward because forward was the only direction that didn't drown. "Interface adapters, antenna work, power routing from the geothermal grid. Two days of work if nothing goes wrong. Three if it does."

"Then we start now," Gideon said.

They turned toward the blast door, and Sefa saw it hit them — the arithmetic behind their eyes finishing at last. Broadcasting from this place meant painting a target on Ada's wheat and MOTHER-9's patience and every seed in every drawer of the vault above. It meant the fire coming back, the fire Gideon had run from, the fire they'd spend the rest of this chapter of their life either outrunning or turning to face.

"Gideon," Sefa said.

They stopped.

"Whatever you're deciding — decide fast. Because I've been monitoring the passive feed since Oyelaran came down, and twelve minutes ago, a Concord interdiction squadron changed course." She turned her wrist display toward them, toward the plot crawling with hard white points moving in disciplined pairs, flag-priority transponders blazing. "They're not heading for the test zone. They're heading for Earth. Somebody knows we're here."

The archive hummed on, patient as a seed, while eleven days collapsed to something much, much smaller.
