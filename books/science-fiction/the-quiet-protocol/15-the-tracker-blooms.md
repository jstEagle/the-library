## 15. The Tracker Blooms

Oyelaran felt the bolt wake before any instrument told her it had.

She was in the *Kestrel's* cockpit running a pre-flight that had become ritual — coolant pressure, thruster gimbal sweep, the long slow prayer of a pilot who no longer believed in luck but kept talking to it anyway — when the hair on her forearms lifted. Not static. Something older than static. The particular hum a ship makes when a piece of itself stops being yours.

The tracker bolt sat in the portside hull plating where it had lodged during the debris run, a sliver of Concord alloy no bigger than her thumb. Sefa had mapped it, isolated it, wrapped its power feed in enough shielding to choke a reactor. For six days it had been dead weight. Now, through the deck plates, through her boots, through the soles of her feet and up into her teeth, Oyelaran felt it begin to sing.

"Everyone up," she said on the comm, calm as ballast. "It's blooming."

* * *

They gathered in the cargo bay around the hull section where the bolt lived, and for a long moment nobody spoke, because there is a specific silence that falls over people watching a countdown they can't read.

Sefa had the diagnostic rig open, her face lit amber by the waveform. "It's not transmitting yet," she said. "It's charging. Building a burst strong enough to punch through the vault's rock canopy and reach relay satellites we're not supposed to know exist." She looked up. "When it fires, every Concord asset in the inner system gets our coordinates to within a meter."

"How long?" Gideon asked.

"At current draw? Four hours. Maybe five if I keep bleeding power off it, but every amp I steal shortens the archive upload window. It's a trade." Her jaw worked. "I hate trades like this."

Ada stood apart from them, arms crossed, lit by the grow-racks' green glow behind her. She hadn't spoken since the alarm. Now she said, quietly, "How long does the archive need?"

"Full replication into MOTHER-9's distributed nodes? Eleven hours minimum. Sixteen to be safe."

"Then we have four," Ada said. "We make eleven behave like four."

Oyelaran watched Gideon take that in — watched the flinch move through them like weather, there and gone. Fifteen years of guilt, and now the vault was going to burn again because Oyelaran had threaded a debris field instead of dying in it. She'd made her peace with that trade somewhere over the Meridian shoals. She wasn't sure Gideon ever would.

"Options," Gideon said. "Go."

"We pull the bolt and run," Oyelaran said, because someone had to say the coward's option out loud so everyone could watch it die. "We can't outrun a strike team with half our cargo gone, but we might outrun their *attention*. Draw them away from the vault entirely, let the upload finish quiet—"

"They'll torch the vault whether we're here or not," Ada said. "You heard the shard. Witnesses are liabilities. The vault has witnesses. It has *MOTHER-9*."

"It has you," Oyelaran said, and hated how her voice cracked on it.

Ada looked at her then, and there was something in her face Oyelaran had never seen in fifteen years of knowing Gideon and three days of knowing Ada: not hardness. Arithmetic. "I've spent fifteen years deciding what this place is worth," Ada said. "I'm not going to stop now because the answer got expensive."

Gideon closed their eyes. Opened them. When they spoke, it was the voice Oyelaran had heard exactly twice before — once over Meridian Ring, once when they'd voted mercy for Ilsa. The voice they used when the decision had already been made and the talking was just courtesy.

"We split," Gideon said. "Sefa, Ada — you fortify the deep archive. MOTHER-9 gives you everything she's got; you get the upload past the point of no return. Oye — preflight, full systems, engines hot but cold-plugged. You're our door out. And I'm going to be very loud and very findable in the flooded sublevels, so when the strike team lands, they come looking for me first."

"No," Oyelaran said.

"Gideon—" Sefa started.

"That's not a plan, that's an obituary with extra steps," Oyelaran said. "You want to play bait in a flooded tunnel system under a Concord strike team with—"

"With the only person alive who knows those tunnels," Gideon said. "I grew up in them, Oye. I hid in them when I was nine years old playing hide-and-seek with my sister. There are passages down there MOTHER-9 sealed off that aren't on any survey, Concord or otherwise. I'm not bait. I'm a ghost in my own house." A pause, and something almost like a smile. "Besides. Somebody has to buy Sefa sixteen hours behaving like four."

Oyelaran wanted to fight it. She catalogued, rapidly and honestly, all the reasons fighting it would fail: Gideon's stubbornness, which was geological; the arithmetic, which was sound; and the fact that every alternative ended with all of them in one place when the charges came through the ceiling. She'd flown enough losing battles to know the shape of a winning one. This was a winning one wearing a funeral suit.

"Engines hot at hour two," she said finally. "If you're not back at the north airlock by hour three, I fly without you."

"You won't," Gideon said.

"Don't test me."

"I won't." But they smiled when they said it, and Oyelaran turned away before her face could say anything back.

* * *

The next three hours were the most honest work of her life.

Preflight on a wounded ship is a conversation between a pilot and everything that's broken. Oyelaran moved through the *Kestrel* deck by deck, hand on every seam, and the ship talked to her the way old ships do — in pressures and temperatures, in the pitch of pumps, in the small dishonest silences where something had given up and hadn't confessed yet. She found a coolant micro-leak in the number two loop and sealed it with epoxy and profanity. She found the portside maneuvering thruster running rough and rebalanced the gimbal by hand, feeling for the sweet spot the way you'd feel for a pulse.

Half her cargo was gone, shredded across four kilometers of debris field along with her margin for error. What remained she strapped down double, and then, on impulse she didn't examine, she went to the small locker aft where Ilsa Voss's effects were stored — the corrupted shard's casing, a data slate, a folded jacket that still smelled faintly of station air — and she strapped those down too, in the seat nearest the cockpit, buckled in like a passenger.

"For the ride home," she told the empty seat, and didn't explain to herself what home meant anymore.

At hour two she brought the drives up. The *Kestrel* woke around her with a shudder that ran nose to tail, and Oyelaran sat in the pilot's chair with her hands flat on the console and let the vibration climb through her arms, and for a moment — just a moment — she let herself imagine the flight out. Full burn up through the rock canopy, atmosphere screaming past the shields, the vault falling away beneath her like a bad memory finally cut loose. She imagined who'd be in the seats behind her. All of them. She made herself picture all of them, because a pilot flies better with a full manifest, and because picturing the empty ones first was a superstition she refused to give room to.

Her comm crackled. Sefa, from below, breathless: "Upload's at forty percent. Bolt's at sixty. We're racing, Oye, and I don't love the odds."

"Tell the bolt I said cheat."

A ghost of a laugh. Then, quieter: "Hey. If this goes sideways—"

"It won't."

"If it does. You were the best pilot I ever crewed with, and I've crewed with legends."

"You've crewed with smugglers," Oyelaran said. "Same thing, worse hours." She killed the channel before Sefa could make her cry, because crying was a pre-flight checklist item she did not have time for.

* * *

At hour two and forty minutes, the bolt fired.

She felt it as a single hard pulse through the hull — a soundless concussion, the ship's whole frame ringing once like a struck bell — and then every passive sensor she had lit up with returning echoes as the burst bounced off relay satellites that officially didn't exist. On her display, the response was already visible: drive signatures kindling across the inner system like a city coming on at dusk. Three ships. Fast ones. Strike-team fast.

"Contact," she said on the all-hands. "Three inbound, ballistic approach, ground assault profile. Time to landing: ninety minutes, maybe less. They're not even pretending to be subtle."

"Upload's at fifty-eight," Sefa reported.

"Ninety minutes," Oyelaran said. "Work faster."

She climbed out of the cockpit and went to the north airlock, because there was one more thing to do and she'd been putting it off. Gideon was there already, suited, checking seals on a pack that held a flare pistol, a cutting torch, and about thirty meters of detonation cord they'd stripped from the mining charges in the old survey lockers. They looked up as she approached, and the helmet hid their face, but Oyelaran had known Gideon Joubert for eleven years and could read their breathing.

"Sublevel access is through the cistern gallery," Gideon said. "MOTHER-9's opening the old flood doors for me. Once I'm in, I'll be moving, making noise, tripping every sensor I can find. They'll chase the ghost and the ghost will lead them a very long way from here."

"I've got the ship hot and the ramp down," Oyelaran said. "North airlock, hour three. You gave me a number, I'm holding you to it."

"I know."

"And Gideon." She stepped closer, close enough that their visors nearly touched, close enough that if the suits hadn't been between them she'd have grabbed a fistful of collar. "I've flown extraction for a lot of people. Most of them I left behind, and I slept fine. You I am *not* leaving behind. So whatever hero math you're doing down there in the dark, you do it with your feet pointed back toward this airlock. That's an order. I know I don't outrank you. Do it anyway."

For a moment Gideon said nothing. Then, through the comm, low: "Fifteen years ago I ran out of a burning building and never looked back. I'm not doing it twice." A pause. "Hour three, Oye. Save my seat."

"Seat's saved," she said. "Buckled in next to Ilsa. She'll keep it honest."

Gideon laughed — a real one, startled out of them — and then the cistern door irised open behind them, spilling cold wet air and the smell of deep stone and standing water, and Gideon Joubert stepped backward into the dark of their childhood with a flare pistol in one hand and a wave of two fingers, and the door swallowed them, and Oyelaran stood alone in the corridor listening to the flood doors cycle shut.

* * *

She flew the last hour from the cockpit with the ramp down and the drives idling at a whisper, watching the sky.

The Concord dropships came in over the eastern ridge in tight formation, beautiful the way weapons are beautiful — three black darts against the afternoon light, atmospheric shields glowing violet at the leading edges. They landed beyond the tree line, out of sight, disgorging their teams into the green. Oyelaran tracked them on thermal until the canopy ate the signatures, and then she tracked them by sound: the distant rhythmic cough of mag-boots on soil, spreading out, converging on the vault like fingers closing.

"Contact at the perimeter," Ada reported, voice flat and steady in a way that frightened Oyelaran more than panic would have. "Twelve signatures, maybe fourteen. They're heading for the main entrance. Gideon's noise is pulling two fireteams toward the sublevels already." A pause, and then, softer, almost wondering: "It's working. They're chasing the ghost."

"Upload?"

"Seventy-nine percent," Sefa said. "And the node replication just went exponential — MOTHER-9 says once we cross eighty-five, the archive exists in enough places simultaneously that purging it becomes a logistics problem bigger than one strike team. We're almost at the point of no return."

"Then hold the line," Oyelaran said, "and somebody tell me the second Gideon's clear, because I am not sitting in this chair one minute longer than I have to."

Outside, the light was starting to go gold. Somewhere below her feet, in tunnels full of cold water and older memories, Gideon Joubert was running loud through the dark, spending themselves minute by minute so that strangers they'd never meet could someday learn the truth about nine seconds. Somewhere deeper, Sefa and Ada were feeding a fire that wouldn't go out. And Oyelaran sat with her hands on the controls of the only way home, doing the thing she knew how to do, which was wait, and count, and believe.

Hour three arrived.

The north airlock stayed dark.

Hour three and ten. The mag-boot sounds were closer now. She could hear shouting, muffled by rock. On thermal, a new signature flared inside the vault's upper levels — then another — the hunters inside the house.

"Oyelaran." Sefa's voice, ragged. "Eighty-six percent. We crossed it. Whatever happens now, the truth is loose. It's done. It's actually done—"

And then, through the cockpit glass, at the treeline, movement: figures breaking cover, running for the north airlock, and Oyelaran's heart slammed once against her ribs before her eyes resolved them — two figures, one limping, one dragging the other, both in vault-side suits, both moving with the desperate ugly speed of people who have decided not to die today.

Behind them, at the edge of the trees, the first of the Concord hunters emerged into the gold light, rifle rising—

Oyelaran's hands were already moving.
