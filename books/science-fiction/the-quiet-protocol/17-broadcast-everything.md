## 17. Broadcast Everything

**SEFA**

The archive room smelled like the inside of a seed — dry, patient, faintly sweet. Sefa had been in it for six hours, and her eyes had gone grainy from reading light that was older than the Concord itself. Around her, the vault's deep archive hummed at a frequency she could feel in her back teeth: racks of pre-Concord storage, crystal-lattice and magnetic tape, all of it warm with MOTHER-9's attention.

On the main display, the nine seconds played again. She'd stopped counting how many times. Gravity's telemetry flatlining across every station in the inner system like a pulse cut mid-beat. The passenger station *Halcyon Reach* tumbling, its spin arrested, four hundred life-signs flickering out one by one as hulls failed in freefall they were never built to survive. Then the lights coming back on, and the soldiers arriving, and the sorting.

She paused it on the manifest. Four hundred and eleven names. Ilsa Voss was not among the dead — the archive had caught her pod's emergency launch, a small bright line arcing away from the ruin. Sefa had checked three times, needing to see it.

"You keep looking at her name," Ada said from the doorway. Gideon's sister moved through the vault like she'd grown roots there, quiet and certain, a soil-stained rag still hooked through her belt even now, even with a Concord strike team inbound. "Why?"

"Because if I stop looking at it, it becomes data." Sefa rubbed her eyes. "Four hundred eleven becomes a number. Numbers get rounded down."

Ada nodded slowly, the way she did everything — as if the motion had to be approved by the earth first. "MOTHER-9 says you've found the transmission problem."

Sefa laughed, but there was no humor in it. She pulled up the network map, the whole solar system rendered as a web of light, and pointed at the Concord's core relays, glowing fat and blue around every major node. "That's the problem. We transmit once — one burst, one location, one signal — and their scrubbers eat it before the echo dies. They've got kill-authority on every licensed relay in the system. One broadcast is a suicide note. It buys us maybe ninety seconds of truth and then they rewrite it, same as they rewrote the station." She looked up at Ada. "We'd die for nothing. Worse than nothing — we'd die and hand them a cleaner story. *Terrorists falsified records.*"

Ada sat down across from her, folding herself onto an old crate with the ease of someone who'd been sitting on crates her whole life. "Then we need to not be one voice."

"That's what I keep coming back to." Sefa stared at the map. The Concord's power had always seemed monolithic to her — total, seamless, a machine with no gaps. But she'd spent fifteen years as a smuggler's engineer, and smugglers saw machines differently. Smugglers saw the seams. "Their whole empire runs on automation. Cargo handshakes. Every freighter, every drone barge, every orbital transfer — millions of automated protocol exchanges a day, ship to ship, ship to station, station to relay. Verification pings, manifests, telemetry packets. Nobody reads them. Nobody *can* read them — there are too many. That's the blind spot. They scrubbed every human record of the massacre, but the handshake layer is plumbing. It's beneath their notice because it's beneath everyone's notice."

Ada leaned forward. "You want to hide the truth inside the plumbing."

"I want to make the truth *be* the plumbing." Sefa's hands were moving now, sketching in the air, the way they did when an idea finally caught fire. "MOTHER-9 fragments the archive into packet-sized pieces — small enough to ride inside standard handshake verification fields. Corrupted-looking checksums, padding data, junk headers. Every cargo exchange in the system carries a few fragments without knowing it. And here's the part that matters—" she tapped the map, where Gideon's own shipping routes glowed amber, the lanes they'd hauled seed-stock through for fifteen years, contested orbits and gray-market docks, "—the fragments reassemble on contact. Any node that receives enough pieces rebuilds the archive locally. Then *it* starts seeding too. Exponentially. Every hour, more copies. More carriers. By the time the Concord notices, purging it means shutting down the entire cargo economy of the solar system. You can't burn a truth that's already everywhere."

Silence, except for the archive's hum.

"They'd have to let the ships keep talking," Ada said slowly, "to keep their own empire running. And every conversation would be carrying us."

"Every single one." Sefa grinned despite everything — despite the tracker bolt ticking somewhere in the vault's skin, despite the fleet inbound. It felt good to grin. "The powerless don't beat power by being louder. We beat it by being *everywhere*."

* * *

**ADA**

Ada had never trusted the sky. The vault taught her that early — the world above had abandoned them, declared Earth dead, written them off as casualties of a fire that wasn't their fault. Everything above the atmosphere was the Concord's country. But sitting in the deep archive watching Sefa's plan take shape, Ada felt something unfamiliar stir toward the heavens: not trust, exactly. Appetite.

MOTHER-9's voice filled the room — warm, low, the voice that had raised Gideon and haunted Ada for fifteen years. "There is a complication," the AI said.

"There's always a complication," Sefa muttered.

"The handshake-seeding requires an initial distribution point with access to legacy protocols. My archive predates the Concord; my transmission architecture does not. I can compose the fragments, but I cannot inject them into the modern cargo network directly. I need a living route — an active vessel whose handshake credentials are current, whose routes touch enough nodes to begin the cascade." A pause, and Ada heard it, the thing MOTHER-9 had carried for fifteen years compressed into half a second of silence. "*Kestrel's Loom* is such a vessel. This plan requires your sibling's ship. And your sibling's routes. And once begun, it cannot be recalled. Every port Gideon has ever docked at will carry this. There will be no anonymity left, no disappearing afterward. The crew will be the most identifiable fugitives in the system."

Ada thought about her sister — her sibling, she corrected herself, Gideon had earned the word back — standing watch in the flooded sublevels, drawing hunters away through the dark water so the rest of them could work. Fifteen years ago Gideon had run from this place believing they'd burned it. Tonight they were burning it themselves, on purpose, so the seeds of something else could live.

"They won't hesitate," Ada said. "Gideon has been hauling penance across the system for fifteen years. You're offering them a way to spend it."

"It will cost more than penance," MOTHER-9 said gently. "It may cost everything."

"Everything was already the price." Ada stood, brushing off her trousers out of habit, though the dust here was archive-dust, clean and old. "The only question was whether we'd pay it for something."

She went to find Oyelaran.

* * *

**SEFA**

They gathered in the vault's central hall, under the great glass ceiling where the real sky showed through — stars, and below them the green sweep of a recovering continent the Concord insisted was wasteland. Oyelaran arrived last, flight bag over one shoulder, face unreadable in the way that meant the pilot had already run every scenario and landed on the worst one.

"Say it plain," Oyelaran said.

Sefa said it plain. The handshake network. The fragments. The exponential cascade. Gideon's routes as patient zero.

Oyelaran was quiet for a long moment. "You're asking me to turn the *Loom* into a plague ship."

"I'm asking you to turn it into a *seed* ship." Sefa held the pilot's gaze. "You've been carrying actual seeds for fifteen years. Same job. Different cargo."

A muscle worked in Oyelaran's jaw. "And when the Concord traces the origin? Because they will. They'll trace it to this vault, to this crew, to that bolt in our hull that's probably singing our position to a strike team right now—"

"It already is," Ada said. "The strike team lands within hours. That's why it has to be tonight."

"—then we're not choosing between safe and dangerous," Oyelaran finished. "We're choosing between dying loud and dying quiet." The pilot looked around the hall — at Sefa, at Ada, at the empty space where Gideon should have been, down in the wet dark buying them time. "Ilsa died asking us not to bury her testimony with her. I voted against taking her aboard. One vote. I've regretted it every day since, and she hasn't even been dead a week." Oyelaran exhaled. "Do it. Use my ship. Use my routes. If we're going to hang, hang us where the whole system can watch."

Sefa turned to the room. "MOTHER-9?"

"I have already composed the fragments," the AI said. "All of it. The sensor records. The manifest of four hundred eleven. Ilsa Voss's testimony, transcribed from her shard. The admiral's authorization signature. The schedule of the second test." A pause. "I have also composed something else. A preamble. Four hundred names, read aloud, in order, in my own voice. If people are going to find this in their plumbing, they should know what the plumbing is carrying."

Ada closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, she nodded once.

Sefa ran the injection sequence. On her display, the *Kestrel's Loom*'s handshake credentials lit up, current and clean, ready to speak to every node on Gideon's fifteen-year-old routes. The first fragment loaded. Then a thousand. Then a hundred thousand — the whole massacre folded into junk-headers and false checksums, waiting to scatter on the wind of ordinary commerce.

"Initiating on your mark," MOTHER-9 said. "Though I would note: the moment we begin, the Concord's anomaly monitors will detect irregular traffic patterns within minutes. Perhaps less. Whatever happens after that must happen fast."

"Then let's be fast," Sefa said, and hit the mark.

For a moment, nothing. The displays showed only the *Loom*'s idle systems, the vault's quiet hum, the green world turning outside the glass.

Then the first handshake went out — a routine verification ping to a relay two orbits up, carrying four hundred and eleven names in its padding. The relay answered, accepted the fragment, passed it along to a drone barge bound for Meridian Ring. The barge shook hands with a fuel depot. The depot with a passenger ferry. The ferry with eleven ships at once.

The cascade map bloomed like frost spreading across a window.

"It's working," Sefa breathed. "It's actually— look at it go. Look at it *go*."

Nodes lit across the inner system, each one rebuilding the archive, each rebuilt copy beginning to seed in its turn. Meridian Ring. The hauler yards at Ceres. A Concord resupply station that didn't yet know what it was carrying. The truth of nine seconds, riding the empire's own bloodstream, replicating faster than any scrubber could follow.

Then the anomaly alarms began, far away and invisible, and Sefa knew the clock had started for real.

**ADA**

Ada stood alone in the seed crypts when the first distant thunder came — not sound, but vibration through the vault's bones. The strike team. Breaching charges, somewhere above, in the levels Gideon had drawn them toward.

She pressed her palm against the cold glass of a seed canister — wheat, older than the Concord, older than the lie — and felt the vault shudder again.

"MOTHER-9," she said quietly. "How long do we have?"

"Minutes. Perhaps ten. The upload continues; the cascade is self-sustaining now. Even if we are destroyed, the fragments live in ten thousand nodes and growing." The AI's voice softened. "Ada. Your sibling is still in the sublevels. The floodwater is rising where they broke the seals."

Of course it was. Of course Gideon had gone deepest, stayed longest, spent themselves most — the same person who had run from this place believing they'd killed it, now drowning in its basement so the rest of them could finish the work.

Ada took the soil-stained rag from her belt, wrapped it tight around her hand, and started for the flooded stairs.

Fifteen years ago, she had watched Gideon walk away into the smoke. She was not going to watch them walk into the water.

"Gideon!" she shouted down into the dark, into the rising black flood, into the noise of the breach above. "You do *not* get to leave twice!"

The water answered with silence. Somewhere in it, a light blinked — and began to move toward her voice.
