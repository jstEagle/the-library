## 1. Nine Seconds

The tea was the first thing to go wrong.

Mara Pemberton had made it the same way for four thousand mornings: water drawn from the condenser at 06:40, leaves measured by eye because the scale had died in her third year and she'd never replaced it, steeped while she reviewed the vault's overnight diagnostics. The ritual was not comfort exactly—comfort implied a choice among alternatives—but architecture. Eleven years alone in a structure built to outlast civilizations required load-bearing routines, and the tea was one of them.

She had just lifted the cup when the gravity failed.

There was no sound. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the detail that would keep her awake in the weeks to come: no groan of machinery, no warning tone from the fault board, nothing. One moment the cup was rising toward her mouth, warm and ordinary and hers; the next, the tea was leaving it.

It came out in a slow brown bloom, a sphere of liquid unfolding like something alive, droplets peeling away from the main mass and drifting upward—or sideways, or nowhere, because *up* had stopped meaning anything. Mara's stomach lurched with the peculiar seasickness of sudden weightlessness, and her hand closed on empty air where the cup should have been.

"Oh," she said.

It was, she reflected later, an inadequate word for the moment. But eleven years of solitude had pared her vocabulary down to essentials, and *oh* was what arrived.

The cup itself rose past her shoulder, rotating gently, a thin ribbon of tea still clinging to its rim like a comet's tail. Beyond it, the whole operations room was coming apart in slow motion. Her logbook—actual paper, actual ink, a stubbornness she'd defended against every efficiency audit—lifted off the desk and fanned its pages open like a bird deciding whether to fly. A pen tumbled end over end. The diagnostic tablet drifted up beside it, screen still glowing with the morning's green columns of numbers, all of them suddenly meaningless.

Mara caught the edge of the desk and held on, though holding on was pointless now; there was nothing pulling her anywhere. She floated anyway, out of instinct, out of some animal conviction that things that fall must be caught.

Through the observation window above the long gallery, she could see the vault itself misbehaving.

The Halcyon Seed Vault was not beautiful. It was a buried fist of ferrocrete and ceramic, sunk four hundred meters into the rock of a moon nobody had bothered naming more precisely than Vault Station, its interior a cathedral of cold storage: tiered galleries of seed canisters, each one sealed, labeled, cataloged, each one a small tin promise that wheat would be wheat and rice would be rice and apples would still be apples whenever humanity got around to needing them again. Two hundred thousand varieties. She knew the number the way other people knew their own heartbeat.

And now every canister in Gallery Three was floating.

They rose off their cradles in eerie unison, hundreds of matte-grey cylinders lifting like a congregation standing for a hymn, turning slowly in the amber light of the gallery lamps. Some drifted into one another with soft, hollow knocks—*tok, tok-tok*—the only sound in a world gone suddenly silent. Frost sublimated off their surfaces in glittering halos, tiny clouds forming and dissolving around each canister like breath in winter air.

Mara pressed her palm flat against the window glass. Her reflection stared back at her, hair drifting loose from its braid, eyes very wide.

"Fault," she said aloud, because saying things aloud was another of her rituals, the way she kept herself a person rather than a ghost haunting her own life. "This is a fault. Report it."

But report it to whom? The vault's systems answered to her. She answered to a Compact oversight committee that sent form letters twice a year. And the form letters were answered by no one at all.

Her heart was doing something strange—not racing, but stuttering, missing beats, as if it too had noticed the absence of the constant downward pull and couldn't decide what to do about it. She pushed off from the window and swam down the length of the operations room, catching handholds along the rail, past the comm station with its dead relay lights, toward the environmental console.

The readouts were worse than useless. Gravity regulation: nominal. Inertial compensation: nominal. Every system reported itself healthy, content, functioning perfectly, while the physical world outside its sensors floated free. It was like watching a man insist he felt fine while his chair was pulled out from under him.

Then the tea found her.

A stray droplet—the vanguard of the escaping sphere—drifted across her field of vision and settled against her cheek, cool and faintly bitter. She wiped it away and looked at her fingertip, absurdly, as if it might explain something.

Four thousand days. Four thousand logs. In eleven years the vault had survived micrometeorite seasons, a coolant pump failure she'd repaired with salvaged parts and three days without sleep, a power fluctuation during a solar storm that had aged her ten years in ten minutes. Never once—not once—had the gravity wavered. The artificial spin-and-compensator system was old technology, older than her assignment, the kind of engineering they'd stopped building because it refused to fail.

*Exactly nine seconds,* said a voice in her head that sounded unnervingly like her sister's, the part of Liora that had always counted things. *You'll see. It'll be nine.*

Liora had been dead to her for forty years, which is to say silent, which is to say—she had learned to stop distinguishing—dead. But her voice lived on in Mara's head with annoying precision, and so when the gravity returned, Mara counted.

It came back the way it left: all at once, everywhere, no transition. The canisters dropped. Hundreds of them, falling together, hitting their cradles with a rolling thunderclap of impacts that shivered through the floor and up through her boots and into her teeth. The tea came down as rain across the desk, across the logbook, across her forearms. The cup struck the floor and did not break—it was vault-issue ceramic, built for clumsy hands—and spun to a wobble against the leg of her chair.

Mara stood in the middle of it all, soaked to the elbows, breathing hard.

"Log entry," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she expected. "Day four thousand."

She crossed to the recorder, thumbed it live, and watched the red light come on. The little device sat on its stand like a patient ear.

"This is Mara Pemberton, caretaker, Halcyon Seed Vault, recording for the colony of Halcyon's Reach." The words came automatically, worn smooth as river stones by repetition. "Time mark 06:52 local. At approximately 06:47, artificial gravity failed across—"

She stopped.

Across what? She had felt it here, in the vault. But the vault's own sensors insisted nothing had happened. Which meant either her senses had lied—a possibility she rated low after eleven years of trusting them with her life—or the failure had extended beyond the vault's monitoring envelope, beyond the station, possibly beyond—

"—failed," she finished. "Duration approximately nine seconds. No damage to primary systems. All two hundred thousand seed canisters accounted for; I will conduct a full inventory check today. Cause unknown. This is—" and here came the sentence she had spoken four thousand times, the sentence that had become less a sign-off than a prayer, "—Mara Pemberton, keeping watch. End of log."

The light went off. The room was quiet except for the tick of cooling ceramic and the distant, familiar hum of the climate system, and beneath that, so deep she felt it more than heard it, the slow geological patience of the moon itself.

Mara looked at her hands. They were shaking. She let them.

* * *

The inventory took six hours, and it saved her.

That was the secret nobody told you about solitude: you didn't survive it by thinking. You survived it by *doing*, by filling the hours with tasks granular enough to crowd out the questions. So she walked Gallery Three canister by canister, checking seals, confirming cradle locks, logging serial numbers with a stylus on the tablet, and by the time she reached row ninety, her hands had steadied and her mind had narrowed to the satisfying arithmetic of *two hundred thousand minus one thousand four hundred and twelve checked equals*—

Except the questions kept leaking in around the edges, the way cold leaks into a poorly sealed door.

Gravity didn't fail. Not locally, not accidentally, not for nine seconds. The compensators were triple-redundant; she'd personally inspected all three stacks during her second year. If the vault's system hadn't failed, then the failure wasn't the vault's. And if the failure extended past the vault—if it had gripped the whole station, the whole moon, maybe the whole system—then whatever caused it was large. Systemic. Deliberate?

Who bent gravity for nine seconds and let go?

She thought of the old physics lectures in the Compact education feeds, the ones about gravitic manipulation as the great locked door of engineering—*promising, theoretically possible, decades away.* She thought about how many decades had passed since those lectures were recorded.

By hour five she had stopped writing serial numbers and started staring at them, and she gave up, sealed the gallery, and climbed the maintenance spine to the surface array.

The antenna was the oldest thing on the station—older than the vault proper, older than her assignment, a lattice dish bolted to the crater rim that predated even the Compact's survey maps. Its official function was receiving the quarterly oversight transmissions, though those had degraded over the years from personal messages to automated confirmations to, lately, nothing at all. Mara kept it maintained anyway. Someone had to keep the ears of the place clean, even if no one spoke.

She came up out of the access hatch into the grey afternoon light and stopped walking.

The antenna was blinking.

Not the steady amber standby pulse it had held for eleven years, the pulse she'd fallen asleep to a thousand times through the bunker's ventilation resonance. This was different: a rapid triple-flash, pause, triple-flash, the indicator lamp at the dish's base strobing like something in distress. Like something trying very hard to be noticed.

Mara stood in the crater's shadow with the wind pulling at her jacket and felt the hair rise on her arms.

An incoming signal. An actual incoming signal, flagged and buffered, waiting in the queue.

In eleven years, the queue had held exactly four items: three quarterly confirmations and one garbled birthday message from an oversight clerk named Doss who had apparently confused her file with someone else's and wished her a happy fortieth, eight years late. She had kept it. She was not proud of how much she'd kept it.

She descended faster than safety protocol recommended, took the spine down two levels to the comm room, and pulled up the signal buffer with fingers that wanted to shake and mostly didn't.

The metadata bloomed across her screen, and she read it three times because it refused to make sense.

Signal origin: automated relay buoy 7-Kilo-9, Halcyon's Reach orbital perimeter.

Timestamp of transmission: forty years, two months, and eleven days ago.

Mara sat down slowly in the comm chair, the old cushion exhaling under her weight, and stared at the numbers until they blurred.

Forty years. The signal had been traveling, or waiting, or lost in some broken corner of the relay network for forty years, and had chosen today—today, nine seconds after the universe forgot which way was down—to arrive at her door.

Her hand hovered over the playback control. The cursor blinked. Somewhere behind her ribs, her heart had begun that stuttering rhythm again.

Every day for four thousand days she had spoken into this room, into the dark, to a colony that had gone silent before she ever took this post—had gone silent while her sister was still on it, breathing its air, walking its streets, and had never said why. The official record said ecological collapse. The official record said everyone was relocated in time. The official record said *do not inquire further*, in the polite, impenetrable grammar of institutions.

And now the dark had finally spoken back.

Mara Pemberton, who had spent eleven years being careful, being patient, being the quiet keeper of other people's futures, pressed play.

The audio that filled the comm room was not words. It was a pattern—a repeating cascade of tones, rising and falling, urgent and mechanical, interleaved with bursts of static that almost, *almost* resolved into a human voice before dissolving again. A distress cadence. Automated, corrupted, looping.

And stamped into its header, plain as a signature, a timestamp from the last night anyone had heard from Halcyon's Reach.

Mara listened to it eleven times. Then she opened her logbook to a fresh page, wrote the date at the top with a hand that had finally, completely stopped pretending to be steady, and began to write everything down.

Outside, the wind moved across the crater, and the ancient antenna blinked its triple-flash into the dark, patient as a lighthouse, calling her toward a story forty years buried.
