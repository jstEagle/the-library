## 2. The Caretaker's Ledger

The ledger was a habit before it was a duty.

Mara Pemberton kept it the way her mother had kept recipes—by hand, in ink that had faded from black to the color of weak tea, in a notebook whose spine had been repaired twice with archival tape and once, in a moment of desperation during a supply delay, with waxed thread pulled from a seed-sack seam. The vault's official inventory lived in the terminal, updated automatically, verified by checksums she ran every seventh day. But the ledger was hers. It recorded what the machines could not: the smell of the rye accession when she opened Drum 14, green and faintly sweet, like a field remembered by someone who had never stood in one. The way the rice canisters from the old Luzon collection rattled differently than the maize—they were packed looser, by people who had trusted there would be time to repack them later. There hadn't been. That was forty years ago now, and the loose rice still rattled, and Mara still wrote it down.

*Day 4,001*, she wrote that morning, though the number was wrong and she knew it was wrong. Four thousand days would have been an anniversary; four thousand and one was just another morning, which was the point. Anniversaries were for people whose lives had shapes. Hers was a circle drawn so many times it had worn a groove.

She descended into the long gallery at 0600 station time, because station time was the only time the vault recognized and because ritual, she had learned, was just loneliness with a schedule. Her boots rang on the grated walkway over Cryo Row A—a sound like a bell struck inside a well—and beneath her, through the frost-fogged inspection ports, the seeds slept in their blue-lit dark. Two hundred and eleven thousand accessions. Wheat bred on terraces that no longer existed. Barley from a valley drowned under a reservoir before Mara was born. A single vial of wild emmer wheat, collected by hand in 2049 by a woman named Oksana Reznik, whose handwriting on the label was small and certain and dead now for longer than Mara had been alive.

"Good morning," Mara said to them, as she did every morning. Not to any of them in particular. To all of them. The words fogged briefly on the inspection glass and vanished, and somewhere below, presumably, nothing heard her, and this too was part of the ritual: to speak knowing that speech without answer is not conversation but devotion.

She worked the gallery top to bottom, drum by drum, checking seals, logging temperatures, rotating stock per the schedule that had been written by a committee before the Compact consolidated the seed programs and then forgotten by everyone except her. Her fingers knew the latches without looking. Eleven years will do that. Eleven years of opening the same two hundred latches in the same order will teach your hands a language your mouth never learns.

It was on Drum 88—the sorghum row, always last, always her favorite because the sorghum accession cards carried pressed flowers between their pages, someone's small vanity decades ago, technically a contamination risk, officially something she should have removed, and which she never had—that the flashback took her. It did that sometimes. Not the memory itself, exactly, but the *approach* of it, the way certain objects were doors left slightly ajar.

Her sister had liked the sorghum too. That was the ridiculous thing. Of all the crops in all the world, Wren Pemberton, age nineteen, standing in the visitor gallery of the Halcyon Vault on the day of her emigration processing, had pressed her palm against the glass above Cryo Row C and said, "That one. If I ever get a garden up there, I want that one."

"You hate gardening," Mara had said. She'd been fifteen and expert in everything.

"I hate *weeding*," Wren said. "Sorghum doesn't care if you neglect it. It just grows anyway, out of spite." She'd grinned, and the grin had been the whole argument, the whole case for leaving, the whole future compressed into a flash of teeth: *I am going somewhere I can grow out of spite.*

* * *

The logs came after the rounds. This was the order of things: seeds first, then the dead colony, then herself, in that sequence, because it seemed right that the living should speak last.

The transmission array occupied the vault's northern cupola, a dish older than the Compact itself, its paint blistered by four decades of sun cycles. Mara climbed the maintenance ladder at 0900, as she did every day, and settled into the operator's chair that some previous caretaker had padded with foam cut from packing material, and she keyed the recorder.

"This is Mara Pemberton, caretaker, Halcyon Seed Vault, day four thousand and one." Her voice came out level. It always did. Eleven years of practice had sanded the tremor off it, mostly. "Vault status nominal. All cryo systems within tolerance. Accession count unchanged at two hundred eleven thousand, three hundred six. Sorghum rotation complete." A pause, the length of a breath. "Weather on the ridge is clear. The ice fields are doing that thing at dawn where they go pink. You'd have liked it."

*You'd have liked it.* Addressed, as always, to Halcyon's Reach.

The colony hung in the system's outer dark like a lamp in a far room—had hung, rather, for the thirty-one years since its founding, and for the eleven years since Mara had taken this post, and for the nine years since anyone had received so much as a carrier tone from it. Officially, the silence was explained. Officially, Halcyon's Reach had suffered cascading ecological failure—soil collapse, atmosphere processor faults, the slow sad arithmetic of a frontier settlement that had asked too much of too little land. Officially, the Compact had mourned, sealed the records as a courtesy to surviving families, and moved on. Officially, there was nothing left to say to the Reach, and certainly no reason for a seed vault on the system's inner rim to keep transmitting daily reports toward it.

Mara transmitted them anyway. Every day. Crop yields at the vault, such as they were—she grew test plots in the atrium, small stubborn gardens under grow-lights, and reported their progress as though the colony's agronomy division were reading. News of the system, filtered through what few civilian channels remained. Once, memorably, a description of a comet that had come through the inner system, visible for six nights, which she had watched alone from the cupola with tea going cold in her hands while narrating it aloud like a sports commentator for an audience of ghosts.

The transmissions went out at 0915. No reply had ever come back. She had stopped expecting one around year three, and had stopped being able to stop sending them around year five, and had made her peace with the contradiction sometime after that, the way you make peace with a limp.

Because here was the thing the ledger didn't record, the thing the official record didn't record, the thing that lived only in the pink dawn light on the ice fields and in the groove worn into her mornings:

Wren was on the Reach.

Had gone out on the third colonial wave, age twenty-two, with a agronomist's certification and a duffel bag and a promise to send for her little sister once the first harvest came in. "Two years," Wren had said at the departure gate, holding Mara's face in both hands. "Two years, maybe three, and then you're on the next transport, and we'll plant sorghum out of spite together."

The first harvest had come in. The letters had come with it—real letters, paper, because the Reach had been sentimental about bandwidth in the early years, and Wren had been sentimental about everything. Mara still had them, boxed in acid-free tissue in her quarters, forty-one letters spanning six years, each one smelling faintly of whatever the colony's paper was made from. The last one described a greenhouse. *We got the polycarbonate panels up finally,* Wren had written, in her slanting, impatient hand. *You would laugh at how proud I am of a building made of windows. Next letter I'll tell you about the school. They're starting a school, Mar. Kids born up here already. Somebody has to teach them what rain is.*

There had been no next letter.

Nine years of silence, and then the official explanation, delivered in a form letter so bland it felt like an insult—*ecological cascade, regrettable loss of contact, records sealed per Compact Directive 7-C*—and then eleven more years of Mara transmitting into the dark, because sealed was not the same as answered, and because some part of her had understood even then, in the wordless way you understand weather coming, that *sealed* meant somebody, somewhere, did not want the question looked at directly.

She had never said this aloud. Not to the Compact liaison who reviewed her post annually. Not to the supply pilots who came twice a year and ate her bread and told her about stations she'd never see. Especially not to herself, most nights, when the thought arrived on schedule like the dawn and she turned from it the way you turn from a door you're not ready to open.

But she thought it now, in the cupola, watching the transmit light pulse its slow green heartbeat toward a colony that had been silent for twenty years total, and she thought it with the particular clarity that comes after four thousand repetitions of anything: *They sealed the records of a death and called it ecology, and I have spent eleven years talking to a grave because no one would tell me where it was dug.*

Then she did what she always did. She logged the transmission—*daily report sent, 0915, standard band, no response expected, none received*—and she climbed down the ladder, and she made tea.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the atrium.

This was the part of the day the Compact liaison had once called, with the gentle condescension of the well-supervised, "your horticultural enrichment program," as though growing food in a seed vault were a hobby rather than a thesis statement. Six raised beds under full-spectrum lights. Test plots for viability—every accession batch had to be germination-checked on a rolling schedule, which meant something was always sprouting, always dying, always being weighed and measured and written down. But Mara had pushed past the mandate years ago. She grew food now. Actual food. Tomatoes that tasted like tomatoes instead of like the idea of tomatoes. Beans climbing twine. And in the corner bed, the one closest to the door, where she'd see it every time she entered—

Sorghum. Out of spite.

She worked the beds until her hands were black with soil, and the work did what work does, which is hold the mind's door shut against what waits behind it. Mostly. The door had gotten heavier lately. She'd noticed it over the past months without naming it: the silences between tasks stretching longer, the ledger entries growing more ornamental, her own voice in the daily logs beginning to sound, to her own ear, like someone reading a letter into a fire. Eleven years was a long time to be the only witness to anything. Even the seeds, she sometimes thought, had each other—two hundred thousand varieties packed shoulder to shoulder in the cold, a whole crowded city of the possible, while she rotated above them alone like a moon.

At 1700 she ate dinner at the observation window, as she did every night: beans and rice from her own plots, tea grown from the vault's own chamomile accession, and beyond the glass, the ice fields going violet, then blue, then black. Somewhere out past all of that, past the patrol corridors and the shipping lanes and the quiet zones where the Compact preferred no one look too carefully, Halcyon's Reach turned in its orbit, dead or sleeping or sealed, and Wren—

Wren was forty-nine years old now, if she was anywhere at all. Older than their mother had ever gotten to be. Mara did this arithmetic sometimes, at this window, and it never got easier, and she never stopped doing it, and she suspected that was the truest definition of grief she'd ever found: arithmetic you cannot stop performing.

"I'm still here," she said to the glass, to the dark, to the colony, to the sister. "In case you're wondering. I'm still keeping everything."

The vault hummed around her, patient as a held breath. Two hundred eleven thousand futures in the cold below, and one woman above them, keeping the ledger, keeping the light, keeping a promise shaped like a question that no one alive had ever answered.

She washed her bowl. She checked the locks—outer seal, inner seal, cryo telemetry, array power, all nominal, all logged. She climbed into her bunk with the current letter-box open on her chest, Wren's sixth letter, the one about the comet Wren had seen from the Reach's observation dome, *bigger than anything, Mar, the kids all pressed their faces to the glass and for a minute nobody was scared of anything*—and she read it again, though she could have recited it, and she slept the way she always slept: lightly, in a fortress full of seeds, guarding a silence.

* * *

She woke at 0347, which was not unusual. What was unusual was the light.

A thin amber blink was strobing at the edge of her vision through the bunkroom's small port—rhythmic, deliberate, nothing like the steady greens and blues of the vault's ordinary systems. She lay still for a moment, cataloguing it the way she catalogued everything, and then she was up, barefoot on the cold floor, moving through the dark corridors with the quick sure stride of someone who knows every meter of her territory.

The cupola. The ancient antenna array, the one the Compact's technicians had flagged as obsolete during her second annual review, the one she'd refused to let them strip for parts because it was *hers*, because it was the voice she'd been speaking into for eleven years—

Its status panel was blinking amber. Incoming signal flag. Priority handshake pending.

Mara stood in the cold blue light of the console, her heart going hard and strange against her ribs, and read the header twice, three times, her breath fogging in air that suddenly felt ten degrees colder than the thermostat claimed.

The signal had originated from the direction of Halcyon's Reach.

And its embedded timestamp read: forty years ago.
