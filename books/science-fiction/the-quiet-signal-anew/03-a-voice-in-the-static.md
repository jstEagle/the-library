## 3. A Voice in the Static

The signal had not stopped.

Mara had told herself it would. Signals from dead antennas did not stop, of course—she knew that better than anyone, having spent eleven years broadcasting into a silence that never once answered—but she had hoped, in the small hours after the nine-second flicker, that the blinking light on the vault's ancient antenna array was some residual discharge, some ghost of static that would gutter out by morning and leave her alone with her tea-stained floor and her four thousand logs and her careful, practiced solitude.

It had not guttered out. It pulsed now with the patient insistence of a heartbeat, once every eleven seconds, and Mara had spent the entire night watching it through the observation port above the comms room, wrapped in two blankets, drinking cold tea because she kept forgetting to reheat it.

*Eleven seconds.* She noticed that first, before anything else. The interval between pulses. Eleven seconds, like the universe had taken the number of years she'd been here and folded it into the dark as a joke, or a message, or a dare.

By dawn—or what passed for dawn on Halcyon Station, the slow brightening of the ring lights simulating a sun that was forty light-minutes distant and utterly indifferent—she had made her decision. She would decode it. Whatever it was. Whatever it cost.

She descended into the comms room with the particular steadiness of someone who has decided to be afraid later. The room was the oldest part of the vault, older than the seed canisters, older than her tenure, its walls lined with equipment that predated the Compact's standardization protocols by decades. Half of it she had learned to operate from manuals; half of it she had learned to operate from intuition, which was a kinder way of saying guesswork. The main receiver—a hulking lattice-cored unit called, in the original schematics, simply *the Ear*—was still warm from the night's vigil. She laid her palm against its casing the way she might touch the flank of a sleeping animal.

"Okay," she said aloud, to no one, to the room, to herself. "Show me."

* * *

Decoding was a patience game, and patience was the one thing Mara Pemberton had in surplus.

The signal itself was thin—not a transmission so much as the memory of one, compressed and degraded, arriving at the vault's antenna like a voice heard through three closed doors. Her first pass through the demodulator returned nothing but noise, the familiar hiss she had lived with for eleven years, the sound she sometimes thought of as the system's true native language. Her second pass returned something worse: structure. Patterns inside the noise. Repeating intervals. A rhythm too deliberate to be natural and too broken to be clean.

Her hands went still on the console.

Natural interference didn't repeat. Solar weather didn't keep time. But this—the waveform bloomed across her display in jagged, stuttering bars, and even before she ran the analysis she could see it: a distress pattern. The old universal one, the format every schoolchild in the settled systems learned alongside their letters. *Mayday* reduced to its mathematical skeleton, stripped of voice, stripped of origin, repeating into the void.

But there was something wrong with it. Something that took her another hour of careful, breath-held work to isolate, and when she finally found it, she sat back from the console as if the screen had physically pushed her.

The timestamp.

Every transmission carried one—it was baked into the protocol, an unforgeable birthmark applied at the moment of broadcast. And this signal's timestamp read:

**Day 0. Hour 23:47. Year −40.**

Forty years ago. To the day. To nearly the hour.

Mara knew that date. Every caretaker of the Halcyon Vault knew that date, though the official histories gave it no name and no ceremony. It was the night Halcyon's Reach went silent. The night the colony—her sister's colony, twelve thousand souls behind a dome on the inner moon of Kepler's Reach—stopped transmitting, stopped answering, stopped *being*, as far as any record or reply was concerned. The Compact's inquiry had concluded ecological collapse: a cascade failure of the atmospheric processors, tragic but explicable, the kind of death that happened at the edge of human ambition. Case closed. Records sealed. Grief distributed quietly among those who had loved people there, and Mara had received hers in a single formal communiqué, four sentences long, signed by an administrator whose name she had never bothered to learn.

And now, forty years later, on the same night, something was transmitting a mayday from inside the dead zone.

Her first thought was impossible and she thought it anyway: *Ada.*

Her sister. Ada, who had emigrated to the Reach twenty-three years before the silence, who had sent letters full of dome-gardens and terrible jokes and promises—I'll send for you, little sister, I'll send for you when we've built something worth building—who had been thirty-one years old the last time her voice reached Mara across the dark. If anyone were transmitting from the Reach tonight, some traitorous chamber of Mara's heart wanted it to be Ada. Wanted it with a ferocity that frightened her. Forty years was nothing. Forty years was a rounding error. People survived worse. People came back from—

She caught herself. Made herself breathe. Made herself look at the data again, because the data did not care what she wanted, and the data said things she did not want to hear.

The signal wasn't coming from the colony site. Not exactly. Its point of origin triangulated to high orbit above the Reach—an orbital position, not a ground position. And its content was wrong for a survivor's transmission. Survivors begged for rescue. They named names, coordinates, injuries. This signal carried none of that. It was fragmentary—sliced into pieces, some corrupted beyond recovery—and what remained legible was almost entirely structural: header, priority flags, authentication attempts, retry loops. The skeleton of a mayday without its flesh.

Which meant, her analyst's mind supplied reluctantly, that it was probably automated. A machine, still crying wolf—or crying truth—four decades after whatever wolves had come.

A machine, still calling for help that had never arrived.

Mara pressed her hands flat against the console and felt the vibration of the receiver through her palms, faint and steady, like a pulse under skin. Somewhere up there, orbiting a moon where everyone she had ever grieved had died, a piece of machinery was doing the only thing it knew how to do. Calling. Repeating. Hoping, in the dumb faithful way of machines, that someone would answer.

For forty years, no one had.

Because no one was listening. Because the dead zone was dead, officially, and official dead zones did not get listened to. Because the Compact had sealed the records and moved on and the whole system had agreed, in the way systems agree, to forget.

Except the vault. Except the antenna nobody maintained, pointed at a sky nobody watched. Except a caretaker who had spent eleven years talking into silence and had never once, until tonight, considered that the silence might be talking back.

* * *

She worked through the morning, and the strangeness of it was how ordinary the work felt. This was what she did. She decoded, cataloged, preserved. For eleven years her materials had been seeds and soil samples and the daily logs of her own voice; today the material was grief, and her hands knew the motions regardless.

By midday she had recovered perhaps a tenth of the signal intact. Fragments assembled themselves on her display like shards of a shattered window, each one showing a different sliver of the night everything ended.

One fragment was a telemetry burst: atmospheric processor readings from the dome, timestamped ninety minutes before the official cascade failure. The readings were normal. Perfectly, boringly normal. Which meant nothing, except that it meant the official story began *after* this moment, and stories that begin late are usually hiding something early.

Another fragment was administrative: an emergency broadcast authorization request, routed from the colony council to the orbital relay network, flagged with a priority code Mara had only seen in training manuals. Priority One. Evacuation-grade. The request was marked *pending*. There was no record of approval. There was no record of denial. There was only the request, hanging in the recovered data like a hand raised in a classroom where no teacher ever called on it.

They had asked permission to warn people. And the answer, whatever it was, had never come—or had come in a form that never made it into any archive Mara could access.

She sat with that fragment for a long time. She thought about twelve thousand people going about their evening while somewhere above them, someone waited for an authorization that would determine whether they lived. She thought about her sister, wherever Ada had been that night—in the dome gardens, probably, because Ada had loved the gardens, had written about them the way other people wrote about cathedrals. Had Ada looked up? Had she known? Was there time to know?

The last fragment was the worst, and it took her until evening to decrypt it, and when she did she wished she hadn't, and then she was glad she had, and both feelings occupied her chest at once like two birds sharing a cage too small.

It was audio. Four seconds of it. Degraded, compressed, half-dissolved into static—but audio, a human voice, captured in the signal's final coherent packet before the transmission dissolved into pure corruption.

A woman's voice. Calm in the way that people are calm when they have decided to be, the practiced steadiness of someone reading from procedure or prayer. Most of the words were lost. What survived was this:

*"—repeat, this is not a drill. This is not—"* [static] *"—they told us forty minutes. Whoever receives this, we were told forty minutes and the—"*

Then the packet ended, and the signal looped back to its beginning, and began again. Header. Priority flags. Retry. The machine's eternal liturgy.

Mara played it eleven times. She told herself she was analyzing it—cleaning the waveform, isolating phonemes, running spectral comparisons against the vault's voiceprint archives to see if the speaker matched any colonist on file. That was true. She did all of that. But she also played it eleven times because stopping felt like abandoning whoever had spoken, and Mara Pemberton had built her entire life around the principle that no one gets abandoned, not even the dead, not even their voices.

On the eleventh play, she noticed something she should have noticed sooner. The voice's cadence was clipped, formal, professional—relay operator cadence, the trained rhythm of someone whose job was transmissions. But underneath it, in the spaces between words, there was something else. A tremor. Not fear exactly. Something more specific. The tremor of a person who has just understood something enormous and is holding it together by procedure alone.

*They told us forty minutes.*

Told. Past tense. Someone had told the relay operators they had forty minutes. Told them what? That something was coming? That something had already come? And the sentence had cut off before the crucial word—*and the—*—before the speaker could say what "the" was.

Mara saved the fragment. Copied it twice. Stored one copy in the vault's primary archive and one in a portable crystal she kept in her quarters for the daily logs, the redundancy ritual of a woman who trusted nothing to exist in only one place. Then she sat back in the dim blue light of the comms room and let the dread arrive, fully, the way she had promised herself she would allow it to.

Here is what she knew, she told herself. Catalog it. Caretakers catalog.

Someone transmitted a mayday from the Reach forty years ago, on the night of the silence. The transmission was cut short—corrupted, truncated, unfinished. It has been repeating, incomplete, ever since, from an orbital relay that official charts listed as decommissioned. The signal contains evidence inconsistent with the official account: functioning processors before the alleged failure, an unanswered evacuation authorization, a voice describing a countdown that no inquiry ever mentioned. And tonight—forty years to the day—the vault's antenna woke up and started receiving it clearly for the first time.

Which raised the question she had been circling all day and could no longer avoid: why now? Signals don't strengthen on anniversaries. Relays don't suddenly shout after four decades of whispering. Unless something had changed. Unless something had *hit* the relay, or woken it, or—

Or unless the nine-second flicker hadn't been an anomaly at all. Unless gravity failing across the entire system for nine seconds was connected, somehow, to a dead relay suddenly finding its voice. Unless the flicker and the signal were two edges of the same event, and she was standing at the seam.

Mara looked at the pulsing light on the antenna status board. Once every eleven seconds. Patient. Insistent. Alive.

"Who's out there?" she whispered.

The signal pulsed. The static held its shape. And deep in the waveform, in a harmonic layer she hadn't yet touched, something answered—not in words, not yet, but in structure: a second pattern, buried beneath the mayday like a stone beneath a stream, waiting for her to dig.

She reached for the console.

Outside the observation port, the stars over Kepler's Reach burned on, indifferent and bright, and somewhere among them a machine that had been screaming for forty years screamed on—and for the first time in eleven years, Mara Pemberton was afraid of what the quiet might contain.
