## 12. First Key

The tissue had taken eleven days to grow.

Idris kept it in a climate cradle he'd cannibalized from the vault's own germination bay—a small glass vessel no larger than a teacup, filled with amber nutrient gel, and floating inside it, a pale disc of living skin the size of a thumbnail. It looked like nothing. It looked like the membrane of an egg, like the skin of milk left too long, like something you would wash off your hands without thinking twice.

It was also, biologically and legally and in every way the Compact's locks cared about, a piece of a dead woman named Odile Ferreiro, schoolteacher, Halcyon's Reach, deceased forty years.

Mara could not stop looking at it.

"It's not her," Idris said, for what she suspected was not the first time. He stood at the workbench in the vault's lower archive with his sleeves rolled and his hands very still, as if the room might notice if he fidgeted. "It's a copy of her fingerpad. The whorls, the ridge density, the subdermal capacitance. The lock doesn't read a soul, Mara. It reads friction."

"I know what it reads." She turned the cradle a quarter-turn on the bench, watching the disc drift and settle. "I've been reading friction for eleven years. That's most of what a daily log is."

Sefu laughed from the doorway—short, surprised out of him—and then stopped laughing, because this was not a room where laughter sat comfortably for long. He came in carrying two mugs of tea and set one at Mara's elbow without asking, which by now was simply what he did, and she had stopped remarking on it, which was its own kind of intimacy neither of them named.

"Is today the day?" he asked.

"Today is the day," Idris said. "Assuming the substrate holds. Assuming the lock hasn't been updated remotely since the Reach went dark. Assuming—" He caught himself, pressed his lips together. "Assuming. Yes. Today."

* * *

They had moved the first quantum core up from the unmapped sublevel three days ago, on a gravity sled that complained the whole way like an old man on stairs. It sat now in the center of the archive chamber under a cone of work light: a cylinder of dark composite taller than Sefu and wider than Mara could have wrapped her arms around, its surface unmarked except for a single recessed panel where a reader waited behind smoked glass. Forty years old. Cold to the touch even in the vault's carefully held warmth, as though it were keeping its own counsel about temperature the way it kept everything else.

The cores were the reason the vault existed, though Mara hadn't known it until Idris arrived. She had thought she was guarding seeds. Seeds, yes—eleven thousand cultivars in cryo-canisters, the agricultural memory of four worlds—but beneath them, below the floor plans, below even the maintenance schematics, the founders had buried quantum storage lattices holding raw telemetry from every colony the Compact had ever seeded. Insurance, Idris called it. The founders' word for it had been *witness*. And when Halcyon's Reach died, someone in the Compact hierarchy had ordered these cores sealed rather than purged—not mercy, Idris believed, but bureaucratic inertia, the same force that let a caretaker be posted to a silent colony and forgotten there for a decade.

Forgotten, or left. Mara had never decided which word fit better. Some mornings they felt like the same word.

The biometric problem had seemed absolute. Each core required a living key: a registered colonist's print, pulse, and galvanic signature, checked against a template stored in the core itself. The dead did not press their fingers to doors. But Idris had spent one long night pacing the seed rows before stopping abruptly in front of the cryo-banks and saying, in the flat voice he used when he was ashamed of an idea and proposing it anyway:

"They kept biological deposits. Tissue samples, blood, cell lines—for medical continuity, for genetic diversity planning. Every colonist. It's standard founding protocol. The samples are still viable. Cryo-preserved cells can be coaxed into differentiation. Skin differentiates fastest."

"You want to grow their fingers," Sefu had said.

"I want to grow enough of their fingers to open a door," Idris said. "And then we decide together whether we ever do it again."

That *together* had been doing quiet work in the vault ever since. They had voted—actually voted, the five of them around the mess table, Mara raising her hand last and highest—to proceed with one sample, one core, one attempt. Odile Ferreiro had been chosen not by lottery but by logic: her deposit was best preserved, her template cleanest. Nobody had said aloud that choosing by viability was easier than choosing by name, and everybody had thought it.

Now the disc of her floated in its gel, and the morning of the attempt arrived the way all mornings arrived in the vault: lights rising slowly through amber to white, the ventilation sighing, the eternal faint hum of refrigeration that Mara had long ago stopped hearing and now heard again, sharply, because everything felt sharpened today.

* * *

Mara insisted on making the log entry first.

She had missed only four days in eleven years—two for fever, one for a coolant flood, one she still couldn't explain—and whatever happened at the workbench today, the record would show that Day Four Thousand and Twelve began normally, with the caretaker noting humidity drift in Bay Six and the germination of the Vesper wheat stock. Her voice sounded steady in the little recorder's pickup. She knew how to make it sound steady; that was nearly the whole craft of it.

"This is Mara Pemberton, caretaker, Halcyon Seed Vault, day four thousand twelve," she said. "Inventory nominal. Weather outside nominal, which is to say hostile and unchanging. And today we're going to try to open a door that a dead woman locked, using a door-key we grew from her own body, so that we can find out what happened to everyone she loved. If anyone is listening to this in the future: we didn't do this lightly. We argued about it. We voted. We're going to carry her name with whatever we find. That's the whole of our intention. End of log."

She clicked the recorder off and stood for a moment in the amber half-light of the corridor, hand resting on the cool wall, letting the hum come back into her bones.

Then she went down to open the door.

* * *

Idris worked with the tenderness of a man defusing something, which in every sense that mattered he was. He lifted the tissue disc from its gel with a microspatula, laid it onto the reader's contact plate, and sealed the plate's hydration film over it with movements so slow they seemed ceremonial. The reader's status light woke: red, then pulsing amber.

"Contact established," he murmured. "Template query running. The core is comparing what we grew against what it remembers."

"What if it remembers wrong?" Sefu asked. He stood with his arms crossed tight, his whole weight on his heels, a man ready to catch a falling thing. Behind him, his crew—Tomasz with his engineer's squint, Priya with her hands jammed in her jacket pockets, young Wren perched on a supply crate with her knees drawn up—had arranged themselves into a loose crescent, the shape people make around sickbeds and launch windows.

"The core doesn't remember," Idris said. "It verifies. There's a difference, and the difference is—" He exhaled. "The difference is that verification doesn't care. It will open for a copy as readily as for the original. That's the horror of it. That's also the mercy."

The amber pulsed. Once. Twice. Mara found she was holding her breath and made herself breathe, because some old instinct told her that the room needed one person in it who was breathing on purpose.

On the third pulse, the light went green.

There was no fanfare. No chime, no voice announcement, none of the Compact's usual liturgical self-congratulation. The smoked glass over the reader simply slid aside, the great cylinder shivered once through its whole mass with a sound like a held note finally released, and a seam appeared along the core's flank, hairline-fine, glowing faintly from within.

"It's opening," Wren whispered, as if volume might break it.

The seam widened. From within the core, on a slender articulated arm that unfolded like something botanical, rose a display pane and a cluster of interface nodes, dusted in forty years of inert atmosphere, each node lighting in sequence as the lattice came up from cold storage. Idris stepped back. His hands, so steady during the procedure, were trembling now.

"Query interface is live," he said. "We can request contents. We can't take everything at once—the lattice serves data in order of index, and the index is enormous. Colony telemetry, infrastructure logs, personal archives, public records. Forty years of a whole world, filed." He looked at Mara. "First retrieval is ours to choose. What do we ask for?"

Nobody answered immediately. In the silence Mara became aware of the sound of the vault itself—the refrigeration, the air handlers, the distant tick of thermal contraction in the seed bays overhead—all of it suddenly audible, as though the building were leaning in.

"Public records," Priya said finally. "Start with the official stuff. Council minutes, civil bulletins. Build the skeleton before we put skin on it."

"No," Sefu said.

Everyone turned. He hadn't moved from his catching-stance, but his face had changed, gone somewhere older.

"Skeletons are for archivists," he said. "With respect," a glance at Idris, "and for later. My mother taught primary school on the Reach. Third level. If—" His voice snagged and he pulled it taut again. "If there are school records in that index, then there's a chance, a real chance, that somewhere in there is my mother's classroom. A recording. An assembly. Anything. I have spent forty years knowing she died and zero years knowing anything about the last day she was alive. I'm not starting with council minutes. I'm starting with the children."

The silence after that had texture, weight, temperature. Tomasz looked at the floor. Priya took her hands out of her pockets.

"School records," Idris said quietly, turning to the interface. "Halcyon's Reach education network. Public-facing media. Assemblies, recitals, classroom recordings." His fingers moved across the nodes. "Index search running."

The core thought. They could hear it think—a soft cascading shimmer from deep in the cylinder, like ice settling on a pond, the sound of a quantum lattice walking forty years of filed memory to find one small warm room.

"Four hundred twelve results," Idris said. His voice cracked on the number and he cleared his throat. "Spring term. Final week. The last indexed item is dated—" He stopped.

"Dated when?" Mara asked, though her stomach already knew the shape of the answer.

"Ninety-one hours before the silence."

* * *

They played it on the core's own display pane, projected wide across the archive wall, because nobody suggested moving it anywhere else and nobody could have explained why.

Static first, a breath of it, and then color bloomed: a hall with high windows, morning sun falling in long gold bars across a floor scuffed by decades of small shoes. Bunting drooped from the ceiling in faded festival colors. Rows of folding chairs, mostly empty—the recording was from the hall's own archive camera, positioned high in the corner, seeing everything and attended by no one. On a low stage stood perhaps thirty children in mismatched finery, ages six to nine, arranged in a wobbly semicircle behind a forest of homemade instruments: tin drums, string boxes, a xylophone that had clearly begun life as scaffolding pipe.

And at the side of the stage, conducting with a wooden spoon, stood a heavyset woman with gray-threaded braids pinned up and her sleeves rolled to the elbow.

Sefu made a sound. It was not a word. Priya's hand found his arm and stayed there.

"That's her," he said. "That's—she wore that spoon out. She carried it everywhere. She said a real conductor's baton makes children nervous." He laughed, wet and terrible. "She was right. Look at them. Not one of them is nervous."

On the stage, a girl at the front—seven years old maybe, gap-toothed, wearing a dress with a sunflower collar—stepped forward, bowed to the empty chairs as she'd been taught, and announced in a voice pitched for a much larger world: *"Good morning! We are the Halcyon Reach Primary Ensemble, and we will now perform 'The Seeding Song,' which our teacher wrote, because there wasn't one yet."*

"Oh," Mara said. Just that. Just the single syllable, escaping her like heat from a cracked seal.

The children played. It was not good music, by any measure that would survive peer review. The tin drums came in late. The pipe-xylophone wandered into a key that did not exist. One boy in the second row lost his place entirely and simply banged with joy. And over all of it the woman with the spoon conducted with her whole body, sweeping, crouching, mouthing the words, and the children sang:

*"Little seed, little seed, / sleep in the dark and dream of green, / the wind may blow and the sky may freeze, / but hold your ground and grow, grow, grow—"*

Mara watched the faces. That was her job here, she understood suddenly; this was the task the moment assigned her, the way the vault had assigned her humidity checks and log entries. Someone had to watch the faces. The girl with the sunflower collar sang with her eyes shut. The boy banging off-beat watched his teacher instead of his instrument, checking, always checking, that he was still welcome. A small boy at the end of the row picked his nose throughout the entire performance with the serene impunity of the very young. And near the middle, a child held a smaller child's hand, both of them singing, neither letting go.

Ninety-one hours. Ninety-one hours before the silence. These children went home from this hall, ate dinner, slept, woke, lived another three days and change—school days, ordinary ones, spelling and lunch and scraped knees—and then the sky over Halcyon's Reach did whatever the sky did, and every face in this frame stopped existing, and the Compact wrote the word *collapse* in a file and sealed it.

*"Grow, grow, grow!"* the children finished, on a chord that scattered like startled birds, and they bowed, and the teacher wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand while pretending to fix her braids, and the recording ran on another few seconds into applause from the empty chairs—recorded applause? No. Real applause. Parents, arriving late, filing in at the edges of the frame. A man lifted a toddler onto his shoulders. A woman in a courier's jacket cupped her hands and whistled.

Then the file ended, and the wall went dark, and the archive chamber was so quiet that Mara could hear Wren crying without any sound at all, just the shaking of her shoulders on the crate, just Priya reaching back to pull the girl against her side.

No one spoke for a long time. The vault hummed. The core waited, patient as geology, full of four hundred more results and ten thousand more files beyond them, a whole murdered world filed and indexed and waiting for someone to ask.

* * *

It was Sefu who broke it, and he broke it standing, wiping his face with both palms like a man coming in from rain.

"She wrote that song," he said. "For a seeding festival, the year I was born. She told me once that colonies don't get anthems, so you have to grow them yourself, like everything else." He looked at the dark wall as if it might give the song back. "Forty years I thought the Reach was a tragedy. A disaster. An act of God or an act of failure. Something that *happened*." His jaw worked. "It wasn't weather. It wasn't collapse. Somebody looked at that room—that exact room, those kids, my mother with her spoon—and signed a paper. Or worse, didn't sign anything at all. Just made a call. Routine. Housekeeping."

"We don't know yet," Idris said gently. "Not proven. Signatures, orders, motive—we need the telemetry, the command logs, the—"

"I know what we need." Sefu turned, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "I know exactly what we need. All of it. Every file in that cylinder and the three cylinders under it. Everything the Compact sealed, everything Chen hid, everything anyone ever wrote down about that night. I want the whole truth, Idris. Not for a tribunal. For *them*. Those kids sang a song about holding your ground and growing, and somebody erased them for it, and the least they are owed is to be remembered by name, by face, by voice, by every damn second of footage that cylinder will give us."

"And then?" Priya asked softly. "After we have it?"

Sefu looked around the room—at Idris, at Tomasz, at Wren, last and longest at Mara.

"Then everyone hears it," he said. "Every station, every dock, every clerk in every Compact office. You can't massacre a choir and file it under *ecological collapse* if four hundred worlds are watching them sing."

Mara felt something settle in her chest, something that had been dislocated for eleven years clicking at last into joint. She had guarded seeds for a decade on the theory that preservation was the point—that keeping things safe was the whole of love's duty. But seeds weren't the point. Seeds were *patience*. The point was the growing. The point was the song, sung loud, in a hall full of people.

"Then we open the rest," she said. "One key at a time."

Idris was already back at the interface, calling up the index, and it was he who found it—his voice going strange mid-sentence, going careful, the voice of a man handling live wire.

"Mara. The personal archives are indexed by household. Cross-referenced against immigration manifests." He didn't turn around. "There's a Pemberton entry. Household of Amara Pemberton, immigrated year minus-forty-two, district seven."

The room tilted slightly. Mara gripped the edge of the workbench.

"My sister," she said. "You're telling me my sister's things are in there. Her files. Her—" The words ran out. What remained was older and simpler than words: Ninety-one hours. Ninety-one hours of her sister's life, recorded, filed, waiting.

Idris turned at last. His face was pale and resolute, the face of a man who had crossed one ethical line and could see the next one from where he stood.

"There are three more cores below," he said. "Odile's key opened the public lattice. But the personal archives—the private households, the final logs—they're partitioned separately. Locked deeper." He paused. "And Mara—I need you to hear the rest before you ask. The second core's key belongs to another colonist. And the third—"

He looked down at the tissue cradle, at the pale disc of a dead teacher's skin resting in its amber gel, and did not finish the sentence, because he didn't have to. Everyone in the room had already done the arithmetic. Three cores. Three keys. Three more names to be chosen, three more deposits to be grown, three more doors to be opened with the hands of the dead.

Above them, faint through the vault's layers of stone and steel, the comm array chimed—an incoming signal, automated, routine.

But when Mara reached the console, the transmission header was not routine at all. It bore a Compact survey code. A vessel was inbound to the Halcyon Seed Vault, requesting docking clearance, and its stated purpose was a sensor-log review concerning a nine-second gravitational anomaly.

They knew about the flicker. They were coming to ask their questions politely.

Behind her, in the archive chamber, the first core glowed green and patient, full of voices, ninety-one hours from the end of the world—and Mara Pemberton, caretaker, day four thousand twelve, understood that the race had already started, and that she had just been handed the starting gun.
