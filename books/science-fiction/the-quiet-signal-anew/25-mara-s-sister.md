## 25. Mara's Sister

The third core woke like a person remembering.

Not the clinical cascade of the first two—telemetry scrolling in cold columns, council minutes rendered in procedural gray. This one opened with color. With sound. With a woman's laugh, tinny and bright through the vault's ancient speakers, and Mara's hand flew to her mouth as if she could catch the sound before it escaped into the sublevel's dark and die there.

"Personal archives," Idris said quietly behind her. He had explained it an hour ago, his voice careful the way people are careful around wounds: *the colonists backed up their own devices to the municipal cores—letters, photographs, home video, everything the cloud of the Reach held in its last days.* Everything, he'd said, that forty thousand people thought would still be there tomorrow.

Mara had made him stop talking after that.

Now the core's index bloomed across her console, and it was not an archive. It was a neighborhood. Family dinners frozen mid-toast. A dog shaking off rain in slow, devoted loops. Birthday candles relit eleven times because someone kept bumping the table. A hundred thousand small certainties that tomorrow would come, uploaded by people who had no reason to doubt it, and Mara understood with a clarity that felt like falling that this was not data at all. This was the colony's heart, excised whole and preserved in quantum glass, beating still if anyone cared to listen.

She cared. God help her, she cared so much her ribs ached with it.

She could have searched alphabetically. She could have been systematic, professional, the caretaker cataloging inventory as she had for four thousand days. Instead her fingers moved on their own, typing a name she had not spoken aloud in over a decade, whispering it anyway under her breath like a prayer she was ashamed of needing:

"Pemberton, Junie."

* * *

The search returned forty-one files.

Forty-one pieces of a life. Mara sat very still in the chair Idris had dragged down from the surface weeks ago, its legs wrapped in padding so it wouldn't ring against the floor, and looked at the file names without opening any of them. *Junie—garden tour.mp4. Junie & Tomas—anniversary.mov. Junie—voice memo 4112.* Voice memos. Her sister had recorded voice memos the way their mother used to write letters—constantly, compulsively, narrating her own life back to herself because silence felt like a room with no windows.

Mara opened the earliest one first. She told herself it was method. It was cowardice, and she knew it, walking backward through her sister's days toward the end because the end was a door she could not make herself approach head-on.

*"Day 2,204 on the Reach, and the tomatoes have finally forgiven me."*

Mara laughed—a broken, startled sound—and pressed her palm flat against the console as though she could steady herself against it. Junie's voice filled the sublevel, warm and slightly hoarse, the way it always got when she'd been talking all day. The recording was visual too: her sister's face, forty years younger than the last time Mara had seen it, lit by greenhouse light gone gold near dusk. Junie had been thirty-four when the Reach went silent. In the recording she was maybe twenty-nine, hair escaping a braid, dirt on her cheekbone like war paint.

*"I know, I know, you're tired of hearing about the tomatoes. But Mara, you have to understand—they were dead when we got here. All of them. The first shipment froze in transit and everyone said start over with the hydroponics, forget soil, but I couldn't. There's something about dirt you can feel. Anyway. They forgave me. Third row came up overnight like they'd been waiting for permission."*

She turned the camera toward the rows, and Mara saw them: green and stubborn and absurdly alive under the dome lights, in soil hauled up from a world that had never asked to be farmed.

*"That's what I wanted to tell you. That's the whole message, really. Things come back. You plant them stupid and late and half-frozen and they come back anyway. So stop writing me those letters where you apologize for staying at the vault. You're not staying, Mara. You're keeping something alive until it can be planted somewhere better. There's a difference. Write it down."*

Mara wrote it down. Eleven years later, in a dark room under a seed vault orbiting a dead colony, she wrote it down in the margin of her maintenance ledger, her pen pressing hard enough to tear the page: *Things come back.*

* * *

She listened to nine more memos that night. Anniversary dinner, where Junie's husband Tomas burned the rice and blamed the gravity—which was funny then, unbearably funny, in a way that made Mara sob into her sleeve forty years later because the gravity had failed for nine seconds once recently and she had thought of nothing but her sister while the tea hung in the air. A garden tour. An argument with a neighbor about a fence, conducted with such theatrical outrage that Mara could hear the neighbor laughing off-mic. A memo that was just wind—the real wind, dome-vented, moving across open ground—because Junie had written: *"You've been in vacuum so long, Mara-girl, I'm sending you weather."*

Idris came down twice. Both times he stood at the bottom of the ladder without speaking, read the shape of her shoulders, and went back up. On his third descent he brought tea and set it beside her without a word, and stayed, sitting on the floor with his back against the core housing like a man keeping vigil, saying nothing, asking nothing, until dawn-cycle dimmed the work lights.

It was Sefu who finally said what none of them would.

He found her on the fourth day, still working backward through the memos, now into years she had never shared with her sister—years when Mara had stopped writing as often, buried in vault certifications, telling herself Junie was busy, the colony was thriving, there would be time. Sefu stood in the doorway holding two ration bars he didn't offer, and said, gently, in the voice of a man who had walked his own childhood block through glassed ruins:

"You're circling it."

"I'm being thorough."

"Mara." He crouched to her eye level. He had his family's manifest page folded in his breast pocket; she had seen him take it out and refold it every night, the creases going soft as cloth. "There's a memo dated the last day. Ninety minutes before impact, per the telemetry timestamps. You're listening to everything except the one that matters."

"I know when it is," she said. "I've known since the index loaded. I can see it right there. *Junie—final voice memo.wav.* I look at it every time the list refreshes."

"And?"

"And it's ninety minutes long, Sefu. Ninety minutes before—" Her voice did something she hated, cracked sideways. "Whatever she said in it, it's the last thing. Once I hear it, there's no version of my sister after that. Every other memo has another one after it. That one just... stops."

Sefu nodded slowly. He didn't tell her she was wrong to wait. He had spent fourteen chapters of his own grief doing exactly the same arithmetic with different numbers.

"But here's what I keep coming back to," he said. "My mother's last transmission to me—I've played it maybe six times in forty years. And every time, it hurts worse, not less. But every time, I also get to hear her say my name again." He unfolded the manifest page, smoothed it on his knee out of habit. "The Compact took everything else from us. Don't let them take the hearing of it too. Ninety minutes is ninety minutes. That's not a dying message, Mara. That's a whole evening of your sister being alive. They don't get to shrink it down to the impact."

Mara looked at the file listing. *Junie—final voice memo.wav.* Duration: 88 minutes, 41 seconds.

Her sister had talked for nearly an hour and a half, ninety minutes before the end, and had no idea. Had probably been making tea. Had probably complained about the tomatoes.

"Stay?" Mara asked.

"Wouldn't miss it," Sefu said, and settled onto the floor beside Idris's empty tea cup like a man taking his post.

* * *

She pressed play at the start of night-cycle, when the vault hummed at its quietest, and Junie Pemberton's voice filled the sublevel one last time.

*"Okay. Memo 4,097. Long one tonight, so settle in, Mara-girl, or skip to the end, I know how you hate preamble."*

A pause. The sound of a kettle. Of a chair scraping. Of ordinary life, preserved in amber, ninety minutes from annihilation.

*"Big news. Huge news. I've been sitting on it for three weeks because I wanted to tell you properly and properly takes preparation, and also because every time I rehearse it I cry, which is undignified, so I figured I'd do it in audio where you can't see my face."*

Mara's breath caught. Beside her, Sefu went very still.

*"The relocation petition went through. Ours—the garden district's. Forty families, approved by the colonial council, forwarded up-chain. We're getting our own settlement grant. New site, southern hemisphere, real open ground—not dome-adjacent, actual dirt under actual sky, well, sky-colored ceiling, but bigger. Room for orchards, Mara. Orchards. I put your name on the application as intended co-settler. Don't argue. I already filed it. You'd have a house with a south-facing window and enough land to plant every seed in that ridiculous vault of yours, and I am officially informing you that you're coming. Not asking. Informing."*

Her sister laughed—that laugh again, the one from the first memo, and Mara realized she was hearing it bookend now, the same laugh at the beginning of the story and the end of it, and there was nothing between those two sounds anymore except forty years of silence and a weapon with a Compact serial number.

*"Tomas thinks I should give you time to decide. Tomas is a saint and a coward. I told him: my sister has spent eleven years guarding other people's futures in a freezer. It's our turn. Come grow something, Mara. Come grow it with me."*

The recording rustled—Junie walking, her breath quickening, out into the evening streets of the colony. Mara could hear other voices passing, children, a vendor calling closing prices, the whole living soundscape of Halcyon's Reach on its final night, unmarked, unsuspecting, golden.

*"I'm walking home the long way. The plaza smells like fried dough because of the festival setup—you'd love it, there are paper lanterns, actual paper, someone's grandmother smuggled the paper in her luggage fifteen years ago and they only bring them out for Founding Night. Tomorrow night, actually. You'd have loved it. I saved you a lantern. It's sitting on my kitchen table right now with your name on it, because I am an optimist and a hoarder and your big sister."*

A long pause. When Junie spoke again, her voice had dropped, gone soft and serious in the way it did perhaps twice a year.

*"Last thing. Then I'll let you go, I know you're on shift. Mara—if anything ever happens to me, and I'm not being morbid, I'm being a gardener, gardeners think about endings professionally—but if anything ever happens, I need you to know the tomatoes came back. Remember? Dead shipment, frozen, written off. Came back anyway. Whatever they tell you happened here, whatever story gets filed—look at the third row. Look at what comes up when nobody planted it. That's how you'll know the truth of a place. Not the official record. What grows back."*

Static. A door chime. Junie's voice, distant, turning away from the recorder: *"Coming, coming—"*

And then eighty seconds of her sister's kitchen, ambient, unremarkable: water running, a cabinet closing, Junie humming something tuneless and happy, the sound of a woman making tea in her home on the last ordinary night of her life—

—and the file ended. Not with impact. Not with screaming. It ended with humming, cut clean at the archive boundary, ninety minutes before the weapons fell, because the backup cycle had closed and no one had thought to reopen it, because no one knew they needed to.

Mara sat in the dark for a long time.

Sefu's hand found her shoulder and rested there, heavy and human, and she let it. Above them, faint through the deck plating, she could hear Idris moving around the galley, keeping his vigil by noise. The vault hummed its eternal hum. Somewhere in the quantum lattice beneath her feet, forty thousand lives waited in the glass, and one of them had saved her a paper lantern with her name on it, and the lantern had burned with everything else, and no one had ever told her.

"They voted to stay," Mara said finally. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "The council. Chapter of their own—it's in the second core. They were ordered to relocate and they voted to stay, together, and Junie was filing petitions to bring *me* out here, to the same ground—" She stopped. Started again. "They weren't collateral. They weren't a failed experiment. They were a community that chose each other, and the Compact melted them for ore access and called it ecology."

"Yes," Sefu said simply.

"So here's what happens now." Mara wiped her face with both hands, briskly, the way she did before maintenance shifts, and turned back to the console. "Every memo. Every file. All forty-one of hers, and then everyone else's—all of it. The recital kid, the birthdays, the dogs, the lanterns. The second core goes to broadcast prep with the rest. Chen's testimony, the telemetry, the weapons signature—and this. The personal archives. The whole heart of the Reach, unencrypted, going to every station in the system at once."

Idris had appeared at the bottom of the ladder. He looked between them, reading the air. "The personal files will make it undeniable," he said carefully. "But Mara—those are private moments. Families. Some of those people have living relatives on stations who don't know—"

"That's exactly why," Mara said. "The Compact killed them twice, Idris. Once with the weapon and once with the erasure. Nobody got to mourn because nobody got to know. Forty thousand people deserve to be grieved by name, out loud, in public, by strangers who never met them." She touched the console, over the file listing, over *Junie—final voice memo.wav*. "My sister spent her last ninety minutes planning a future and saving me a lantern. The least I can do is make sure the whole system hears her humming."

No one argued. The work lights came up. Somewhere far above, the deep-space array waited in its cradle, patient as a planted row.

It was Idris, climbing back toward the surface, who paused on the ladder and said it quietly, almost to himself:

"There's a problem. The fourth core—Chen's case notes—it needs an Iris-level archivist key. Living clearance. Mine died the day I defected." He looked down at them from the rungs, and in the half-light his face was pale and set, a man standing at the edge of a line he had been approaching for weeks. "But my mentor's biometric template is still in the Compact archives. And I can still reach the archives remotely—for now."

Mara looked up at him, her sister's laughter still ringing in her ears, and understood exactly what he was offering to burn.

"Do it," she said.
