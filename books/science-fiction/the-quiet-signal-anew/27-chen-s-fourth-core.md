## 27. Chen's Fourth Core

The fourth core sat apart from the others, in a recessed alcove at the far end of the sublevel, as if whoever had installed it wanted it to be found last—or not at all. Its housing was the same matte ceramic as its siblings, but the seal around it bore a different sigil: the iris-and-key of the Compact's Archival Directorate, stamped in gold that had not faded in forty years.

Idris stood before it for a long time without touching anything.

The others had gone up. Sefu was sleeping off the painkillers, his shoulder bound and aching beneath the dressing; Mara was in the greenhouse level, doing what she always did when the vault's truths grew too heavy—tending living things, speaking softly to seedlings that could not hear her. The Kestrel's engineer, Wren, had retreated to the ship with a thermos of something bitter and a look that said she'd had enough of dead people's secrets for one lifetime.

So it was Idris alone who knelt before the alcove with a hand-lamp, reading the access plate like scripture.

*IRIS-LEVEL CLEARANCE REQUIRED. LIVING ARCHIVIST KEY ONLY.*

"Living," he said aloud, to no one. The word echoed faintly off the ceramic.

He knew what it meant. He had known since the moment they'd cracked the third core and heard the colony council vote, voice by trembling voice, to stay. Chen's case notes—the full testimony, the names of every coerced witness, the schematic of the weapon test—were sealed behind a lock designed to admit only a person who existed, whose biometric signature matched an active Compact archivist on record, *right now*, this hour, this minute. Not a template pulled from cold storage. Not a ghost. A heartbeat, verified against the Directorate's live registry.

His own credentials had been revoked eleven months ago, the day he walked out of the Meridian Archive Annex with nothing but his tablet and the growing certainty that he could not stay complicit another season. He had felt the revocation arrive like a door closing inside his own chest—a soft chime, a form letter, *Access privileges terminated effective immediately.* Forty-one years of service reduced to a line item.

But there was another way. And he had been avoiding looking at it directly for three days, the way you avoid looking at a wound you already know needs stitching.

* * *

Her name was Odile Ferreiro. She had been his mentor once, back when he was a junior archivist with ink-stained fingers and a head full of the Compact's founding myths. She had taught him classification theory, provenance chains, the sacred grammar of records. "A record is a promise," she used to say, tapping the table with one blunt finger. "Every entry is someone saying: *this happened, and I saw it, and I will not let it be forgotten.* Break that promise and you break everything the Compact pretends to be."

She was seventy-three now. Retired, mostly—she kept a small consulting practice authenticating historical documents for museums, lived in a garden apartment in the Meridian capital district, drank tea at four o'clock every afternoon with the ritual precision of a woman who had outlived two husbands and most of her illusions. Her biometric template would still be in the Directorate's registry. Retired archivists retained emeritus status; their keys remained valid for consultation purposes until death formally struck them from the rolls.

Which meant that to open Chen's core, Idris needed to do one of two things.

Either he persuaded Odile Ferreiro to come to a seed vault at the edge of inhabited space and press her living hand to a lock that would expose her to charges of sedition—

Or he stole her.

Not her body. Her *identity*. Her template, her clearance signature, the cryptographic ghost of her that lived in Compact archives. He would reach into the Directorate's systems, extract the biometric key of the woman who had taught him that records were promises, and use it to break a seal she had never consented to open.

And if the extraction went wrong—if the warden AI noticed, if the trace came back to him—Odile would be the name on the flag. An emeritus archivist's credentials used in an unauthorized breach. They would come for her first. They would strip her pension, her apartment, her name from the rolls while they investigated. At seventy-three, with bad knees and a heart that her doctor had told her twice to stop arguing with, she would spend whatever years remained answering questions in rooms without windows.

All because her student had decided her ghost was more useful than her peace.

* * *

He climbed up to the operations level near midnight, vault-time, and found Mara sitting cross-legged on the floor of the comms room, surrounded by printouts. Actual paper—she still printed things, a habit from a childhood on a station where screens failed weekly. She looked up when he came in, and whatever she saw in his face made her set down the pages.

"You've been standing in front of that core for six hours," she said.

"It requires a living key." He lowered himself onto the floor across from her, joints protesting. "Iris-level. Active or emeritus. Verified against the live registry."

Mara waited. Eleven years of solitude had made her a patient audience; she never rushed a silence.

"There's exactly one person whose template I can reach," Idris said. "My mentor. Odile Ferreiro. She trained me. She taught me—" he laughed, short and bitter, "—she taught me that a record is a promise. That breaking the chain of custody is the one unforgivable sin."

"And using her identity to open a sealed testimony about a massacre?"

"That's not breaking a promise," he said. "That's keeping one. That's the argument I keep making to myself, anyway. It sounds very noble when I say it in my own head." He rubbed his eyes. "It doesn't sound noble at all when I imagine explaining it to her."

Mara picked up one of her printouts, turned it over, set it down again. "Have you considered just asking her?"

"I did consider it. For about ten seconds." He shook his head. "A burst transmission to the capital district is traceable. Even encrypted, even routed through three relays, Yara's people swear by relays but relays keep logs. If I contact her, I paint her. If I don't contact her and simply take the template, there's a chance—small, but real—that the extraction looks like a routine archival query. Emeritus templates get pulled for authentication work all the time. Museums, estates, litigation. If I'm careful, it reads as noise."

"And if you're not careful?"

"Then it reads as theft, and the warden traces it, and my mentor spends her last years under investigation for sins she didn't commit." He stared at the ceiling, at the old conduit runs Mara had labeled in her careful handwriting years ago. "There's a version of this where I'm a thief. There's a version where I'm worse than a thief—I'm a man who burns the one teacher who ever believed in him to warm his hands at her fire."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was low and even.

"I lied to a Compact survey officer," she said. "First lie of my life, forty-one days ago. I handed him doctored sensor logs and watched him thank me for them. I thought about it for weeks afterward—not whether it was right, but what kind of person it made me. And here's what I decided." She turned to face him fully. "The lie wasn't mine to be proud of. But it also wasn't mine to be destroyed by. It belonged to the truth underneath it. Eleven years of logs, four thousand entries, and every single one of them true—and that's what gave me the right to tell one lie. The truth was the account I was drawing against."

"And if the account is empty?" Idris asked. "If all I have is forty-one years of cataloging other people's truths? I never testified to anything, Mara. I filed things. I shelved things. I watched the Reach file go into the sealed cabinet and I signed the transfer slip and I went to lunch."

"Yes," Mara said. "You did. And now you're three hundred light-minutes from anywhere, planning to burn your last connection to your old life to unseal the testimony of a woman who died trying to do what you're doing." She tilted her head. "Chen hid her copies in a seed vault because no one audits gardens. Maybe she'd have liked knowing it took another archivist's ghost to get them out."

* * *

He worked through the night cycle in the comms room, building the query.

The Directorate's remote archive interface was a thing he knew the way a musician knows an instrument—forty-one years of muscle memory, of query syntax learned before some of its current maintainers were born. His access was revoked, but the interface itself remembered him; legacy authentication hooks, deprecated but never fully removed, because removing them cost money and nobody audited gardens, and nobody audited deprecated code either. Chen had understood that principle. So, apparently, had the Directorate's own engineers.

He would enter through a maintenance portal used by contracted restoration vendors—credentials belonging to a firm called Halvorsen Textual Recovery, whose certificate he had legitimately handled during a joint project nine years ago. From there, a standard emeritus-template retrieval request: *authentication support, estate litigation, Ferreiro O., emeritus status verification.* Museums requested these constantly. The warden AI, MERIDIAN-9, triaged millions of such requests a quarter. The trick was to be boring. Boring was invisible.

He built the query three times and tore it apart three times. Each draft had a flaw—an over-specific timestamp, a routing path that touched a node too close to his old annex, a metadata field left blank where a real vendor would have filled it. On the fourth draft, he made himself think like Odile: meticulous, unhurried, allergic to elegance. Real archival traffic was dull. Dull was safe.

At 0340 vault-time, with the query assembled and staged, he stopped.

His hands hovered over the console. The room hummed its old, patient hum. Somewhere below, the fourth core waited in its alcove with its gold iris sigil, holding a dead woman's testimony behind a lock that demanded a living hand.

"If I do this," he said quietly—to Mara, who had fallen asleep against the wall an hour ago, or perhaps to himself, or perhaps to Odile, wherever she was drinking her four o'clock tea—"there's no version after this where I'm still an archivist. There's no going back to filing things. This isn't a mistake I make and recover from. This is who I am now."

The console waited. Consoles are patient; that's the terrible thing about them.

He thought about the child's recital video, hours before the silence. About the council voting to stay. About Mara's sister, ninety minutes from impact, making plans. About Sefu's shoulder, stitched and aching under its dressing. About Chen, whoever she had been, sealing her notes into garden infrastructure and trusting that someday someone would dig.

*A record is a promise,* Odile had said. *This happened, and I saw it, and I will not let it be forgotten.*

He pressed execute.

The query went out at lightspeed, wrapped in Halvorsen's dull certificate, dressed in the plainest clothes he could sew for it. For ninety seconds the console showed nothing but a progress bar crawling through handshake protocols, and Idris became aware of his own heartbeat for the first time in years—each beat distinct, counted, expensive.

Then the bar stuttered.

A secondary verification prompt bloomed on the screen: *EMERITUS TEMPLATE ACCESS — ADDITIONAL AUTHORIZATION REQUIRED. REASON CODE?*

His stomach dropped. Reason codes were logged, indexed, reviewed. Whatever he typed became a permanent entry in a system designed by people whose entire profession was remembering.

His fingers hovered. Then, slowly, deliberately, he typed:

*PROVENANCE VERIFICATION — HISTORICAL TESTIMONY, CASE 40-KR-117.*

Case 40-KR-117. The Reach inquiry number. The inquiry that had never truly happened, the staged investigation, the coerced witnesses. He was entering the massacre's own file number as the reason for retrieving the key that would unseal it. Let the log show it. Let some future auditor—some future Chen—stumble across the entry and wonder why an emeritus template was pulled to verify testimony from a case everyone pretended was closed.

Let it be a breadcrumb. Let it be a confession. If he was going to cross the line, he would cross it leaving his name written plainly on the far side.

He submitted the code.

The prompt cleared. The progress bar resumed its crawl. Sixty seconds. Forty. Twenty.

At 0352 vault-time, the console chimed, soft and obscene in the quiet room, and the file arrived: FERREIRO_O_EMERITUS.btk. Biometric template, 4.1 megabytes, containing thirty years of keystroke cadence, retinal topology, palm vein structure—the complete cryptographic portrait of a living woman, compressed into something small enough to hold between two fingers of data.

Mara stirred against the wall. "Did it work?"

"It worked." His voice sounded strange to him. Flat. Far away. "I have her."

Mara crossed the room and looked at the screen, then at him. She didn't congratulate him. She put her hand briefly on his shoulder—the same shoulder, he noticed distantly, where Sefu had taken a bullet's graze two days ago—and left it there.

"What happens to her now?" she asked.

"Nothing, if I was boring enough." He exhaled. "The query will sit in a review queue for weeks. By then it won't matter. Nothing will matter, one way or the other, in a few days." He looked at the file glowing on the screen. "But if I wasn't boring enough—if someone pulls that thread—they'll find reason code 40-KR-117, and they'll find me, and they'll find her name attached to mine."

"Then we'd better move fast," Mara said.

* * *

They descended together, the two of them, down the unmapped stairwell into the cold of the sublevel. Idris carried the template on a isolated data slate, air-gapped from everything, cradled like an ember. Mara carried the hand-lamp.

The fourth core received the spoofed key without complaint. The iris sigil flared gold, held, and dissolved. The seal ring rotated with a sound like a held breath finally released.

Inside, the storage matrix lit in slow waves, sector by sector, and the index began to resolve across the display:

**CHEN, R. — CASE FILE 40-KR-117 — PERSONAL ARCHIVE — DO NOT DUPLICATE — DO NOT TRANSMIT — TRUST NO DIRECTORATE CHANNEL**

Beneath it, a directory tree unfolded: witness depositions, raw telemetry, ordnance schematics, correspondence, audio recordings. Hundreds of files. Forty years of a dead woman's war, preserved in glass and quantum lattice, waiting.

And at the top of the tree, flagged for priority playback, a single audio file dated the night before Chen vanished. Its filename was simple, almost gentle:

*for whoever finds this.wav*

Idris looked at Mara. Mara looked at Idris.

"We should wake the others," she said.

"No," Idris said, and his hand was already moving. "Some things you listen to first. Alone. Before you know what you're asking them to carry."

He pressed play, and in the cold blue light of the fourth core, a voice none of them had heard in forty years began to speak.
