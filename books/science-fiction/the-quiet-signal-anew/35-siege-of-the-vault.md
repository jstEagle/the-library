## 35. Siege of the Vault

The first shell came down through the storm layer like a falling star that had lost its patience.

Mara felt it before she heard it—a pressure change in the vault's deep bones, a shudder running up through eleven kilometers of rock and ice and engineered silence, and then the sound arrived: a long, rolling thunder that was not thunder at all but atmosphere being torn open by something moving far too fast for anything made by careful hands.

She was on the operations deck when it hit, standing at the master console with Idris, watching three crimson signatures crawl across the tactical display like wounds opening in the dark. The broadcast had ended four hours ago. Nine seconds of truth, flung to four hundred stations, and now the Compact had come to collect the debt.

"Kinetic penetrators," Idris said, his voice flat in the way she'd learned meant he was holding himself together with both hands. "Non-nuclear. They want the vault intact. They want *us* intact." He almost smiled. "They want us to stand trial."

"They want us quiet," Mara said.

Above them, the sky answered.

* * *

Yara Quill had once told Sefu Adeyemi, over cheap liquor in a dockside bar on Vesper Drift, that she would never die for anyone's principles. She'd said it with the particular pride of a woman who had survived thirty years in the black precisely because she believed nothing enough to bleed for it.

Now her ship—the *Cormorant's Debt*, hull scarred from two decades of smuggling runs—banked hard over the vault's northern ridge with its weapons array burning hot, and Yara discovered that principles were less like beliefs and more like debts. You didn't choose them. They chose you, usually at the worst possible moment.

"Incoming, incoming, *incoming*—" Her gunner, a wiry Belter kid named Osei who'd been with her six years, was shouting numbers she didn't need shouted. Two Compact interceptors had peeled off from the cruiser formation and were dropping toward them through the storm, sleek as knives, patient as knives.

"I see them," Yara said. "Osei, give me everything on the port battery."

"That'll drain the capacitors—"

"*Everything*, Osei."

The *Debt* rolled, thrusters screaming, and put itself between the descending interceptors and the vault's exposed antenna array—the same ancient dish that had caught the flicker signal all those months ago, still radiating the last echoes of the broadcast out into the system like a lighthouse refusing to go dark. The Compact couldn't unsend what had been sent. But they could destroy the transmitter. They could erase the source. And erasure, Yara knew—had always known—was the one thing the Compact did better than anyone in human history.

The port battery fired. The capacitors screamed. One interceptor tumbled end over end, venting atmosphere, its pilot punching clear in a spray of emergency foam. The other swung wide and came back around, and this time it wasn't alone.

"Second flight inbound," Osei said. His voice had gone very calm, the way young men's voices did when they were frightened past the capacity for panic. "Captain, we can't hold this corridor."

"We're not holding it," Yara said, hands moving across the board. "We're renting it. Thirty more minutes is all they need. Get me the *Kestrel* on tightband."

* * *

Down in the vault's throat, the world was coming apart in slow motion.

Sefu ran through corridors he'd walked a hundred times in the past months—corridors lined with seed drawers, each one labeled in Mara's careful handwriting, each one containing a small paper envelope of the future—and behind him the ceiling shed dust in gray curtains. Every impact above sent a new tremor through the structure. The vault had been built to survive centuries of neglect. It had not been built to survive this.

"The sublevel lift is cycling," Ade called from ahead, the Kestrel's engineer waving them forward. "Idris says the blast doors will hold. He says the quantum cores are shielded under forty meters of basalt. He says—" She stopped, catching herself. "He says a lot of things."

"He's an archivist," Sefu said. "Saying things is how we know he's alive."

They were carrying the cores themselves—the four quantum storage drums, each one heavy as a coffin, cradled in padded harnesses that Mara had improvised from cargo webbing. Chen's testimony. The council session. The colony's final transmissions. His sister's face, mid-laugh, ninety minutes before the end. All of it in their arms, all of it heavier than any mass could account for.

The third shell struck closer than the others. The corridor lights died, came back amber, died again. In the emergency glow, Sefu saw the others clearly for a moment: Ade's jaw set, sweat cutting lines through the dust on her face; old Tomas hauling his harness strap with both hands, breathing in the ragged rhythm of a man who'd run too far too fast at an age when no one should ask him to; Mara, ahead of them all, moving with a certainty that still startled him sometimes—the invisible woman who had spent eleven years learning every centimeter of this place, now leading them through its bones in the dark.

And then the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. A section of it—three meters of composite paneling shaken loose by a near miss, groaning, tilting, beginning its long slow fall toward the passage where Ade and the second core were just passing beneath.

Sefu didn't decide anything. Later, he would search his memory for the decision and find only motion—his legs driving forward, his shoulder hitting Ade's hip and spinning her clear, his arms going up over the second core as if his body could be a roof, as if flesh and bone were architecture, as if forty years of grief had been building toward this single stupid, perfect gesture.

The panel caught him across the shoulders and the back of the head.

The world went white, then silent, then full of voices calling his name from a great distance, as if from the bottom of a well.

* * *

Mara knelt in the dust and the amber light and pressed her hands against Sefu's back because that was where the blood was, warm and shocking, soaking through the torn fabric of his jacket faster than her mind could process.

"Sefu. Sefu, look at me."

His eyes were open. That was the first thing. He was on his side on the deck plating, and his eyes were open, and they found hers with an effort that she could see cost him something.

"The core," he said. His voice came out wrong, wet and thin. "Is the core—"

"It's fine. It's fine, you idiot, you landed on it." She was laughing or crying or both; she genuinely could not have said which. "You absolute fool, you used yourself as a shield for a *drum*—"

"Forty years," he whispered. His hand moved, clumsy, and closed around her wrist. "Forty years I carried them. Their names. Every day, like you and your logs." A breath that whistled. "Wasn't going to drop them now."

"Tomas! Ade! Help me lift this—gently, *gently*—" They got the paneling off him in pieces, and Mara tore open his jacket with hands that shook and then, deliberately, stopped shaking, because shaking was a luxury and she had spent eleven years learning to do without luxuries. The wound ran diagonally from his left shoulder blade to his spine, deep, ugly, bleeding in pulses. Not arterial. Not immediately fatal. Immediately bad.

"Med kit," she said. "Ade, the Kestrel's kit, go. Idris—"

"I'm here." He dropped to his knees beside her, sleeves already rolled, and she realized with a distant part of her mind that the archivist knew field medicine, that of course he did, that everyone in this story had been carrying skills for a war none of them had chosen. "Pressure here. We need to get him to the sublevel—the med bay in the old staff section still has power."

"Can he walk?"

"He's going to walk," Sefu said, "or you're going to carry me, and I've seen you try to move a seed drawer, Pemberton, so let's not embarrass ourselves."

Even Tomas laughed, a short broken sound, and then the lights went out entirely and stayed out, and somewhere far above them another shell found the earth.

* * *

On the surface, the *Cormorant's Debt* was dying by degrees.

Yara flew it anyway. The starboard engine had taken a glancing hit twenty minutes ago and was running rough, coughing plasma, threatening at irregular intervals to simply stop. Her hull was scored in half a dozen places. Osei had a burn dressing on his forearm and hadn't mentioned it. And still they held the corridor, weaving between the ridgelines, drawing fire, spending fuel and hull and luck like a gambler who understood exactly which hand was losing and kept betting it anyway.

"Third flight's breaking off," Osei reported. "They're regrouping with the cruiser. Captain—we did it. The ground team's underground. We can disengage."

Yara looked at the tactical display. Three crimson signatures. One battered smuggler with a limping engine and a crew of nine. The arithmetic was not complicated. It had never been complicated, not once in her entire mercenary life, and she had built that life on never pretending otherwise.

"Disengage," she repeated.

"You said thirty minutes. It's been fifty-one."

"I know what I said." She banked the *Debt* around the eastern ridge, low, engines snarling, and looked down at the vault complex spread beneath her—the scarred landing apron, the shattered greenhouse domes, the antenna array still standing, still transmitting its stubborn whisper into the dark. Somewhere under all of it, a man who had once given her free passage when her ship was dead in the water and her crew was starving, who had never once mentioned it in fifteen years until she showed up at his airlock ready to repay a debt she'd pretended didn't exist—that man was underground with a hole in his back, guarding the memory of a murdered world.

"Osei," she said. "New heading. We're going to make one more pass."

"Captain—"

"Not to fight. To be seen. Big, loud, obvious pass right over their formation." She grinned, and it was not a kind grin. "Every second they spend tracking me is a second they're not digging after our people. Let them watch the radar. Let them wonder what we're up to. Fear costs them attention, and attention is the only currency they've got left to spend."

Osei looked at her for a long moment. Then he turned back to his console and started feeding power to the transponder, broadcasting their position to every Compact sensor in range, loud and proud and suicidal.

"You know," he said, "you told me once you'd never die for anyone's principles."

"I'm not planning to die," Yara said, and pushed the throttles forward. "I'm planning to be *extremely inconvenient*. There's a difference. Ask anyone who's ever tried to collect a debt from me."

The *Cormorant's Debt* rose over the ridgeline, trailing smoke and defiance, and every eye on every Compact warship turned to follow it.

* * *

They got Sefu down into the sublevel on a stretcher made from a seed-drawer shelf and two cargo straps, Mara and Tomas taking the corners while Ade cleared the way. The old staff med bay was exactly what Idris had promised: cramped, dim, stocked with supplies that had passed their expiration dates before Mara was born—but functional. Clean. Quiet, in the way of places buried under forty meters of stone.

The bombardment sounded different down here. Muffled. Almost polite. Like weather on another planet.

Mara worked while Idris assisted, sealing the wound, threading sutures by hand because the auto-doc's cartridge had crystallized decades ago. Sefu drifted in and out, surfacing to make terrible jokes, sinking again before anyone could properly respond. At some point Ade took his hand and simply held it, saying nothing, and he gripped back with the fierce unconscious strength of a man whose body understood what his mind kept forgetting: that he was not done yet.

It was during one of these silences, Mara bent over her work, that Idris spoke quietly beside her.

"The tribunal feed is live."

She didn't look up. "What?"

"The emergency tribunal. The Compact convened it two hours ago—public channel, mandatory carriage on all stations." His voice had a strange quality she'd never heard in it before. Wonder, maybe. Or terror. "Your nine seconds, Mara. They're watching it replay on every screen in the system. The Vesser ships have been impounded. Their own escort crews refused orders." He paused. "There are people in the streets on Meridian Prime. Hundreds of thousands of them. They're chanting your sister's name—they pulled the names from the manifest, all of them, reading them aloud in shifts."

Mara tied off the final suture. Her hands were steady. Outside, the bombardment continued, shells falling on a vault that had already done its work, on seeds that had already become something larger than plants, on witnesses who could no longer be unwitnessed.

"They're still shooting," she said.

"Yes."

"Then it isn't over." She straightened, wiped her hands, and looked down at Sefu—breathing, alive, his face slack with exhaustion and pain and something underneath both that looked almost like peace. Then she looked at Idris, and at Ade, and at old Tomas leaning in the doorway with his chest heaving, and she felt the strangest thing rise in her chest, familiar as her own heartbeat: the caretaker's instinct. The refusal to leave.

"They'll breach the sublevel within the hour," Idris said. "Blast doors won't stop a determined boarding team. We should move deeper—there are the old cold-storage galleries, the maintenance tunnels—"

"No," Mara said.

Everyone looked at her.

"We don't run anymore." She touched the nearest quantum core, resting in its harness against the wall, cool and patient as a sleeping animal. "Everything that matters is in this room. Everything that matters is *in this room*, and if they want it, they're going to have to walk past us to get it—and every minute they spend down here is a minute the whole system spends watching them do it." She met Idris's eyes. "Let them come. Let the tribunal watch them do it. Let every station in the system watch the Compact send soldiers into a seed vault to murder the people who told the truth."

For a moment, nobody breathed.

Then, from the stretcher, weak and cracked and utterly unmistakable, Sefu Adeyemi began to laugh.

"There she is," he murmured, closing his eyes. "The woman who guarded a garden for eleven years." His hand found Mara's and squeezed. "Give 'em hell, caretaker."

Far above them, muffled by forty meters of stone, the bombardment paused—and in the sudden ringing silence, every person in the room heard it: the deep mechanical groan of the vault's main doors, grinding open for the first time in eleven years.

Someone was coming down.
