## 37. Ash and Seed

The smoke found her before the fire did.

It came down through the ventilation shafts in slow gray ribbons, tasting of scorched polymer and something older—the vault's own breath, eleven years of filtered air and carefully maintained humidity now unraveling into heat. Mara stood in the third sublevel with her back against a seed rack, listening to the ceiling groan like a ship in weather, and understood that she was watching the upper half of her life die.

The assault had lasted forty minutes. It felt like forty years. Above her, Compact breaching teams had cut through the loading bay, through the cataloging hall where she'd spent four thousand mornings weighing canisters and logging germination rates, through the archive rooms where Idris had spread Chen's testimony across every available screen. They had come to erase. That was all they knew how to do. The Vessers' name was ash on every feed in the system by now—*impounded mid-orbit*, *escort crews refusing orders*, *emergency tribunal convened*—but institutions burned slowly from the top, and the people at the bottom still followed the last orders they'd been given. So they had come for the vault. For the seeds. For the quietest thing in the system, because it had stopped being quiet.

Mara had watched it happen on the sublevel monitors: the first breach charges blooming white against the loading bay doors, the intruder alerts cascading red, Yara's ships screaming overhead in atmosphere—a sound no ship should make, a sound like tearing cloth the size of the sky—and then the fire suppression systems failing one by one as power was cut, section by section, floor by floor.

Sefu was in the medbay of the Kestrel with a plasma burn down his left side and three of Yara's crew keeping him conscious through sheer force of argument. Idris was at the relay console, hands shaking, feeding the last of Chen's testimony to a tribunal that might or might not survive its own convening. And Mara stood alone among four hundred thousand seeds and did the arithmetic she had been dreading since the first alarm.

The vault could not be saved. She had known it the moment the second suppression system failed. The upper levels were a chimney now, drawing fire downward toward the climate-controlled heart of the facility, and every minute she spent grieving was a minute the fire spent winning.

She wiped her face with the back of her hand. Her cheeks were wet and she couldn't remember starting to cry.

"All right," she said aloud, to no one, to everyone, to the racks of sleeping life surrounding her. "All right. We're leaving."

* * *

There is no way to choose between children. Mara learned this in the next hour, moving through the sublevels with a cargo sled and a headlamp and a list that kept rewriting itself in her hands.

The vault's collection was organized by purpose, and purpose was supposed to make choosing simple. Priority One: staple crops. Wheat from the old Terran steppes, rice paddies preserved in amber silence, potatoes bred across ten thousand generations of patient selection. Priority Two: heritage varieties, the odd and beautiful branches of the tree—purple carrots and black barley, apples that tasted of anise, corn whose kernels gleamed like oil slicks. Priority Three: everything else, the wild cousins, the forgotten kin, the seeds collected from places that no longer existed even before the Compact existed, from valleys drowned and forests cleared and islands gone under rising water.

Priority Three was the largest category. Priority Three was most of the world.

The sled held maybe six hundred kilograms if she overloaded it, and the lift to the surface would take ninety seconds each trip, and she did not know how many trips she had. The fire above was patient but not merciful. Already the temperature readouts along the walls were climbing in increments that made her stomach tighten—eighteen degrees, twenty-one, twenty-four. The climate systems were dead. The vault was becoming what it had always defied: just another place where things ended.

She started with the staples. Of course she did. Wheat and rice and maize and potato, the four pillars, loaded in their silver canisters with the Halcyon Consortium's old logo stamped on the lids—*FEED THE FUTURE*, the slogan said, in letters that had seemed naive when she arrived and now seemed like a prayer someone had answered without noticing. She stacked them with the efficiency of eleven years' practice, her hands knowing the weight of each canister before her mind registered it, and she told herself the tightness in her chest was only exertion.

Then she reached the heritage section, and the arithmetic broke.

A canister of Martian-adapted rye sat beside a canister of Terran saffron crocus corms, and there was room for one. Which one? The rye would feed a settlement dome; the crocus was worth more per gram than gold and fed nothing but beauty. The ledger in her head—the caretaker's ledger, the one she'd kept for eleven years, the one that weighed everything in grams and viability percentages—said rye. But her hands had already closed around the crocus, and she stood there hating herself, and then understanding, dimly, that this was the whole lesson of the last month: you cannot reduce the world to priorities. The world insists on being particular. The world insists on being loved in specifics.

She took both. She left behind a case of ornamental gourds and felt like a murderer anyway.

"Take the Ancestral Row," Idris's voice said in her earpiece, thin through the static. He was still at the relay, still transmitting, refusing to move until the tribunal confirmed receipt. "Mara—the Ancestral Row. The wild relatives. Everything else can be regrown from what we carry out, but those lines exist nowhere else. Not on any station. Not anywhere."

"I know," she said. "I know."

But she didn't go to the Ancestral Row first. She went instead to a small rack in the corner of Sublevel Two, unmarked on any official inventory, holding forty-seven canisters that she had labeled herself, in her own handwriting, during her first year in the vault. Seeds from Halcyon's Reach. Not from the Compact's collection—from the colonists themselves. Sefu's mother had sent them, decades ago, a care package of garden seeds from the colony's communal plots: tomatoes that grew sweet in the Reach's engineered light, beans that climbed the trellises outside the school, sunflowers tall enough that children hid beneath them. The colony had grown food, yes, but it had also grown gardens, and gardens were how people said *we are staying here* in a language older than any treaty.

The canisters were warm from the ambient heat. She pressed one against her forehead for a long moment, feeling the warmth like a hand, and then she loaded all forty-seven onto the sled, and she did not feel guilty about the space they occupied, and she knew that someday she would have to explain why, and she also knew the explanation would be simple:

Because these were not seeds from a place. These were seeds from people. And people were what she was trying to save.

* * *

The second trip up, the lift stalled between levels for eleven seconds.

She counted them. Of course she counted them. Nine seconds had started all of this—nine seconds of falling tea and floating canisters and a signal blinking in the dark—and now the universe offered her eleven more, suspended in a steel box while somewhere above her the world filled with smoke. She stood in the dark with her arms full of wheat and thought: *this is what my sister felt.* Ninety minutes before impact, Amara had recorded her last log, hopeful, planning, sending love across the void to a sister who wouldn't hear it for forty years. She had not known. Or maybe she had known—maybe everyone on the Reach had known, in the way people know storms are coming, in the way the bodies in the glass had arranged themselves in shelter positions, waiting for evacuation orders that never came.

They had known something. They had stayed anyway. They had voted to stay.

The lift shuddered and resumed. Mara breathed out.

On the surface, the world was wrong. The sky over the vault was the color of a bruise, streaked with contrails and the drifting smoke of Yara's damaged escort ship limping toward the horizon on thrusters that coughed blue flame. The Kestrel sat on its landing struts two hundred meters from the entrance ramp, hull scorched, cargo bay open, and two of Yara's crew were running a makeshift conveyor from the ramp to the bay, shouting to each other over the wind. The air smelled of ozone and burning insulation and, faintly, impossibly, of rain coming—a storm front rolling in off the southern flats, as if the planet itself had decided to attend.

"Three more trips," she said to the conveyor operator, a broad woman with a chemical burn on her jaw whose name Mara didn't know and who had nonetheless spent the afternoon hauling seeds for strangers. "Maybe four."

"Make it three," the woman said. "Yara says the cruisers are standing down but she doesn't trust standing down. Nobody stands down. They reload." She took the canisters from the sled with surprising gentleness, reading the labels as she stacked them. "Wheat. Rice. What's this one?"

"Saffron," Mara said. "It's a flower. It's—" She stopped, surprised by the catch in her own throat. "It's from Earth. Original stock. There's no other."

The woman looked at her, and something passed between them that needed no names—the look of one person handing another person a fragile thing and both of them understanding the weight. "Flower goes on top," the woman said. "Nothing stacks on the flower."

* * *

The third trip, the fire reached Sublevel One.

She heard it before she saw it—a new quality to the roar overhead, a wetness in the sound, and then the sprinkler heads in the corridor above her burst all at once, a false mercy, steam instead of relief, and the temperature warning on the wall turned from amber to red and began to pulse. Through the observation window into the archive level she could see flames moving along the ceiling in slow orange rivers, consuming the server racks, the display screens where Chen's testimony had glowed for days, the long tables where five people had once sat and argued about witness versus survival and chosen witness.

Her office was up there. Eleven years of daily logs—the physical backups, the drives she'd kept because digital felt too easy, too erasable—were in a cabinet beside her desk. She had already copied everything to the cores and the broadcast array had already flung it across four hundred stations; the data was safe, distributed, beyond anyone's reach. But the cabinet itself, the object, the room where she had spoken into a microphone every morning for four thousand days saying *good morning, Halcyon's Reach, this is Mara Pemberton, day whatever-it-was*—

"Mara." Idris's voice, urgent now. "Get out. Tribunal has confirmed full receipt, all archives authenticated, the array's done its work. Get out. That's an order from the only person on this rock with authority over you, which is me, temporarily, by unanimous acclamation."

Despite everything, despite the smoke stinging her eyes, she laughed. "Noted, Archivist."

"The Ancestral Row," he said, softer. "Did you get it?"

She looked at the sled. Stacked beneath the staples and the Reach garden seeds and the saffron: the wild ancestors. Teosinte, the grass that became corn before human hands taught it ambition. Wild emmer wheat, the original covenant between grass and people. Rice's shy riverine forebears. The potato's bitter mountain root. Every crop humanity had ever loved, traced back to its first wild promise. She had wrapped each canister in thermal blanket material, and they sat there in the sled's belly like relics, like saints' bones, like the beginning of everything.

"Got it," she said.

"Then come home," Idris said, and the word *home* hung in the static between them, unexamined, undeniable, meaning the Kestrel, meaning the crew, meaning something that had never once meant the vault in eleven years and now meant it completely.

She took one last look down the length of Sublevel Two—the racks receding into smoke-haze, four hundred thousand canisters she could not carry, the whole library of green patience that she had guarded through eleven years of silence—and she did the thing she had not let herself do until now. She spoke to them.

"Thank you," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle and she let it. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry I couldn't—I tried. You're going to grow somewhere else. I promise you that. Somewhere better than here." A pause. The fire agreed with her by dropping a beam somewhere far above, a deep structural sigh. "Grow somewhere better than here."

Then she ran.

* * *

The fourth trip never happened. There wasn't time, and she knew it, and she made peace with it on the lift ride down from the surface after delivering the third load—made peace the way you make peace with anything, badly and all at once, in the space of ninety seconds.

When she came back up the final time, empty-handed, the storm had arrived. Rain fell in fat warm drops, hissing against the vault's outer shell, and the smoke column bent eastward under it, and through the haze she could see the Compact cruisers in high orbit as points of reflected light, motionless, watchful, doing exactly what Yara had predicted: reloading. Standing down was not surrender. Standing down was a sentence with a comma in it.

But the comma was enough. The comma was everything.

The Kestrel's cargo bay was full. Full of wheat and rice and maize and potato, full of saffron on top where nothing stacked on the flower, full of teosinte and emmer and the forty-seven canisters of Halcyon's Reach, full of the quantum cores wrapped in padding with Sefu's bloodstained jacket draped over them because he had insisted, from the medbay, that the cores travel under guard. Full of the future, packed tight, smelling of rain and smoke.

Mara stood at the bottom of the ramp in the rain and looked back at the vault.

Fire had taken the upper levels entirely now. Through the shattered loading bay she could see the glow, orange and total, and the structure groaning its long surrender, and she understood that by morning there would be nothing but a blackened shell and a story. People would come here. Pilgrims, historians, the merely curious. They would stand where she stood and they would say *this is where the caretaker lived, this is where the signal was quiet for eleven years,* and the ruin would become a kind of text, and she would be a character in it rather than a person, and that was the price of witness too. She had seen it happen to Chen. She was not afraid of it anymore.

"You did good," said a voice beside her.

Yara Quill stood at her shoulder, rain streaming off her cropped hair, one arm in a sling, watching the fire with the flat professional assessment of a woman who had burned a lot of things and rarely regretted them. "For what it's worth," Yara said, "I've torched six facilities in my career. Warehouses, labs, one monastery—long story. This is the first one I've ever helped save instead." She nodded at the smoking vault. "Your seeds are going to outlive every one of us, Pemberton. Including me, and I plan to be extremely annoying about it for decades."

"They'll need somewhere to grow," Mara said.

"Yeah." Yara was quiet for a moment. "About that. My people pulled the orbital survey data before we jumped in-system. The Reach ruins—the soil under the glass, where the domes fused? Radiation's decayed past viable thresholds. Forty years of rain on volcanic substrate." She looked sideways at Mara, and for the first time since Mara had known her, Yara Quill looked uncertain. "It would grow. If anyone wanted to try."

Mara felt the words land somewhere below her ribs, in the place where grief and hope had been sharing cramped quarters for weeks. The Reach. The fused streets where Sefu had walked his childhood block. The shelter positions in the glass. The relay buoy turning its broken mayday overhead. Planting seeds there—in that soil, over those streets, among the witnesses in glass—it was either desecration or resurrection, and she suspected it was both, and she suspected that was the only honest way anything had ever been built.

"We'd need to ask the dead," she said quietly.

"Or bring them with us," Yara said. She tapped the ramp with her boot, indicating the cargo hold, the cores, the forty-seven canisters of garden seeds from a colony that had voted to stay. "Seems like we've got the means."

Inside the Kestrel, the medbay door opened and Sefu appeared, upright by willpower alone, bandaged, gray-faced, leaning on the doorframe. Behind him, Idris. Behind Idris, the rest of them—the crew, Yara's wounded, the whole improbable congregation that a nine-second flicker had assembled out of loneliness and guilt and grief.

Sefu looked at Mara across the rain. He didn't smile—he was too hurt, and the ruins were too fresh—but he inclined his head slightly, the way you greet someone at a graveside, and Mara understood the question he was asking before he asked it, because it was the same question living inside her chest, growing there like something planted:

*Do we go back? Do we take them home?*

The vault groaned behind her, settling deeper into its burning. Ahead of her, the Kestrel hummed on its struts, full of seeds and testimony and the saved remains of four hundred thousand futures. The rain came down on all of it equally, on the ruin and the ship and the woman standing between them, and somewhere far overhead, past the clouds, past the cruisers, a broken relay buoy turned its patient circle above a dead city that was about to stop being dead.

Mara Pemberton wiped the rain from her face, took a breath that tasted of ash and water, and stepped aboard.

"Set a course," she said. "For Halcyon's Reach."
