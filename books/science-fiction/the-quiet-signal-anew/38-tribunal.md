## 38. Tribunal

The tribunal convened at 0900 station time, which meant nothing on a world where the sun came through smoke the color of old pewter. But Mara had kept station time for eleven years—had woken to its chime, logged to its rhythm, measured her tea by it—and so she woke to it now, in the half-collapsed med bay of the Halcyon Seed Vault, and dressed with the same care she'd once given her daily recordings. Clean shirt. Hair braided back. The small ritual of a woman who understood that how you presented yourself was itself a kind of testimony.

They had set up the link in what remained of the vault's communications room. The upper levels were still smoking; the smell of scorched polymer and burned seed stock hung in everything, sweetish and wrong, like flowers left too long in a vase. Sefu lay two rooms away, his leg splinted and packed with biofoam, refusing the stronger painkillers because, he said, he wanted to hear every word. Idris sat beside Mara at the console, his hands folded, his revoked credentials displayed openly on the table before him like a confession he no longer needed to make.

"You don't have to do this," he said, for perhaps the fourth time since dawn.

"I know." She checked the feed. Four hundred stations. The tribunal chamber on Meridian Prime, all pale stone and procedural dignity, already filling with advocates and clerks and, somewhere behind the gallery glass, the Vesser family's lawyers arranging their faces into expressions of grave concern. "That's why I'm doing it."

The connection opened with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

Presiding Arbiter Osei was an older woman with a voice like dry paper, and she began, as institutions always begin, with procedure. The emergency tribunal of the Meridian Compact, convened under Article Nine, in the matter of the destruction of the settlement known as Halcyon's Reach. Witnesses would be heard. Evidence would be entered. The record would be preserved.

"The record will be preserved," Osei repeated, and something in her tone suggested she knew exactly how often that promise had been made before, and broken.

Mara testified from a room full of ash, and the contrast was not lost on anyone watching. The tribunal's own feed showed her seated before a wall panel cracked down the middle, a scorch mark fanning across the ceiling above her head like a black fern. Behind her, visible through a shattered bulkhead window, the vault's eastern wing still smoldered against the morning.

"State your name and function for the record."

"Mara Pemberton. Caretaker, Halcyon Seed Vault, Meridian Charter 114-C. Eleven years, four months, six days."

"And your duties consisted of...?"

"Seed preservation. Climate maintenance. Structural integrity checks. And daily audio logs." She paused. "Four thousand and ninety-three of them, as of yesterday."

A clerk somewhere off-frame murmured. Osei leaned forward slightly. "Daily logs. To whom were these logs addressed?"

"To the colony of Halcyon's Reach." Mara kept her voice level. It took effort. "The colony stopped responding forty years ago, before my tenure. I was never told why. I was told only to keep recording—that the archive might be needed someday, if contact resumed. So I recorded. Weather reports. Inventory counts. Vault status. And—" here her throat tightened, and she let it, because hiding it would have been a lie of omission, and she had decided, lying to the Compact survey officer aside, that she was finished with lies—"and things I thought they should know. What the auroras looked like. When the ice birds came back one spring. That I missed them."

The gallery was very quiet.

"Advocate Renn," Osei said, "you may proceed."

Renn was the tribunal's lead investigator, a compact man with the restless energy of someone who had spent three weeks reading four thousand hours of audio logs and had come out the other side changed. Mara had watched him do it—or rather, watched the progress bars crawl across the vault's restored systems while he listened, sometimes at triple speed, sometimes slowing to real time, once stopping entirely for forty minutes on a single entry from year three.

He rose. "Caretaker Pemberton. I'd like to enter into evidence the complete audio log series, entries one through four thousand ninety-two, along with associated vault telemetry, atmospheric records, and sensor data covering the eleven-year period of your tenure."

"So entered," said Osei.

"For the benefit of the tribunal," Renn continued, turning to face the chamber, "I want to be precise about what this evidence is. It is not dramatic. There are no confessions in it. No hidden transmissions, no intercepted orders. It is eleven years of a woman counting seeds and describing weather to people who never answered." He let that sit. "And that is precisely what makes it unimpeachable. Every entry is timestamped against vault atomic clocks calibrated to Compact standard. Every sensor reading cross-verifies against orbital telemetry we have independently confirmed. There is no gap in this record. Not one day. Not one hour. In eleven years, Caretaker Pemberton's vault recorded continuously—and in doing so, it recorded the night of the flicker. The nine-second gravitic event. Entry three thousand nine hundred eighty-one."

On the gallery feeds, someone requested playback.

Mara heard her own voice fill the tribunal chamber—the voice of a woman eight months younger than she was now, calm and slightly puzzled, narrating the strangest morning of her life: *Gravity fluctuation detected at 0417 local. Duration approximately nine seconds. All canisters secured, no loss of stock. Tea recovered. Noting anomalous antenna activity post-event; investigating.* Then the pause, then, softer: *Something is transmitting. First signal in eleven years. Logging coordinates.*

"That entry," Renn said, "correlates within 0.003 seconds of the weapons test conducted by the vessel *Vesser's Pride* on the date in question. The same waveform signature appears in the telemetry recovered from the relay buoy at Halcyon's Reach, in Archivist Chen's sealed case notes, and in the quantum core archives entered as evidence yesterday. Three independent sources. One weapon."

He turned back to Mara. "Caretaker, I have one question for you, and it is not about the flicker."

"Ask it."

"In four thousand logs, you addressed a colony that never answered. You described sunrises to the dead—or to people you believed dead. Why didn't you stop?"

Mara looked at the camera. Through it, she knew, four hundred stations were looking back—dockworkers on their breaks, students crammed into lecture halls, Compact clerks watching on screens they were supposed to be working behind. She thought of her sister, who had boarded a colony ship with a potted fern and a promise to send for her. She thought of Chen, who had hidden truth in garden infrastructure because no one audits gardens. She thought of the child's recital video, the small bowed figure, the applause.

"Because someone had to remember them properly," she said. "Not as a casualty figure. Not as an ecological failure. As people who liked certain songs and complained about the recycled air and grew tomatoes that were too ambitious for the hydroponics. The Compact erased them twice—once with a weapon, once with a file seal. My logs were the third version. The true one. Nobody ordered me to make it. That's what makes it evidence. It's just what happened, kept faithfully, by someone with no reason to lie."

She glanced at Idris, whose eyes were bright and wet. "Unlike some people in this proceeding," she added, "I had nothing to confess. Only something to keep."

* * *

They broke for recess at midday, and the feeds showed her what the system was doing with her words.

Idris had the public channels open on a secondary display, and neither of them pretended not to watch. On Ceres Deep, dockworkers had walked off shift—not in protest, the union statement read, but in observance. A school on Thessaly Station had suspended lessons to listen to the log entries in sequence, starting from entry one, and the teacher's note posted beneath the stream said simply: *We are going to hear the whole thing. It will take eleven years. We have time.* On Meridian Prime itself, outside the tribunal hall, a crowd had gathered that the Compact's own news service estimated at sixty thousand and growing, standing in the rain, holding printed images of colonists pulled from the restored archives. Faces. Thousands of faces, each one now named, each name now spoken aloud in rotation by volunteers who had begun reading the manifest at dawn and did not intend to stop until they reached the end.

"They're reading the manifest," Sefu said from the doorway, propped on crutches, his splinted leg stretched out behind him. His voice was rough. "All of it. Every name. They've got the Adeyemis queued up for tonight." He laughed, or something adjacent to laughter. "My mother hated crowds. She'd be furious. She'd be so furious."

"Is furious good, in this case?" Mara asked.

"It's the best thing there is."

Yara Quill appeared behind him, arms crossed, one of her ships' engineers patching the doorway's breached seal behind her with the bored efficiency of a person who had done far stranger repairs that week. "Your tribunal," she said, "is being carried live on two hundred stations with a combined audience the Compact hasn't seen since the last eclipse lottery. My comms officer says your testimony clip has more replays than the eclipse." She looked at Mara with an expression that on another face might have softened into respect. "You sounded like a woman reading a grocery list. That's why it works. Don't change it."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good." Yara turned to go, then paused. "For the record—I sold your position to the Compact once. False trajectory. Best trade I ever made." She tapped the doorframe twice and was gone.

In the afternoon session, the tribunal heard Archivist Emerita Chen.

She appeared by secure link from a location the Compact had agreed not to disclose—a thin woman in her seventies with white hair cropped short and the particular stillness of someone who had spent forty years being careful. Her testimony lasted four hours. She spoke in the flat, precise cadence of a career archivist, and she dismantled the cover-up joint by joint: the staged inquiry, the coerced witnesses, the court order sealing all colony data, the mining claim filed by House Vesser eleven days after the silence, the gravitic-lattice ore surveys that predated the "ecological collapse" narrative by two years.

At one point, an advocate for the Vesser family interrupted to question her credibility, noting that she had acted without authorization, had stolen archival materials, had vanished rather than submit to review.

Chen considered him for a long moment.

"Yes," she said. "All of that is true. I broke every rule you're citing. And I want the record to show why." She lifted a data slate. "Because I filed a formal objection through proper channels first. Here is the acknowledgment of receipt. Here is the response: none. Because I petitioned three oversight committees. Here are the receipts. Here are the non-responses. I spent six years doing it correctly, Advocate, while I knew what had been done to a hundred and fourteen thousand people. So then I did it incorrectly. And the incorrect way worked in six weeks." She set the slate down. "You may enter both timelines into evidence. I recommend comparing them."

The gallery feed showed the advocate sitting down slowly, as though his own skeleton had become unreliable.

* * *

It was evening when they called Mara back.

The final session of the day was devoted to the question of remedy, and it was there, in the procedural thickets of reparations and jurisdiction, that the Vesser advocates made their stand. The family's senior counsel, a silver-haired man named Corvane, argued with impeccable legality: whatever had occurred forty years ago occurred under prior administrations; current corporate structures bore no liability; the mining claims were validly filed; and the tribunal, however moved by testimony, could not legislate justice retroactively without destabilizing the legal order itself.

"Destabilizing the legal order," Osei repeated, in her paper-dry voice. "Counsel, the legal order was destabilized the moment this tribunal's evidence became public. We are merely documenting the tremors."

But Corvane pressed, and his argument had teeth: the Compact's courts ran on precedent, and precedent required that punishment attach to persons, not abstractions. Who, precisely, gave the order? Who fired? The responsible officers were dead or scattered. The family itself—could a tribunal prove that the living Vessers knew?

It was then that Osei did something no one expected.

She turned to the feed. "Caretaker Pemberton. You are still linked. Before we recess, I have a question for you—not from the advocates, not from the investigators. From the bench." Her old eyes found the camera. "For eleven years, you kept a record no one asked you to keep, for people who could not thank you. This tribunal exists because of that record. So I ask you, as the longest continuous witness this Compact has ever produced: what does remedy look like? Not law. Witness. Tell us what you see."

The room went silent. Even the ambient hum of the vault's failing systems seemed to hold its breath.

Mara thought about it honestly, which is to say she let the silence stretch until the answer arrived on its own, the way answers did when you'd spent eleven years talking to emptiness and learned to wait out your own noise.

"When I first found the signal after the flicker," she said, "I thought it was hope. A voice from the dead zone. It took me weeks to understand it was a mayday, corrupted, cycling forever—an automated buoy screaming into the dark for forty years because someone cut its transmission short and never told it to stop." Her hands were steady on the console. "That's what erasure looks like. Not silence. A scream on a loop that no one is permitted to hear.

"So here is what remedy looks like to me. It looks like the manifest being read aloud in the rain on Meridian Prime—it looks like names, spoken, in order, all of them. It looks like the school on Thessaly listening to eleven years of weather reports because the weather mattered to someone. It looks like seeds planted where domes used to be. It looks like the Vesser claim voided—not fined, not negotiated, *voided*—and the lattice ore under Halcyon's Reach declared a memorial in perpetuity, so that no one, ever, can look at that ground and see anything but what happened there.

"And it looks like this—" she touched the console, and the vault's systems, patched and limping, brought up the number she had prepared, the number she had counted through the night while Sefu slept and Idris drafted briefs and the vault smoked around her— "four thousand ninety-three logs. Entered into the permanent public record. Unsealed. Unedited. Available to every citizen of every station, forever, so that the third version of the story—the true one—outlives everyone in this chamber, including me.

"You asked what a witness sees, Arbiter. A witness sees that justice isn't a verdict. It's a memory that refuses to die quietly. Everything else—the tribunals, the sentences, the reforms—is just the shape memory leaves behind."

Osei was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her dry-paper voice had something else in it, worn smooth by decades of procedure but unmistakable now.

"So entered," she said. "All of it. We recess until tomorrow, when this tribunal will consider the disposition of the Vesser claims." She paused, and looked directly into the lens, and for one unguarded instant she was not an arbiter but a witness herself. "Caretaker Pemberton. Eleven years ago, someone told you to keep recording, and no one thanked you. On behalf of a hundred and fourteen thousand people who cannot: thank you."

The feed closed.

* * *

Afterward, the vault was dark except for the emergency strips and the glow of the consoles, and the four of them sat in the ruined communications room sharing a meal that Yara's engineer had conjured from salvage: rehydrated protein, actual tea—Mara's last hoarded tin, opened at last for an occasion worth it—and a jar of preserved tomatoes that Sefu produced from the Kestrel's stores without explanation, though everyone understood.

"Hydroponic," he said. "Reach strain. My father's line. I've been keeping them going for twenty years without knowing why." He passed the jar around. "Now I know why."

They ate in the kind of silence that isn't empty. Outside the cracked bulkhead, the smoke had thinned, and the first stars were coming through—hard, cold, indifferent, and somehow, tonight, bearable.

"Tomorrow they decide," Idris said. He was turning his tea tin cup slowly, watching the light move in it. "Void the claims, refer criminal charges, maybe even dissolve House Vesser's charter. Or they find some procedural escape hatch and we're back to fighting in the streets." He looked up. "Either way, it's out. It can't be re-sealed. Four hundred stations heard your voice coming out of a burning building, telling the truth like a grocery list. That part's done. Whatever happens tomorrow, that part's done."

Mara nodded, but she was looking at the manifest display still open on the auxiliary screen—the long column of names, the reading volunteers' progress marker showing they had reached the letter K. Somewhere in the rain on Meridian Prime, a stranger was saying *Kessiah Adeyemi* aloud, and somewhere in the dark of space a buoy that had screamed for forty years was finally, officially, heard.

Her sister's name was further down the list. Under L. She would hear it spoken by a stranger's voice soon. She found she was ready.

Sefu shifted on his crutches and winced, then grinned at her. "So, caretaker. Day four thousand ninety-four tomorrow. You logging it?"

Mara looked around the ruined room—at Idris with his burned identity and steady eyes, at Sefu with his father's tomatoes, at the scorch marks and the seed canisters stacked for planting and the deep-space array that had carried her voice to every corner of the system in nine seconds.

"Yeah," she said. "I think the address list has changed, though."

Outside, the stars kept coming out, one by one, like names being read.
