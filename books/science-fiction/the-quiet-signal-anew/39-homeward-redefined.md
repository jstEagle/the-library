## 39. Homeward, Redefined

The wind on Halcyon's Reach smelled different now. Sefu Adeyemi noticed it first, standing at the edge of the landing field where the Kestrel had set down forty years after his family's last departure—the only one in the family, as it turned out, ever to leave. The wind used to carry the mineral tang of the processing plants and the green breath of the hydroponic domes. Now it carried ash that had finished becoming soil, and something else beneath it: the faint, impossible sweetness of turned earth.

They had come with three ships and no plan beyond the first one. Yara Quill's two armed vessels hung in high orbit like patient birds of prey, keeping watch for Compact survey craft that, for once, did not come. Down here, the work was quieter than any of them expected.

Sefu walked the street where he'd learned to ride a bicycle, its pavement fused to glass by heat that had arrived faster than sound. His boots crunched over what had been a playground. He didn't kneel. He had done his kneeling already, back in Chapter Eighteen of his own private grief, when he'd pressed his palm flat against the doorway of his childhood home and felt forty years of silence push back. Today he just walked, and behind him came the others—Idris with a soil analyzer, Mara with a crate of seed canisters against her hip, two of Yara's people whose names he was still learning, and a dozen Compact defectors who had requested this assignment the way penitents request hard labor.

"Substrate's viable," Idris announced, crouching by a crack in the vitrified ground where windblown dust had gathered into something almost like dirt. "Forty years of weathering. The glass is breaking down. There's carbon here." He looked up, and there was wonder in it, the archivist's wonder, the same look he got when a sealed file opened. "They're still here, Sefu. In the chemistry. Everything they grew, everything they composted—it's in the soil."

Sefu crouched beside him and took a handful. It was gray and gritty and it smelled like the beginning of something.

"Then we start where they stopped," he said.

* * *

Mara Pemberton had spent eleven years learning how to be alone in a place full of life waiting to happen. She knew seeds the way some people knew scripture—dormant wheat from the old Terran collections, cold-hardy barley bred for marginal light, nitrogen-fixing legumes, a heritage apple cultivar whose scion wood had traveled farther than most colonists ever would. She had cataloged them all, four thousand times over, whispering their names into a recorder for an audience of ghosts.

Now she knelt in the ruins of Halcyon's Reach and put her hands directly into the ground.

The first row went in along the old commons, where the market stalls had stood. The defectors worked in pairs, one opening the soil with hand tools because machines felt wrong here, obscene somehow; the other placing each seed with a care that bordered on liturgy. Nobody had told them to be reverent. They simply were. Mara watched a young woman in a patched Compact uniform—a logistics clerk who had walked out of her posting the day the broadcast reached her station—cup a single barley seed in both palms before setting it down, the way you might hold water in a drought country.

"You don't have to be gentle," Mara said quietly. "Seeds want to grow. That's the whole trick of them."

"I know," the woman said. "It's not for the seed."

Mara understood. It was for the ones underneath. For the bodies in shelter positions behind fused doors, the families who had hidden when the warning came and been denied the evacuation that should have followed. The whole colony was a grave, and they were planting above it, which was either desecration or the only honest form of mourning left. She had decided, somewhere between the tribunal summons and the last armful of canisters carried out of the burning vault, that it was mourning. Gardens were how the living kept talking to the dead.

By midday the first row stretched thirty meters along the commons. Someone—Iris, one of Yara's gunners, a woman with burn-scarred hands and no patience for ceremony—had rigged drip lines from the Kestrel's water recycler. The water came out gray with recycled minerals and fell onto the soil dark and clean.

Sefu stood at the row's end, holding the manifest tablet. He had read names aloud every night since the broadcast, but today he read them differently—not as an accusation flung at the Compact, but as a dedication spoken over the ground. "...Okafor, Adaeze. Okafor, Chidi. Okafor, Nnamdi, age six." His own family, planted into the air of the place that had killed them. When he finished the O's, he looked up and found half the work crews had stopped to listen, heads bowed over their seed trays.

"Keep reading," someone said. So he did, letter by letter, while the seeds went into the earth one name at a time.

* * *

Idris Okafor had spent his career believing that records were the highest form of memory—that a file, properly preserved, was a soul made durable. He had burned his own identity to prove it, breaching the Compact archives, extracting Chen's template seconds before lockdown, and he had never regretted it, not even in the tribunal chamber when the prosecutors tried to make his crime the story instead of the massacre.

But standing in the ruins now, watching defectors plant wheat over the graves of people his institution had murdered, he understood that records were only the skeleton of memory. What fleshed it out was this: hands in soil, names said aloud, water given freely to ground that owed no one anything.

He found Mara at dusk, apart from the others, at the head of the first row. She held a small urn—ceramic, unadorned, the kind issued by station morgues for remains recovered from sealed sites. Her sister's remains, recovered from the sublevel archive where Amara Pemberton had apparently carried them herself, or arranged for them to be carried, in the last days before the end. Mara had told him almost nothing about it. Some griefs are load-bearing walls; you don't knock on them.

"She wanted me to come here," Mara said, without turning. "Her last log. Ninety minutes before impact. She said she'd send for me. She said the Reach was going to be a real place someday, not an outpost. She wanted me to see it."

Idris said nothing. Eleven years of logs had taught him when silence was the correct response.

"It took me eleven years and a massacre to get here." Mara laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle. "She'd be furious. She was always furious about how slow I was. How careful. She used to say I'd miss my own life waiting for permission to live it."

"And now?"

Mara opened the urn. The wind took the ashes gently, the way it takes everything on this world now—without violence, without appetite, just movement. Gray dust drifted out over the first planted row, over the barley and the wheat and the legumes, settling into the cracks in the glass, into the carbon-rich soil that Idris had sworn was still alive with the colony's chemistry.

"She's in the ground with them now," Mara said. "With everyone. With the soil they made." She closed her hand around the empty urn and finally turned to face him, and her eyes were wet and steady at the same time, which Idris would remember for the rest of his life as the truest expression he had ever seen on a human face. "I guarded seeds for eleven years, Idris. I thought that was the work. Keeping things safe so someday someone could use them." She looked down the row, where the defectors were still planting by lamplight, where Sefu's voice was still reading names into the dark. "But seeds aren't for keeping. They're for giving away. That's the whole point of them. I just never let myself be planted anywhere."

"You're planted now," Idris said.

"Yes," Mara said. "I think I am."

* * *

Yara Quill came down from orbit on the third day, ostensibly to inspect her investment, actually because she could no longer stand being up there while other people did the meaningful work. She landed the shuttle crooked, complained about the dust, insulted the drip-line engineering, and then spent six hours hauling water without being asked, and if anyone mentioned it she threatened to space them.

She found Sefu at the row's end at sunset, sitting on the foundation stone of what had been the colony school. He was tired in the way that meant happy, which she distrusted on principle.

"This is a terrible idea," she told him, sitting down beside him. "A settlement on a massacre site, inside Compact territory, staffed by defectors and smugglers. No defenses, no supply chain, no legal standing. It'll fail in a year."

"Probably," Sefu agreed.

"The tribunal hasn't even finished. The Vesser assets are frozen, but the family has friends. Half the Compact council wants this place paved over and forgotten properly this time."

"Probably," Sefu said again.

Yara glared at him. "You're not going to argue with me?"

"Nope."

"Because you know I'm right, or because you know I'll help anyway?"

Sefu smiled at the horizon, where the sun was going down behind the hills that had sheltered the colony's first greenhouse, forty years ago, in another life. "Both. Same as always, Yara."

She muttered something profane and affectionate and pulled a flask from her jacket, and they passed it back and forth while the stars came out over the first planted field on Halcyon's Reach in four decades. Below them, lamplight moved among the rows. The defectors had stopped working and were sitting in the soil they'd turned, eating together, and someone was singing—something old, something Terran, something about rivers—and the sound carried across the ruins the way sound carries in quiet places, which is to say completely.

"My people are asking to stay," Yara said eventually. "Two of them. Maybe three. Iris wants to run the water systems." She shook her head. "I run a smuggling outfit, Sefu. I don't run a charity."

"No," Sefu agreed. "You run a family. You just bill them for it."

Yara laughed despite herself, and the laugh echoed off the fused glass of the ruined domes, and somewhere in the dark below, the singing paused and then resumed, louder.

* * *

On the seventh day, the first shoot appeared.

It was barley, in the third row, and the logistics clerk—young Okonkwo, who had walked off her station—found it at dawn and woke the entire settlement with her shouting. They came running in various states of dress and disbelief, and they crowded around a single green thread no taller than a fingernail, pushing up through forty years of ash and glass and grief, and nobody spoke for a long time.

Mara knelt last. She touched the soil around it, checking moisture with fingers that had checked ten thousand canisters, and found it good.

That evening she set up the recorder on the foundation stone of the old school, facing the field, facing the people. The daily log. Four thousand and forty-one days, or something near it—she had lost exact count in the fire, and found she didn't mind. The ritual remained. The ritual was hers.

But tonight, when she pressed record, she wasn't alone. Sefu sat cross-legged at her left. Idris at her right, tablet balanced on his knees, already drafting tomorrow's planting schedule. Young Okonkwo leaned against the stone. Iris, Yara's gunner-turned-hydrologist, stood with her scarred hands in her pockets. Others drifted close, settling into the frame of the little ceremony without being invited, because belonging doesn't require invitation, only presence.

"This is Mara Pemberton, caretaker of the Halcyon Seed Vault, logging day—" she paused, and smiled, and let the number go. "Logging day. Location: Halcyon's Reach. Population: thirty-one and rising. First germination confirmed. The dead are named, the ground is open, and the seeds are in."

She looked down the row toward the place where the ashes had gone into the soil, where her sister was becoming, slowly, patiently, part of the green thing that would feed whoever came next.

"We're home," she said. "Whoever's listening—we're home."

And across the system, in a hundred small rooms and a hundred open channels, the answers started coming in before she'd even lowered the recorder.
