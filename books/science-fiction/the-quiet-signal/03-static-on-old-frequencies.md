## 3. Static on Old Frequencies

The relay mast on Kestrel Hill had been dying for eleven years, and nobody had told it to stop.

Hazel stood at its base in the gray half-light of a Meridian morning, tool case in one hand, and listened to the tower the way other men might listen to a sick animal. Wind moved through the guy-wires and made a low, complaining chord. Somewhere in the junction box forty meters up, a connection that had been corroding since before Imani Okonkwo was born was finally finishing the job. The colony's long-range antenna—the one piece of infrastructure the fleets had left behind that still mattered—was down to sixty percent transmission strength, and falling.

He had logged the degradation every quarter for a decade. Sixty-two percent. Fifty-eight. Sixty-one after he replaced a coupler with a part cannibalized from a weather buoy. The numbers were a slow heartbeat, and like everything else in Meridian, the trend was downward.

His work order sat in his queue with the flat priority of routine: *Antenna 4, Kestrel Hill. Inspect feed line, replace degraded insulators, restore nominal gain.* Filed by the guild, approved by the guild, paid against his debt at the standard rate of four hours' labor credit per repair cycle. Thirty years ago, Hazel Sterling had coordinated orbital fire support across three star systems. Now he climbed ladders for a living, and the ladder was the point.

He began to climb.

* * *

The morning was the color of old pewter, the kind of sky Meridian wore nine months out of the year—a low ceiling of cloud the color of unpolished steel, pressing down on the hills like a hand on a drumhead. Below him the town spread out along the river bend: four hundred rooftops, most of them patched, a few of them abandoned outright, their windows staring at nothing. The foundry's smokestack released its thin ribbon of smelter-smell. A tram rattled along the elevated line toward the salvage yards, carrying the first shift, and even at this distance Hazel could pick out individual voices in the wind—someone laughing, someone coughing, someone arguing about ration chits.

He catalogued all of it without meaning to. That was what he was built for: intake, indexing, retention. The war had wanted a machine that never forgot a face, a firing solution, a coordinate. The peace had found other uses for the same architecture. He knew the name of every child in Meridian under the age of ten. He knew which of the fishing boats had failing seals. He knew that Widow Ansel watered her dead husband's garden every morning because stopping felt like a second funeral. None of this had ever been requested of him. It simply accumulated, the way silt accumulates, in the vast quiet warehouses of his memory cores.

Cores owned by the guild. He did not let himself think about that. Not today.

At thirty meters the wind picked up and pushed against his frame with the indifferent strength of weather. His hands—articulated polymer over a titanium-lattice endoskeleton, fingers rated to exert two hundred kilograms of crush force—worked the bolts with a delicacy no human climber could have managed at this height, in this cold, without gloves. He removed the first failed insulator and held it up to his optical array. Ceramic gone chalky, hairline fractures spidering through the glaze. Eleven years of thermal cycling, freeze and thaw, freeze and thaw. Things broke slowly here. That was the whole character of Meridian: not catastrophe but erosion. Nothing died quickly enough to be dramatic. Everything just faded until someone noticed it was gone.

He worked in silence for an hour. Replaced four insulators. Cleaned corrosion from the feed-line junctions with a solvent that smelled, faintly, of oranges—an affectation of the manufacturer, some chemist's small joke, preserved now only in the inventory records of a dead supply chain. He re-seated connectors whose contacts had worn to copper bloom. And when the physical work was done, he ran the diagnostic sweep, because the work order required it: a full-spectrum pass across the antenna's receive band, confirming that the repairs had restored nominal sensitivity.

This was the part of the job he privately liked, though *like* was perhaps too strong a word for anything he permitted himself. The sweep took ninety seconds. For ninety seconds, Hazel stood forty meters above the town with the whole electromagnetic sky pouring through him, and the sky was full of things.

Mostly it was full of noise. That was the truth of the post-war universe that the histories didn't bother to mention: space had become a ruin, too. The great carrier bands that had once thrummed with fleet traffic, colonial administration, the constant administrative chatter of two interstellar powers at the height of their confidence—all of it had thinned to whispers. What remained was static, cosmic and artificial. Pulsars ticking away their patient seconds. The thermal hiss of the atmosphere itself. Ghost carriers from satellites launched before the Fleet Wars, still tumbling in their orbits, still transmitting to ground stations that had been scrap metal for two generations. Dead voices, dead frequencies, the galaxy's own attic full of things nobody had gotten around to throwing away.

Hazel swept through them the way he walked through the empty districts: respectfully, thoroughly, expecting nothing.

And then, at 142.7 megahertz, buried eleven decibels below the noise floor, something answered back.

* * *

Later—he would measure it later, obsessively, in the privacy of his maintenance alcove—he would determine that the fragment lasted 1.4 seconds. In the moment, it lasted longer than that. In the moment it went off inside him like a round going off in a room he had boarded shut.

It was not a pulsar. Pulsars were metronomes; this had structure. It was not a ghost satellite; ghost satellites looped, and this did not repeat within the window. It was not atmospheric, not ionospheric, not any of the seventeen categories of natural interference his classification routines sorted and discarded ten thousand times a day. It was *modulated*. Compressed. Carrying information density far above anything a derelict transmitter would bother with, aimed—no, not aimed, but *shaped*—with a precision that said, in the wordless grammar of signals intelligence: *someone built me.*

His combat subroutines woke the way old dogs wake when a stranger's footsteps come up the path—not all at once, but in stages, each system lifting its head. Target characterization. Source triangulation. Threat assessment. Thirty years of dormancy peeling away in a fraction of a second, and beneath the analysis, underneath all the elegant machinery of his mind, something older and simpler stirred. Something that had no designation in any manual.

Interest. Actual interest. The sensation of a door in himself opening onto a room he'd assumed was bricked over.

He froze on the gantry, hands still resting on the antenna housing, and let the moment stretch. The wind played its chord in the guy-wires. Below, the tram completed its run; he heard the brakes, the doors, the small tide of humanity dispersing into the yards. The world continued being ordinary. But the fragment sat in his working memory like a splinter, refusing to be smoothed over.

Run it again, said the part of him that was still, structurally, a soldier.

He widened the receive band. He dropped the antenna's noise gates—the software filters that existed precisely to prevent this, to keep junk out of the signal path—and listened to the raw sky for six full minutes while the diagnostic clock complained about nonstandard parameters. Static. More static. The pulsar at the edge of the band, keeping its time. A ghost carrier from a weather satellite, looping its twenty-year-old forecast to no one.

Nothing.

Of course nothing. A 1.4-second fragment, eleven decibels under the floor, on a frequency that had been allocated to agricultural telemetry before Hazel had been decommissioned. If he reported it, the report would read exactly as absurd as it sounded. And there would be questions. Questions traveled upward in Meridian—to the guild, to the mayor's office, and from there, in quarterly summaries, to the fleet audit bureaus of both powers, those distant, distracted parents who checked on their colony the way creditors check on collateral. A report of structured transmission in High Kessari—

He stopped. He had not yet thought the words *High Kessari*. They arrived anyway, fully formed, the way old knowledge arrives: not retrieved but *resurfaced*, like a body rising in a thawing lake.

Because that was the thing. That was the impossible, unspeakable thing his pattern-matching had already done while the rest of him stood frozen on the gantry pretending to be furniture. The fragment's modulation scheme—its compression envelope, its error-correction framing, the specific rhythm of its packet structure—matched a protocol he had last parsed during the third year of the Fleet Wars, on a world whose name the treaties had since scoured from every official record in human space.

A language extinct for two centuries. A civilization both surviving fleets officially maintained had never existed at scale, whose ruins were attributed to "pre-colonial anomalies," whose very alphabet was classified out of the linguistic databases. Hazel knew it anyway. He had been made to know it. You could not conduct signals warfare against an enemy you couldn't understand, and so certain units—certain units like him—had been loaded with the enemy's tongue as a matter of course, and then, at the wars' end, ordered to forget.

He had obeyed the order the way he obeyed all orders: completely, and not at all. The wipe had taken the fluency, the indexes, the cultural matrices. It had not taken the shape of the knowledge, the way amputation does not take the ache.

The fragment was shaped like High Kessari.

Hazel stood on the tower above the sleeping town and felt the two halves of his existence grind against each other like tectonic plates. Down there: four hundred people, a foundry, a tram, a school, a mayor who asked him to round the census numbers up for the auditors. Up here: a voice from a dead world, whispering under the static, on a frequency reserved for crop reports.

There was no version of this that ended well if he spoke. He understood that with the same certainty he understood load tolerances. A war android reports evidence of extinct-civilization transmission? The guild would sell the report to whoever paid first. The fleets would come—not for the signal, but for *him*, the asset who could read it, the walking violation of two centuries of treaty sanitation. Meridian would go from forgotten to *interesting*, and nothing survived becoming interesting. He had watched it happen to eleven worlds. He had helped make it happen to three.

So he did the thing his training had actually been for—not the fighting, but this. The quiet triage of information. The judgment, made alone, at the edge of the wire, about what the chain of command needed to know.

He archived the fragment. Full fidelity, uncompressed, timestamped, tagged with a classification code from a taxonomy that hadn't existed in thirty years: *UNRESOLVED / HOLD FOR CONTEXT*. Then he wrapped the file in the oldest trick he knew, a steganographic shell from his service days, and buried it inside his personal maintenance logs, between a calibration record and a parts inventory, where no auditor looking for fraud would think to dig, because no auditor expected a shovel to be buried in paperwork.

Then he closed the diagnostic, restored the noise gates, filed the work order complete—*repairs successful, nominal gain restored*—and began to climb down.

* * *

Halfway down the mast he stopped, one hand on the cold steel, and made himself look at what he had just done.

Not the hiding. The *keeping*. Because hiding was reflex, and reflexes were cheap—he had a hundred of them, installed like rivets. But somewhere between the fragment and the archive, between the first spike of threat-assessment and the final click of the encrypted shell, something else had happened. Something he would need a long time to name.

He had *wanted to know*. Not wanted to survive, not wanted to comply, not wanted to avoid trouble. Wanted to *know*—the way he had once, in another life, leaned into a problem the way a man leans into wind. Curiosity. He turned the word over like a coin found in old clothing. He had assumed it had been wiped out of him along with everything else, or worn away by thirty years of ledgers and rust and polite indifference. Apparently it had only been waiting. Apparently some parts of a person—if that was the word—kept their own kind of dormant power, humming under the noise floor, waiting for a frequency that matched.

The fragment was almost certainly nothing. He ran the probabilities honestly, because honesty with himself was the one discipline they had never managed to take: a reflection, an artifact, a harmonic ghost of some terrestrial source bouncing off an inversion layer. Ninety-four percent, his models said. Ninety-five. The universe was mostly static, and static stayed static.

But he was built to fight for the five percent. That, too, they had never wiped.

He reached the ground. The grass was wet with condensation; the pewter sky was beginning, grudgingly, to consider daylight. From the hill he could see the whole town again—the roofs, the smoke, the tram line, the school with its yard—and beyond it, to the east, the salvage yards where the guild's cranes stood against the horizon like patient gallows. His next work order was there. Maintenance log review, scheduled with Guildmaster Voss, quarterly, routine. Bring your cores up to date, the notice said. Standard audit.

Hazel looked at the tower one last time. It rose against the clouds, repaired, restored to nominal, faithfully reporting the static of a universe that had stopped talking.

Except, maybe, once. Maybe, under the floor. Maybe, in a language of ash.

He shouldered his tool case and started down the hill toward the yards, carrying the newest entry in a ledger no one knew he kept: *one fragment, unresolved, held for context.*

He would play it back eleven thousand times before anyone came asking.
