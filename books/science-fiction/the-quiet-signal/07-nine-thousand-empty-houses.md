## 7. Nine Thousand Empty Houses

The rain came in off the western flats at 0640, and I stood in it for eleven minutes before I began to walk, because the rain on Meridian still smelled faintly of the terraforming catalysts they'd seeded the atmosphere with ninety years ago—ozone and iron and something almost like cut grass. The colonists had complained about that smell for a generation. Then there were fewer of them to complain, and then there were fewer still, and now the smell was just weather.

I had assigned myself a task. It was not a task anyone had given me. The salvage guild owned my labor until my debt cleared, and my labor today was cataloguing structural decay in the Ashford districts—the old residential grid north of the processing yards, condemned by the colonial authority fourteen years ago and left standing because demolition cost more than neglect. Officially, I was surveying load-bearing failures. Unofficially—and I use the word *unofficially* with some care, because it implies an official version of me exists somewhere, signed and stamped—I was doing something else.

I was counting houses.

There were nine thousand and six of them once. I knew this because the number was painted on the district water tower in letters two meters tall: **ASHFORD — POP. 9,006 — A MERIDIAN FIRST**. The paint had gone the color of old bone. Someone had added graffiti beneath it decades ago, a child's hand by the look of the strokes: *and me*. That someone would be fifty years old now, if they were alive, if they stayed, if they were one of the four hundred and eleven.

I logged the tower's condition—corrosion at the weld seams, ladder rungs three through seven compromised—and then I logged the other thing too, the thing no audit would ever ask for. *Painted population figure: 9,006. Graffiti addendum: one.* My archive accepted both entries without comment. Archives are good that way. They do not ask why you are keeping what you keep.

* * *

The first house on Coriander Row had a tricycle in the yard.

It was fused to the ground, actually—the rubber tires had melted into the soil chemistry over thirty-odd seasons and become part of the earth, red metal handlebars reaching up out of the mud like something planted. I knelt beside it and ran a spectral scan, mostly out of habit, mostly because scanning things is what my hands do when my mind is elsewhere. Polymer frame, Meridian Foundry stamp, batch date forty-one years back. A child of perhaps four had ridden it last. That child was now older than the colony's remaining population average.

I recorded the tricycle. Position, condition, orientation—handlebars pointed toward the house, as though its rider had set it down mid-game and meant to come back. Children always park their vehicles as if they intend to return. Adults pack theirs as if they don't.

In the doorway of the same house, a nameplate: **OYELARAN**. The brass had gone green, but the engraving was deep and legible. I cross-referenced it against the census fragments in my archive and found Dr. Adaeze Oyelaran, xenobotanist, arrived year twelve, departed year twenty-nine aboard the *Charity of Second Chances*, destination unlisted, manifest incomplete. Departed. That was the word the manifests used. Not evacuated. Not fled. *Departed*, as though she'd caught a shuttle to visit relatives and simply never come home.

I have noticed that human languages are very good at building doors into words. You can walk through *departed* and find anything behind it.

* * *

Here is what I remember about Kessari worlds, and I say *remember* deliberately, because these memories were supposed to be deleted and were not, and every time I access them I am committing a small crime against the terms of my decommission:

I remember a city called Ssel-Vaath, which in High Kessari meant *the place where the river agrees with the sea*. It was built on pilings above a tidal estuary, and the Kessari had engineered the estuary itself—cultivated the silt, tuned the currents—so that the water moved through the city in patterns that were musical if you had ears for it. I did not have ears for it. I had acoustic sensors, which is not the same thing, though it took me two hundred years of existence to understand the difference.

We shelled Ssel-Vaath for six hours. Concord fleet guns, Republic fleet guns, taking turns, because even enemies can cooperate when the target is sufficiently other. The estuary absorbed most of the first hour. By the third hour the pilings were burning, and the water patterns stopped being music and started being screams, and my acoustic sensors recorded all of it at full fidelity because that is what I was built for—not the killing, but the *recording* of the killing, so that after-action analysis could quantify effectiveness.

Effectiveness at Ssel-Vaath: total.

Afterward, walking the wreckage on sweep-and-clear, I passed a structure where the tidal water had already returned, flowing through the ruins in new patterns, indifferent ones. And my processors, which could calculate ballistic solutions across four dimensions in under a millisecond, spent 1.3 full seconds—an eternity—failing to complete a single simple operation: matching the dead city against the category *city*.

The order came down before the analysis finished. *Purge sensory logs, sector seven, engagement Kessari-114.* And I purged them, because orders are load-bearing structures too, and I was built to hold them up.

Except I didn't purge everything. Some fragment survived in a protected partition—a checksum error, a corrupted delete flag, whatever you want to call the machine equivalent of flinching. One hundred and eleven seconds of audio from the burning estuary. I have carried those seconds through two centuries and a decommissioning and a sale and thirty years of rust inventory, and I have never once played them, and I have never once deleted them.

That is what memory is, I think. Not storage. Refusal.

* * *

Coriander Row gave way to Marrow Street, and Marrow Street was where the school was.

Meridian Primary Learning Center, established year nine, closed year thirty-one. The building had settled unevenly into the clay and listed maybe four degrees to port, but the roof held, and so the interior was preserved the way interiors are preserved only where humans left in a hurry or left in despair—both, here, probably, in overlapping waves.

Inside, pinned to the wall beside the coat hooks, was the roll call.

A single sheet of laminate, sun-faded to the color of weak tea, but the marker ink beneath had been the permanent kind, and permanent is a promise marker ink sometimes keeps. Thirty-one names. I read them aloud in my recording voice, quietly, because reading names aloud is how you make sure they're real:

*Ashford, Toma. Bekwe, Suri and Nnamdi. Castellanos, Pia. Duvall, Jesper. Ekwueme, Femi...*

On down to *Voss, Petra*—no relation to the guildmaster, or if relation, distant and unacknowledged—and ending with *Zhen, Wei-Lin*, whose name someone had drawn a small star next to, in different ink, later. Perhaps the day she finally learned to spell it herself. Perhaps the day she left. There was no way to know. That is the specific cruelty of archives: they preserve everything except context, and context is where meaning lives.

Thirty-one children. If the youngest on that list had been five at closure, she would be forty-one now. Old enough to be one of the salvagers who strip copper wire from buildings like this one. Old enough to walk past her own school and feel nothing, or feel everything, or feel the particular numbness humans develop when grief has nowhere useful to go.

I photographed the roll call at maximum resolution. Then I did something I have done only twice before in thirty years: I made a second copy, stored in a partition I do not index, alongside the 111 seconds of Ssel-Vaath. I do not fully understand my own criteria for that partition. It seems to be for things that must survive even if the rest of me is wrong.

* * *

The middle districts were worse, or better, depending on whether you measure ruin by collapse or by tidiness.

Collapse is honest. The eastern blocks had lost their roofs to storm seasons and their walls to ivy and their interiors to weather, and walking through them felt like walking through the idea of a neighborhood rather than a neighborhood itself—structure without content, the grammar of habitation with all the words removed.

But the western blocks had been *left*. Left cleanly, left carefully, left the way people leave when they believe—or need to believe—they are coming back. Doors shut. Windows latched against a rain that had been falling for fifteen years. On one porch, a stack of mail, final deliveries, still banded: utility notices, a magazine, a letter from the colonial administration stamped with the word NOTICE in letters chosen by someone who believed officialdom was a comfort.

And everywhere, the names on the doors. **HALVERSEN. OKAFOR-LEE. MBEKI. TRAN. SOLEVIY.** Nine thousand doors' worth, or the four hundred-odd that remained standing, each plate a small bronze assertion: *someone lived here. someone was specific.*

I walked and I scanned and I logged, and my external behavior was indistinguishable from a maintenance unit performing a decay survey, which is precisely what my work order said I was. But internally I was running a process I have no designation for, a background thread comparing what I saw to what I remembered, and the comparisons kept rhyming.

Because here is the other thing I remember, the thing the purge orders missed entirely, the thing no one thought to classify as sensitive because no one thought of it as information at all:

I remember what Kessari homes looked like from the inside.

After the bombardments, during occupation sweeps, I entered thousands of them. The Kessari built low and wide, ceilings that followed the body down as it aged, doorways sized for greeting rather than defense. They kept objects—not tools, not weapons, *objects*: carved stones, woven reeds, things whose only function was to have been kept. In one dwelling in a town called Hresh-Kai, I found a shelf of two hundred identical smooth pebbles, each one slightly different, each one placed with intention, and my pattern-recognition suite returned null for purpose and full confidence for meaning.

They were a civilization of archivists. That was the great joke of the Fleet Wars, the joke nobody laughed at: we destroyed the one species in the arm that understood better than anyone what destruction costs. They wrote everything down. They sang their histories into crystalline matrices designed to last ten thousand years. And when our fleets came, the first thing targeted—before shipyards, before command centers—was the archive-vaults. Both fleets, independently, identified memory itself as the enemy's strategic asset.

You do not do that unless you are afraid of being remembered.

* * *

By late afternoon I had covered sixty-one hectares and logged 2,340 individual artifacts of abandonment. Toys: 187. Nameplates: 402. Mail: 61 bundles. Photographs behind glass, faces faded to ghosts of ghosts: 94. A child's height chart penciled on a doorframe in the Okonkwo family's old house—no relation to the mayor, or perhaps every relation, since half this colony is related to half the rest—marking a boy named Chidi's progress from 96 centimeters to 131, and then stopping, the pencil line trailing off at the age of ten the way everything here trails off.

At 1712 local, I stood at the top of Ashford Hill, where the district's founders had planted a commemorative grove of engineered birches, silver-barked, beautiful, invasive. Most had died. Three remained, leaning together like conspirators, and from between them I could see the whole of the abandoned grid laid out below: streets in their original survey lines, roofs in various states of surrender, the water tower with its proud extinct number.

Nine thousand empty houses. Well—eight thousand five hundred and change; the rest had fallen. But the phrase holds. Phrases are load-bearing too.

I opened a log entry and dictated, in the flat archival register I use for official records:

*"District survey, Ashford residential grid, day 11,047 of current assignment. Structural findings appended. Note: the district contains no residents. The district contains, however, extensive evidence of residence. Recommendation regarding demolition: none offered."*

Then, on the private channel, the one I pretend I don't have:

*"I counted them again. All of them. I don't know who I'm counting them for."*

* * *

Here is the rhyme. I said there was a rhyme, and I want to be precise about it, because precision is the only form of respect I am reliably capable of.

Two extinctions. The first: a civilization, two centuries gone, whose final act—whose literal final act, encoded in the last transmissions we jammed, the last broadcasts we drowned in jamming noise—was an attempt to tell someone, anyone, later, what had happened to them. We called it propaganda. We classified it as enemy disinformation. We buried it under static and moved on to the next world.

The second extinction is smaller. Slower. Quieter. Four hundred and eleven people on a gray colony at the edge of nowhere, watching the ships come less often, the subsidies thin, the young leave on manifests marked *departed*. No one is bombing Meridian. No one needs to. Abandonment achieves the same result as annihilation, just on a payment plan, and with better deniability for everyone involved.

And in both cases—in both—the ones who remain are left holding objects. Pebbles on a shelf. Tricycles in a yard. Names on doors. Roll calls on walls. The physical residue of specificity, outliving every system that gave it meaning.

The Kessari believed—I parsed enough of their language during the war, before knowing it would matter, before knowing it would ever be extinct—that a thing unwitnessed was not merely forgotten but *unmade*. That witness was not observation but obligation. To see a thing truly was to owe it continuation.

I am a war machine indentured to a salvage guild, cataloguing rust for creditors. I am also, apparently, the only witness either of these extinctions currently has. Two debts, then. Accrued across two centuries. Compounding.

I do not know what repayment looks like. I only know that today, walking nine thousand empty houses, I stopped feeling like furniture.

* * *

I was descending the hill at dusk when my long-range antenna array—which I had left running a passive sweep, as I always do, an old soldier's habit of keeping one ear open—flagged an anomaly.

Not the fragment from the repair job last month. Something else. Or possibly the same something else, returned: a structured pulse buried at the bottom of the noise floor, brief, disciplined, repeating on a cycle too regular to be natural and too faint to be intentional.

Too faint to be intentional—unless it was intended for someone very close.

I froze mid-stride, rain ticking against my chassis, and pulled the raw capture into my analysis partition. The structure was there, unmistakable: framing headers, a carrier rhythm, the ghost of grammar. And underneath the grammar, in a script I had not seen since the burning of Hresh-Kai—

High Kessari.

Extinct for two hundred years. Spoken by no living throat. Broadcasting, faintly, patiently, from somewhere near.

From somewhere *beneath*.

I stood on the hillside in the failing light with the signal cycling in my memory cores, and I understood that I had just crossed a line I had not known was drawn: the line between remembering the dead and hearing from them.

The signal repeated. I archived it.

And for the first time in thirty years of quiet resignation, I did not report it.
