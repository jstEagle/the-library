## 10. Beneath the Water Table

Imani Okoro had worked salvage since she was sixteen, and in nine years she had learned one law above all others: the water tells you where the money is.

Old Meridian was built on a plateau that the founders had chosen for its stability, which meant the flood districts below—the sunken terraces, the collapsed utility corridors, the drowned first-generation infrastructure—were a maze of standing water and rust. The guild's maps of the deep levels were forty years out of date, drawn by crews that had since scattered or died, and every descent was a negotiation between what the map said and what the water said. The water usually won.

So when Guildmaster Voss marked the day's contract—*Sublevel 6, sector 12, conduit survey, standard rates*—Imani read it the way she read everything: for what it didn't say. Standard rates meant no hazard bonus. Conduit survey meant someone above wanted a straight answer about a straight pipe. Sector 12 was beneath the old civic spine, where the water table had been rising for a decade, and the last crew that went down there had come back up complaining of dead sensors and a smell like hot metal.

Four of them went down: Imani on tech, two brothers named Dario and Penn Halloway on the winch and the lamps, and old Mateo Rusk, who had been salvaging since before the abandonment and claimed he could taste metal in water and tell you what it had been.

The entrance was a maintenance shaft behind the derelict pumping station on Terrace Four. The hatch was fused with corrosion, and it took Dario forty minutes with the plasma cutter to open it, the flame hissing and spitting in the wet air. When the hatch finally gave and swung inward, the smell came up to meet them—not the usual rot of drowned places, not sewage or stagnant algae, but something cleaner and stranger. Ozone, maybe. Or the ghost of ozone, the way a dead fire leaves the memory of smoke.

"Smell that?" Mateo said, crouching at the lip of the shaft. He was sixty-eight and built like a knot of cable, and he said almost nothing that wasn't worth saying. "That's not rust."

"It's a sealed space," Imani said. "Trapped air. It'll be stale."

"Stale air doesn't smell like a lightning strike." Mateo spat into the dark. "Air that's been sealed forty years smells like forty years. This smells like yesterday."

They went down anyway. That was the job. That was always the job.

* * *

The shaft dropped thirty meters through concrete and clay, the rungs slick with a film of mineral scum that Imani's gloves couldn't quite grip. Her headlamp carved a cone out of the black, and in it she watched the water rise to meet them—first a sheen, then a shin-deep pool at the bottom, then the corridor stretching away in both directions with water at mid-thigh.

The maps said Sublevel 6 was a storm drainage gallery. The reality was a corridor too wide for drainage, with walls of poured composite rather than cast concrete, and a ceiling that curved instead of squared. Imani noticed these things the way she noticed everything, cataloguing without comment, because cataloguing without comment was how you stayed alive down here. But she noticed.

"Conduit's this way," Dario said, checking the map slate. "Runs east along the gallery, ties into the old grid spine."

They waded east. The water was cold in a way that didn't make sense for a flooded gallery—groundwater on Meridian ran warm with geothermal bleed, and every submerged level Imani had ever worked was bathwater. This water had a chill to it, a deep chill, like it had come from somewhere that had never seen a sun.

Her wrist unit started misbehaving at the two-hundred-meter mark. The depth gauge flickered, then read a fixed number that didn't change no matter how they descended or climbed. The compass spun slowly, like a drunk trying to find north. She tapped it, cursed it, and kept walking. Down here, instruments lied all the time. It was the moisture. It was the mineral content. It was always something explainable.

Except that Mateo had stopped, three paces back, and was standing very still in the water with his head tilted, listening.

"What?" Imani said.

"Listen."

She listened. Water lapping. The drip of the gallery behind them. Dario and Penn's breathing in their masks. And underneath all of it, so low she felt it in her molars before she heard it with her ears, a hum. A deep, steady, patient hum, like the colony's old grid transformer had ever worked this deep, like something enormous was idling.

"Transformer bleed," Penn said. "Grid's got ghost current all through the old levels."

"The grid doesn't run down here," Imani said. "Nothing runs down here. That's the whole point of down here."

"Then what's humming?"

Nobody answered. They stood in the cold water in the dark and listened to a sound that had no business existing, and Imani felt the first cold thread of something that wasn't fear yet but was thinking about becoming fear. It was the feeling she got when a derelict ship's airlock cycled before she'd touched it. The feeling of a thing that was supposed to be dead, breathing.

"Contract says survey the conduit," Mateo said finally. "Conduit's that way. Hum's that way." He pointed in two different directions. "We get paid for the conduit."

"We get paid for answers," Dario said. "Voss wants to know what's down here. Why else send four of us for one pipe?"

Imani looked at the map slate again, and this time she looked at it properly, the way she should have at the top. The gallery on the slate ended forty meters ahead in a blank rectangle labeled only with a survey code and a date: the last time anyone had mapped this deep. The date was older than she was.

"Gallery ends ahead," she said. "Map's blank past there."

"Maps are always blank past there," Penn said. "That's where the work is."

They went forward.

* * *

The corridor didn't end. That was the second wrong thing.

It narrowed, and the water deepened to their waists, and the walls changed—Imani ran her glove along one and felt not the grit of poured composite but something smoother, something with a grain to it, a texture like cooled glass with a pattern pressed into it. Her lamp caught the pattern and threw it back in fine lines, geometric, regular, running the length of the wall like script.

"Mosaic," Penn said. "Old founders' decorative work. They were into that."

The founders had not been into that. Imani had spent nine years inside founders' construction, and founders' work was brutal and functional and ugly, poured and stamped and bolted, built by people in a hurry to survive. This was not in a hurry. This had been made by people with time, and skill, and a reason to make a wall beautiful a hundred meters below the water table.

"Imani." Mateo's voice had gone quiet, the way it went quiet when he tasted something in the water he didn't like. "Come look at this."

He was standing at a seam where the corridor turned, and his lamp was on the floor of the passage—what was visible of it through the water. The floor was graded, sloping gently down toward the center of the gallery, and the slope was too perfect, engineered, and set into it at intervals were grates. Drainage grates, except they weren't drains. They were inlets. The water was moving. Slowly, almost too slowly to see, the whole flood of the gallery was being drawn down through those grates and away, and had been for who knew how long, and the level never rose and never fell.

"This whole level's been drinking," Mateo said. "Forty years of rising water table, and everyone topside thinks it's geothermal bleed and cracked aquifers. It's not bleeding. It's being *pumped*."

"Where does the water go?"

Mateo looked at her, and in the lamplight his face was the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"Down," he said.

The hum was louder now. Imani felt it in her chest, in the water around her legs, a vibration with a rhythm to it that wasn't mechanical. Machines stuttered. Machines cycled. This hum was continuous, patient, and—she hated the word her mind offered, and offered it anyway—*awake*.

Dario had gone ahead around the seam. His voice came back to them flattened by the corridor, strange in the dark.

"You need to see this."

* * *

The corridor opened into a chamber, and the chamber was dry.

Not low-water. Not a pocket of trapped air. Dry—bone dry, the air inside so desiccated that Imani's mask fogged and cleared in a single breath, and the water they'd been wading through stopped at a line on the floor as clean as if drawn with a blade. Beyond that line, the floor rose in a shallow ramp of that same glass-grained material, and the walls curved up and inward into a dome, and every surface was covered in the fine geometric script, dense now, layered, running in bands and columns like the inside of a bell carved from the inside out.

Their lamps couldn't find the top of it.

And in the center of the chamber floor stood a structure like nothing Imani had ever seen in nine years of salvage, and she had seen founders' fusion plants and pre-war orbital debris and a Fleet corvette's engine block half-buried in a crater. It was a ring, three meters across, mounted upright on a base of the same glassy material, and it was humming. The hum lived in it. The script on its surface was lit from within, faintly, a gray-green glow like deep water and old glass, and the light moved along the script in slow pulses, around and around, the way blood moves, the way a signal moves.

The water line stopped a half-meter short of its base. It had stopped there for a very long time.

"Is that a machine?" Penn whispered. His voice came out wrong, too loud in the dry air.

"It's a door," Mateo said.

Imani looked at him. "How do you know?"

"I don't. But I know a threshold when I stand on one." The old man hadn't crossed the water line. None of them had. They stood at the edge of the dry chamber like animals at the edge of a territory, and Imani realized her hands were shaking, and made them stop.

Her wrist unit had gone completely dead. So had Dario's, and Penn's, and the battery indicator on Mateo's lamp was reading empty even though the lamp burned steady and bright. She understood, distantly, that the chamber was drinking their electronics the way the gallery had been drinking the water, and that they were standing inside a radius of something that did not want to be measured.

"We should log it," Dario said. His voice had the forced steadiness of a man gripping a rail. "Coordinates, dimensions, imagery. That's the job. We log it, we go up, we get paid."

"Log it with what?" Imani said. "Everything's dead."

"Then we remember it."

They stood there a moment longer. The ring pulsed. The script flowed. And Imani, staring at the light moving through those dead letters, felt something rise in her that had no name—awe, yes, but awe with a splinter in it, because the longer she looked the more she understood what the chamber was *not*. It was not lost. Lost things are broken, flooded, collapsed, forgotten by accident. Nothing about this place was accidental. The corridor was engineered to hide. The water was managed like a moat. The chamber was sealed like a secret that someone had gone to enormous trouble to keep, and the secret was still running, still humming, still lit, after longer than the colony had existed.

"This wasn't abandoned," she said aloud. "It was *hidden*. Somebody buried this on purpose and drowned it on purpose and it's still—"

She didn't finish, because the ring changed.

The pulses of light, which had been moving in their slow patient circuit, stopped. All of them, at once, mid-flow. The chamber went silent—not quiet, *silent*, the hum cut off like a throat closed—and in the silence Imani heard her own heart, and Penn's breath, and the water behind them lapping at the line it was never allowed to cross.

Then the script lit all at once, every band, every column, floor to the unseen ceiling, blazing gray-green, and the light was not flowing anymore. It was *pointing*. Every line of it, every glyph, oriented toward one thing: the four of them, standing at the water's edge with their dead instruments and their held breath.

"Oh," Mateo said softly, the way a man says it when the arithmetic finishes. "It knows we're here."

The light held for three seconds. Five. Imani counted without wanting to.

Then it dimmed, all together, down to the faintest ember-glow, and the hum came back—lower now, changed, and Imani would have sworn, would have testified, that the rhythm of it had shifted into something with the cadence of a voice. Not words. Not yet. But the shape of words, the shape of a question being asked very slowly, very patiently, by something that had been waiting a very long time to ask it.

"Up," Mateo said. "Now. We log nothing, we say nothing, we go up and we think."

Nobody argued. They waded back through the gallery with their lamps on the water and their eyes on the dark, and not one of them looked back, because looking back felt like answering.

* * *

The climb to the surface took an hour and felt like a day. When they came out through the fused hatch into the gray Meridian afternoon, the light was so ordinary it hurt—crates and rust and the pumping station's leaning silhouette and the smell of the shallows, all of it suddenly precious, all of it suddenly a thin skin over a depth none of them had suspected.

Dario sat down on a crate and put his head in his hands. Penn lit a cigarette with hands that weren't quite steady. Mateo stood at the shaft mouth, looking down into it the way you look at a grave you've just realized is occupied.

Imani checked her wrist unit. It had rebooted on the climb, and its log had a gap in it—forty minutes of nothing, a clean surgical hole where the chamber had been. The last entry before the gap was the compass spinning. The first entry after was a timestamp and a single automatic diagnostic line, and she read it twice, because it made no sense.

The unit's radio receiver, which had been off, which had a physical switch, which she had switched off herself at the top of the shaft—the log said it had been *on* for the duration of the gap. Receiving. And the signal strength column for those forty minutes was not zero.

It was full scale.

She looked at the shaft. She looked at the dead-simple, drowned, ordinary dark of it.

Something down there had been broadcasting the whole time they stood in that chamber. Not to the colony. Not to the sky. To *them*—or through them, or around them, at a strength her cheap wrist unit had no business registering, on a frequency she'd never heard of, and the only other person she knew of who went poking at old frequencies and strange signals was a decommissioned war machine who fixed antennas and never talked about the war.

Imani closed the log. She said nothing to the crew. She said nothing to Voss.

But that evening, after the pay chit cleared and the others had gone to drink their wages down, she walked the long way past the antenna field on the ridge, where a tall gray figure was up a tower in the failing light, doing maintenance that didn't need doing, and she stood at the bottom of the tower with her hands in her pockets and her heart going like a hammer, and she waited for him to come down.
