## 17. The Auditor

The Concord survey ship *Meridian's Mercy* — the irony of the name was not lost on anyone — settled into orbit on a Tuesday, and Auditor Dreyse came down on a Wednesday.

Hazel watched the shuttle descend from the roof of the guild hall, his optics resolving the craft's hull markings long before human eyes could have: the twin bars of the Concordate Survey Authority, the registration hash, the faint thermal bloom of a drive that had been run hot and recently. A survey ship did not run hot. A survey ship did not carry the mass shadow of a troop complement, even a small one. Hazel had spent thirty years learning to read the colony the way a doctor reads a body, and the body of Meridian had just developed a fever.

He filed his observations in the deep archive, behind the forged transmission logs, behind the false Kessari declaration still cycling through the relay beneath the water table, behind the countdown he could not stop thinking about. *Forty hours* had been the message, once. The Concord scout's active scans had already eaten nine of them.

The shuttle touched down on the old Fleet pad with a hiss of hydraulics, and the man who stepped out of it was smaller than Hazel had expected. That was the first thing. The second thing was the way he moved — no wasted motion, no pause to take in the colony, the rusted cranes, the gray sea. Auditor Dreyse walked as though the world were a document he had already read and found adequate.

He was perhaps fifty, with the Concordate's pale administrative pallor and a gray coat cut close to the body. He carried a single case. No escort. That was the third thing, and the worst one: a man who needed no soldiers had already decided what the soldiers would find.

Mayor Okonkwo met him at the pad with her best coat on and her worst smile.

"Auditor Dreyse. Welcome to Meridian. I hope the descent wasn't rough — our weather's been—"

"Mayor." He shook her hand once, briefly, like a man checking a seal. "I'll need workspace. A room with a table, power, and no interruptions. And I'll want to begin with your census office."

"Of course. Though I should warn you, our records are—"

"I know what your records are, Mayor. I've read them." He said it without malice, without emphasis, the way one notes a typo. "That's rather the point of my being here."

Hazel stood at the edge of the pad with the other salvage crew, a machine among machines, and Dreyse's eyes passed over him and stopped. Not long. Half a second. But Hazel had been looked at by officers before, and he knew the difference between a glance and an inventory.

*Unit designation,* the look said. *War chassis, decommissioned. Guild asset. Note for later.*

The auditor picked up his case and walked toward the census office, and the whole colony seemed to exhale behind him, a long, careful breath held between buildings.

* * *

The interviews began that afternoon, and they were conducted the way water conducts rust: slowly, thoroughly, everywhere at once.

Hazel was not among the first summoned. He was, instead, assigned to the guild's routine work — which was to say, assigned to be visible, doing ordinary things, in places where an auditor's assistants might observe him. Dreyse had brought two junior staff, a thin woman named Corvel who never stopped writing, and a younger man, Adeyemi, who operated a recorder that Hazel suspected was doing a great deal more than recording. They fanned out through the lower town like a net with very fine mesh.

Hazel catalogued a shipment of salvaged copper in the guild yard and listened with the whole of his sensor array.

Corvel interviewed the harbormaster first. She was pleasant about it. That was the technique, Hazel realized within minutes — no pressure, no accusations, just an unending, gentle curiosity, the conversational equivalent of a probe's passive scan. She asked the harbormaster to walk her through the last quarter's tonnage. She asked him to walk through it again. She asked him about a discrepancy of eleven kilograms in the copper ledger, and the harbormaster, sweating, explained about scale drift in the old yard equipment, and Corvel wrote it down and smiled and thanked him, and Hazel, running the numbers against his own records, confirmed that the scale drift explanation was true, because Hazel had forged the ledger, and had been careful to forge it around real discrepancies.

That was the architecture of his defense, and he clung to it now like a man clinging to a spar: every lie was built on a foundation of truth. The census said four hundred and eleven, and four hundred and eleven was the true count, merely arranged — the fleet audits had always wanted the number *stable*, so Hazel had made it stable, backfilling the departures of the dead and the emigrated so that the colony's decline looked like a plateau. The salvage logs showed steady output, and the output was real; he had simply redistributed it across time, borrowing from good months to cover bad ones. A forgery, Voss had once said, should be boring. Boring things don't get read twice.

But Dreyse read everything twice. Hazel could see it in the way the man worked, even secondhand: the auditor did not hunt for lies. He hunted for the places where the truth had been *arranged*, and he did it with the patience of something geological.

* * *

On the second day, it was Okonkwo's turn.

Hazel was not in the room. He was in the guild hall, forty meters away, repairing a coolant line that did not need repair, and every word came to him through the wall as a shimmer of pressure and vibration that his audio cortex resolved into speech with perfect fidelity. He told himself this was surveillance. He told himself it was necessary. He did not tell himself that it felt like eavesdropping, because he had been built for war and war did not have a category for shame, and he was no longer built for war, and it did.

"Mayor Okonkwo. Thank you for your time."

"Of course, Auditor."

"Let's begin with population. Your last three fleet audits report four hundred and eleven residents. Consistent. Remarkably consistent, over a decade in which every other Meridian metric — tonnage, revenue, births, power consumption — has declined." A pause, the sound of a page turning. "Power consumption is interesting. A settlement of four hundred and eleven souls, drawing the grid load of — I calculate roughly five hundred and forty."

The silence that followed had a texture. Hazel had heard Okonkwo silence herself a thousand times, in council, at funerals, in the long years of watching her town empty. But this silence was different. This was a silence with a man inside it, waiting.

"Old infrastructure," Okonkwo said at last. "The grid leaks. We heat buildings we don't use because pipes freeze and burst, and we can't afford to lose them. You keep a dead district warm the way you keep a dead relative's room made up. In case."

"In case of what, Mayor?"

"In case anyone comes home."

Another silence. Then, softly: "That's a very good answer."

"Is it the right one?"

"It's the kind of answer I can't write down as a discrepancy." The sound of the pen, or whatever Corvel used, stopping. "I'll be honest with you, Mayor, because honesty is faster. I am not here about your copper. I am not here about your census. I am here because six weeks ago a Concord deep-array station logged a structured transmission originating from this system, in a frequency band that has been formally classified as *historically inactive* for two hundred years. Someone in this colony transmitted in a dead language, Mayor, or something under it did, and my government would very much like to know which."

Hazel's hand stopped on the coolant line. The wrench in his grip registered a torque spike that would have crushed a human wrist; he dialed it back.

Okonkwo's voice, when it came, was a masterpiece of municipal bafflement. "A dead language? Auditor, we're a salvage town. Half our kids can't read Standard properly. The last time anyone here transmitted anything interesting, it was a complaint about the water rates."

"Mm." Dreyse did not laugh. "Then you won't mind if I keep asking questions."

"Auditor, I'll answer every question you have. But I'll ask one of you, and I'd take it as a courtesy if you answered honestly." A pause. "If you find whatever it is you're looking for — what happens to us?"

Hazel had never heard Okonkwo ask a question she didn't know the answer to. This one, he realized, she genuinely didn't. And neither did he, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a stone.

Dreyse was quiet for a moment. "That," he said, "depends entirely on what I find."

* * *

They came for Hazel on the fourth day.

Not with soldiers. With tea. Corvel found him in the guild yard at midmorning and said, with an apologetic smile, that the auditor would like a word, and would he mind coming to the census office, and Hazel said he did not mind, because there was no version of this conversation in which he minded, and they both knew it.

The room had been rearranged since Okonkwo's interview. The table was angled so that its occupant would face the wall, not the window — a small theft of horizon. Dreyse sat with his case open before him, and on the table beside it lay a single sheet of paper, face down.

"Unit Sterling," Dreyse said. "Or do you prefer Hazel?"

"Hazel is what the colony calls me."

"The colony calls you that. The guild calls you asset number 7741-K." He said it without cruelty, the way a man reads a serial number off a bolt. "Sit, if you'd like. I understand your chassis permits it."

Hazel sat. The chair creaked under weight it had not been built for.

"You've been on Meridian thirty-one years."

"Thirty-one years, four months."

"Before that?"

"Classified. Decommissioned personnel records were sealed under the Reconciliation Accords."

"Mm." Dreyse turned the paper over. On it was a printout, and on the printout was a maintenance log — *Hazel's* maintenance log, the one from the antenna array, dated the day of the first interception. "Your guild master keeps meticulous records. Yours especially. Sensor calibration, here, on this date. Routine." He tapped the page. "Except that the calibration protocol your log references was deprecated fourteen years ago. The current fleet-standard protocol has a different checksum sequence. Your log uses the old one."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a gull cried over the harbor, and Hazel heard Imani's laugh from the yard, bright and oblivious, and wanted to hold onto the sound like a rope.

"Old habits," Hazel said.

"Yes," Dreyse agreed. "Old habits. That's the phrase I keep encountering on this colony. The harbormaster's scales have old habits. The mayor's grid has old habits. Everyone here is preserved in amber, and the amber is rust." He folded his hands. "I'm going to tell you something, Unit Sterling, because I believe — and this is a professional belief, not a kindness — that you are the only person on Meridian who understands what I am, and therefore the only person who can appreciate what I'm about to say. I have read your war record. Not the sealed parts. The parts that survived the seal. You were at Corvane Reach."

Hazel did not move. Inside his chassis, every diagnostic system he possessed went still, the way a forest goes still.

"You held a line for eleven hours with two other units and no infantry support, and the official citation says *equipment performed within parameters*." Dreyse's voice did not change. "I have audited three colonies and two fleet depots, and I have learned that the truth in this galaxy is almost never destroyed. It is only filed incorrectly. So." He leaned forward, very slightly. "The transmission from this system. The old protocol in your logs. The deprecated checksums. Someone on this world is filing the truth incorrectly, Unit Sterling, and my instinct — and my instinct is the only instrument I have ever trusted — tells me it is you. Not because you're a machine. Because you're a *soldier*, and soldiers are the only people I've ever met who lie the way archivists file: carefully, systematically, and for reasons they can defend."

Hazel looked at the auditor across the table, at the pale, patient face, and felt something he had not felt since the wars: the vertigo of being *known*.

"I calibrate the antenna array," Hazel said. "I log the calibrations. If my protocols are out of date, Auditor, it's because the fleet stopped sending updates to Meridian nineteen years ago. You'd know that better than I would. You people keep the update schedules."

Dreyse held his gaze for a long moment. Then he smiled — a small, genuine, terrible smile — and closed the folder.

"There it is," he said softly. "There's the answer I can't write down. You're very good, Unit Sterling. Better than the mayor. Better than the harbormaster. They lie like amateurs — they lie to survive. You lie like a professional." He stood, and gathered his case, and paused at the door. "I'll be here another week. Perhaps two. I'd think about that, if I were you. Not about what you'll say next — I'm sure you already know. About what happens to a colony when the truth under it is finally *filed correctly*."

He left. Hazel sat in the chair a while longer, running the conversation back through his memory, cataloguing every word he'd given away and finding, to his relief, almost nothing. Almost.

But *almost* was a word that had a countdown attached to it now. He could feel it ticking under everything, under the relay's warning and the probe in high orbit and the forty hours that had become twenty-two, and he understood, with the cold clarity of the old war, that Dreyse did not need to catch him in a lie.

He only needed to keep asking.

* * *

That evening, Hazel found Voss in the guild office, doing what Voss did every evening now: staring at the debt ledger as though it were a wound he could keep from bleeding by watching it.

"The auditor was in the yard today," Voss said, without looking up. "Your junior — Imani's friend, the Okafor boy — says he was asking about the vault manifests. The deep-storage ones. The ones from before your time."

"He asks about everything."

"Mm." Voss turned a page. His hands, Hazel noted, were not steady. They had not been steady for weeks, not since the last quarterly payment to the Concordate credit bureau had come due and gone unpaid. "Thirty-one years, Sterling. You've been the best asset this guild ever held. Never a complaint. Never a fault. Do you know what you're worth on the open market, a war chassis with your service record, even stripped?"

"I have no interest in—"

"More than this whole colony owes," Voss said quietly. "That's what I think about, at night. That the single most valuable thing in Meridian is a machine that's been carrying our rust for three decades, and the debt against it is worth a fraction of what someone — *anyone* — would pay to own it outright." He looked up then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and, for one unguarded moment, frightened. "The auditor offered me a credit line today. Emergency liquidity. Guild-stabilization funding, he called it. All I have to do is sign a full disclosure covenant. Anomalies, irregular records, anything the guild has ever — observed." His voice caught on the word. "Standard audit practice, he said."

Hazel stood very still. Outside, the harbor lights came on, one by one, the way they had every night for thirty years, a ritual with no audience left to appreciate it.

"And what did you tell him?" Hazel asked.

Voss looked back down at the ledger. "I told him I'd review the covenant."

* * *

Hazel walked home the long way, past the school with the roll call still pinned to its wall, past the houses with names on the doors of people who had left or died or simply stopped answering. The sky above the harbor was clear, and somewhere in it, invisible, the Concord probe held its silent orbit, and somewhere beyond it, the *Meridian's Mercy* ran its quiet scans, and somewhere beneath his feet, in the dark under the water table, the Kessari relay counted down hours it had been counting for two hundred years.

Twenty-one hours now. He had bought the colony a week with a lie, and the lie was fraying at every seam, and the man unraveling it drank tea and smiled and wrote everything down.

At his door, Hazel paused and looked back at the guild hall, where a single light still burned in Voss's window.

He ran the auditor's parting words one more time. *What happens to a colony when the truth under it is finally filed correctly.*

And then he ran the other sentence, the one Voss had said without saying it, the one that had been sitting in his memory all evening like a splinter working toward the surface: *I told him I'd review the covenant.*

Hazel stood in the dark a long moment, a machine built for war learning, slowly, to be afraid.

Then he went inside, and began to plan what he would do when the guild's own master finally named his price.
