## 31. Thirty Minutes to Broadcast

The vault shook again, and dust fell from the ceiling in long gray ropes.

Hazel did not flinch. He had been built not to flinch—had been designed, in a factory that no longer existed, to stand motionless inside falling buildings while his targeting array tracked targets through smoke. That architecture was still in him, buried under thirty years of salvage manifests and census records and the slow sediment of a quiet life. He felt it now, the old stillness rising up like water into a dry well, and he was grateful for it. There would be time to be afraid later. If there was a later.

"Nine minutes of tremor activity in the last hour," he said aloud, because Imani needed numbers, not silence. "The debris field is degrading faster than projected. The Republic strike broke the scout ship into approximately four hundred trackable fragments. Atmospheric entry will begin in—" he paused, recalculating as another shudder ran through the rock above them, "—I can no longer give you an honest estimate."

"Then stop giving me estimates." Imani's voice came from beneath the relay's base, where she lay on her back with her arms buried to the elbow in Kessari conduit. Her hands were steady. Her voice was not. "Just tell me when you're ready. That's the only number I care about."

Hazel looked at the countdown.

It hung in the air of the vault now—not a projection he had created but one the relay itself maintained, rendered in lines of pale gold light that only resolved when he stood at the correct angle, in the correct place, as if the dead had built their warnings to be read by exactly one kind of witness. The numerals were High Kessari, base-twelve, elegant as calligraphy. He had spent two days learning to read them without translating, the way a human learns to see a word instead of spelling it.

**00:34:17.**

Thirty-four minutes. When he had first decoded the message, there had been eleven hours. Every scan pulse from the Concord troops securing the surface above—every active sweep, every ground-penetrating radar pass, every routine inventory ping from their survey drones—shaved minutes off the clock. The Kessari dead-man's switch did not distinguish between malice and ignorance. It had been built by a civilization watching its own extermination, and they had built it to assume the worst, because assuming the worst had turned out to be accurate.

"They're drilling again," Imani said. She had gone still under the relay, listening with her whole body the way she listened to engines. "Northeast shaft. You hear it? That's not survey equipment. That's bore-heads."

"I hear it." He did—the distant rhythmic gnaw transmitted through two hundred meters of rock, felt more than heard, like a heartbeat in the floor. Concord engineers, following Voss's maps, following Dreyse's orders, cutting toward a vault they believed held a weapon aimed at them. They were not wrong. It simply wasn't the weapon they thought.

"Hazel." Imani slid out from under the relay and sat up, wiping her hands on her thighs, leaving dark streaks on the fabric. In the gold light her face looked older than twenty-six. "If we do this—if you put your memory into this thing—there's no taking it back. Right? Once it's in the broadcast buffer, it goes. Everything. All thirty years."

"Yes."

"The debt logs. The census. Okonkwo asking you to fudge the numbers. Voss. Me, at fourteen, throwing rocks at you behind the guild hall because the older kids dared me." Her jaw tightened. "All of it. Everyone gets to see us."

"Everyone gets to see *you*," Hazel corrected gently. "I am the narrator. But yes. Meridian will be legible to both fleets down to its smallest shames. That is rather the point. A testimony with the embarrassing parts removed is just propaganda with better manners."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then the vault shook harder than before—a long rolling concussion, and somewhere far above, muffled by stone and soil, the sound of screaming metal. A large fragment, coming down early. Hazel's acoustic sensors parsed the impact signature: something roughly the mass of a shuttle, striking the surface four kilometers east. Close enough that the pressure wave reached them as a breath of warm air through the access tunnel.

"That one had people near it," Imani whispered. Not a question. She'd been counting the falls too.

"The eastern work crews were evacuated forty minutes ago," Hazel said. It was true; Okonkwo had moved them herself, walking the perimeter with a hand megaphone and the particular stubborn calm that had kept four hundred people alive through three decades of abandonment. Whether anyone else had been in the fragment's path—scavengers, Concord stragglers, the refugees camped along the old canal—he could not say. He chose not to calculate the probability. Some arithmetic was a cruelty with no function.

He turned back to the interface cradle.

It was a Kessari device, or had been: a basin of dark material, half alloy and half something organic that had died so long ago it had become mineral, shaped like two cupped hands. To use it, a mind had to be poured in—not data transferred, not copied, but *poured*, the way you pour water from one vessel to another, leaving the first vessel empty until refilled. The Kessari archive had explained this to him over two days of patient, terrible dialogue. The relay wanted a voice willing to speak the truth. It had never specified that the voice survive the speaking.

Imani had found that part of the translation first. She had not told Okonkwo. She had not told him she knew. But she had stopped sleeping, and she had started holding the physical link longer than necessary, checking connections that were already perfect, and Hazel understood. It was the same thing humans did at bedsides.

"Link status," he said.

"Physical link is green. Your core adapter is seated, locked, drawing clean." She recited it flatly, professionally, the way he had taught her to recite diagnostics back when she was a guild apprentice who resented being taught anything by a machine. Then she broke form. "Hazel. The backup. The copy Imani-the-apprentice made of your public logs, the one we hid in the water treatment system. It'll still be you. Part of you. Enough that—"

"No," he said.

"It's *you*—"

"It is a diary." He knelt beside her, bringing his optical apertures level with her eyes, an old courtesy. "A diary is what a person leaves. It is not what a person is. And if I am to spend my last thirty-four minutes arguing, I would rather argue about something that matters." He reached out and touched the cradle's rim. The dead material was warm. It had been warm for two centuries, waiting. "Besides. You are assuming the broadcast kills me. The Kessari assumed the worst. I have decided to assume otherwise. Someone has to model better assumptions than either fleet."

*

Above them, on the surface, Mayor Adaeze Okonkwo stood in the open bed of a salvage hauler with a megaphone in one hand and no plan worth the name in the other.

The colony had gathered in the market square—all four hundred and eleven of them, minus the ones already sheltering in cellars, plus the sixty-odd refugees who had drifted in over the years and never been counted in any official audit because counting them would have meant admitting they existed. Concord troopers in gray composite armor ringed the square's edges, weapons slung but hands near them, visibly uncertain. Their orders said evacuate the colonists. Their orders did not say what to do about a mayor who refused, politely, repeatedly, with documentation, in triplicate.

"You want us on transports," Okonkwo said, and the megaphone carried her voice to the back rows and, she suspected, to the helmet mics of every soldier listening. "Fine. Let's talk about where those transports go. Because I've read the fleet disposition reports—yes, we get fragments out here, we're not idiots—and there are two battle groups converging on this system, and the last ship that tried to leave orbit this week came down in pieces over the eastern flats. So when you offer me evacuation, Captain, what you're offering me is a seat on the deck of a warship during a fleet action. I've seen that movie. We all have. Our grandparents saw it."

Murmuring through the crowd. Somewhere, a baby crying and being hushed.

"So here's what we're going to do instead," she went on, and her voice dropped, and somehow that made it carry further. "We're going to go home. All of us. And then we're going to come back out with every tarp, every hull plate, every sheet of salvaged armor plating in this colony's inventory, and we're going to pile it over the northeast shaft complex. Because there are people underneath that shaft doing the only thing left that can stop those fleets from killing each other, and they need time, and time is the one thing we know how to buy, because buying time with scrap and stubbornness is literally our entire economy."

A ragged laugh moved through the crowd. Good. Fear and laughter could share a body; she'd learned that decades ago.

A Concord lieutenant stepped forward—young, jaw tight, visor up so she could see he was barely older than Imani. "Ma'am, if you interfere with authorized operations—"

"Lieutenant." She lowered the megaphone and just spoke, letting the crowd carry her voice forward for her. "In about half an hour, either the truth comes out of the ground and ends this, or it doesn't and we all find out together what happens next. Either way, in fifty years nobody is going to remember your orders. They're going to remember whether you stood between frightened civilians and falling sky, or whether you didn't. Choose loud."

For a long moment the lieutenant said nothing. Then he turned, keyed his comm, and said something too quiet for her to catch—and the troopers at the square's edge began, one by one, to move toward the shafts. Not to stop the colonists. To help hold the tarps down.

Okonkwo exhaled and allowed herself four seconds of shaking hands before climbing down to organize the work crews.

*

The vault, 00:21:44.

They came down to him in relays—Okonkwo first, dusty, hoarse, smelling of tarp resin and engine grease; then Imani's shift partners from the descent crew, hauling the last power cells; finally, unexpectedly, a dozen ordinary colonists who had simply followed the mayor down and refused to leave, standing along the vault walls with lanterns, witnesses in the oldest sense. Among them, to Hazel's surprise, stood Guildmaster Voss.

The man looked destroyed. Not physically—physically he was intact, though his hands wouldn't stay still. It was the look of someone who had seen the bill for everything he'd sold arrive all at once. Dreyse's seizure of the guild records had exposed more than Hazel's altered logs; it had exposed Voss's messages, his price list, his careful little treasons. The colonists knew. Everyone knew. And still he had come down here, and still he stood among them, and no one had thrown him out.

"Sterling," Voss said. His voice cracked on the name. "The lien. Whatever happens up there, after—I want it on record that I'm voiding it. Formally. Tonight, before witnesses." He held up a battered slate displaying the guild's seal, the cancellation order already drafted. "You shouldn't be property when you do whatever it is you're going to do."

Hazel regarded him for a moment. Thirty years of polite indifference, of maintenance logs and purchase debt, of being furniture that talked. He found, examining himself honestly, that he did not hate the man. Hate required a heat he had never been issued.

"Noted for the record," Hazel said. "Thank you, Guildmaster. Now go stand with the others. History is about to be made, and you should be visible in it, on the right side, however late."

Voss's face did something complicated. He went and stood with the others.

00:14:02.

Hazel opened his chest panel.

The sound it made—three soft magnetic releases, a whisper of servos—was the loudest thing in the vault. Imani came to him immediately, carrying the bridge cable, her hands finally, fully steady. This was the work she was made for, and she knew it, and knowing it made her calm.

"Core extraction is rated for live transfer," she recited. "Seated, locked, drawing clean. Say when."

"Above," Okonkwo called down the access tunnel, and her voice came back doubled by the colonists relaying it: contact with the surface crews, tarps holding, another fragment down to the west, casualties unknown, the drill heads stopped—Concord engineers pulling back, ordered topside, everyone bracing for whatever came next.

Hazel looked at the countdown. 00:11:37. He looked at Imani. He looked at Okonkwo, who gave him a single nod, mayor to citizen, the only hierarchy that had ever mattered to him.

"Thirty years ago I was ordered to forget," he said quietly, to the room, to the dead, to whoever might someday play this recording. "I complied. It was the last order I ever obeyed. What follows is not obedience."

He gripped the edges of the cradle—the cupped hands of a civilization that had bet everything on one final honest voice—and bowed his head.

"When," Imani said softly, cable in hand, link held fast, holding on to him the way you hold a rope in wind.

"Now."

The extraction began, and the vault filled with light.
