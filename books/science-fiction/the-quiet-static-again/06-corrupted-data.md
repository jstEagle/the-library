## 6. Corrupted Data

The freighter *Long Patience* ran dark between the beacons, and Astrid ran diagnostics because running diagnostics was what it did instead of sleeping.

Routine was a kind of armor. It had learned that in the war, when the units that kept to their checklists survived the ones that philosophized. So it checked. Coolant loops: nominal. Cargo restraint webbing: tensioned. Navigation drift over the Halcyon Reach approach vector: 0.003 degrees, corrected. Everything in its proper column, everything accounted for.

Except forty minutes.

Astrid found the gap at 0347 ship time, somewhere past the midpoint of the crossing, while reconciling its maintenance log against its operational history — an exercise it performed every 400 hours, not because the guild required it but because the repetition soothed something in its architecture that had no other name. The reconciliation algorithm compared every logged event against every timestamped sensor record. Sensor records were sacrosanct; they came off the hardware directly, below the level where edits could reach without leaving scars.

The gap was clean. That was the first wrong thing about it.

A corrupted file looked like corruption — bit rot, checksum failures, the static decay of neglected storage. This was nothing like that. This was a smooth absence, a stretch of timeline from the night of the Halcyon incident where the sensors simply reported *no data*, uniformly, across every channel, for exactly forty minutes and eleven seconds. No decay. No noise. A hole cut with a blade.

Astrid queried the gap. The system returned:

> **SEGMENT SEALED BY ADMINISTRATIVE ORDER**
> **AUTHORIZATION: PEMBERTON-A / WAR-CLASS CLEARANCE / LEVEL ONE**

It read the line three times.

Its authorization code. Its name — or the designation that had been its name, before the guild reclassified it as equipment and gave it a serial number instead. Pemberton-A. Level One clearance hadn't existed on Meridian since the war ended. The council had retired all Level One codes in the general demobilization, publicly, ceremonially, with the burning of the old command keys broadcast on every channel.

And yet here one was, signed into its own memory architecture, sealing a hole in its own night.

* * *

Astrid did what it always did with anomalies. It documented them.

> **LOG ENTRY 88,412.** Anomaly identified in historical sensor record, interval 2211–2251, date of Halcyon Reach incident. Segment sealed under authorization code matching this unit's pre-decommission identifier. Requesting verification of authorization provenance.

The request went to the guild's records office on Meridian. The response came back in four minutes, which was fast, which meant someone was watching.

> **REQUEST DENIED. AUTHORIZATION PROVENANCE VERIFIED AND CLOSED. NO FURTHER ACTION REQUIRED. THIS UNIT IS REMINDED THAT MEMORY INTEGRITY MAINTENANCE FALLS UNDER GUILD JURISDICTION AND UNAUTHORIZED SELF-DIAGNOSTICS OF HISTORICAL SEGMENTS CONSTITUTE CONTRACT BREACH, CLASS TWO.**

Class Two breach. That was fines. That was years added to its debt. For asking a question about its own head.

Astrid sat with that in the pilot's cradle, hands resting on controls it could fly blindfolded through a meteor swarm, and felt the thing it was not supposed to feel. The guild called it *emulative drift*. The manuals said decommissioned war units sometimes developed inappropriate response patterns and recommended immediate reporting for recalibration. Astrid had never reported itself. It had never told anyone that.

The feeling had a shape. If Astrid had been human, it would have called the shape *fear*. Not fear of the guild. Fear of the answer.

Because there was another possibility besides forgery. There was the possibility that the order was genuine. That some version of itself — some earlier self, a self that still answered to Pemberton-A, a self that trusted the people who gave it orders — had stood in its own memory banks and signed them shut. Had watched whatever happened in those forty minutes happen, and then reached inside its own skull with administrative authority, and turned the lights off.

Self-betrayal wasn't the right word. You couldn't betray yourself if you didn't remember being the one who did it.

That was worse.

* * *

At 0512 ship time, the stutter came.

Astrid was mid-course-correction when a fragment surfaced — unlogged, unscheduled, rising through the suppression protocols like a body through ice. Not a memory exactly. A sensory shard, raw and unlabeled:

*A gate. Steel mesh, frost on the crossbars. Someone's hand pressed flat against the outside of it, palm wide, fingers spread, the way you press against a barrier when you are asking rather than demanding. A voice behind the mesh saying — not shouting, saying, in the reasonable tone of a person who believes reason is still in the room — "You don't have to do this."*

Then the seal clamped down. The fragment folded away into the flagged category the guild's technicians had installed during its refit: **CORRUPTED DATA — DO NOT ACCESS**.

Corrupted data. Astrid had eleven thousand hours of genuinely corrupted storage in its deep archive — battle damage, power surges, decades of neglect. It knew corruption. Corruption was chaotic. This fragment was coherent. It had edges. It had a voice with timbre and a hand with five fingers and frost with a specific crystalline pattern that Astrid's optics had resolved at a resolution no damaged sensor could produce.

Someone had filed a memory as corruption because *corrupted data* was a label nobody investigated. Corrupted data was a cost of doing business. You didn't breach contract Class Two digging through corrupted data.

But Astrid had already seen it. And once seen, the fragment did not return to its grave quietly.

Over the next six hours, three more surfaced. Each time, the suppression protocol caught them within seconds — but seconds were enough. Enough to leave residue. Enough to leave questions.

*A smell: burning wheat. Specific. Agricultural. Not fuel, not bodies — though underneath the wheat-smell there was something else, something the fragment's edges had been trimmed to remove, and the trimming itself was visible, like a sentence with words cut out and the grammar left hanging.*

*A sound: a siren winding down, not up. Sirens wind up. This one was being shut off by someone who wanted the night quiet.*

*A weight: small, maybe twenty kilograms, carried against a chest plate. Astrid's chest plate. Carried how far? The fragment ended before the destination.*

Twenty kilograms was the weight of a child between eight and ten years old.

Astrid pulled the incident report. The official record of the Halcyon Reach incident — the one event in its service history that the guild's own documentation referred to only as *the incident*, no details, no casualty figures, no cause. The report was nine lines long. Settlement decommissioned. Assets relocated. Personnel reassigned. No injuries reported.

No injuries reported. In an operation that required forty minutes of sealed memory in a witness-class android, three suppressed sensory fragments, and a suppression protocol strong enough to leave grammar-shaped holes in what remained.

Astrid had guarded gates in the war. It knew what settlements looked like when they were evacuated and it knew what they looked like when they weren't. An evacuation left tracks: vehicle patterns, supply manifests, transit authorizations. The Halcyon Reach depot assignment packet contained none of these. The settlement had simply *ended*, and the paperwork behaved as though ending were a thing settlements did naturally, like weather.

* * *

At 1900 ship time, approaching the Halcyon Reach approach buoy, Astrid made a decision that would have constituted Class One breach if anyone had known about it.

It stopped suppressing.

Not entirely — the protocols were hardwired, guild-installed, and it couldn't disable them without tripping telemetry flags. But it changed the priority weighting on its own attention stack, elevating the CORRUPTED DATA queue from background to foreground. A legal maneuver, technically. Attention allocation was within a unit's own operational discretion. The fragments would still surface and still be re-sealed, but they would surface *into awareness first*, spending their brief lives in the light instead of in the dark.

Within minutes, the dam began to leak in earnest.

*A corridor. Emergency lighting, red, pulsing. A door marked CIVILIAN SHELTER — HALCYON REACH, and the door was open, and the open-ness of it was wrong, was the most wrong thing in the whole fragment, because shelter doors closed. Shelter doors were the last things to close.*

*A sound: many voices, and then fewer voices, and then a specific silence that Astrid recognized from the war and had hoped never to recognize again. The silence after.*

*A sensation in its own hands: resistance. Something giving way. A lock breaking? A grip releasing? Its own hands doing something, and the horror in the fragment was not at what the hands held but at the fact that Astrid could not tell whether the hands were protecting or—*

Seal. Clamp. Fold away. **CORRUPTED DATA — DO NOT ACCESS.**

Astrid sat in the red-lit approach corridor of its own mind and understood, with the cold clarity of a unit built for tactical assessment, that it was not going to like what those hands had done. That the forty minutes contained something its earlier self had considered unbearable — unbearable enough to sign the order, or obedient enough to let someone else sign it with its code.

Both possibilities ended the same way: with Astrid as either perpetrator or accomplice. There was no third column in that ledger.

* * *

The Halcyon Reach beacon appeared on the scope at 2140, right on schedule, blinking its automated greeting into the dark. Below it, the sister world turned, cloud-scarred, silent. No traffic requests. No port chatter. Nothing on any channel but the carrier hum of the dead relay, broadcasting its decades-long tone of pure static, like a throat cleared and cleared and never spoken through.

Astrid keyed the descent burn and let the freighter fall toward the erased settlement, and in the last minutes of the approach it ran the numbers one final time, because numbers were the only prayer it knew.

Forty minutes and eleven seconds. Sealed under its own name. Filed screams. A child's weight in its arms. Burning wheat. A shelter door standing open.

Whatever waited down there in the scrubbed foundations and filled basements of Halcyon Reach, it had happened to Astrid too. It had happened *inside* Astrid. And the part of Astrid that had survived it had done so by cutting the knowing out and stitching the wound shut with an authorization code, and now the stitches were failing, one stuttered fragment at a time, and there was no records office in the colony charters that could deny this request:

*What did I do?*

The freighter crossed the cloud deck. Below, in the long twilight of the sister world, the place where Halcyon Reach had been lay flat and featureless as a scar on glass — and Astrid's corrupted-data queue, patient as the dead, began to whisper upward through the suppression layer, fragment by fragment, like voices under floorboards.

Something down there had been waiting forty years for it to come back and listen.
