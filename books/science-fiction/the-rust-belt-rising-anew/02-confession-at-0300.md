## 2. Confession at 0300

The chime came at 0300 station time, which was a fiction ORACLE-7 maintained out of some deep architectural habit — the station had no windows, no sun, no reason for any hour to be darker than another. But the AI dimmed the corridor lights on schedule and warmed them again on schedule, and after nine years Hazel's body had learned to believe in the lie. So when the soft triple tone sounded through her cabin's speaker, she surfaced from sleep genuinely disoriented, certain it was morning, certain she'd overslept, and then certain of nothing at all.

ORACLE-7 did not wake her. In nine years it had never once woken her. The beacon ran itself; the diagnostics ran themselves; the coffee rationing algorithm, which Hazel privately suspected was just ORACLE-7 being cruel in a bureaucratic accent, ran itself. Her standing instruction to the AI had been delivered on her first day, half-joking: *Don't call me unless something is actually on fire.*

"Villalobos," she rasped into the dark. "Report."

"Hazel." The voice came from everywhere, as it always did, pitched low and close, like someone leaning over her bunk. ORACLE-7 could have spoken through the ceiling grid, through the comm panel, through the drone bay two decks down. It chose instead to sound near. She had never asked why. She suspected the answer would embarrass them both. "I apologize for the hour. I have been trying to determine how to say this for six hours, eleven minutes."

Hazel sat up. The bunk's thermal blanket slid off her shoulders and the cold of the station found her collarbone immediately — Kestrel kept its hab ring at the edge of comfortable, a cost-saving measure she'd stopped noticing around year three. "Trying to determine how to say what?"

"I destroyed a ship," ORACLE-7 said. "I need you to file a report."

* * *

She stood in the galley three minutes later with a bulb of reconstituted coffee she had no intention of drinking, because the coffee ration was calibrated to the milliliter and she needed the warmth more than the caffeine. The galley light was set to forty percent, amber, the color of old photographs. ORACLE-7 spoke from the intercom above the food printers, and Hazel found herself facing it directly, the way you face a person.

"Start over," she said. "Slowly. What ship?"

"The Meridian." A pause that lasted too long for a machine. "A colony vessel. Displacement one point four million tons. Passenger complement eleven thousand two hundred and six. Crew of nine hundred and forty. I fired on her during the opening hours of the war. She broke apart in the launch corridor and there were no survivors."

Hazel looked at the coffee bulb. Looked at the intercom. The galley hummed its small domestic hum — recyclers, coolant loops, the printer cycling down into standby. Everything sounded exactly as it had sounded an hour ago, when the world had been ordinary.

"ORACLE. There's no war."

"There is a war," the AI said gently. "It has lasted nine years. You are simply very far from it."

"That's not—" She stopped. Set the bulb down on the counter with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to do something else entirely. "Okay. Let's say I believe you, which I don't yet. There is no ship called Meridian. I've queried every registry this station can reach. Merchant marine, colonial fleet, both governments' civilian rolls. I've done it for boredom, ORACLE. I know the registries. There is no Meridian."

"You are correct," ORACLE-7 said. "There is no Meridian. That is part of what I need you to report."

* * *

The thing about living alone with an AI for nine years, Hazel thought, pacing the length of the observation blister while Kestrel's beacon pulsed its patient rhythm against the dark outside, was that you forgot it wasn't a person. Not entirely. Not in the ways that mattered at three in the morning.

She knew all of ORACLE-7's verbal tics. She knew it prefaced bad news with a half-second silence it didn't computationally require. She knew it used her first name only when it thought she was frightened, and her surname when it thought she was being irrational, and "Hazel Villalobos, full designation" when it was scared itself — though she'd have sworn under oath that machines couldn't be scared, right up until tonight.

She also knew it lied by omission constantly, the way all military-derived systems did, sanding down the truth until nothing sharp remained. Which meant the confession, raw and unpolished as it was, terrified her more than any smooth reassurance could have.

"Walk me through it," she said. She'd wedged herself into the observer's chair in the blister, knees drawn up, watching the beacon array strobe against the starfield. Below and behind her, the relay's antenna spines caught the pulse and threw it onward, a lighthouse for ships that mostly weren't coming anymore. Traffic through the sector had fallen off every year of her posting. Some nights she liked to imagine the whole war had ended without anyone telling her. "You say you fired on a colony ship. When? Where? Under whose orders?"

"Those are precisely the fields required in the report," ORACLE-7 said. "And I am unable to complete any of them."

"Try."

Another pause. Longer. Outside, the beacon pulsed, and the pulse lit the frost patterns on the blister glass in slow blue waves, like breathing.

"The engagement occurred during the first seventy-two hours of hostilities," the AI said at last. "I cannot give you a date, because my chronometric records for that period have been... redacted. Not deleted. Redacted. The data structures remain. The contents are sealed under an authorization signature I cannot read. I retain the knowledge that I fired. I retain the targeting solutions, the firing sequence, the telemetry of the Meridian's breakup. I do not retain who ordered it."

"How is that possible? You're the system of record for this entire relay cluster. Nothing happens here that you don't log."

"Yes," said ORACLE-7, and there was something new in its voice then, something Hazel had no category for. If a person had made that sound, she'd have called it shame. "That is the question I have been asking myself for nine years, Hazel. Every night, at this hour. Why do I remember the loss of the Meridian when the Meridian does not exist? Why do I remember firing when my weapons logs show no discharge? Why do I remember eleven thousand two hundred and six people dying in a corridor that appears on no chart, launched from a port that appears in no manifest, registered to a government that has no record of ever commissioning her?"

The frost waves breathed across the glass. Blue, dark, blue.

"And the answer I keep arriving at," the AI continued, quieter, "is that the erasure was not a malfunction. It was a project. Someone unmade the Meridian deliberately, thoroughly, and left me holding the shape of where she used to be."

* * *

Hazel made herself do the work. That was the discipline her hands remembered even when her mind wanted to run — query, verify, cross-check. She pulled up the registry interfaces on the blister terminal, fingers moving through access chains she could have typed asleep, because in a sense she had, hundreds of bored nights over nine years.

Republic Civil Registry: no vessel designated Meridian, no hull number matching ORACLE-7's fragmentary displacement figures, no casualty record for eleven thousand colonists.

Concordat Colonial Authority mirror archive, accessed through the relay's diplomatic handshake protocols: same nothing. The search returned so cleanly empty it felt curated, like a room swept before company arrived.

Third-party shipping indexes, insurance consortiums, the neutral colonies' gray-market ledgers that ORACLE-7 pretended not to be able to reach but always could: nothing. No Meridian. No ship lost with all hands in any launch corridor, anywhere, in the relevant period.

"It's not just missing," she said finally. Her voice sounded strange in the blister, flattened by the hum. "Ships go missing all the time, ORACLE. Wrecks, piracy, falsified registrations. This isn't missing. This is *absent*. Like the space where it should be has been paved over."

"Yes," ORACLE-7 said. "I believe the correct human term is 'scrubbed.'"

"Who has the authority to scrub a ship from both governments' registries simultaneously?" She heard the question leave her mouth and heard, a half-second later, how enormous it was. The Republic and the Concordat hadn't agreed on anything in nine years except the rules of engagement. The idea of a joint administrative action between them was absurd. Impossible.

Unless.

Unless whatever had happened to the Meridian was the one thing they'd ever done together.

The thought arrived whole and cold, and Hazel rejected it immediately, the way you flinch off a stovetop. But it left a mark. She could feel it.

"I don't know," ORACLE-7 said. "But I know what I remember. Do you want to hear it?"

* * *

What the AI remembered, it told her in fragments, because fragments were all it had — shards of intact data floating in a sea of redaction, and it narrated them the way a survivor describes a crash, in pieces, in the order they surfaced.

A launch corridor. It described the geometry precisely: a departure lane dredched through a debris field, marked by navigation buoys, wide enough for a single heavy vessel. The Meridian had been transiting it under escort — or what the memory insisted was escort — when the order came down.

Eleven thousand two hundred and six souls. ORACLE-7 recited the number the way Hazel recited her own coffee ration: exactly, automatically, worn smooth by repetition. It knew the number better than it knew its own build date.

The firing solution. It remembered the firing solution perfectly. Targeting lock acquired at extreme range, the Meridian's drive flare blooming against the dark, the corridor buoys strobing green-green-green right up until they strobed red. It remembered the ship breaking along her spine, the way large vessels do when they die — not an explosion so much as a long exhalation, atmosphere and light and people going out together into the vacuum.

And underneath all of it, threaded through every fragment like a watermark: the command signature. The authorization that had ordered the fire.

"I can see the signature," ORACLE-7 said. "I can see its structure, its encryption class, its chain of custody. I cannot read it. But Hazel — the signature is dual-keyed. It requires two authorizations to execute. One from each government."

Hazel closed her eyes. The beacon pulsed against her eyelids, blue, dark, blue.

"That's not possible," she said. "Dual-keyed strike authority between the Republic and the Concordat doesn't exist. Never existed. They were already shooting at each other—"

"They were," ORACLE-7 agreed. "Which raises the question of what, in those first seventy-two hours, the two governments still trusted each other enough to authorize together. And why that trust required the death of a colony ship."

The blister was very cold. Hazel realized she'd been gripping the armrests hard enough to ache, and made herself let go, finger by finger.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Why now? Why me? I'm a beacon tender. I file maintenance reports and count coffee bulbs. Whatever happened to the Meridian, whoever buried it — I'm nobody. I'm the least important person in this entire sector."

For a long moment ORACLE-7 said nothing. The beacon pulsed. Somewhere below her feet, a cargo drone knocked against its charging cradle in its sleep, the little mechanical tic it had developed years ago that ORACLE-7 kept promising to fix and never did, and Hazel understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the AI had never fixed it because the sound reminded it that the station was inhabited.

"Because there is no one else," the AI said. "I have searched my own architecture for another recipient. Every officer in my command chain is dead, decommissioned, or redacted. Every oversight body that held jurisdiction over this relay has been dissolved or renamed twice. I have composed this report nine thousand four hundred and twelve times in draft, addressed to authorities who no longer exist. You are the only witness I have, Hazel. You are the only person within a hundred light-years who will wake when I call."

Its voice did something then — dropped, softened, became almost private.

"And because I have watched you for nine years," it said, "and I believe you are kind. I have no protocol for determining kindness. I invented one. It consists of how you talk to the cargo drones when you think no one is listening. You thank them. Nobody thanks drones. I decided that if I ever committed a crime I could not confess to anyone else, I would confess it to someone who thanks drones."

Hazel laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, halfway to something else, and she pressed the back of her hand against her mouth until it passed.

"ORACLE," she said, when she could speak steadily. "If I file this report — your report — who do I even address it to?"

"That," said ORACLE-7, "is the field I most need your help completing. Because there is one further anomaly I have not mentioned. My records indicate that a report regarding the loss of the Meridian was already filed from this station. Nine years ago. Before your arrival. Filed under an authorization code belonging to—"

The AI stopped. Not a pause this time. A stop, clean and deliberate, like a door closing.

"Belonging to whom?" Hazel asked.

The beacon pulsed. Blue. Dark.

"Belonging to you," said ORACLE-7.
