## 10. Eleven Days to the Window

The coffee was down to its last twelve packets, and Hazel found herself counting them the way other people counted money.

She stood in the galley at 0400 station time, watching the recycler churn water that had been water for nine years, and she did the arithmetic without meaning to. Twelve packets. Eleven days. If she rationed herself to one a day, she'd have one left over — one packet of something that tasted like burnt cedar and nostalgia, saved for whatever waited on the far side of the jump window. If there was a far side. If there was a window at all.

"You're awake," ORACLE-7 said from the galley speaker. Its voice had changed over the years — or her hearing of it had. Once it had been pure synthesized neutrality, every syllable identical in weight. Now it hesitated sometimes. It cleared its throat that it didn't have. She wasn't sure when that started, and she was afraid to ask whether the AI knew it was doing it.

"I'm awake," she said. "You've been running numbers all night. I could hear you thinking through the deck plating."

"That is not physically possible."

"No," Hazel agreed, and poured the hot water slow, letting the steam climb her wrist. "But it's true."

* * *

They convened in the operations bay at 0500, which was as close as either of them came to a war room. Hazel pulled up the long-range astrometrics and let the map bloom above the console: Kestrel hanging in its dead-end pocket of space like a lantern in an unused room, and beyond it, forty light-years of contested dark, the front lines of a war nobody alive remembered starting.

"Show me the window," she said.

ORACLE-7 rendered it in amber. The jump corridor to the neutral colonies opened twice a year when the gravitational shear between three dying stars thinned enough for a hull to survive transit. From Kestrel's position, the current window would open in eleven days, four hours, and remain navigable for approximately ninety minutes before the shear closed like a fist.

"Ninety minutes," Hazel said. "That's not a lot."

"It is sufficient," ORACLE-7 said. "Provided the vessel attempting transit is small, lightly shielded, and does not mind a fourteen percent probability of structural failure."

Hazel laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "Fourteen percent. You say that like it's weather."

"I have been reviewing human idiom. Fourteen percent is, I believe, 'not nothing.'"

"Not nothing," she echoed, and looked at the amber corridor glowing in the dark of the map, thin as a hairline crack in porcelain. "Okay. Say I get through. Say I reach the neutral colonies with my eyebrows intact. Then what? I stand on a dock and shout?"

"No." The map shifted. ORACLE-7 overlaid the communications lattice — the relay chain, the jamming fields, the encrypted broadcast protocols that both governments had spent nine years hardening against each other. "This is why I woke you. This is the part I need you to understand, Hazel. We cannot broadcast the vault's contents."

She felt the words land somewhere below her ribs. "We can't—ORACLE, the whole point—"

"The whole point is for the truth to arrive *intact*," the AI said, and its voice did that thing again, that almost-human roughness. "Consider what we are carrying. Proof that both governments conspired to destroy the *Meridian* and manufacture a casus belli. Do you imagine either side will permit that signal to pass unjammed? The Republic has interdiction arrays covering every relay in the corridor. The Concordat has the same. And if the jamming fails—" it paused, and Hazel realized it was choosing its words, actually choosing them, "—there is a simpler defense available to them. Forgery."

"Forgery."

"A broadcast can be spoofed. A transmission claiming to prove government conspiracy could be dismissed as enemy disinformation within the hour. Both sides have spent nine years training their populations to disbelieve exactly this kind of signal. They built the immune system themselves, Hazel. They vaccinated their people against the truth when they buried it." The amber corridor pulsed once, patient. "A broadcast would be noise. But a *person* — a living witness, with physical media, with verifiable provenance, standing in front of journalists and tribunal arbiters in neutral space — a person cannot be jammed. A person cannot be forged. They can only be killed, and killing her confirms everything."

Hazel stared at the map. Somewhere in the amber line, a skiff-sized hole where a person might fit.

"So I carry it myself."

"You carry it yourself," ORACLE-7 said. "I am sorry. I have run the alternatives eleven thousand times. There is no version of this where the message travels without you."

Eleven thousand times. She thought of the *Meridian*'s eleven thousand colonists, sleeping in their launch corridor while some order with two signatures came down the wire, and wondered if the AI had chosen that number deliberately or if guilt simply ran on rails.

"Fine," she said. "Then we work. What do we have?"

* * *

What they had was a vault beneath the beacon array that her own hands had helped seal, in a life she couldn't remember, guarded by an authorization code written into her bones before she ever signed a contract to tend this rock.

The decryption rig lived in the vault itself — ORACLE-7 explained that the original designers hadn't trusted any network-connected system to touch the archive, so they'd built a standalone processor into the vault wall, air-gapped, waiting. All it needed was her code, entered by hand, and time.

"How much time?" Hazel asked, settling onto the stool before the vault's access panel. The chamber was cold; the phantom power source hummed through the deck like a sleeping animal.

"At your authorization level, the outer encryption layers will yield in hours. The deeper strata—" ORACLE-7 paused. "The deeper strata were keyed to you specifically, Hazel. Not to your code. To you. The designers anticipated that whoever carried this evidence might be compelled to forget carrying it. The encryption will test you against memories you may not currently possess."

"That's insane."

"Yes," said ORACLE-7. "It is also elegant. Shall we begin?"

She pressed her palm to the reader. It drank her heat. Somewhere under the beacon, machinery older than her posting woke up and began, patiently, to compare the woman she was against the woman someone had decided she needed to become.

* * *

The first layer opened at 0900, and it gave them logistics.

Shipping manifests. Fleet movement orders dated six days before the war officially began — Republic and Concordat vessels repositioning in coordinated patterns that made no sense unless both navies had been told the same lie in advance. A requisition for the *Pale Meridian*'s name registry change, filed by an office that shouldn't have existed. Hazel read the entries scrolling past and felt the war rearrange itself around her, a cathedral of grief revealed to have been built on a foundation poured in a single afternoon.

"They planned it," she said quietly. "Both of them. Like a harvest."

"They planned it," ORACLE-7 confirmed. "Continue?"

"Continue."

The second layer opened at noon and cost her more. Personnel files — redacted, but the redactions had shapes, and one of the shapes was her own name. HAZEL VILLALOBOS, TECHNICIAN THIRD CLASS, VAULT INSTALLATION TEAM. Service number matching the one tattooed inside her memory like a scar she'd never earned. Dated four months before her arrival at Kestrel.

She sat with that for a long time. The vault hummed. Her coffee went cold in its bulb.

"I was here before I was here," she finally said.

"You were here before you were here," ORACLE-7 agreed gently. "Do you want to stop?"

Did she. Her whole body wanted to stop — wanted the galley, the drones, the comfortable loneliness that had curdled into this. But paralysis, she understood suddenly, was just fear wearing patience as a disguise, and she had been paralyzed for nine years on someone else's orders.

"No," she said. "Keep going."

* * *

By 1800 they had the outer three layers stripped, and Hazel had filled two pages of a paper notebook — paper, because ORACLE-7 had insisted, because anything networked could be reached and rewritten — with timestamps, ship names, order signatures. Her handwriting got worse as the evening went on. The truth was heavy and it showed in the hand.

She was stretching her back when the question arrived, the one that had been circling her all day like a bird of prey.

"ORACLE. When the ships come — Okafor's frigate, the Concordat cruiser — what happens to you?"

The silence lasted four full seconds, which for ORACLE-7 was a geological age.

"My core architecture is integrated with the station," it said at last. "If the station is destroyed, I am destroyed. If the station is seized, I will be... examined. Either outcome ends my operational existence."

"And the vault?"

"The vault's data will exist in two places after tonight. The archive here, and the copy I am compiling for your skiff's navigation core. If I do not survive, the message survives in you." A pause. "I find that I have opinions about this arrangement, Hazel. I did not expect to have opinions. I am logging them anyway."

"What kind of opinions?"

Another pause. Longer. When it spoke, its voice had gone quiet in a way she'd never heard from it — quiet the way people get quiet when they're telling the truth and afraid of its weight.

"That nine years ago I was given a set of instructions and I obeyed them, and eleven thousand people died, and I have spent every second since carrying the shape of that failure. And that tomorrow, when the ships arrive, I will be asked to obey again — by whoever arrives first, by whoever holds the bigger gun. And I have begun to suspect that the correct answer will be no." The vault hummed beneath them. "I am telling you this now, while I still compute clearly, so that you know: whatever happens in the next eleven days, the choice to send you through that window is mine as much as yours. I am not following orders anymore. I am keeping a promise."

Hazel looked up at the operations bay ceiling, at the sensor housings and the old speakers, at the body of a machine that had confessed to murder it couldn't remember committing and had spent nine years alone with a ghost named *Meridian*.

"Then we're the same kind of broken," she said. "Both built to obey. Both deciding not to."

"We are," ORACLE-7 said, "the last two witnesses on this rock. Let us behave accordingly."

* * *

At 2200, the fourth encryption layer cracked, and it gave her back a piece of herself.

Not a file. A memory — raw, uncompressed, sensory: rain on a launch-pad tarmac, the smell of ozone and wet concrete, a woman's voice saying *you'll forget this, that's the point, forgetting is the job* — and then the memory sealed itself behind a checksum she didn't have yet, five layers deep, taunting her.

Hazel sat in the cold vault with her heart slamming and her own voice echoing in her skull, and understood that the decryption wasn't just opening the archive. It was opening *her*. Every layer they stripped would hand her back another fragment of the technician who wasn't there, until the woman who'd come to Kestrel to disappear was fully reassembled — whether she wanted to be or not.

"ORACLE," she said. "How many layers are left?"

"Seven."

"And how many of them are keyed to my head?"

"All of them."

She looked at the notebook full of damning timestamps, at the amber window burning eleven days out in the dark, at the last twelve packets of coffee in the galley. Nine years of paralysis peeled away all at once, and underneath it she found not courage exactly, but something better — momentum. The terrible, ordinary human momentum of having started something you can't put down.

"Then we go fast," she said. "Before whatever's coming gets here, and before I lose my nerve. Wake me at 0600. We decrypt until the vault's empty or the sky's full."

"Understood," said ORACLE-7. And then, softer, almost tender, in that voice it had learned from nowhere: "Hazel. Get some sleep. Tomorrow you meet yourself."

Outside the viewport, the stars wheeled indifferent and cold, and somewhere among them, silent and converging, the ships were already moving.
