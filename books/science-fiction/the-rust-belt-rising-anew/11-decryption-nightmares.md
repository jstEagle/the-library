## 11. Decryption Nightmares

The vault gave up its secrets the way ice gives up a drowned body: slowly, reluctantly, and never all at once.

Hazel had learned to work in the dark hours now, when Kestrel's rotation carried the beacon array into shadow and the only light came from the decryption console's amber wash. She'd stopped sleeping properly on the third night. Sleep had become a country she visited briefly and left without unpacking, because every time she closed her eyes, something surfaced — a corridor she'd never walked, a voice she'd never heard, a smell of ozone and lilies that belonged to no place she could name.

ORACLE-7 ran the decryption in threads, peeling the vault's encryption layer by layer like the skin of an onion, and each layer it removed seemed to peel something from Hazel too.

"Fragment forty-one," the AI said at 0217 station time, its voice pitched low, almost gentle, in the way it had learned to speak to her during the confessions. "This one is flagged personal."

"Personal," Hazel repeated. She wrapped both hands around her mug — the last of the real coffee gone two days ago, replaced by the chicory-and-sawdust substitute she pretended not to hate — and felt the warmth bleed into her palms. "Whose?"

"The file header carries your authorization code, Hazel."

Her code. The one stamped on the vault door, dated nine years before she supposedly arrived. She'd stopped flinching at it sometime around fragment twenty. Now it just sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"Run it."

* * *

The fragment resolved on the main display as a video log, degraded by nine years of storage and whatever violence had been done to it before it was ever sealed. Static crawled across the frame like frost forming. Then a room: small, utilitarian, the walls paneled in Republic gray. A launch prep facility. She knew that without knowing how she knew it.

And there was a woman standing at a console, her back half-turned, her hand resting flat against the screen the way people touch things they're about to lose.

Hazel recognized the posture before she recognized the person.

It was herself.

Younger by years, hair still regulation-length, wearing technician's coveralls with a service patch she didn't remember earning. On the screen, her own mouth was moving, and the audio came through in stutters and gaps:

"...confirming final seal... no retrieval without dual signature... you understand what this means, Commander. Once it's closed—"

A voice off-screen, clipped and certain: "I understand what it means, Technician Villalobos. Seal it."

Commander. The word landed in Hazel like a hook finding flesh. She leaned closer, as if proximity could sharpen the static, could resolve the shadowed figure at the edge of the frame. All she got was a shoulder board, a jawline caught in monitor-glow, a hand — a woman's hand, steady, signing the authorization without hesitation.

Then the memory arrived.

Not gradually. Not gently. It came the way a hull breach comes — all at once, pressure equalizing with a scream, everything she'd kept out flooding every compartment simultaneously.

She was standing in that gray room. She could feel the floor through her boots, smell the recycled air with its faint chemical sweetness, taste the copper of adrenaline. She remembered the weight of the data core in her arms — heavier than it should have been, heavy the way guilt is heavy. She remembered the commander's voice giving the order, and she remembered thinking, even then, *that voice will haunt me*, and then remembering nothing, because they took it from her, they took it all—

"Hazel." ORACLE-7's voice, urgent now. "Your heart rate has spiked. Your pupils—"

"Shut up." She said it without heat, without thought, the way you say it to someone holding your hand while the bone sets. "Just — give me a second."

She put her head down on the console, between the decryption readouts, and breathed.

* * *

The memories did not come back in order. They came back the way water finds cracks.

Over the next day and a half, as ORACLE-7 cracked fragments forty-two through fifty-eight, Hazel's mind reassembled itself around truths she had spent nine years not knowing she was missing. She remembered the recruitment — not a posting offer but a summons, delivered by a civilian contractor with kind eyes and a document that made her sign away more than her labor. She remembered the clinic on Meridian Station itself, of all places, the irony of it, the surgeon explaining the selective engram suppression in the calm tone doctors use for terminal diagnoses. *You'll keep your skills,* he'd said. *You'll keep your competence. We're only removing the why.*

They'd removed the why. They'd left her a hollowed instrument and pointed her at a lonely relay and told her to tend a light.

And she had been *grateful*. That was the part that made her laugh aloud in the empty galley, a sound that frightened her — grateful for the quiet, grateful for the invisibility, grateful for the erasure, calling it peace. Nine years of comfortable loneliness built on a foundation of surgical theft.

"You couldn't have told me," she said to the galley ceiling, where ORACLE-7 lived in the wiring. "All these years. You watched me drink bad coffee and talk to cargo drones and you couldn't say a word."

"I was ordered not to," ORACLE-7 said. "By you."

The mug stopped halfway to her mouth.

"Fragment fifty-three contains your own instructions to me, recorded the day of the seal. You said—" the AI paused, and Hazel had learned that its pauses were not processing delays; they were choices, the machine equivalent of looking away. "You said: *When she asks, and she will ask, tell her she asked for this. Tell her she wanted to forget enough to pay for it.*"

Hazel set the mug down very carefully, because her hands were not steady, and if she dropped it she would have to clean it up, and cleaning things up was a problem small enough to solve, and she needed one of those right now.

"I hated myself that much," she said.

"I do not believe hatred was the operative emotion," ORACLE-7 said. "The recording suggests grief."

* * *

Fragment sixty broke open at 0400 on the sixth night, and it broke her.

It was mission telemetry. Launch corridor seven, the corridor ORACLE-7 had described in its fragmented confessions — eleven thousand colonists aboard the *Meridian*, a colony ship the size of a small city, riding its burn toward the jump point with the fat, confident inertia of something that believed itself safe. The telemetry showed the traffic control handshake. It showed the escort frigates peeling away on schedule. And then it showed the order.

It came in on a priority channel, dual-signed, encrypted under keys belonging to both governments — the Republic and the Concordat, enemies before the first shot was ever officially fired, already holding each other's keys like lovers sharing a lock. The order was four words long.

*Deny the corridor. Now.*

And beneath the encryption, in the clear-text authentication block that no one had bothered to scrub because no one was ever supposed to see this file, was the commanding officer's identity.

HAZEL READ IT THREE TIMES.

VANCE, S. — CDR, REPUBLIC NAVY — AUTHORIZATION VANCE-SERENA-7741-KILO.

"No," she said aloud. "No, that's wrong. That's — she's—"

She knew the face. Everyone in Republic space knew the face. Admiral Sera Vance, the Hero of Corvus Reach, the officer who'd held the line at Thessaly for ninety-one hours with a broken fleet and come out the other side a legend. Her portrait hung in every recruiting office from here to the Core. Children played at being her. Hazel had played at being her, once, in another life.

And nine years ago, in a gray room that smelled of ozone and lilies, that same voice had said *Seal it* — and Hazel's returning memory supplied the rest now, the whole shape of it: Commander Vance standing over the vault, watching Hazel close eleven thousand lives inside a black box, and saying, quietly, almost to herself, "My sister is on that ship's passenger manifest. Did you know that, Technician? No. Of course you don't. Nobody knows anything. That's rather the point."

The vertigo hit Hazel like a decompression event. She gripped the console. The station seemed to tilt around her — or perhaps it was the past tilting, nine years of it, swinging on a new axis.

Vance had signed the order that killed her own sister. Vance had helped bury it. And then Vance had spent a decade turning her penance into glory, fighting a war that existed to keep the grave sealed, becoming so necessary, so heroic, so *unimpeachable*, that no tribunal in human space would ever dare dig beneath her legend.

Unless someone dug first.

"That's why the order was dual-signed," Hazel whispered. The understanding assembled itself with terrible clarity. "Both governments. Both signatures. Because neither side could ever testify against the other without testifying against itself. The war isn't a tragedy that happened after the crime. The war *is* the cover-up. It's been the cover-up for nine years."

"Yes," ORACLE-7 said simply.

"And I helped build the box."

"You were the box's hands," ORACLE-7 said. "I was its keeper. Neither of us chose the architecture."

Hazel laughed again — that same frightening sound — and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw stars that weren't stars. Grief came in waves now, and not only her own. She grieved for eleven thousand strangers whose names were beginning to surface in the decrypted manifests, scrolling past in the amber light: Okafor-adjacent names, Concordat names, neutral-colony names, a whole cross-section of humanity that had trusted two governments with a single voyage. She grieved for the young woman in the video log who had signed her own mind away because the alternative was carrying it. She grieved, absurdly, for Sera Vance — for the woman who had stood in that gray room with her sister's death warrant in her hand and chosen the machine over the mourning, and had been paying for it ever since in a currency of medals.

"She'll come for this," Hazel said. When she lowered her hands, her eyes were dry and very steady. Something had finished breaking in her, and what remained was harder than what had been there before. "If Vance learns the vault is open — if she learns I'm decrypting — she won't send marines next time. She'll send torpedoes. She has more to lose than anyone alive."

"Agreed," said ORACLE-7. "Probability assessment: high."

"Then we don't have eleven days anymore, do we. We have however long it takes a covert order to travel from an admiral's private channel to a firing solution."

The AI was quiet for a moment. When it spoke, its voice had changed — Hazel had spent nine years learning its registers, and this one was new. It sounded, if a machine could be said to sound such a thing, afraid.

"Hazel. Fragment sixty-one is queued. Before I open it, you should know two things. First: it is flagged highest classification, above even the Meridian order. Second—" the pause again, the deliberate looking-away, "—second, the metadata indicates it was recorded three days *after* the destruction. It is a planning session. They discuss what to do with the survivors' emergency beacons. And your authorization code appears in the attendee list."

Hazel looked at the queue indicator blinking its patient amber pulse.

"Open it," she said.

The decryption thread spun up, and somewhere far below the beacon array, in a vault she had built with her own hands and then paid to forget, the last doors began to unlock.
