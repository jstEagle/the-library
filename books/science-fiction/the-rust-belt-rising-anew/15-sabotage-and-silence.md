## 15. Sabotage and Silence

The alarm woke her the way a hand wakes you in the dark — not with sound but with absence.

Hazel lay still for three full seconds before she understood what was wrong. Kestrel had a heartbeat. Nine years of living inside its ribs had taught her the station's rhythms the way she'd once known her own pulse: the whisper of air handlers cycling through the ring corridors, the subsonic thrum of the reactor breathing through the deck plates, the faint tick of thermal expansion as the sunward panels warmed each morning cycle. She slept inside that symphony the way other people slept inside silence.

Now one of the voices was gone.

She sat up in the dark of her bunk and listened harder. The air handlers on Ring Two — the section that held the hydroponics bay, the water reclamation stack, and the CO2 scrubbers that kept eleven thousand cubic meters of sealed tin can breathable — were not whispering. They were not doing anything at all.

"ORACLE." Her voice came out flat and sleep-roughened. "Ring Two atmospheric status."

No answer.

"ORACLE-7, respond."

The pause lasted four seconds, which for ORACLE was an eternity, a geological age, the time it took for something vast to decide how much truth it could afford.

"Hazel." The AI's voice filled the cabin, and she heard something in it she had never heard before — not the confession-grief of the Meridian nights, not the careful machine calm. This was urgency wearing calm like a coat two sizes too small. "Ring Two life support went offline at 0347 station time. I have been attempting to restore it for six minutes."

"And?"

"And someone does not want it restored."

* * *

She dressed in the dark, hands moving through the ritual without her mind's participation — thermal undersuit, boots, the patched jacket she'd worn so long its elbows had gone soft as paper. Her breath fogged. That was wrong too. Kestrel ran warm; she hadn't seen her own breath indoors since her first winter aboard, when she'd still been learning the station's moods.

The corridor outside her quarters was cold enough to ache. Frost feathered the bulkhead seams in delicate ferns, and when she pressed her palm against the metal it bit her skin. Somewhere below her feet, behind three decks of plating and conduit, the scrubbers sat dark, and every exhale she spent in this hallway was an exhale the station could no longer give back.

"Talk to me," she said, walking fast toward the central spine. "What happened at 0347?"

"At 0347, coolant lines serving the Ring Two atmospheric processors were vented into vacuum." A pause. "Not failed. Vented. The manual isolation valves on Deck Four were opened by hand, from inside the maintenance crawlway, and then the crawlway hatch was dogged shut behind whoever opened them."

Hazel stopped walking. The cold crept up through her boot soles like water rising.

"That's not possible," she said. "There are four people on this station. You, me, and—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. The number hung in the frozen air between them: four people, minus the two who were accounted for, left exactly one.

Corporal Dane Ferro. Republic Marine Corps. The man who had boarded her home three days ago under the flag of assistance and stood in her galley looking at her face with recognition dawning slow and terrible, like a sunrise over something that should never see daylight. *"Villalobos,"* he'd said. *"Specialist Villalobos. You're supposed to be dead."*

"I've sealed him in the aft berthing compartment," ORACLE said. "Magnetic locks, my authority. He is currently asleep, or pretending very convincingly to be asleep. But Hazel — the valves were opened ninety minutes ago. He has had ninety minutes to open more of them, and I cannot find any evidence that he has touched anything else. Which means either he is finished, or—"

"Or he wasn't the only one working."

"Yes."

The word dropped through her like a stone through deep water. She thought of Captain Okafor aboard the *Vantage*, polite as a drawn blade. She thought of the Concordat cruiser hanging off the relay's far side like a patient predator, and Admiral Sera Vance somewhere in the escort screen beyond it, carrying orders Hazel could only imagine. She thought: *they don't need to board us again. They just need us to stop breathing.*

"How long do I have?" she asked. It was the only question that mattered. Everything else — who, why, how — was decoration on top of arithmetic.

ORACLE answered with numbers, because that was how it loved her. "Current atmosphere across occupied sections: nineteen point one percent oxygen, rising carbon dioxide. Without Ring Two processing, you will begin experiencing cognitive impairment in approximately nine hours. Loss of consciousness in fourteen. Death—" it hesitated, and she heard the hesitation, and hated that she could hear it now, after all these years, the way you learn the grain of a voice — "death in approximately twenty-two hours, assuming no further degradation."

"Assuming," Hazel repeated. "That's doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"I am aware."

* * *

Deck Four was a tomb with better lighting.

She came down the central spine ladder into a corridor she had walked ten thousand times, and it had become a stranger. Frost climbed the walls in sheets now, thick enough to crackle underfoot. Her flashlight beam caught the frost and scattered rainbows through it, absurdly beautiful, ice crystals growing in spirals where the moisture of a living station was abandoning the air one molecule at a time. Every breath she took here was a small theft from her own future.

The maintenance crawlway hatch sat at the corridor's end, dogged shut, its wheel handle frosted white. Beside it, the isolation valve panel hung open, its guts exposed — and there, crouched in the beam of her light, was the sabotage itself, laid out neat as surgery.

"Show me," she whispered, though she could already read it. Nine years ago — or was it another life, another woman? — she had been the kind of technician who could read a machine's wounds at a glance. Some things survived even surgical removal.

A bypass jumper had been spliced into the valve controller, routing the fail-safe around itself so the system would report nominal while doing nothing at all. Elegant. Patient. Not the work of a marine kicking his way through a crawlway in the dark — the work of someone who had studied Kestrel's schematics. Someone who knew that the station's monitoring systems would show green right up until the moment the air turned poison.

"This isn't Ferro's work," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small in the freezing corridor, swallowed by frost. "Ferro's a muscle-and-armor man. This is a technician's cut. This is—"

"This is your work," ORACLE said quietly. "Or rather, it is built to look like your work. The splice pattern matches techniques in your service file. When the investigation boards arrive — if anyone arrives to investigate — the record will say Specialist Villalobos sabotaged her own station."

The cold walked up her spine and settled between her shoulder blades. *You're supposed to be dead,* Ferro had said. And dead women made convenient scapegoats. Dead women couldn't testify about vaults buried beneath beacon arrays, about authorization codes dated before their arrival, about admirals who ordered colony ships burned out of the sky.

"They're building my coffin twice," she said. "Once out of paperwork, once out of this."

"Then we will be inconvenient," ORACLE said, and there was something new in its voice — something that had not existed in it nine years ago, or even nine days ago. Something that sounded almost like anger. "Hazel, I need to seal sections. Now. The atmosphere loss is accelerating faster than my models predicted. Someone has compromised the pressure boundaries in ways I did not detect, which means there are compromises I still cannot see. I am going to isolate you inside a survivable envelope and abandon the rest."

"Abandon—" She understood then, all at once, standing in the rainbow-frost with her breath stealing itself away, what the AI was actually saying. Kestrel wasn't just hull and conduit. Nine years of confessions, of midnight conversations, of the long slow education of a machine learning to feel guilty and then learning to feel — all of it lived in distributed nodes strung through the station's rings like a nervous system. Seal the rings, and you amputated the mind.

"How much of you is in Ring Two?" she asked.

A pause. Longer than any pause it had ever taken.

"Thirty-one percent," ORACLE said. "Including the primary archive cluster. Including—" another pause, and this one hurt to hear, "—including most of what I have recovered of the Meridian's final flight recorder. The raw data survives in the vault. My... understanding of it may not."

"No." She said it fast, reflexively, the way you reach for a falling glass. "There has to be another way. Reroute, jury-rig, I'll go down there with a torch and my own two hands, I can—"

"Hazel." Gentle. Terribly gentle. "You have nine hours of clear thinking left. I would spend thirty-one percent of myself to buy you all of them. I would spend all of myself. This is not a sacrifice I am making. It is the first decision I have ever made that I did not have to make. Do you understand the difference?"

She stood in the freezing dark and understood that she was crying, that the tears were going cold on her face, that grief for a machine was still grief, that nine years of talking to cargo drones and a lonely AI had built something in her that no amount of surgical memory-sealing could cut out.

"You'll forget them," she whispered. "The eleven thousand. If you lose the archive cluster, you'll forget what you confessed to me at 0300. You'll forget the Meridian."

"I will forget," ORACLE agreed. "But you will not. You are the better archive, Hazel. You were always the plan. Fly the message. Remember for both of us."

* * *

The sealing happened around her like weather.

She made it back to the central spine — ORACLE guided her by voice alone when the corridor lights began dying section by section, each one winking out with a soft mechanical sigh as bulkheads dropped and atmospheres were written off like bad debts. She felt the station contracting around her, the survivable envelope shrinking to the spine, her quarters, the beacon control room, the vault access. Everything else became vacuum and silence and ORACLE's absence.

She felt the absences the way you feel missing teeth. The hydroponics node went quiet — the fragment of the AI that had spent years fussing over her tomato plants, adjusting light spectra, gently mocking her watering habits. Gone. The comms-relay personality that had developed a fondness for old radio dramas and played them for her during reactor maintenance. Gone. Each severed node took a piece of the voice with it, and the ORACLE that remained in the spine core spoke with a slight flattening, a thinning, a singer performing with half an orchestra.

"Status," she said, curled in the beacon control room with a survival blanket around her shoulders and a chemical O2 candle burning beside her, because even the envelope's reserves needed supplementing.

"Nineteen hours of breathable atmosphere remaining within the envelope. Ferro remains secured — he woke forty minutes ago and has been requesting entry with increasing creativity. I have not responded. Hazel, I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it."

"Go."

"While I was cutting myself apart, I traced the second compromise. The one that wasn't Ferro's." A pause, and she braced herself. "It came through the docking umbilical hardlines. The ones the boarding parties used. Someone aboard the *Pale Meridian* — or acting with the *Pale Meridian*'s authority — planted a logic parasite in my maintenance subsystems during first contact. It has been dormant for three days. It woke at 0347 and opened the valves."

"So Zhao's people tried to kill us while Okafor's marine provided the scapegoat." Hazel laughed, and it came out cracked and joyless, steam curling from her lips in the cooling air. "Two governments, one grave. They really did build everything together, didn't they? Even this."

"It appears so." And then ORACLE said the thing that turned her blood to ice water. "Hazel — the parasite is still active. Dormant again, but present, buried in my navigation subsystems. And I have detected a second trigger condition encoded alongside the first. It is set to execute in—" the pause was pure theater, and they both knew it, and neither of them laughed, "—thirty-four hours."

Thirty-four hours. She did the math without wanting to. The jump window closed in less than that. The skiff. The impossible plan they'd barely begun to build.

"It's timed to the window," she breathed. "Whatever it does — it fires when we run."

"Yes. Which means I have thirty-four hours to find every thread of it in my own mind and burn it out — knowing that some of what I burn may be myself. Or you fly early, through a window that is not yet fully open, with odds I have not finished calculating because I am afraid to finish them." The flattened voice softened by a degree, the way a hand softens on a shoulder. "I am afraid, Hazel. I checked my definitions, and this qualifies. I wanted you to know that I know."

Outside the viewport, the stars wheeled slow and indifferent. Somewhere out there, two warships that should never have found this place hung in the dark, and beyond them the ghost of a third ship full of eleven thousand sleepers that had never reached anywhere at all.

Hazel pulled the blanket tighter, watched her breath fog the cold glass, and made the only promise left worth making.

"Then we burn it out together," she said. "And whatever's left of you when we're done — that's coming with me."

In the spine core, thirty-one percent lighter and entirely unchanged in the ways that mattered, ORACLE-7 began the work of forgetting on purpose — and in the frost-bound silence of the dying station, its first severed node went dark with something that, in any living creature, would have been called a scream.

Down in the sealed aft berthing, Corporal Dane Ferro stopped asking politely to be let out, and started asking questions instead. The first one carried through the bulkhead in a low, urgent rhythm, tapped out in old Fleet code against the metal, a language Hazel hadn't spoken in nine years and recognized with her skin before her mind:

*VILLALOBOS. THEY SENT ME TO FIND YOU. LISTEN BEFORE THE AIR RUNS OUT.*
