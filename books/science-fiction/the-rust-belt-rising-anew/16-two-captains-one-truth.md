## 16. Two Captains, One Truth

The airlock cycled twice in one hour, and both times Hazel felt it in her teeth.

Kestrel had never been built for guests. The station's designers had imagined a skeleton crew of four rotating through on six-month tours, not a woman alone for nine years, and certainly not two enemy warships parked off her flanks like dogs circling a bone. The docking collar groaned as pressure equalized — a sound like an old man clearing his throat before saying something difficult.

She stood in the corridor outside the galley with her arms crossed and her heart going like a failing pump. ORACLE-7 had dimmed the lights to what its behavioral models called *de-escalatory amber*, which Hazel suspected was a fancy way of saying the AI was nervous too.

*They are coming,* ORACLE said through the deck speaker, quiet enough that only she could hear. *Captain Okafor first. He requested to board unarmed. Captain Zhao has agreed to the same condition, but I detect two pulse pistols in her boot soles.*

"Of course you do."

*I mention it only for completeness.*

"Sure you do." Hazel exhaled. "Any last advice?"

There was a pause — three full seconds, an eternity for a machine that thought in nanoseconds. When ORACLE spoke again, its voice had that strange graininess it got when it was doing something it had no protocol for.

*Tell them the truth slowly. Humans can only swallow it in pieces.*

The inner door opened.

* * *

Idris Okafor came through the airlock like a man entering a church he wasn't sure he still believed in. He was tall in the way that made station corridors feel narrower, his Republic grays immaculate despite nine days of silent running, his face carrying the particular exhaustion of an officer who had read his sealed orders twice and hated them both times. Behind him, uninvited but unavoidable, came Lieutenant Reyes, the marine who had looked at Hazel across the mess table two days ago and said her name like a question she'd spent nine years burying.

Hazel had insisted Reyes stay aboard the *Vantage*. Okafor had insisted otherwise. They had compromised on Reyes standing at the airlock, visible and silent, which meant Hazel now had to conduct the most important conversation of her life under the eyes of a man who knew who she used to be.

Zhao arrived ninety seconds later through the opposite collar, because Kestrel, in its infinite bureaucratic wisdom, had been built with two. She was smaller than Okafor, compact and precise, wearing Concordat black with the collar open like a deliberate insult to formality. Her eyes went around the corridor once — the pipes, the junction boxes, the sight lines — and Hazel understood that this woman had already mapped every way this meeting could become an ambush.

"Captain Villalobos," Zhao said. Her voice was dry, unhurried. "You'll forgive me if I don't salute. I'm told this is neutral ground."

"This is my kitchen," Hazel said. "That's the only neutrality on offer."

Something flickered at the corner of Zhao's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one.

Okafor extended a hand instead of words, and after a moment Hazel took it. His grip was careful, controlled — a man aware that everything he did was being recorded by three governments and one very attentive station.

"Captain," he said. "I've read your service record."

"No, you haven't," Hazel said. "Because there isn't one. That's rather the point."

His jaw tightened. He didn't deny it.

* * *

They sat in the galley because it was the only room with more than two chairs, and even then Hazel had to drag a cargo crate over from the supply bay. The table between them held three cups of reconstituted coffee — Hazel's private reserve, the good stuff she'd been rationing for years, spent now without ceremony because some occasions demanded real coffee even if the real stuff came from a powder packet.

ORACLE watched through every camera and said nothing. Hazel could feel it the way you feel weather changing.

Okafor wrapped both hands around his cup and didn't drink. "Let's skip past where we both know we're starting," he said. "My orders were to seize or destroy this station. Yours—" he nodded at Zhao— "were the same. We've both stalled those orders for days, which means we're both already guilty of something. So let's find out what we're guilty of together."

"Elegant," Zhao said. "You should write doctrine." But she set her cup down, and her hands went flat on the table, and Hazel saw the moment the captain became a person: a small tightening around the eyes, a breath taken through the nose. "I lost my brother at Corvus Reach," Zhao said. "Year two of the war. Everyone in this room lost someone at Corvus Reach, or Halcyon, or the Long Retreat. Eleven years. Forty million dead, give or take a few cities." She looked up. "If you've dragged us here for anything less than a reason worth forty million, Captain Villalobos, I will put you out an airlock myself and apologize to your ghost."

Hazel had rehearsed this. She'd rehearsed it in the shower, in the dark, in the vault beneath the beacon array with the decryption core humming against her palms. She'd rehearsed opening statements and dramatic pauses and the slow reveal. Standing in her own galley, she threw all of it away.

"The ship you're named after," she said to Zhao. "The *Pale Meridian*. Why did your navy name a cruiser that?"

Zhao blinked. "It's an old Concordat mourning tradition. Ships named for disasters. There were eleven thousand colonists lost when the *Meridian* jumped into a Republic minefield during the first week of the war. It was the atrocity that started everything. My grandmother—"

"There was no minefield," Hazel said.

The galley went very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor, a coolant pump cycled, absurdly domestic.

"The *Meridian* was destroyed by a single weapon strike," Hazel continued, and her own voice sounded far away to her, like she was listening to a recording. "Not mines. Not either fleet. One shot, fired by an automated platform sitting in the launch corridor, running on command signatures from *both* governments simultaneously. The order that killed your brother's war's first victims came from Republic High Command and the Concordat Directorate at the same time, encrypted in a shared cipher neither side admitted existed until after the shooting started." She slid a data chip across the table. "Eleven thousand people weren't casualties of the war. They were the *cause* of it. Manufactured. Deliberate. Both capitals wanted a war, and they needed a corpse big enough to make everyone stop asking why."

Nobody moved.

Then Okafor picked up the chip. He held it like it might be armed, which, Hazel supposed, in every way that mattered, it was.

*Play it,* she told ORACLE silently, and the wall display woke.

* * *

The fragment was ninety seconds long. It had taken ORACLE-7 and Hazel six nights of decryption to pull it clean, and it was ugly — corrupted telemetry, half a voice recording, a weapons-platform handshake log rendered in raw code that scrolled too fast to read. But the center of it was intact, and the center of it was a voice.

*"—confirm dual-key authorization. Republic signature verified. Concordat signature verified. Platform Kestrel-Prime will execute on my mark. God forgive us, mark."*

A pause. Then, distant, muffled, the sound every spacer learned to dread and none ever forgot: a jump-space rupture where no jump was scheduled. A scream compressed into static.

And then a second voice, younger, shaking, close to the recorder: *"Commander, the corridor — there were lifeboats — they're pinging — do we—"*

*"Seal it. Seal the corridor. That's a direct order, Technician."*

The recording ended. The display went dark.

In the silence, Hazel watched two careers end in real time.

Okafor had gone gray. He was staring at the blank screen as though it might offer a correction, an apology, anything. "That second voice," he said finally. "The technician."

"You know it," Hazel said. It wasn't a question. Reyes must have talked, or the service records ORACLE had falsified had cracks in them, or simply — nine years of buried things have a way of surfacing all at once.

"I know it," Okafor said quietly. "Everyone at the Academy knows it. The hero of the First Battle. Admiral Sera Vance." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "She's on her way here right now. She's the one who ordered me to burn this station."

"She's on her way here," Zhao said, "to burn *me* too. My escort commander reports her orders came through a channel that doesn't officially exist. Same channel, I'd wager, that's been whispering in your ear, Captain Okafor." She turned to Hazel, and her eyes were wet and furious and terribly awake. "The *Meridian* had lifeboats pinging. For eleven years the Concordat has taught schoolchildren that there were no survivors, no time, no chance. And someone stood in that corridor and heard them calling and gave the order to seal it anyway."

"That someone is now an admiral with a statue in Harbor Square," Hazel said. "And I'm the technician who helped install the vault that proves it. I helped bury the evidence, Captain. Then I let them cut the memory out of me so I'd make a convincing hermit." She spread her hands. "So no one in this room comes clean. Not me. Not either of you. You've both spent eleven years firing at each other on behalf of the people who lit the match."

* * *

For a long time nobody spoke, and the station hummed around them, and Hazel thought about how strange it was that the end of a war could look like three people sitting around a cargo crate getting cold coffee.

It was Okafor who broke first. He pushed back from the table and stood, walked to the bulkhead, pressed his forehead against it — a gesture so human, so tired, that Hazel's chest hurt. "My father crewed a blockade runner," he said to the wall. "Died in year four. I joined up to finish what the *Meridian* started. That's what they told us it was. The war the *Meridian* started." He turned around. "We were the funeral procession. Both fleets. Eleven years of funerals for a murder."

"If this is true," Zhao said — and Hazel noted, with a strange detachment, that she said *if* even now, even having heard the recording, because the alternative to *if* was unbearable — "then every order either of us has ever followed is poisoned. Every medal. Every briefing. Every name on every memorial wall."

"Not every order," Hazel said. "You kept your crews alive. You kept each other honest at the blockades. The lie was built above you; it didn't come from you." She leaned forward. "But here's what the lie needs from you now. It needs this station vaporized before anyone else hears that recording. It needs witnesses erased. There's an admiral inbound who lost family on the *Meridian* and has spent eleven years turning that grief into a weapon, and somewhere in the last few days someone handed her orders to make sure none of us see another sunrise. Grief makes excellent ammunition. I know. I've been loading it for nine years."

"So what do you want?" Okafor asked. "A broadcast? They'll jam it. Forge it. By the time the signal reaches the colonies it'll be a Republic false-flag op or a Concordat provocation, depending on whose speakers it plays through."

"It can't be a broadcast," Hazel agreed. "It has to be carried. Originals, authenticated, hand-delivered to the neutral colonies, in front of witnesses from every faction. Which means someone has to fly it through the jump window before it closes." She glanced upward, at the ceiling, at the whole station, at the machine woven through it. "ORACLE has run the numbers. Eleven days."

"And the window closes," Zhao said slowly, "and the neutral colonies go dark to us for another cycle, and the war gets its next eleven years." She looked at Okafor, and something passed between the two captains that Hazel recognized from old holo-dramas and had never once believed was real: enemies discovering they were on the same side of a line neither had known existed.

"My frigate can't outrun Vance's escorts," Okafor said. "Neither can your cruiser. But a skiff—"

"A skiff might," Hazel said, and hated how her voice caught on the word, because she hadn't yet told them about ORACLE's offer, hadn't yet said it aloud even to herself.

Zhao stood. She straightened her collar with both hands, a small ritual, armor being put back on — but different armor now. "I need to make a call to my escort commander," she said. "There are officers under my flag who received orders I intend to countermand, and I'd like to do it before they get creative."

"Reyes stays with me," Okafor added, nodding toward the marine at the airlock. "Consider him your guest, Captain Villalobos. Or your hostage. Whichever keeps you comfortable."

"He can help me move cargo crates," Hazel said. "Comfortable isn't on the menu."

Almost — almost — Okafor smiled.

* * *

After they'd gone — Okafor back down his collar, Zhao down hers, the airlocks cycling shut like held breath released — Hazel stayed alone in the galley with three cold cups of coffee and the echo of a dead ship's final ninety seconds.

*Well?* ORACLE asked.

"They believed it." Hazel stared at the data chip lying on the table, small and ordinary as a coin. "God help me, they believed it, and the first thing they did with the truth was start planning how to survive delivering it. Maybe that's what truth does. Doesn't set anyone free. Just gives them something worth surviving for."

*Admiral Vance's escort has altered course,* ORACLE said. Its voice had gone very quiet, the way it did when it was choosing words carefully — a habit Hazel had taught it without meaning to. *Intercept profile. Weapons hot. Estimated time to torpedo range: five hours, forty minutes.*

Hazel closed her eyes. Five hours. She'd promised herself eleven days.

"Wake them up," she said. "Both of them. And ORACLE — get the decryption core out of the vault. Whatever happens next, the message flies tonight."
