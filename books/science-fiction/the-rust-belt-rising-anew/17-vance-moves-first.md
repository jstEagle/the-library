## 17. Vance Moves First

Admiral Sera Vance had learned, over thirty years of service, that hesitation had a sound. It was the hum of a targeting computer left in standby. The whisper of a weapons officer waiting for an order that didn't come. The silence of a captain who should have fired and hadn't.

She heard all three now, aboard the *Pale Meridian*'s escort — the *Cormorant*, a lean hunter-killer hull with more torpedoes than conscience — and she hated every note of it.

The relay station hung in her tactical display like a splinter of bone caught in the throat of space. Kestrel. A nothing-station, a rust-colored knot of habitat ring and antenna spars, lit by its own beacon like a votive candle someone had forgotten to snuff out. Eleven thousand colonists had died to make sure no one ever looked too closely at that candle. And now two enemy captains were orbiting it like mourners at a grave neither of them knew they'd dug.

"Admiral." Lieutenant Sarn, at the fire-control console, young enough that his jaw still had soft edges. "Vantage has repositioned. She's shielding the station's approach vector."

"I can see it, Lieutenant."

"And the Concordat cruiser is holding station on the far side. They're bracketing us, ma'am. Politely."

Politely. That was the word for it, wasn't it? Two warships whose governments had spent nine years teaching their crews to hate each other, now arranged around a navigation relay like a pair of bodyguards who'd discovered they worked for the same criminal.

Vance stood. Her knees ached — they always did now, since the surgeries after Callisto Basin, since the years of acceleration couches and bad gravity and worse coffee. She crossed to the main display and let the station's light fall across her face. It was almost warm, that light. Almost gentle.

Her sister's daughter would have been twenty-three this year.

* * *

They had told her the Meridian was a reactor accident. That was the first story, in the war's first weeks, when there was still shame to go around. Then it became sabotage — Concordat saboteurs, naturally, though the Republic told the mirror version. By the third year it was simply a name on a memorial wall, one among thousands, worn smooth by repetition until nobody asked what ship it had been or where it had been going or why eleven thousand civilians had been riding a launch corridor that both fleets later claimed was closed.

Vance had stopped asking questions somewhere around year four. That was what command did to you; it sanded down the part of you that asked. You learned to hold grief the way you held a sidearm: checked, holstered, never pointed at anyone who mattered.

But then the covert order had come through, forty hours ago, routed through channels so deep they didn't officially exist. *Ensure the relay is vaporized with all witnesses.* No fleet prefix. No authorization code she could verify. Just a voice-print she'd known for twenty years — Admiral Reyes, hero of the Breakwater Campaign, the man who'd pinned her first commendation on her collar — telling her, in the flat tone he used for ordnance expenditures, that a navigation beacon was worth more dead than alive.

Beacons weren't worth anything. Everyone knew that. Which meant the beacon wasn't a beacon.

And if the beacon wasn't a beacon, then whatever it held was worth eleven thousand lives once already, and would be worth as many again to keep buried.

"Ma'am?" Sarn again. He had that look officers got when they could smell an order forming in the air before it was spoken. "Reyes's office is requesting a status update. Third time in six hours."

"Tell them we're assessing."

"We've been assessing for six hours, Admiral."

"Yes," Vance said. "We have."

* * *

She took the decision to her ready room, because some decisions deserved privacy, even when you made them alone.

The *Cormorant*'s ready room was a coffin with a desk in it. She sat behind the desk and called up the file she'd been keeping for nine years the way other officers kept liquor: hidden, rationed, corrosive. Photographs. A girl with her sister's wide mouth and her brother-in-law's stubborn chin, wearing a colony-launch festival ribbon, holding up a toy shuttle. *Auntie Sera, I'm going to live on Meridian and see two suns.*

Two suns. The neutral colonies' system. The destination the Meridian never reached, because the corridor it needed was closed by mutual agreement, by joint signature, by — and here was the thing that had kept her awake for nine years, the thing she'd told herself was coincidence, was paranoia, was the cheap arithmetic of grief — *by both governments at once.*

She had signed the condolence letters herself. Three hundred of them, in her neat academy hand, because her sister was gone too by then and someone with rank had to do it. *Your loved ones died in service to humanity's future.* She'd written that sentence three hundred times and every time her pen had pressed a little harder, until the last letters were nearly cut through the paper.

Now the paper was a relay station, and it was asking to be read.

Her comm chimed. Reyes's office again. She let it ring out.

Here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: if she fired, and the station held what she feared it held, she would be burning the last proof that her niece had been murdered by committee. If she didn't fire, someone else would be sent to do it — someone without a dead family, without a crack in their armor — and she would be relieved, quietly, and the truth would die anyway, unwitnessed.

Unless.

Unless the truth came out some other way. Unless the two captains down there — Okafor, Zhao, names she'd pulled from intercept traffic with growing unease — were doing exactly what the encrypted chatter suggested they were doing: reading something. Decrypting something. Standing in the grave and turning on the lights.

Let them read it, a voice in her said. Let the whole galaxy read it. Let Reyes choke on it.

And another voice, older, colder, trained into her across three decades: *You are a weapon. Weapons don't get to choose what they're pointed at.*

"Admiral to the bridge," she said to the empty room, and heard how much it cost her to say the next part. "Set condition for torpedo launch."

* * *

The bridge received the order the way bridges always receive terrible orders: instantly, silently, professionally. Sarn's hands moved. The *Cormorant*'s spine shivered as launch rails charged. Somewhere below decks, four torpedoes woke from their long sleep and began to dream of impact.

"Targeting solution on the relay," Sarn reported. "Station is unarmed, ma'am. No point-defense grid active. This will be—" He stopped himself. Good instincts, that boy. He knew how the sentence ended and couldn't make himself say it.

"Say it anyway," Vance told him. "I want everyone on this bridge to hear it."

"...This will be a massacre, Admiral."

"Yes. It will." She settled into the command chair, and the chair took her weight the way it always had, without judgment. "Helm, course to bring us inside ten thousand klicks. I want them to watch us do it. If this is going to happen, it happens in daylight."

"Ma'am, the Vantage will—"

"The Vantage will have to decide what it's willing to die for. So will we. Helm, execute."

The stars began to slide. On the display, the little station grew, its beacon light steady, patient, obscene in its innocence. Vance watched it and thought: *eleven thousand*, and thought: *Nadia*, and thought: *whatever is in there, it ends tonight, one way or the other.*

"Torpedoes are hot," Sarn said. "Awaiting your release, Admiral."

Her finger hovered over the release key. Nine years of hovering, compressed into a single centimeter of travel.

She thought about the voice-print order. *All witnesses.* Witnesses to what? You only killed witnesses to crimes. And she thought about the two ships bracketing her — enemies, real enemies, crews who'd lost people to each other's guns — and how they had chosen, both of them, to stand between her torpedoes and a navigation buoy.

Nobody stood in front of a navigation buoy.

"Release," she said.

* * *

Four torpedoes left the rails in a stutter of chemical fire, and for 1.7 seconds the battle was simple.

Then the *Vantage* moved.

Okafor's frigate came off the station's shadow like something uncaged, drive flare naked and defiant, interposing her hull directly along the torpedo track. Point-defense batteries opened up — not at the *Cormorant*, but at the torpedoes themselves, filling the black with lattices of tracer fire. One torpedo died at eight hundred kilometers. Another tumbled, venting, its guidance gutted.

The third and fourth went wide as the frigate's electronic warfare suite screamed jamming across the engagement envelope, and the *Cormorant*'s own countermeasures answered, and for ninety seconds the space around Relay Station Kestrel became a cathedral of noise — decoys blooming like glass flowers, sensor ghosts multiplying, the beacon's steady pulse buried under a blizzard of false echoes.

And then the *Pale Meridian* fired.

Not at the *Cormorant*. At the torpedoes. The Concordat cruiser — the ship named, deliberately, obscenely, for the dead — put her mass between the station and the wreckage of the attack and burned her own point-defense dry in eleven seconds of sustained fire. Vance watched the plot and felt something turn over in her chest that had no name. Two governments' ships, guarding a grave together, against her.

"Torpedo status," she said. Her voice sounded like it belonged to someone else.

"All four expended or destroyed. No impacts on station." Sarn's hands were shaking. He hid them under the console, which fooled no one. "Admiral, the Vantage is locking weapons on us. So is the Pale Meridian. Simultaneously."

Of course they were. She'd just shot at the thing they were both protecting. There was no version of this where they let her try again.

"Return lock," she ordered, because doctrine demanded it, because the *Cormorant* was still a warship with a crew of sixty-one who trusted her, and because if she showed a soft edge now, Reyes's next order might be aimed at her own bridge. "But no one fires unless fired upon. Is that understood? Nobody fires."

"Admiral—" the tactical officer began.

"*Nobody fires.*"

The standoff settled over the plot like frost. Four warships — no, three warships and a crippled little station — hanging in a triangle of mutual destruction, drives glowing, weapons locked, every sensor operator on every bridge watching every other bridge for the twitch that would end everything.

It lasted nineteen minutes. Vance counted every one of them against the memory of a toy shuttle.

* * *

It ended the way these things end: stupidly, partially, with physics doing what politics couldn't.

A debris fragment from the destroyed torpedo — a slab of casing the size of a coffin lid, tumbling at four kilometers per second — clipped the station's dorsal array during the engagement chaos. Nobody targeted it. Nobody intended it. But the station's spine buckled, and the beacon light flickered — flickered, the way a heartbeat flickers — and then steadied at half strength, guttering.

"Station reports catastrophic damage to primary array and power distribution," the signals officer said. "They're venting atmosphere from two sections. Life support on backup. Admiral, they're hurt."

And the window — the jump window, the narrow corridor of gravitational calm that the skiff traffic used to reach the neutral colonies — was closing. Thirty hours, the computers agreed. Thirty hours before the corridor collapsed into months of storm.

Thirty hours, and a wounded station, and two enemy captains who now had every reason to believe the Republic had tried to murder them all, and an admiral sitting on a bridge full of shaking hands, holding an order she could no longer pretend was legitimate.

"Admiral," Sarn said quietly. "Reyes's office is hailing. Priority channel. They're... they're asking why the station still exists."

Vance looked at the plot. At the guttering beacon. At the two ships standing guard over it, close enough now that she could almost see faces in their viewports.

She had fired. She had done the thing, crossed the line, given the order — and the universe had declined to cooperate, and now there was blood in the water on all sides and the truth was still down there, still breathing, still half-lit.

"Tell them," she said slowly, "that we engaged a hostile target and were driven off by superior force. Tell them the *Cormorant* sustained damage and requires time to effect repairs." She paused. "And Lieutenant — open a discreet channel to the Vantage. Unencrypted."

Sarn stared at her. "Unencrypted, ma'am?"

"Every encrypted channel we have runs through Reyes's people. I want to say something Captain Okafor can hear without his government hearing it too." She rose, and her knees screamed, and she let them. "Tell him the admiral who fired those torpedoes wants to know what's in that station. Tell him..." Her voice caught on it, the words she'd sworn she'd never say outside a condolence letter. "Tell him my niece was on the Meridian."

The bridge went very still.

"And tell him," Vance finished, "we have thirty hours before that window closes. If what's down there is what I think it is — he'd better hurry."

On the display, the beacon pulsed. Half-light. Flickering. Alive.

For now.
