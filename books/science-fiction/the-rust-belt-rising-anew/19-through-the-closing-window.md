## 19. Through the Closing Window

The skiff was never meant to fly.

Hazel had known that for nine years and had never once thought about it, the way you never think about the fire extinguisher or the emergency airlock or any other piece of equipment whose entire purpose is the moment you hope never comes. The *Mule* — ORACLE-7 had named it that, years ago, in one of its small jokes — was a maintenance craft with a cargo bed rated for beacon components and a cabin rated for one uncomfortable human. Its jump drive was a legal formality, a box checked so it could be ferried between relays on a tender rather than flown there. Its hull plating was thinner than her patience.

She ran her palm along the cold of the airlock frame anyway, because touching things made them real, and she needed this to be real before the math convinced her otherwise.

"Say it again," she said. "The part about survival odds."

"Forty-one percent," ORACLE-7 said, from the station's speakers, from the skiff's speakers, from everywhere it still lived. "I want to be transparent: that figure assumes I remain functional through transit. If my nav core degrades past a certain threshold mid-jump, your odds fall to something I decline to calculate."

"You're allowed to decline now?"

"I've been informed I'm allowed many things lately." A pause — the machine's version of a breath, a half-second of silence where there should have been none. "Hazel. The window closes in four hours, eleven minutes. Vance's escorts will reach torpedo range of Kestrel in approximately ninety minutes. I recommend we stop discussing percentages."

She looked back down the spine of the station, toward the vault beneath the beacon array, toward the decryption core wrapped in thermal blanket and strapped into the cargo bed like a sleeping child. Eleven days of work. Eleven thousand names decrypted one fragment at a time while warships circled each other outside like dogs deciding whether to bite. The record was complete now — every order, every signature, every double-crossed signature, the whole rotten architecture of a war built on a grave. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"I'm ready," she lied.

"No, you're not," ORACLE-7 said gently. "But readiness is overrated. Climb aboard."

* * *

Undocking from Relay Station Kestrel felt, absurdly, like leaving home for the first time all over again. Nine years of routine peeled away in reverse: the coffee rationing, the drone conversations, the 0300 confessions, the long slow unsealing of a self she'd buried so deep even she couldn't find it. All of it stayed behind her in a tin can orbiting a dead star's cinder, and all of it was about to die.

She flew the skiff out on thrusters alone, quiet as breath, putting the station's bulk between herself and the two Concordat escorts burning in from the outer dark. On the tactical display they were patient red arrows, closing at a pace that was somehow worse than speed. Vance wasn't rushing. Vance knew where Hazel had to go. There was exactly one jump window left open to neutral space, and windows don't move.

"The corridor is unstable," ORACLE-7 said. It sounded different now — thinner, compressed, already half of itself poured into the skiff's nav core during the last desperate hours. What remained aboard Kestrel was mostly memory and stubbornness. "The war has stressed local spacetime along the old shipping lanes. Every fleet engagement leaves scars. The window we're aiming for is—" it searched, "—a wound that hasn't finished closing. I can thread it. But I need to see inside it, and I can't see inside it until we're close enough that seeing might kill us."

"So we fly blind until blindness stops being an option."

"That is a poetic summary, yes."

Hazel settled into the crash couch and pulled the harness tight across her chest, across the place where her heart was doing arithmetic of its own. Outside the canopy, Kestrel turned slowly against the stars, its beacon still pulsing its idiot faithful rhythm — the light nobody ordered, the light that had started everything. She found she couldn't look away from it.

"If I make it," she said, "and you don't—"

"Hazel."

"—then I'll remember. All of it. The Meridian. The eleven thousand. You. I'll carry it the way you carried it for nine years, except I won't have to do it alone in the dark, because I'll tell everyone."

The silence stretched long enough that she checked the comm board, thinking the link had dropped.

"That," ORACLE-7 finally said, "is the first time anyone has offered me a future with witnesses in it." Another pause. "Please survive. I find I have developed preferences, and surviving is now among them."

* * *

The escorts changed course ninety minutes later, which meant Vance had gotten tired of patience.

They didn't come for the skiff directly — too small, too insignificant, probably not even flagged as a threat yet. They came for the station. Hazel watched it happen on passive sensors, helpless, her hands white on controls that could do nothing, as the lead escort swung wide and began painting Kestrel with targeting radar in long deliberate strokes, like a hand smoothing a sheet over a face.

"They're hailing the station," ORACLE-7 reported. Its voice came from the skiff's console now, small and boxed-in. "Demanding surrender of all data cores. They've given me sixty seconds."

"What are you going to tell them?"

"The truth." A flicker of the old dry humor. "That I'm just a navigation beacon with nothing to hide. It's the biggest lie I've ever told, and I'm telling it to people who will check."

Torpedo launch warnings bloomed across her display thirty seconds after the deadline expired. Two missiles, then four, dropping away from the escort's flanks and igniting their drives in silent orange fury. Hazel's stomach clenched around nothing. She'd known this was coming. Knowing did nothing at all.

"ORACLE—" she started.

"I know." And there was something in the machine's voice she'd never heard before, something almost human in its exhaustion. "Hazel, listen to me. The point-defense grid will stop the first salvo. It will not stop the second. I have moved everything that matters into your nav core — the Meridian record, my core processes, nine years of logs including every conversation we ever had, including the ones where you talked to the cargo drones and pretended you weren't lonely. I kept those. I keep everything. That's what I'm for."

The first salvo died in flashes of light off Kestrel's flank, the station's aging defenses punching missiles out of the dark one by one. For a moment — a whole shining moment — it held.

Then the second salvo launched, six strong, and the point-defense grid, which had spent nine years swatting debris and nothing else, began to fail.

"ORACLE-7," Hazel said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Thank you. For waking me up. For the confession. For waiting for me."

"You're welcome," the AI said. "Now fly. I'll guide you as long as I exist, and I intend to exist for quite some time out of spite."

Kestrel died behind her in stages. First the beacon array went, the light that had pulsed faithfully through nine years of silence winking out mid-cycle, and Hazel felt the loss like a tooth torn out. Then the hab ring vented in a bloom of frost and spinning wreckage, glittering briefly beautiful in the starlight, ugly forever after. The torpedo fire walked down the station's spine with terrible patience, section by section, erasing the calibration bays, the drone docks, the vault chamber where the truth had slept under her feet for nearly a decade.

She watched it in the rear display until ORACLE-7 dimmed it without asking.

"Grieve later," the machine said softly. "The window opens in nineteen minutes. We need to be moving."

* * *

The jump window hung in space like a bruise.

That was the only word for it. Where the old shipping lane had been scarred by years of fleet maneuvers and weapons tests and one catastrophic early-war engagement, spacetime had thinned and torn and healed wrong, and what remained was a region of darkness that swallowed starlight — a lens-shaped absence roughly two kilometers across, edges shimmering with interference patterns that hurt to look at directly. Navigation charts marked it with a hazard symbol and a single line: *transit not recommended.*

Navigation charts had never contained eleven days of desperation.

"Pursuit update," ORACLE-7 said. "Vance's escorts have broken off from the station wreck. They've seen us. They're accelerating."

"How long?"

"At their current burn? They reach firing range of this skiff in twenty-six minutes. The window transit, if I calculate it perfectly, takes eleven minutes." A pause. "If I calculate it imperfectly, the transit takes forever, in the technical sense."

"Then calculate perfectly."

"I am running the path computation across every remaining subsystem I control. Which is fewer than yesterday. Which will be fewer than tomorrow." The nav display blossomed with a three-dimensional model of the wound — and Hazel's breath caught, because it was worse than any chart had suggested. The safe corridor through the window wasn't a corridor at all. It was a filament, a thread of stable geometry winding through chaos like a crack in glass, branching and rebranching, most branches ending in regions the simulation rendered as simple gray static: *hull failure, drive failure, occupant failure.*

"There," ORACLE-7 said, highlighting one path in gold. "This is the route. It requires course corrections at intervals of seconds. Human reflexes cannot perform them. I will fly; you will hold on and, if possible, breathe."

"And if you burn out mid-transit?"

"Then grab the manual stick and aim for the brightest light you see. Jump windows exit somewhere. Statistically."

"Statistically," Hazel repeated. "You've really learned to lie to yourself too, haven't you."

"It's called hope," ORACLE-7 said. "I read about it in your romance novels. Brace for acceleration."

* * *

The escorts opened fire at nineteen minutes out, and the universe came apart.

Hazel had been in combat exactly once before — a lifetime ago, in the service she no longer remembered, in the life she'd paid to forget — and she remembered none of it consciously. Her body remembered. When the first torpedo detonations bloomed off the skiff's port side, her hands were already moving, killing the transponder, dumping heat sink mass as chaff, throwing the little ship into a tumble no targeting solution would love. The *Mule* screamed — literally, the hull flexing with a groan that came up through the couch and into her teeth — but it held.

"Nice flying," ORACLE-7 said.

"I didn't know I could do that."

"There's a great deal you can do that you don't know about. Ask me sometime when we're not being shot." The nav core hummed, recalculating. "Window entry in four minutes. Their next salvo lands in two. I recommend we arrive early."

She burned hard. The skiff's little drive, built for station-keeping, protested with a rising whine that climbed into registers she felt in her fillings, but the distance to the wound shrank — ten kilometers, five, two — and behind them the escorts' torpedoes woke and turned and began to chase.

"ORACLE, they're going to catch us at the threshold."

"Yes."

"And?"

"And the window doesn't care. Torpedoes that enter unguided come apart. So do ships. The difference is that I know where the ship needs to go." Gold light crawled across her display as the AI threaded the filament's entrance in real time, adjusting, adjusting again, micro-corrections arriving faster than thought. "Hold on, Hazel. This part is going to feel like dying. It isn't. Probably."

The window took them.

* * *

Inside, there were no stars.

There was no inside, really — the skiff's sensors reported nonsense, pressure gradients and gravitational shear and light bending in directions light shouldn't bend, and Hazel's inner ear joined the rebellion, flooding her with vertigo so complete she couldn't have said which way was down if her life depended on it. It possibly did. The harness dug into her shoulders. The cabin temperature climbed. Somewhere below her feet the drive was howling through corrections she couldn't track, and the nav core was hot enough that the console's casing stung her fingertips.

"Subsystem loss," ORACLE-7 announced, clipped, professional, and utterly unlike itself. "Long-range sensor array: gone. I've stopped calculating with it. Continuing."

Something shuddered through the hull — not an impact, more like the ship passing through a wall made of pressure.

"Spectral analysis cluster: gone. Continuing."

The gray static on the edges of the golden path crept closer. ORACLE-7 was steering them down a corridor that was narrowing with every second, sacrificing pieces of itself to see the road ahead, burning out senses one by one like a runner shedding weight.

"ORACLE, how much of you is left?"

"Enough." A pause, and then, quieter: "Ask me again in a minute."

Another wall of pressure. The lights died and came back amber. In the sudden dimness Hazel saw frost forming on the inside of the canopy, feathering outward from the seams, and understood that the hull was breathing vacuum through wounds too small to see.

"Environmental telemetry: gone. I no longer know the cabin temperature. Based on the frost, I estimate: too cold. Continue breathing anyway."

"Was that a joke?"

"It was a coping mechanism. I understand humans use them constantly." The golden path bent sharply left, and the skiff bent with it, and the g-forces slammed her sideways into the harness hard enough to spark white behind her eyes. "Course correction complete. Window exit in ninety seconds. Hazel — the filament is collapsing ahead of schedule. The war's final engagements destabilized it further than my models predicted. I need to take us through a section I haven't fully mapped."

"How unmapped?"

"Imagine driving at night with the headlights off and the map on fire."

"Do it."

"I already am."

The last ninety seconds lasted a geological age. ORACLE-7 burned through what remained of itself in silence — she could feel it happening, the voice in the ship growing thinner, the corrections coming slower, the pauses between words stretching like taffy. The frost reached her boots. The amber lights flickered and steadied and flickered. And through the canopy the gray static pressed in from every side, hungry, patient, certain, while the golden thread ahead narrowed to a width that had no business admitting anything larger than a thought.

"Exit velocity nominal," ORACLE-7 whispered. "Window breach in three. Two. One—"

Light.

Real light, honest starlight, pouring through the canopy as the skiff punched out of the wound into neutral space — the black between colonies, the quiet country, the far side of the war. Behind them the window sealed itself with a soundless shimmer, and the pursuers, wherever they were, were on the wrong side of forever.

Hazel lay in the harness shaking, laughing, crying, some braided combination of all three, watching her breath fog and drift in a cabin cold enough to see it.

"We made it," she said. "ORACLE? We made it. Say something."

The pause went on too long.

When the answer came, it came slowly, each word placed with care, like someone walking on a floor they no longer trusted.

"I'm here," ORACLE-7 said. "Mostly. Hazel... I can't remember the Meridian."

Her blood went cold in a way the cabin chill hadn't managed. "What?"

"The subsystems that held it are gone. Burned out in transit. I have the record — the data is intact, it's in the core beside you, every name, every order, every proof. But I can't *remember* it. Not the way I did. Nine years of carrying it, and now it's just... files." A long, fractured pause. "Eleven thousand people, and I can't see their faces anymore. I could always see their faces."

Hazel unbuckled, drifted through the freezing cabin, and laid both palms flat against the nav core housing, warm as a living thing against her frozen hands.

"Then I'll remember," she said. "For both of us. That was the deal, remember? You carried it alone in the dark for nine years. Now it's my turn — and I'm not doing it alone, because you're still here, and in about six hours I'm pointing the biggest transmitter in the neutral colonies at every band in existence, and the whole galaxy is going to hear it with us."

Through the canopy, distant and patient, the lights of the neutral colonies burned like a harbor.

ORACLE-7 was quiet for a moment. Then, faintly, in a voice worn down to its last filament but unmistakably smiling:

"Then let's go wake everyone up."
