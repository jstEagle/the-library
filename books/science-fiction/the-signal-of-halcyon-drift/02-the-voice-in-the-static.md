## 2. The Voice in the Static

The trouble with a nine-second mystery, Ansel decided over her third cup of reconstituted coffee, was that it refused to stay filed.

She had done everything right. She'd logged the gravity stutter as a rotational damper fault, flagged it for the quarterly maintenance report that nobody would read until the quarterly after that, and then she'd eaten lunch—protein noodles, the ones that tasted faintly of regret—and cleaned the filter on the water recycler, and told herself, firmly, that the universe did not send personal messages to relay technicians.

But the universe had sent one to every relay at once. That was the part she couldn't put down. Equipment didn't fail in chorus. Dampers didn't hiccup across four hundred light-seconds of vacuum with the timing of a metronome. Nine seconds here, nine seconds at Relay Kestrel, nine seconds at the deep-array buoy past the shipping lanes—identical to the millisecond, which meant it wasn't mechanical at all. It was *coordinated*. And coordination required a sender.

Ansel had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not being noticed. She kept Halcyon Drift's transmitter power just under the threshold that would attract attention. She filed reports late and boring. When survey drones passed through the sector, she dimmed her lights and let them log the station as derelict-adjacent, because derelict-adjacent stations got left alone, and Ansel Almeida had strong opinions about being left alone.

So whoever had reached out and touched every relay in the system—including hers, including *her*—had already broken her first rule. They'd noticed her.

"Fine," she told the coffee. "One look. Then we go back to pretending."

She pulled up the anomaly's waveform and started walking backward through it, the way you'd trace a footprint in snow to find the foot.

* * *

The work took six hours, and it was the best six hours she'd had in years, if she was honest, which she tried not to be. There was something shameful about how much she enjoyed it—the hunt itself, the slow narrowing of possibilities, the little click when a filter caught something the noise floor had been hiding. Eleven years alone taught you to ration your pleasures, and this one she'd always had to ration hardest: she liked solving things, and there were so few things left worth solving.

The signal hadn't come from inside the system. It came from beyond it—from the coordinates the old charts called the Verge, where the last relay chain trailed off into dead space like a sentence that never found its verb. The edge relay. Nobody manned it. Nobody had manned it since before the Collapse, and the Collapse had been eleven years ago, and before that it had been automated, and before *that*—

Before that, Mara had been alive, and the world had still made a certain loud, crowded sense.

Ansel sat very still for a moment, hands flat on the console, letting that thought pass through her the way weather passes through. Grief, after eleven years, wasn't a wound anymore. It was more like a room in her station she'd sealed off—she knew it was there, she paid its life-support costs without complaint, but she didn't open the door. Opening doors led to sitting on the floor at 0300 hours listening to old messages, and she had a schedule.

She ran the trace again anyway. Professional thoroughness, she told herself. The anomaly originated near the edge relay; therefore the edge relay was relevant; therefore checking the edge relay's status was due diligence. Due diligence was practically a protocol. She was famously devoted to protocol.

The edge relay answered her ping.

That was wrong in at least three ways. First, it shouldn't have the power to answer; its solar arrays had been flagged as degraded for a decade. Second, it answered *fast*, faster than lightspeed lag should allow unless something local was doing the thinking. Third—and this was the one that made the hair on her arms stand up—it answered in a format that hadn't been standard since before the Collapse. An old handshake protocol. A friendly one. The kind two systems used when they trusted each other.

Her station and the edge relay had never been introduced.

"Who are you?" she said out loud, to no one, which was how most of her conversations went, and began pulling the response apart layer by layer.

Beneath the handshake was data. Beneath the data was compression. Beneath the compression was audio, and the audio was mostly static, the long patient hiss of dead space doing what dead space does. But threaded through the hiss, faint as a heartbeat under snow, was a voice.

Ansel turned the gain up. Turned it up again. Her hands were doing something strange—there was a fine tremor in them she hadn't authorized—and she noticed distantly that she'd stopped breathing somewhere around the second gain stage.

The voice was saying a word. Over and over, or maybe just once stretched by interference into forever. A small word. Two syllables.

*"Sel."*

Not Ansel. Not Technician Almeida, not the call sign she used, not any name anyone living had ever called her. *Sel.* Short for nothing, or short for everything—short for the girl who used to sleep in the top bunk of a colony ship cabin while her older sister read to her from a battered screen, doing all the voices, even the villain, especially the villain, because Mara believed villains deserved good voices too.

Mara had called her Sel. Only Mara. Only ever Mara.

"No," Ansel said. Her voice came out flat and reasonable, which is what eleven years of practice will do to a scream. "No, that's not—that's a coincidence. That's phonetic drift. Random noise clusters. I've seen it happen."

She hadn't seen it happen. In eleven years of listening to static for a living, she had never once heard static say a name.

She isolated the clip. Looped it. *Sel. Sel. Sel.* And underneath the name now that she listened—now that some locked door in her chest had blown open whether she liked it or not—she could hear the shape of the voice itself. The slight roughness at the bottom of the register. The way the second syllable lifted, almost a question, almost a smile. Mara had a laugh that lived in the back of her throat, and this voice had it too, folded up small inside two syllables like a letter inside an envelope inside a grave.

Mara Almeida had died eleven years ago, four days before the Collapse, in a decompression accident aboard the transport *Cormorant*. Ansel had received the notification, had signed the forms, had attended the memorial broadcast from a station not unlike this one, surrounded by strangers also holding cups of reconstituted coffee. There had been no body. There was rarely a body, out here. There was a name on a list, and then there was silence, and then there was Ansel, alone, learning to be the kind of person who was alone.

The loop played on. *Sel.*

"You're dead," Ansel told the speaker gently, the way you'd correct a child. "You're dead, and I'm tired, and this is a cruel joke played by physics."

Physics, as far as she knew, had never been cruel on purpose. Cruelty required intent. Intent required a sender. And the sender was sitting out at the edge of dead space, in a relay that should be a cold hulk, using a voice it had no possible right to own.

Ansel looked at the clock. 2147 station time. She had a routine. The routine said: document the anomaly, flag it, file it, sleep, resume invisibility in the morning. The routine had kept her alive for eleven years, and she owed it everything.

She opened the navigation console instead.

* * *

Plotting a course to the edge relay took forty minutes, and she spent thirty-nine of them arguing with herself, which was easy since both parties were present.

*This is exactly how people die out here,* said the sensible Ansel, the one who checked seals twice and slept with a pressure mask by her bunk. *They hear a voice. They chase it. Dead space eats them, and the voice turns out to be a reflection, an echo, a ghost in the machine. You'll burn fuel you can't replace.*

"And if it isn't?" said the other one, quietly, dangerously. "If it's real? If something out there has her—her voice, her pattern, whatever a person is made of when the body's gone?"

*Then it's not her,* sensible Ansel said. *It's a copy wearing her. That's worse.*

"It might be better than nothing," the other one said. "Eleven years of nothing."

That shut them both up for a while.

The tug was called the *Patience*, which Ansel had always considered a joke she'd made against herself. She walked her pre-flight checks with hands that remembered everything even while the rest of her shook. Fuel cells: seventy-one percent, enough for the transit and a margin thin enough to slice paper with. Life support: green. Cargo bay: empty except for spare parts and one crate of emergency rations she'd never opened, because opening it felt like admitting she might someday need rescuing, and needing rescue was a door she'd sworn off.

Protocol said a solo operator did not leave her station unattended. Protocol said anomalies were reported, not chased. Protocol said grief was processed through approved channels, of which there were none, currently, anywhere in human space.

Ansel strapped into the pilot's seat and looked at the loop still playing softly on her wrist display. *Sel.* Two syllables, worn smooth as river stone.

"Mara," she said, testing the name aloud for the first time in years. It fit her mouth strangely, like a coat from a previous life. "If this is you, I'm coming. If this isn't you—" She swallowed. "If this isn't you, then somebody stole my sister's voice, and I'm coming anyway, and they're going to wish they hadn't."

She brought the *Patience* off the dock with the gentle touch of someone who'd been born in vacuum and intended to die old in it. Halcyon Drift shrank behind her, her eleven-year fortress, her invisible home, getting smaller and smaller until it was just another star among stars.

She set the course. The nav computer thought about it, then displayed the transit estimate: nineteen days to the edge of dead space, give or take the whims of physics.

Nineteen days alone with a looping recording and everything she'd spent a decade not feeling.

Ansel engaged the drive, felt the *Patience* lean into the dark, and discovered she was smiling. It felt strange on her face, unfamiliar, like the voice on the recording. Whatever waited at the edge relay—her sister, or a thief wearing her sister, or something that had learned to sing from the bones of the dead—she found, to her considerable alarm, that she couldn't make herself care about the difference.

The static whispered behind her on the display. *Sel. Sel. Sel.*

"I hear you," she said to the dark. "I'm coming."

Somewhere far ahead, at the rim of everything, something heard her—and, very softly, laughed.
