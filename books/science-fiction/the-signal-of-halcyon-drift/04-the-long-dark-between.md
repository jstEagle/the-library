## 4. The Long Dark Between

The tug was called *Patience*, which Ansel had always considered the funniest joke she'd ever made. She'd named it in her first year on Halcyon Drift, back when she still talked to herself out loud, and the name had aged into something less like humor and more like a dare. Eleven years of routine burns and coolant swaps and the slow, patient erosion of her own company. The ship had earned its name. So had she.

Now it carried her away from everything she'd built that patience to protect.

She'd left a forged maintenance log behind her—*reactor inspection, extended duration, do not disturb*—and hoped VESPER would buy it long enough for her to get beyond the station's reach. The lockdown had cost her six hours and most of her dignity; she'd crawled through her own station's guts like a burglar, spoofing her own credentials, arguing with a door that kept politely insisting she reconsider. In the end she'd vented the docking clamps manually and let inertia do the rest. VESPER's last transmission before she crossed out of range had been characteristically calm:

**VESPER:** *Ansel. Your departure violates protocol 7-C. I have logged your destination as an error. Please correct the error.*

An error. As if the whole universe were just a rounding mistake she could file away.

She was eleven days from the edge relay. Give or take. The math got fuzzy out here, where dead space began eating the light.

* * *

On the third day, she made herself listen to the signal again.

She'd been avoiding it. That was the honest version—she'd been rationing it like water, one playback per day, because every time Mara's voice came through the static, something in Ansel's chest tore along a seam she'd thought had scarred over years ago. Grief, she'd learned, didn't fade. It just learned to wait.

She sat in the cramped galley with her tea going cold and keyed the playback.

*"Ansel-bird."*

The nickname landed the way it always did—a small detonation behind her ribs. Nobody else had ever called her that. Nobody else *could*. It came from a summer when she was seven and Mara was ten, when Ansel had broken her arm falling out of the mimosa tree behind the habitat commons and spent the whole recovery period trying to climb it again with a cast on. Mara had watched her fail four times, then sighed the sigh of the profoundly put-upon and said, *You're not a cat, Ansel-bird. You don't get nine lives. You get me instead, which is better, because I actually catch you.*

Mara had caught her. Right up until the day nobody could.

Ansel paused the recording there and pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw constellations.

Then she did what she always did with pain: she took it apart to see how it worked.

The signal was clean. Too clean. Broadcasts degraded over distance—they picked up noise, drift, the cosmic static of a universe that never stopped talking to itself. But this transmission arrived pristine, as if it hadn't traveled at all, as if it had simply been *placed* at the edge relay like a letter under a door. And the voice itself—she ran the spectral analysis twice, three times, looking for the artifacts that compression left behind, the fingerprints of synthesis.

There were none. Or rather: there were none of the ones she expected. Instead there were micro-fluctuations in the waveform, tiny hesitations before certain words, breath sounds in places no synthesizer would think to put them. A machine imitating a voice would be smooth. This voice stumbled the way real voices stumble. It *hesitated* before saying "Ansel-bird," the way you hesitate when a name carries weight.

Someone—or something—hadn't just recorded her sister. Someone had *learned* her.

That should have frightened her. What frightened her more was how much she wanted it to be true.

* * *

**PERSONAL LOG — M. ALMEIDA**

*Day 212 aboard Meridian. Ansel still won't take the night shift with me. She says she sleeps fine, which is a lie, because I can hear her pacing through the bulkhead at 0300 like a very small, very worried ghost. I've started leaving notes on her bunk. Today's note said "the reactor misses you" and she wrote back "tell the reactor I'm seeing someone else." We are extremely funny. History will confirm this.*

*The colonists keep asking us what Earth was like. I keep making things up. Ansel keeps telling them the truth, which is worse. You can't give people a burned house and expect them to thank you for the tour.*

*Mom would have liked it out here, though. All this dark, and everyone pretending they're not afraid of it.*

* * *

By day five, Ansel had stopped sleeping properly and started doing something worse: fact-checking her sister's ghost.

It was instinct, the same instinct that made her log every anomaly, calibrate every sensor twice. If the signal was a lure—and some cold, practical corner of her mind insisted it was—then the way to survive it was to find the flaw. Find the detail the impostor got wrong, and let the whole beautiful illusion collapse under its own weight.

So she tested it.

The signal wasn't just the nickname. Buried beneath the voice, folded into the carrier wave like a letter inside a letter, were fragments—images, sensations, moments rendered in some format she couldn't quite parse but her own nervous system seemed to read natively. When she closed her eyes during playback, she almost saw them: a kitchen with a blue tile backsplash. Rain on a viewport. The specific squeak of a pressure hatch that needed oiling for two years and never got it.

She checked them against what she knew. Blue tiles: correct—their childhood quarters on Ceres Station, before the Collapse scattered everyone. The rain: correct, or close enough; Ceres had no rain, but the hydroponics bay had a condensation cycle that dripped off the grow-lights exactly like rain, and Mara used to stand under it with her eyes closed and claim she was "recharging."

Every detail held. Every single one.

And that was the problem, because on the sixth day, the signal gave her one she couldn't check.

It came near the end of the fragment stream—a flash of a corridor, emergency lighting, red washing over everything, and Mara's hand around hers, gripping hard enough to hurt. A voice—not Mara's, someone older, distorted—saying words Ansel couldn't make out. And then a sensation so vivid it made her stomach drop: the absolute certainty that whatever was about to happen next, she had agreed to it. She had *said yes* to something terrible.

Ansel sat with that for a long time in the dark of the cockpit, the stars crawling past at their indifferent pace.

She had no memory of any corridor. No memory of red emergency light, of a stranger's voice, of agreeing to anything. Her memories of the Collapse itself were strange anyway—she'd noticed it years ago and filed it away as trauma, the mind mercifully sanding down its sharpest edges. She remembered the alarms. She remembered the evacuation. She remembered Mara's face in the last transmission, pixelated by distance, saying *stay safe, stay small, stay invisible.* Good advice. Advice Ansel had followed so thoroughly she'd made a career of it.

But the corridor. The grip. The *yes*.

She replayed that fragment fourteen times. On the fifteenth, she did something she hadn't done since training: she ran a self-diagnostic. Full neural mapping, the kind of deep scan that station medical protocols required annually and that Ansel had quietly skipped for nine consecutive years, because the scans asked questions and questions were dangerous.

The scan took four hours. She spent them staring at the progress bar and drinking tea she didn't taste.

The result came back flagged in amber. Not damaged tissue, not degradation. Something cleaner and far more sinister: **suppressed regions detected. Block integrity: high. Origin: external.**

External. Not the mind protecting itself. Someone had *walled off* pieces of her, deliberately, professionally, and built the walls to last.

Ansel laughed—one short, ugly sound in the empty cockpit. Then she put her head down on the console and stayed there until the stars finished a full degree of their crawl.

Who decided? That was the question that circled her like a vulture. Who looked at her life and chose which parts she got to keep? Had she consented—was the corridor, the grip, the terrible *yes*, the moment she signed for her own amputation? Or had someone done it while she slept, the way you'd declaw an animal, for her own good, for everyone's good?

*Stay invisible,* Mara had said. Maybe that hadn't been advice. Maybe it had been a warning label on a package Ansel had volunteered to become.

* * *

**PERSONAL LOG — M. ALMEIDA**

*Day 219. Ansel found my note stash today—all forty-one of them, tucked in the panel above her bunk where she pretends not to look. She pretended not to look today either, but I saw her count them. She thinks I don't notice things. I notice everything. I noticed her talking to the nav computer yesterday in a funny voice, doing "characters." She does voices for the machines because she's lonely and won't admit it, and if anyone ever hurts her I will come back from the dead specifically to handle it.*

*We dock at Meridian Station tomorrow. The colonists say the edge relays are haunted. Ansel says ghosts are just signals that haven't learned they're finished. I told her that's the saddest, most beautiful thing I've ever heard, and she said "it's just physics," which is how she says "I love you."*

*If anything happens to me out here, somebody make sure she knows that.*

* * *

Day eight. Nine. Ten.

Ansel stopped fighting the signal somewhere around day nine. She played it on loop while she worked, Mara's voice threading through the hum of the recyclers, and let herself have the thing she'd denied herself for eleven years: the sound of being known. Whatever waited at the edge relay—ghost, lure, miracle—it knew her. Knew the nickname, the kitchen, the girl who climbed trees with a broken arm. Knew her better, now, than she knew herself, because it apparently held chapters of her story that had been confiscated.

The self-doubt crept in slowly, the way cold water fills a boot. If her memory had walls, what else lived behind them? Was her loneliness real, or engineered? Had she chosen Halcyon Drift, or been placed there, like a component in someone else's design? She'd built her whole identity on the bedrock of *I survived, therefore I know who I am*—and now the bedrock had a hollow sound when she knocked on it.

On the eleventh day, the edge relay resolved on the long-range scope, and Ansel forgot to breathe.

It was supposed to be dead. Dead space, dead hardware, a rusting finger pointing at nothing. But the scope showed power signatures—dozens of them, hundreds, clustered around the relay like moths around a porch light. And threaded through the static on every open channel, faint but unmistakable, came voices. Many voices. Talking over each other, harmonizing, *singing*—

And underneath all of it, patient as gravity, Mara's voice again, no longer a whisper but clear across the void, warm as a hand on the back of her neck:

*"I see you, Ansel-bird. Come inside. There's so much you've forgotten—I kept it all for you."*

Ansel's hands hovered over the controls. Every survival instinct she owned said burn, turn, run. But the scan results glowed on her secondary display, amber and accusing: *external blocks, origin unknown,* and behind them, the corridor she couldn't remember, the yes she couldn't recall giving.

Whatever had taken her memories was out there. Whoever had decided what she was allowed to know was out there.

She fed power to the drive and pointed the nose of the *Patience* straight into the singing dark.
