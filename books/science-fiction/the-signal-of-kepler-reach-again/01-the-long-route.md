## 1. The Long Route

The corridor between Kepler Prime and Halcyon Deep was eleven days of nothing, and Jude Amberdale had learned to love nothing the way other people loved a lover or a god.

She sat in the Meridian's cockpit with her boots on the console, watching the stars hold still. The freighter was old enough that its engines hummed in a key just below hearing, a note she felt in her teeth rather than heard. Forty years ago the corridor had been thick with traffic — ore haulers, passenger liners, diplomatic couriers running the eleven-day hop between the two human colonies like blood through a single body. Then came the schism. The Assemblies on both worlds had voted within a season of each other to sever contact, and the channel went silent, and the ships stopped, and the two halves of humanity turned their backs across four light-minutes of vacuum.

Now there was one ship.

Hers.

Not officially, of course. Officially the Meridian was a decommissioned survey vessel sold for scrap to a salvage consortium that existed only on paper, registered out of a port authority office whose records had been quietly corrupted by a customs officer with kind eyes and a talent for forgery. Officially she didn't exist either — no transit logs, no crew manifest, no flight plan filed with either colony's traffic control. She was a ghost running a ghost route, and every eleven days she crossed the silence carrying the only cargo that still mattered.

Letters.

* * *

They were stored in the old water reclamation tanks, sealed in vacuum bags by the hundred: handwritten pages, printed photographs, children's drawings done in crayon that had been scanned and reprinted because the originals were too precious to risk. A grandmother on Kepler Prime writing to a granddaughter born on Halcyon Deep after the severance, describing snowfall on mountains the girl would never see. A man who'd been nineteen when the channel closed, now fifty-one, writing his first letter to the brother he'd left behind, because it had taken him thirty years to find words for goodbye. A widow who wrote the same letter every month — *I am alive, I am well, I remember you* — and asked only that it be delivered, never answered, because the answer would take another eleven days and another eleven days and she couldn't bear to hope twice.

Jude read them sometimes, on the long watches. She told herself it was quality control. It wasn't. It was the closest thing she had to company.

The schism hadn't been about war, not exactly. It had been about memory — about which version of the founding would be taught in schools, which names carved on which monuments, which crimes acknowledged and which buried under new paint. Two colonies grown from the same seed, each deciding the other had forgotten something essential. The Assemblies cut the channel rather than argue. Easier to stop talking than to be wrong.

But people kept writing letters. People always did. And someone had to carry them.

* * *

On the fourth day, drifting through the dead zone where neither colony's relays reached, Jude pulled up the file.

She didn't look at it often. Once a year, maybe. But the corridor was long and the nothing was loud tonight, and she found herself opening the folder she'd inherited along with everything else — the face, the debts, the name.

AMBERDALE, JUDE. Case 4471-C. Homicide, unsolved.

Her original's murder file.

That was the thing about being a clone that nobody warned you: you didn't inherit memories, but you inherited everything the memories had left behind. The original Jude Amberdale — Jude-Prime, the registries called her, though she'd hated that — had been an investigator with the Kepler Reach Joint Authority, back when "joint" still meant something. She'd worked the corridor in its last years, before the schism, before the silence. And she'd died badly, eight years ago, in a rented room on Halcyon Deep with her case files spread across the bed and a killer who had never been found.

The clone contract had been simple, the way ugly things are simple. Jude-Prime had no family. She'd purchased a clone license years earlier — standard precaution for investigators with enemies — and when she died, the vats grew a woman from her cells, and the estate transferred, and the debts transferred, and the case transferred.

Case 4471-C. Homicide of Tomas Vey.

Jude had read the file so many times she could recite it. Vey, forty-four, archivist, found dead in his quarters on Halcyon Deep forty years ago — not eight, forty; the murder predated even her original, who'd picked up the cold case as a personal obsession late in her career. Blunt trauma, no weapon recovered, no witnesses. Vey had worked in the deep archives during the war scare years, the tense decade when both colonies had quietly built evacuation infrastructure nobody talked about. He'd known things. His death had been clean, professional, and completely forgotten by everyone except a young investigator named Jude Amberdale, who'd spent her last eight years pulling at a thread everyone else had agreed to leave alone.

Then she'd died, and the thread had passed to Jude-the-second like a debt collector's letter.

The clone woke with no memories of any of it. Just the name, the face, the apartment full of another woman's belongings, and a case file flagged with a standing instruction in Jude-Prime's own hand: *Do not let this close.*

So she hadn't. She'd learned the route instead — learned it from her original's flight logs, because Jude-Prime had run contraband mail too, in her final years, using the investigation as cover. The letters had been her original's way of keeping the corridor alive while she hunted. They'd become Jude's way of staying alive while she waited for the hunt to mean something.

Eight years. No closer.

* * *

On the seventh day, the comm crackled.

It shouldn't have. The dead zone was dead — that was its whole nature. But the Meridian's ancient array caught a fragment, a whisper of signal bouncing off some piece of debris older than the schism, and for three seconds Jude heard music. Actual music. An orchestra, strings, something old and Earth-made that both colonies still claimed as heritage and neither would admit it.

Then it was gone, and the silence came back like water closing over a stone.

She sat with her hands off the controls for a long moment, heart going hard. Eleven days of nothing, and the nothing had spoken.

It wasn't a signal. She knew that. Probably debris, probably echo, probably her own loneliness composing ghosts out of static. But her hands were shaking slightly as she logged it anyway — because her original had logged things like this too, Jude knew from the journals. Signals in dead zones. Patterns in noise. Jude-Prime had believed the corridor remembered what the colonies wanted forgotten, and everyone had thought it was grief talking, the grief of a woman whose own sister had chosen Halcyon in the severance and been lost to her forever.

Maybe it was grief talking. Grief and loneliness sharpened into purpose — that was the whole architecture of Jude's life, hers and her original's, load-bearing walls she'd built her self upon.

She ran the route because no one else would. That was the true answer, the whole answer. Not heroism. Not justice. Just: the letters existed, and the families existed, and the silence between the worlds was a wound somebody had to keep crossing until it healed or killed her.

Four more days to Halcyon Deep.

* * *

Halcyon port control hailed her at the edge of their sensor shadow, exactly on schedule, exactly as they always did — a customs officer with a bored voice reading a script about unregistered traffic, while beneath the script ran the older language, the real one: approach vector confirmed, berth nine open, water tanks will require inspection, welcome home.

"Meridian, you are cleared for approach," the voice said, and then, softer, almost lost in the static: "She'll want to know you made it."

*Ilya.* Jude allowed herself half a smile as she brought the freighter around, the gas giant Halcyon orbiting filling her viewport, banded amber and cream, beautiful as always in the way that dangerous things are beautiful.

Berth nine. The water tanks. The letters. Ilya Marsh waiting at the airlock with his forged manifests and his careful hands and the thing he'd never said in eight years of saying everything else.

And somewhere beyond all of it, patient as ice, Case 4471-C. Tomas Vey, dead forty years. A thread her original had died pulling.

She docked at 0400 station time. The tanks came off without incident. Ilya met her at the airlock looking tired and glad and afraid, in that order, and handed her a data chip with the routine paperwork.

"Port manifest updates," he said. "New arrivals, cargo registrations. Nothing interesting."

Nothing ever was.

She plugged the chip into her wrist terminal in the lift down from the docking ring, scrolling idly through cargo declarations while the station hummed around her, and that was when she saw it — a passenger manifest, forty years old, flagged for archival review, stamped with an authorization code she recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting.

Because it *was* her own handwriting. Her original's code.

And beside it, listed as a live passenger aboard a ship that had burned in its cradle before it ever flew:

VEY, TOMAS. Status: ACTIVE.
