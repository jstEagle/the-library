## 4. Letters Across the Silence

The letters came to Jude in bundles wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the kind of knots sailors used when they wanted a package to survive drowning.

She kept them in a false panel beneath the Meridian's nav console, behind the coolant lines where a customs sweep would find nothing but heat. Forty-one letters this run. She knew the number without counting because she always counted — a habit inherited from her original along with the murder file and the debts, though she suspected the habit was older than either of them. Some things you didn't inherit so much as arrive already carrying.

She read them on the long hauls, when the corridor between Kepler Prime and Halcyon Deep went quiet enough that her own heartbeat sounded like company. A grandmother writing to a granddaughter born after the schism, describing rain because the girl had never seen it. A man who signed his name and then crossed it out and wrote *forget me* and then wrote his name again underneath. A child's drawing folded eight times, all crayon and gravity, two worlds hanging from a string.

Jude never answered them. That wasn't the job. The job was motion: take the words from one silence, carry them across another, deliver them into a third. She was not a messenger so much as a gap that happened to have an engine.

But tonight, four hours out of Halcyon Deep with the port's traffic control already pinging her transponder, she caught herself reading one letter twice. It was from a woman named Ada Vey-Roshan. The surname snagged in Jude like a fishhook.

*Vey.*

Tomas Vey had been dead for forty years. Dead before Jude's original ever opened the file, dead in a way the coroner's report called accidental and the missing page of that report suggested was nothing of the kind. And now there was a woman on Halcyon Deep signing herself with his name, writing to a sister on Kepler Prime about a nephew who "asks when Uncle Tomas is coming home, and I no longer know what to tell him, because I no longer know what I believe."

Jude sat with that sentence for a long time while the Meridian fell toward the ice.

She should have felt dread. What she felt instead was something worse: the sense that the case had started reading *her*, turning pages she hadn't authorized, at its own pace, in its own order. First the manifest with Tomas Vey listed as a living passenger on a ship that never flew. Now a letter that moved like it knew things.

She sealed the bundle back into the panel and put her hands on the stick.

"Meridian," said Halcyon traffic control, in the flat voice of a system that had flagged her before, "you are cleared for berth nine. Customs inspection on arrival. Have a pleasant descent."

*Customs inspection on arrival.* They didn't usually announce those.

Jude began, quietly, to rehearse being boring.

* * *

Ilya Marsh watched the Meridian come down through the port glass and hated how much he loved watching it.

That was the problem with loving against regulation: regulation got there first. Every feeling he had arrived pre-logged, pre-classified, stamped with a violation code. Fraternization with an unlicensed carrier, section eleven. Failure to report contraband correspondence, section fourteen. He'd stopped reading the sections years ago. He just knew the shape of them the way you know the shape of a cell you've lived in long enough.

He ran the manifest queue anyway, because duty was also a thing he loved, and the two loves had been strangling each other in his chest for six years without either winning.

Her queries were still sitting in the archive log where he'd found them that morning. Three pulls from the sealed historical manifests, all routed through a research credential that fooled the automated screen and fooled nobody human. The Cassiopia. Passenger roster, forty years sealed. And then — this was the one that made his hands go cold on his coffee — a request for the ship's construction authorization, which lived in a directory even senior officers needed a directorate countersign to open.

He closed the log. Opened it again. Closed it.

At 0900 he walked her letters through the checkpoint in his coat lining, past Okafor's junior archivist, past the scanner that had been miscalibrated in his favor by a technician who owed him a favor that owed someone else a favor, all the way down the chain of small corruptions that held the corridor together. That was the secret nobody said aloud: the silence between the colonies wasn't maintained by law. It was maintained by men like him choosing, daily, not to see.

Berth nine smelled of coolant and old metal, the way the Meridian always did, the way he suspected home would smell if he'd ever been allowed one.

"You're early," Jude said, not looking up from the console.

"You're flagged." He set the letters on the deck beside her boots. "Three pulls off the sealed manifests in forty-eight hours. The Cassiopia request went up to a supervisor's queue automatically. Somebody in Continuity Liaison pulled it this morning."

She looked up then. Her face did the thing it always did — nothing moved, and everything rearranged itself behind the stillness like ice shifting over deep water.

"How bad?"

"Not arrest bad. Not yet. But your research credential will be reviewed by end of week, and when it dies, every query you've made gets attached to it. Including the ones you haven't made yet." He paused. "Including the ones you're planning right now. I can see you planning them."

"I'm not planning anything," Jude said, which was true in the way that a loaded weapon is unloaded until it isn't.

"Jude."

"Ilya."

They stood there in the noise of the berth, the Meridian ticking as her hull cooled, and neither of them said any of the sentences that were actually available. Six years of practice. He could have taught a masterclass in everything they weren't saying.

"I can slow the review," he said finally. "A week, maybe two. After that it's out of my hands, and I need you to hear that I mean *out of my hands*. There's a line where helping you stops being smuggling and starts being treason, and I don't know where the line is anymore. That's what scares me. Not getting caught. Not knowing where the line went."

"Then stop helping me," she said.

It came out flat, and he knew — he *knew* — it was meant as mercy. Push me away before I take you down with me. He'd heard her say it before, in smaller ways, in the grammar of returned favors and unopened personal channels. She thought she was protecting him. She had never once understood that being protected from her was the one injury he wouldn't survive cleanly.

"No," he said.

"That's not a negotiation."

"Good thing I'm not negotiating." He took the empty return bundle from under her console — outgoing letters, thirty-eight of them, Kepler-bound — and zipped it into his coat. His hands were steady. His voice wasn't, quite. "You want to know what I did this morning, before I walked your letters through? I pulled Tomas Vey's death record. Coroner's file, forty years cold. I shouldn't have. I had no operational reason. I did it because your name was attached to the query and I wanted to know what you were carrying."

"And?"

"And the file has a page missing. Torn out, not lost — the stub's still in the binding. Someone removed it deliberately, decades ago, with care." He met her eyes. "People tear pages out of love letters, Jude. Out of diaries. Out of evidence. The tearing isn't the question. The question is what was on the page, and who was afraid enough of it to make it disappear and leave the rest standing."

For a moment she looked almost young. Almost like someone who hadn't inherited a dead woman's war.

"The letter," Jude said. "In this bundle. Ada Vey-Roshan. Read it before you send it."

"Why?"

"Because I think the dead man on that manifest might not be as dead as the paperwork says. And because if I'm wrong, I want one person besides me to have read it and be able to say I was wrong."

He wanted to ask what she'd give him if he was right. He wanted to ask what she'd give herself. Instead he said, "Two days. Then I burn the credential trail myself, before their review does it for us."

"It'll look like guilt."

"It'll look like housekeeping. There's a difference, and the difference is whether anyone's looking." He turned for the hatch, then stopped, because he was a coward in exactly one direction and it pointed at her. "Jude. Whatever you find in that vault or that manifest or that torn page — whatever it costs — don't decide alone. That's not a regulation. That's just me."

She didn't answer. She never answered that one. But as the hatch cycled shut he saw her hand rest, briefly, on the false panel where the letters slept, the way you'd touch a door you weren't ready to open but couldn't stop standing near.

* * *

That night, in the customs officers' quarters, Ilya read Ada Vey-Roshan's letter three times.

The first time he read it as a smuggler: routes, risk, weight. The second time he read it as a man in love with the person who'd told him to read it, trying to see what she'd seen. The third time he read it as himself, and his skin went cold.

*"My brother Tomas served on the Cassiopia project. Everyone says the ship never flew. Everyone says my brother died in a docking accident the same winter the project was buried. But the compensation payments never stopped coming. Forty years, every quarter, from an account with no name. You don't pay a dead man's family forever unless the death is doing work for you. My nephew asks when Uncle Tomas is coming home. Lately, I have started to wonder what I would do if the answer was: he never left."*

Ilya sat very still in the grey light of his quarters, listening to the port breathe around him.

Then he did the thing he'd sworn he wouldn't do. He opened the archive terminal, entered his own credentials — clean, senior, traceable straight back to his name and no one else's — and requested the Cassiopia's construction authorization file.

The system thought about it for four seconds. Long enough for him to reconsider. Not long enough for him to mean it.

**REQUEST NOTED,** it said. **COUNTERSIGN REQUIRED: JOINT CONTINUITY AUTHORITY.**

And beneath that, in the small indifferent type of systems that keep perfect records of everyone's ruin:

**QUERY FLAGGED TO DIRECTORATE LIAISON. REFERENCE: AMBERDALE, J. (DECEASED) / AMBERDALE, J. (ACTIVE).**

Two names. One of them hers. One of them a dead woman's, filed as if the file itself couldn't tell the difference — or as if someone, forty years ago, had built the paperwork so that no one ever would.

Ilya stared at the screen until the port lights cycled to night shift, and somewhere below his feet, under the ice, in a vault that officially did not exist, something that had been waiting forty years continued to wait — patient now, because patience had always been on its side, and the countdown, though no one on Halcyon Deep knew it yet, had already begun.
