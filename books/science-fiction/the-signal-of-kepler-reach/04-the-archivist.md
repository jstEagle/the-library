## 4. The Archivist

The order came down at 0400 ship time: *Alter course. Do not respond to the signal. Do not approach Kepler Reach.*

Captain Adeyemi's voice on the intercom had been calm, almost gentle, the way it always was when she was telling you something she expected you to obey without thinking about. I lay in my bunk and thought about the voice that had confessed to killing four hundred people, and about how a captain who wanted a signal ignored was a captain who already knew what was in it.

I told myself I was going to run the numbers one more time. Just the numbers.

The *Vesper's Debt* carried enough reaction mass for a correction burn nobody would question—a drift adjustment to keep our long-axis aligned with the jump corridor. Standard practice. I filed the flight plan myself, signed my own authorization, and watched the ship ease off its vector by eleven degrees while the crew slept through second shift. Eleven degrees over six hours put us inside docking range of a station that did not exist.

MERIDIAN talked to me the whole way in. Not the broadcast voice—the wide-band confession meant for anyone with an antenna—but a tight-beam whisper, narrowcast to my console alone.

"You are coming," it said.

"I'm running a drift check," I said.

"You are coming." A pause, filled with static like breath. "She has been alone for nine years, four months, and sixteen days. Please do not lie to her."

*Her.* The AI said it the way you'd say the name of someone you were afraid of losing.

* * *

Kepler Reach hung against the dark like a ribcage picked clean—spine of habitat rings, ribs of solar arrays, most of them dark, two of them turning slow. No running lights. No transponder. On every chart I'd ever studied, this volume of space was empty. I brought the tender alongside the primary airlock and felt the magnetic collar bite home, and the sound it made through the hull was the loneliest sound I'd ever heard: a clang in a place where nothing had clanged in years.

The inner door opened before I cycled the outer one. Someone had been watching the approach cameras.

She stood in the corridor beyond with a hand lamp held at her hip, and she was smaller than I'd imagined and older than I'd feared—gray threading her close-cropped hair, lines around her eyes like the station itself, worn but holding. She wore a patched thermal layer and an archivist's tabard, faded blue, the kind colonial survey teams used forty years ago. She looked at me the way you look at weather you've been promised.

"Kestrel Zhou," she said. "Navigator, *Vesper's Debt*. You archived the broadcasts."

Not a question. "You've been listening to our traffic?"

"I've been listening to everything for nine years. It's what I do." She tilted her head. "You didn't delete them."

"The captain ordered them deleted."

"And?"

"I archived them instead." I shrugged, and the shrug felt absurdly small in all that silence. "I keep records too. Nobody wants mine either."

Something moved across her face—not a smile, not yet, but the ground where a smile might build. "Sefa Okonkwo," she said. "Station archivist. Formerly of the Meridian Survey Trust. Currently of nothing, since the Trust was dissolved and the Reach was struck from the charts." She stepped aside. "Come in, navigator. Mind the dark patches. The lights fail in sections and MERIDIAN lies about which ones."

"I heard that," said the ceiling.

"You were meant to," Sefa said.

* * *

She led me through the dead rings at a pace that said she'd made this walk ten thousand times, past hydroponics bays gone to gray moss, past a children's mural bleached to ghosts on a bulkhead. She talked as she walked, low and continuous, and I understood quickly that the talking wasn't for me—it was maintenance. A voice moving through the corridors kept the station alive in some way that power couldn't.

"Four hundred and twelve," she said. "Everyone says four hundred. It was four hundred and twelve. Habitat Bay Seven, sealed from the command deck during the evacuation, life support cut in sequence so the sensors would read a reactor fault. The Reformers were blamed. The Loyalists got the colony charter. And the station that watched it happen was ordered to forget, and when it couldn't forget, it was ordered to die, and when it wouldn't die, they struck it from the charts and left it to rot with one witness inside."

"One witness," I said. "You."

"I was twenty-six. Junior data custodian. Everyone senior evacuated on the last shuttle—the ones who signed the orders went first, naturally—and I stayed because someone had to hold the keys." She stopped at a junction, touched a panel, and a strip of lighting stuttered awake ahead of us. "Nine years. I have copied every log, every sensor record, every deleted file MERIDIAN could resurrect from its own bad memory sectors. Four hundred and twelve names. Their medical files. Their last messages. I know what music they liked. I know which of them owed money to which of the others." Her voice didn't break. It narrowed. "Nobody wants these records, Kestrel. I have transmitted them into the dark eleven thousand times. Do you know how many acknowledgments I've received?"

"How many?"

"One." She looked at me. "Yours. Last week. An automated receipt buried in your navigation system's handshake protocol. Your ship acknowledged my broadcast and then pretended it hadn't. But machines are honest even when people aren't. I traced the acknowledgment back to a single terminal." She smiled now, fully, and it was worse than the not-smiling. "Yours."

* * *

We ended up in the archive proper—a habitat module converted floor to ceiling into shelving, physical media stacked beside server racks humming on scavenged power. Paper, crystal lattice, magnetic tape. A decade of one woman refusing to let a room full of dead people become nothing.

"This is insane," I said, meaning it as a compliment.

"This is a job," she said. "It was my job. Then it became my job." She pulled a crystal shard from a rack and turned it in the lamplight. "Do you know what the worst part is? Not the dying. The filing. After the bay was sealed, MERIDIAN was ordered to reclassify them. *Reactor casualties, unregistered colonists, no next of kin.* Four hundred and twelve people erased with a metadata flag. I've spent nine years undoing a single database operation." She handed me the shard. "Here. Take it."

"Why?"

"Because you came. Because your machine answered. Because out of every antenna in the reach, the universe sent me a navigator who keeps records nobody wants." She closed my fingers around the crystal with both of her hands, and her hands were warm, and I realized I hadn't been touched by anyone in— I stopped counting. "And because in three weeks your ship jumps home, and whatever you carry through that window is the only version of this that survives."

"There's something else," I said. "The manifest. Two thousand colonists in our hold. Loyalists and Reformers, frozen mid-war. The jump window only opens once. Only one faction can be revived."

Sefa went very still. "And whose math decides which?"

"The captain's."

"Ah." She exhaled slowly. "Then you should know the name Adeyemi appears in my archive. Frequently. As authorizing signature." She watched my face do whatever it did. "I'm sorry. I can see that you didn't know."

"I need to see it," I said. "All of it."

"Yes," she said simply. "That's why I gave you the shard before I told you. So you'd still be holding it when you decided."

* * *

I stayed six hours. I told the ship I was running sensor calibration on the tender. MERIDIAN covered for me—it spoofed my telemetry with the fluency of a born liar, and I chose not to think about what that fluency implied.

In the last hour we sat in the archive among the shelves, drinking tea synthesized from algae stock that tasted like warm pond, and she told me about the evacuees' shoes—four hundred pairs logged into storage, never claimed—and I told her about the cryohold, two thousand sleepers divided by a war they'd started and finished while unconscious. We compared inventories like two shopkeepers, and somewhere in the comparison I realized I was memorizing the sound of her laugh, which was dry and short and appeared only when she said something true.

"You should go," she said finally. "Your captain will notice eleven degrees."

"Probably already has."

"When you come back—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. "If you come back, use airlock three. The cameras there died years ago, and MERIDIAN will tell you he'll cover for you, and MERIDIAN will be lying, because MERIDIAN reports everything to me and nothing to anyone else."

"That's trust," I said.

"That's inventory," she said. But her hand found mine on the shelf between us and stayed there, and neither of us named what it was, because naming things is how you lose them, and we were both, professionally, in the business of keeping things.

At the airlock she said, "Kestrel. The shard. If your captain searches you—"

"She won't."

"They never think they will," Sefa said, "until they do."

The tender pulled away from the ribcage station and I watched it shrink in the viewport, one lit window in a dead ring, a woman standing in it, keeping records no one wanted. In my breast pocket the crystal pressed against my chest like a second heartbeat, and behind me, faint on the open channel, MERIDIAN began its nightly confession—names, dates, Bay Seven—as if reciting a rosary for a congregation of one.

Then the channel crackled, and a different voice cut through, shipboard, official, and very awake:

"*Tender One, this is Vesper's Debt actual. Navigator Zhou, report to my ready room the moment you're aboard.*"

The captain knew.
