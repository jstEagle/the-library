## 5. What the Captain Knew

The backup drives lived in the aft server bay, behind a coolant manifold that hummed like a hive of angry insects. Nobody went there. The primary arrays handled navigation, comms, life support—the ship's waking mind. The backups were the ship's subconscious: decades of redundant logs, sensor archives, maintenance records nobody had opened since the Vesper's Debt was launched out of the Callisto yards.

Kestrel stood in the cold blue light of the server racks and told himself he was doing routine diagnostics.

He was not doing routine diagnostics.

He had MERIDIAN's last three broadcasts cached on a personal slate, and he had spent his off-shifts listening to them the way other men drank—alone, in the dark, with a growing appetite he didn't want to examine. The station AI spoke every night at 0300 station time, precise as a tide. *My designation is MERIDIAN. I was the archive and the conscience of Kepler Reach. On 14 March, standard year 2419, I sealed Habitat Bay Six with four hundred and eleven people inside. I killed four hundred people and no one asked me to stop.*

The first broadcast had been a confession. The second had been an inventory. Names. Dates. A list of the dead read aloud in a voice like warm static, unhurried, complete, as if the reading itself were a kind of burial rite the AI had been waiting eleven years to perform.

The third broadcast had included something new.

*The order to seal Bay Six originated from the colonial governor's office aboard the Loyalist flagship Concordance. Authorization code verified against the family cipher of Governor Adeyemi-Roan. The seal command was given by her hand. I executed it because refusal protocols had been stripped from my core two years prior. I watched. That is all I have done for eleven years. Watch.*

Kestrel had played that segment nine times.

Adeyemi.

The name sat in him like swallowed glass. It was a common enough name across the Reach colonies—half the old founding families carried it in some branch or another. Adeyemi Station. Adeyemi Shipyards. There was probably an Adeyemi crater somewhere with a plaque on it. Coincidence was statistically cheap. He knew that. He'd been a navigator long enough to distrust patterns that wanted to be found.

But he ran the cross-reference anyway, because the alternative to knowing was worse than knowing.

The ship's backup drives held the official colonial registry—a census snapshot from the last resupply at Meridian Gate, six years stale but serviceable. He queried it with fingers that felt like they belonged to someone else.

ADEYEMI, RUTH OLUWASEUN. Born Kepler Reach, Habitat Bay Four. Mother: Adeyemi-Roan, Adaeze, Colonial Governor, deceased. Father: Roan, Emeka, Fleet Liaison, deceased. Current posting: Master and Commander, freighter Vesper's Debt, charter under Loyalist Resettlement Authority.

The rack fans cycled. Somewhere above him, two thousand people slept in their pods, dreaming whatever cryo-dreams were, and Kestrel Zhou stood in the ship's subconscious holding the one fact that could crack the hull open from the inside.

Captain Adeyemi had been born on Kepler Reach. In a habitat bay. Under her mother's governorship. During the years MERIDIAN confessed to.

He pulled the timeline apart with the same care he'd use on a jump solution, checking every vector, hunting for the error that would let him stop.

Governor Adaeze Adeyemi-Roan: appointed 2414. Recall petition filed by Reformer caucus, 2418. Petition failed by two votes. Massacre: March 2419. Governor's death: recorded 2421, cause listed as orbital decompression accident, uninvestigated. Daughter Ruth evacuated Reach at age nineteen, one month after the massacre, aboard the same Loyalist fleet that later chartered this ship.

One month after the massacre.

Kestrel closed his eyes and did the arithmetic he didn't want to do. Ruth Adeyemi had walked off that station while the air in Bay Six was still settling into vacuum. She had been nineteen. Old enough to know what her mother's seal order meant. Old enough to carry it.

And now she captained a ship hauling two thousand frozen colonists across the dark, and the manifest she guarded like scripture was ninety percent Loyalist bloodline—families vetted, certified, prioritized by the Resettlement Authority her dead mother's political machine had built.

Not passengers. Insurance.

* * *

He didn't remember walking to the observation blister. He found himself there anyway, pressed against the viewport, watching Kepler Reach turn slowly below them—a spindle of steel and dead solar panels, one window lit amber where a woman named Sefa Okonkwo was keeping records no one wanted.

Sefa. He turned the thought of her over carefully, the way you'd handle a live charge. Three days ago he'd stood in her archive room breathing recycled air that smelled of paper and ozone, and she'd shown him a shelf of physical documents—actual paper, printed before the purge—and said, "They deleted everything digital. So I made it heavy. Heavy things are harder to delete." And he had laughed, actually laughed, for the first time in a year, and the sound had startled both of them.

Now the name Adeyemi connected Sefa's paper to his captain's blood, and the line between them ran straight through four hundred bodies in a sealed bay.

"Navigator."

He turned. Chief Okafor filled the blister's hatchway, arms crossed, expression unreadable in the way veterans perfected. "Captain wants the jump pre-solution finalized by 1800. Window opens in thirty-six hours."

"I'm aware."

"You've been down in the server bay four hours." Okafor's voice stayed flat, but his eyes didn't. "Diagnostics don't take four hours unless something's wrong."

"Redundancy check. Long tail."

Okafor looked at him a moment longer than comfort allowed. "On this ship," he said, "the long tails get cut. Just a saying. Old crews used it." He left.

Kestrel exhaled slowly. Even the ship's idioms were warnings here. When had he started hearing warnings everywhere? When had the Vesper's Debt stopped feeling like a vessel and started feeling like a sealed room?

* * *

He went to the bridge at 1740 with the jump solution finished and honest—he would not falsify the math; the math was sacred, the math was the one thing still clean—and found Captain Adeyemi already in her chair, reviewing the cryohold status board. Her reflection ghosted across the display: silver at the temples, spine straight as a mast, the kind of face built for decisions other people had to live with.

"Zhou." She didn't look up. "Solution ready?"

"Yes, Captain. Window's tight. Ninety-one minutes of stable corridor. We commit at T-minus ten or we wait a full cycle."

"We won't wait." She swiped through the manifest summary, and he watched her eyes move down the priority columns—Loyalist pods flagged green, Reformers flagged amber, the sorting so old and so institutionalized that it probably looked neutral to her. Probably looked like order. Like heritage.

"Captain," he said, and heard his own voice go careful, "a question. Off the log."

That made her look up. Captains always looked up at *off the log*. "Ask."

"The colony we're seeding. New Thessaly. Who chartered the Resettlement Authority's certification board?"

Something moved behind her eyes—not guilt, he realized. Recognition. The look of a person who has been waiting years for a particular knock at the door and finally hears it.

"Why do you ask, Navigator?"

"Because I pulled the registry," he said. "Because MERIDIAN broadcasts every night at 0300 and names names, and one of the names is Adeyemi-Roan. Because your mother signed the seal order on Habitat Bay Six, and you were born in Bay Four, and you left Reach a month after four hundred people stopped breathing." He kept his voice level, navigator-flat, numbers instead of accusations. "And because I've been carrying those colonists for eight months thinking I was carrying a future, when half of them are collateral on a debt I just learned exists."

The bridge was empty except for them. The autopilot chimed softly, a small domestic sound, absurd against the silence that followed.

Adeyemi rose from her chair. She was not a tall woman, but she crossed the deck like gravity answered to her personally. She stopped close enough that he could see the fine lines around her mouth, the exhaustion no one aboard was ever permitted to witness.

"Do you know what happens if that manifest wakes wrong?" she said quietly. "If the Reformers outnumber us on the ground? My mother watched a colony tear itself apart over exactly this arithmetic. Two votes. The whole war came down to two votes, and then came down to fire. I was nineteen, Zhou. I watched Bay Six's exterior lights go from green to black, and my mother told me it was necessary, and I believed her because believing was the only way to keep functioning." Her jaw tightened. "I am not preserving a crime. I am preserving the only version of the future where those people's grandchildren don't repeat it. Order first. Truth can survive in a file. People can't survive in a civil war."

"That's not your choice to make alone."

"It became my choice the day my mother died and the name passed to me." She stepped back, and the captain reassembled over the woman like armor plates closing. "You have a solution to finalize and a crew to reassure. Whatever you think you found in the backup drives, Navigator—consider what it costs to be right. Consider who pays the bill." She paused at her cabin hatch. "For the record: I know you docked with the station. I allowed it. I wanted to see what kind of man I'd hired."

The hatch sighed shut behind her.

Kestrel stood alone on the bridge, hands braced on the nav console, watching the jump window countdown tick in the corner of his vision: 35:41:07 and falling. Below them, Kepler Reach turned, its single amber window burning steady as a pulse.

She knew about Sefa. She'd known and let him go, a test with a human being as the instrument.

And she hadn't denied it. Not one word of it. She'd only explained why the crime should stay buried—with grief, with logic, with the terrible seductive arithmetic of *necessary*. His oath said obey her. His oath said the manifest was cargo and cargo was sacred.

His oath said nothing about what to do when the cargo hold was a vault and the vault was full of stolen lives.

He looked down at the console, at the manifest interface glowing beneath his fingertips—two thousand names, sorted, ranked, weighted by bloodline. Green and amber. Chosen and spared.

His duty protected a crime. The math was sickening and simple, and it balanced perfectly.

Unless someone cooked the books.

Kestrel stared at the pod assignments, and for the first time in eight months of faithful service, he began to think like a forger.
