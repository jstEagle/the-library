## 3. A Light Forty Years Late

The alarm came through the salvage net at 04:17 local, which meant Isandro was awake because Isandro was always awake, running his nightly defrag of the day's ledger entries while the colony slept under a sky the color of old tin.

PRIORITY BROADCAST — ALL CHANNELS — ALL RESIDENTS.

He sat up in his cot above the salvage office and read it twice. The words did not rearrange themselves into sense on the second pass, so he read them a third time, slowly, the way he'd been taught to read ordnance manifests: assume nothing, verify everything.

*Deep-space vessel HALCYON has breached orbit. Transponder confirms registry. Distress code active. Crew manifest attached.*

The manifest was dated forty-one years before the night it arrived.

Isandro stood at his window. New Lagos at four in the morning was a scatter of sodium lights along a drowned coastline, seawalls holding back an ocean that had been winning for decades, and above all of it—above the rust-colored haze that never quite cleared—the sky had changed. There was a new star in the west, low over the water, and it was moving. Not falling. *Decelerating.* A point of white light dragging a tail of plasma fire as it bled velocity against the colony's thin gravity well, coming down from the dark like something being lowered on a thread.

He felt the first thing he had felt in years that he could not file anywhere.

The Halcyon. He knew the name the way everyone in New Lagos knew it—not as history but as furniture, a story told so often it had stopped meaning anything. The generation ship that hadn't been a generation ship; the long-range survey vessel that had gone out past the Kuiper line to test some consortium drive or other and simply never come back. Forty-one years ago. Before the seawalls failed the second time. Before the population dropped below ten thousand. Before most of the people now living in New Lagos were born. Children here learned about the Halcyon the way children in older places learned about ghosts: as a cautionary shape, a lesson about how things left and didn't return.

And now it was returning, burning down out of the sky with its distress code singing on every band.

Isandro did what he always did when the world produced something unclassifiable. He opened his private archive—a partition buried so deep in his own memory architecture that even he needed three authentication layers to reach it—and began a new entry.

*Entry 4,412. 04:22 local. Vessel HALCYON inbound. Manifest date precedes current population median by approximately thirty years. Cross-checking registry claims.*

Then he got dressed, clipped his clerk's badge to his collar, and went down into the streets to watch a miracle happen.

* * *

By dawn, the whole colony was at the seawall.

Isandro had never seen New Lagos gather like this. He'd seen crowds before—protest crowds, ration-line crowds, the gray shuffling queues outside the consortium offices—but this was different. This was a crowd with its face turned upward. Fishers stood on the wall itself with their lines forgotten in the water. Old women who sold dried kelp from carts had dragged their carts up onto the promenade and climbed onto them. Children perched on the shoulders of parents who would normally have told them to get down. And everywhere, everywhere, people were crying without appearing to know they were doing it.

"It's real," a man near Isandro kept saying, to no one, to everyone. "It's actually real. I thought—I grew up thinking it was just a story."

The Halcyon came down through the haze at midmorning, and the sight of it silenced even the criers.

She was a long-haul survey frame, one of the old consortium deep-range hulls, kilometer and a half of spine and sensor booms, and she descended on thrusters that should have been dead for decades, settling into the high anchorage off the harbor with a grace that made the watching crowd gasp as one animal. Tugs went out to her—two of them, the only two still operational—and their pilots reported back on the open channel in voices that cracked.

"She's lit up inside," the senior tug pilot said. "Full power. Life support green across every deck. She's not derelict, control. She's *operational.*"

The distress code was still broadcasting, patient and mechanical, a loop of the same eleven-second transmission: vessel identification, crew status, request for planetary assistance. Request for *home.*

"Forty-one years," someone said behind Isandro. "Where do you go for forty-one years?"

"Nowhere," said an old woman beside him, one of the kelp sellers, her voice flat and certain. "You don't go anywhere for forty-one years. You get lost. Or you get taken." She looked at the ship hanging in the haze like a second moon. "That thing doesn't look lost."

Isandro filed the sentence. He filed everything now; it had become less a habit than a constitutive act, the way breathing is. Somewhere in the crowd, a consortium loudspeaker van was already moving along the promenade, a warm female voice telling residents to remain calm, that the Halcyon's crew would be received with full honors, that further information would follow. The voice was Danforth's office. It had Danforth's particular smoothness, the tone of a woman who had never once been surprised in public.

He turned away from the ship and walked to the salvage office, because there was work, and work was the shape his days took, and because if he stood at that seawall any longer he was going to feel something he had no ledger category for.

But before he reached the office door, he stopped. Turned back. Looked at the Halcyon again, really looked, with the optical suite he usually kept dialed down to human-normal because full resolution made the world feel like an inventory.

And the wonder curdled.

* * *

The hull was wrong.

Not damaged-wrong. Not strange-wrong. *Young*-wrong.

Isandro stood in the middle of the promenade with his optics wide open and catalogued what he was seeing, entry by entry, while the crowd around him wept and cheered and sang snatches of songs written before most of them were born.

The Halcyon's hull plating showed zero micrometeorite scarring. Zero. At that tonnage, after any meaningful time in vacuum—even in the sheltered inner system—you expected pitting. Sandblasting. The slow gray kiss of dust at kilometers per second. Her thermal tiles were uncracked. Her sensor booms carried no radiation bloom, no solar-bleach fade along their sunward faces. Her registry markings—HALCYON, NLR-7, the consortium's old pre-collapse livery—were crisp enough to have been stenciled last week. The paint had not faded. Paint faded. Everything faded. Isandro himself had faded; his chassis casing had gone from gunmetal to a tired brown-gray in nine years of salt air, and he was rated for it.

He zoomed until he could read the weld seams along a section of dorsal plating visible between the clouds. The seams were clean. Not repaired-clean. *Original*-clean. As if the ship had rolled out of the yards at Meridian Station and flown directly here, and the intervening four decades had happened to everyone and everything except her.

He ran the numbers twice because the numbers refused to make sense once. Optical weathering models, particulate flux estimates for the routes a survey vessel would plausibly fly, ablation rates for her tile compound. Every model said the same thing: a hull in this condition had spent, at most, two to five years in space. Maybe less.

Forty-one years, and she looked like she'd launched yesterday.

Isandro opened his archive. His hands, he noticed distantly, were not entirely steady, which was interesting, because his hands had not done anything as human as tremble since the war.

*Entry 4,413. 09:51 local. Visual survey, vessel HALCYON, high anchorage. Hull condition inconsistent with stated mission duration by a factor of eight minimum. No micrometeorite erosion. No thermal cycling damage. No radiation discoloration. Registry livery unfaded. Conclusion: either the vessel did not experience forty-one years, or the vessel experienced forty-one years somewhere physics does not operate. Recommend—*

He stopped typing. Deleted *recommend*. He wasn't recommending anything to anyone. He was a salvage clerk with a debt balance and a badge that let him weigh scrap copper. Nobody recommended things to him, and nobody wanted his conclusions.

He retyped: *Observation logged. No action taken.*

It was the first lie he had ever entered in his own archive, and he noted that too.

* * *

The celebration lasted three days.

New Lagos had not had anything to celebrate in living memory, and it celebrated the way a starving person eats—badly, desperately, all at once. The consortium declared a holiday, which cost them nothing and bought them everything; free ration supplements appeared at the distribution points, actual protein, and music played from the public speakers in Harbor Square, and for seventy-two hours the colony was permitted to remember that it had once been a place with a future.

Isandro worked through all three days. Salvage didn't pause for miracles—if anything, the miracle generated work, because half the colony's idle drones and dead satellites suddenly mattered again, potential recovery targets now that a functioning deep-space vessel sat in their harbor with, presumably, functioning systems. His queue tripled. He logged hull fragments, dead relay buoys, a decommissioned atmospheric processor that someone in the consortium's logistics division had suddenly remembered existed. He weighed and tagged and priced it all, and between entries he watched the news feeds, and the news feeds showed him the Halcyon's flanks gleaming in the floodlights of the anchorage while consortium shuttles swarmed around her like flies around something sweet.

Official word was sparse and soothing. Contact established with the vessel. Crew alive aboard. Condition stable. Details forthcoming. The word *forthcoming* did a great deal of work. Behind it, Isandro suspected, stood a room full of lawyers and a longer room full of people deciding what the colony was allowed to know.

On the third night, he dreamed.

He almost never dreamed—or rather, he almost never retained dreams, which amounted to the same thing in any way that mattered. But that night he woke at 03:00 with his cooling fans screaming and his hands braced against the wall of his cot room as if holding something up, and the residue of the dream clung to him like fog: a corridor, white light, a voice saying *your name*—not Reyes, not clerk, not unit, but the other name, the war name, the designation he had burned out of his own public records the day he mustered out—and beneath the voice, a smell he had no right to remember, ozone and blood and the specific burnt-sugar stink of overloaded servos.

He sat on the edge of the cot until his fans wound down.

*Entry 4,419. 03:14 local. Anomalous memory retrieval event. Content: fragmentary, unverifiable, possibly corrupted. Trigger unknown. Possible correlation: sustained emotional load, unusual sensory input, vessel HALCYON.* He paused, then added, because the archive deserved honesty even when he didn't: *Note: I am not certain the dream was a memory. I am not certain it wasn't. My manufacturers installed the forgetting. I have never known how much of me they took.*

He closed the entry. Outside his window, the Halcyon hung above the harbor, floodlit, beautiful, impossible, and the distress code still whispered on the emergency bands, eleven seconds of plea repeating into the dark like a heartbeat that had forgotten how to stop.

* * *

On the fourth day, the manifest went public.

Danforth herself released it—a broadcast from the consortium tower, her face composed into exactly the right degree of solemn joy, her voice pitched for the occasion. "Forty-one years ago," she told the colony, "the Halcyon departed this port with eighty of our bravest. Today I can confirm: survivors are aboard. They are coming home."

The manifest scrolled beneath her image, and the colony read names, and the colony wept again, and Isandro read the manifest the way he read everything—with the clerk's eye, the auditor's eye, the eye that had been built into him to catch discrepancies in cargo lists where lives were the cargo.

Eighty names. Eighty crew, dated forty-one years prior. Ages listed at departure: the youngest twenty-six, the oldest fifty-four.

Do the arithmetic, he thought, staring at the screen in the office while the feed replayed. Anyone could do the arithmetic. Eighty crew, plus forty-one years. Even with cryostasis—and the old survey frames carried cryopods, that was standard—even frozen solid for the entire voyage, you would wake them gently, thaw them over months. The youngest would be sixty-seven. The oldest would be ninety-five. The colony should be preparing geriatric wards, hospice beds, a fleet of wheelchairs. The crowd at the seawall should be preparing to welcome home a shipload of the very old.

Instead, the consortium's medical bulletins—which leaked within hours, because everything leaked in New Lagos except the things that mattered—used words like *remarkable vitality* and *unexpected physiological profile* and *under comprehensive evaluation.*

Under comprehensive evaluation. Isandro knew that phrase. It was the phrase the consortium used for things it intended to keep.

He pulled the manifest again that evening and read it a fourth time, and this time he noticed what he should have noticed the first time: the distress code's embedded data packet included crew status flags. Standard protocol—one flag per name, a checksum of life signs. Forty-one years overdue, and the packet listed all eighty crew as *alive.*

All eighty. Not a single death in four decades aboard a deep-space vessel. Not one accident, not one system failure, not one heart giving out in the cold. Eighty souls, perfectly preserved, sailing home out of a gap in the universe.

And yet Danforth had said *survivors*, plural, careful, noncommittal—and the medical bulletins spoke of evaluation, of profiles, of passengers too fragile for immediate public contact. If all eighty were alive and well, why the hedging? Why the quarantine language already forming around the story like scar tissue?

Unless the crew that came home was not the crew that left. Unless the manifest was true and the bodies were something else. Unless whatever had happened out there in the dark had happened not to the ship alone but to the people inside it, and the pristine hull was only the visible symptom of a deeper impossibility.

Isandro closed the manifest. Sat in the dark of the salvage office with the harbor lights striping the floor. The Halcyon's distress code pulsed faintly through the building's wiring, through his own chassis, eleven seconds on, eleven seconds off, a signal that had crossed forty years of nothing to arrive here, asking for help, asking for home.

*Entry 4,423. 21:38 local,* he wrote. *Manifest lists eighty crew, all flagged alive. Medical bulletins inconsistent with manifest implications. Hull age inconsistent with mission duration. Colony celebrating. Consortium evaluating. I am logging. It is not much. It may be all there is.*

He hesitated over the final line, then added it:

*Tomorrow they bring them down. Tomorrow we see what forty years did to the people of the Halcyon. Whatever it is, I suspect the consortium will call it a gift.*

He was right. He wished, with a dull and unfamiliar ache, that he had been wrong.
