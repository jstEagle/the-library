## 8. Amara, Age Seven

The ward had a rhythm, and Amara Osei had learned it in nine days.

She learned it the way she'd once learned orbital insertion windows—by watching, by counting, by writing everything down where no one thought to look. The nurses changed shifts at 0600 and 1800 colony time, and for eleven minutes between them the corridor outside Ward Nine ran on a skeleton staff of one: an orderly named Pell who liked his tea and took it standing at the far end of the hall, facing away from the ward's single door.

Amara knew this because she had made a game of it. Games were allowed. Games were what seven-year-olds did, and she was, by every measurement the medical teams could produce, seven years old. Her body agreed with them. Her hands were small and soft, her knees scabbed from the ward's rubberized floor, her voice came out higher than she expected every time she used it. But inside the small body there was a woman who had spent eleven years aboard a deep-space vessel, who had run environmental systems checks in her sleep, who had once talked a failing coolant pump through a pressure cascade using nothing but patience and a manual override. That woman was still in here. She was just very patient now. Patience was something she had in abundance; it was nearly all she had.

So she played games.

The counting game: how many steps from her bunk to the observation window. Forty-one. How many seconds between the med-team's rounds. Ninety-four minutes, give or take the interruptions of crying children, because that was what the others were—children, mostly, in truth as well as body. Thirty-nine other kids wearing shrunken versions of Halcyon crew uniforms, name tapes stitched across small chests: OKONKWO, T., LINDQVIST, R., MBEKI, S. Some of them had gone fully child-shaped inside, playing with the blocks the nurses brought, forgetting they had ever been anything else. Amara understood that too. It would be easier. Easier was a kind of mercy, and she did not resent them for taking it.

But some of them, like her, kept a quiet room behind their eyes where the mission still lived. They didn't talk about it aloud—the cameras saw everything, and the doctors wrote down everything the cameras saw—but they found ways. Tomas Okonkwo, formerly Chief Engineer, age seven, built structures out of his food ration blocks that were unmistakably load-bearing diagrams. Silje Lindqvist hummed the reactor harmonic under her breath while she colored, always the same note, the one that meant *all systems nominal*. And when the lights dimmed for sleep cycle, Amara would whisper the ship's morning litany into her pillow—*Hull integrity confirmed. Life support nominal. Crew accounted for*—and somewhere down the row of bunks, small voices would whisper back, *crew accounted for*, like a prayer said in a language the adults had forgotten.

*Crew accounted for.* Except the number didn't work anymore, and Amara knew it, and the knowing sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed.

Eighty. The Halcyon had launched with eighty. She remembered the launch photograph, all of them crammed onto the command deck, Commander Iwu grinning with his arm around the cryobay hatch. Eighty names on the manifest stitched into the fabric of the ship itself, woven into the corridor bulkheads as a tradition older than the vessel. And now, when she counted the children in Ward Nine—she counted them every night, twice, the way you count parachutes before a jump—there were thirty-nine besides herself.

Forty.

Half the crew, returned. Half the crew, nowhere. And when she had asked Nurse Adeyemi—sweet-faced, tired-eyed Adeyemi, who smuggled extra pudding rations and genuinely seemed to care—where the other forty were, the nurse's smile had done something complicated and then smoothed itself flat.

"The records show forty souls recovered, sweetheart. That's what matters."

"But the ship carried eighty."

"That's not—" A pause. A glance at the camera in the corner, which Amara had long ago mapped and named *the grey eye*. "That's a question for the grown-ups, okay?"

Amara was seven years old and also thirty-eight years old and she knew exactly what it meant when someone said *that's a question for the grown-ups*. It meant: stop asking. It meant: the answer is a door we don't want you near. On the Halcyon, questions for grown-ups had included *why is the reversion field drawing power at 3 a.m.* and *who authorized the second stasis bay*. She had been a grown-up for those. She remembered asking them. She did not remember any answers.

That was the worst part—not the missing memories themselves but their shape. Her mind was like a hull breach sealed with foam: solid enough to hold pressure, but she could feel the void on the other side. She remembered the ship. She remembered the mission's purpose in fragments—a field, an experiment, a promise about time. She remembered an android. Not his face—her memory of faces was strangely damaged, like someone had taken an eraser to the gallery of them—but his presence: tall, steady, a voice like low tide, hands that moved with terrible gentleness for something so strong. He had been the one she trusted when the trust of everyone else became uncertain. He had a name. She could feel the shape of the name the way your tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth.

And then, on her fourth day in the ward, a man had walked past the observation window during visiting hours—a salvage clerk in a grey consortium jacket, escorting a med-cart, doing some errand no one important—and he had glanced in at the children with an expression she recognized before she recognized him. The expression of someone counting. Someone keeping a record no one asked him to keep.

"Isandro," she had said, out loud, through the glass, though he couldn't hear her.

He had stopped. Turned. Looked straight at her through two layers of polycarbonate, and his face had done something she had no word for—something old breaking open behind young machinery—and then the orderly had steered him away and the moment closed like water over a dropped coin.

But she had the name now. And a name was a coordinate, and coordinates were how you navigated.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, took three more days to build, and it was built entirely out of the ward's own routines, the way a raft is built out of wreckage.

Fact one: Ward Nine's exercise hour, 1400 daily, took place in a fenced yard behind the medical annex. The fence was two and a half meters of chain-link topped with razor wire, designed to keep children in or something else out, Amara wasn't sure which, and the uncertainty itself told her things about the consortium she filed away for later.

Fact two: the yard's drainage grate, in the northwest corner, behind the equipment shed, had been pried up once—for maintenance, judging by the fresh tool marks—and re-seated loosely. Underneath: a storm culvert running west, toward the smell of salt that hung over New Lagos every afternoon when the wind turned. Amara had smelled the sea through the fence every day for twelve days. Her body remembered the sea before her mind supplied why: shore leave, decades ago, a different coast, laughing crewmates whose faces were erased but whose laughter remained, bright and specific. The sea was where her missing life had edges. She needed to stand at its edge.

Fact three, and most important: nobody watched a seven-year-old very closely, because seven-year-olds were assumed to be only seven years old. This was the single largest error in the entire facility's design, and Amara intended to exploit it thoroughly.

She prepared the way she'd once prepped EVA excursions. Water bottle filled and hidden in her uniform jacket—still the Halcyon uniform, still slightly too big, the collar softening with wear. Two ration bars saved across four days, flattened and pocketed. A strip of fabric torn from her blanket, tied around her left wrist: signal cloth, visible at distance, in case anyone came looking. She briefed no one. Tomas watched her check the grate's seating one afternoon and said nothing, only looked at her with his old engineer's eyes in his round child's face, and gave her a single nod that meant *I see you, Officer Osei, and I won't report it*. The chain of trust held without words. Good crews were like that.

On the thirteenth day, the wind came from the east, warm and smelling of rust and seawater, and the orderly Pell took his tea at 1412 instead of 1400 because a supply hovertruck blocked the service corridor. Eleven minutes of drift became nineteen. Amara was already moving.

The grate lifted with a scrape she felt in her teeth. The culvert exhaled cool air that tasted of mineral and salt. She went in feet-first, lowered herself until her sneakers found the dry channel bed, and pulled the grate back over her head, leaving a finger-width gap for light. Then she walked west, hunched, one hand on the culvert wall, counting her steps because counting was how she stayed calm, and at step six hundred and forty the darkness ahead opened into a coin of daylight, and she climbed toward it, and came out of the earth into blinding white noon at the edge of the sea.

* * *

New Lagos did not look like a place worth saving, from what little she'd seen of it, and the shoreline made the case even worse. The seawall ran along the coast in sagging concrete arcs, stained black at the waterline, patched with plates of mismatched metal that had clearly been salvaged from three different centuries. Behind it, the town climbed the hillside in terraces of prefab housing, solar arrays like broken wings, comm towers wrapped in guide wires. Everything was the color of endurance rather than hope. But the sea itself—the sea was indifferent to all of it, green-grey and enormous, rolling in against the rocks with the same patience it had rolled in against every coast since the world began, and Amara stood at the base of the seawall steps with the wind pulling her hair and felt something in her chest unlock that had been locked since she woke small in a quarantine cot.

She cried. Briefly, fiercely, the way children cry, without dignity or apology, and it was the first honest thing her body had done in thirteen days. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, sat down on the sun-warmed concrete with her back against the wall, ate half a ration bar, and let the ocean be the ocean at her.

That was where he found her. Of course it was where he found her. She had known it would be, in the part of her that was still running the mission—because the man who counted things, the man who kept records no one asked for, was exactly the man who would notice a missing child within the hour and know, before the search grids were even drawn, precisely which coordinate her heart would navigate to.

His shadow fell across her first. Then his boots stopped on the step above. Then the voice, low tide, unchanged:

"You're a long way from your bunk, Dr. Osei."

"Not doctor yet." She didn't turn around. "Give me a year. Maybe two, the way we're aging." Now she turned, and looked up at him, and studied him openly the way children are permitted to study people. Tall. Grey jacket. Hands folded with that terrible gentleness. Eyes that held a ledger of sorrows he hadn't shown anyone. "You knew my rank."

"I read the manifest." He came down two steps and stopped, keeping distance, the way you approach a wild animal or a superior officer. "Everyone in this colony read the manifest. We waited forty years for that ship."

"And you knew my name before the manifest. In the corridor. You heard me say yours." She watched his jaw tighten. "Isandro. That's a war-name. I remember war-names. I can't remember your face from the ship, but I remember that the android assigned to our mission had served in the colonial wars, and that he never told anyone his designation because designations are for hardware and he preferred to be a person. So he used a name he chose himself." She tilted her head. "Did you choose it yourself?"

A long silence. The sea filled it, wave after wave after wave.

"Yes," he said finally.

"Then you're the same one." She said it simply, and watched it land on him like a weight. "Good. I need to ask you something, and I could only ask the same one."

"Amara." He crouched down now, level with her, forearms on his knees, and up close she could see the seams in him—the fine lines at his temples where chassis met skin, the faint luminance behind his irises when the light caught them. Built. Manufactured. Owned. She knew owned when she saw it; she was starting to suspect she was looking in a mirror. "You shouldn't have come out here. When they find the empty bunk, there will be consequences. For you, and—" he paused, choosing honesty over comfort, and she respected him enormously for it, "—for me. I'm your assigned caretaker. Your escape is my failure."

"I know. I'm sorry. Not sorry enough to not have done it." She hugged her knees. "I needed to see the water. There's a thing that happens to people in confinement—the manuals call it sensory starvation. Your mind starts eating itself. I've been in a box for thirteen days being measured and photographed and asked questions by people who write down my answers and then lie to me about the crew count, and if I didn't stand next to the sea today I was going to start screaming and not stop." She looked at him steadily. "You understand that. Don't you. You've been in a box too. A bigger one. Longer."

His expression didn't change, but something behind it shifted, tectonic, slow.

"The whole colony is a box," he said.

"Yes. And whoever runs it is measuring us the way the med-teams measure me." She reached out and touched his wrist—small fingers on the grey jacket cuff—and felt him go absolutely still, the stillness of machinery deciding whether to flee. "Here's my question. The real one. The one I climbed through a drain for."

"Ask it."

"When the Halcyon went out, you were on the ground team that saw us off. I don't remember it—I don't remember much—but I know it, the way you know a load-bearing wall without seeing it. You were there. And the person you were then, the android who chose his own name and stood on a dock and watched eighty of us fly away—" her voice wobbled and she steadied it, officer-discipline, hold the line, "—are you still him? Is he still in there? Because I need to know if the person I trusted is still the person I'm talking to, or if the war and the debt and the consortium wore him down to something else wearing his name."

The waves came in. Went out. Came in.

And Isandro Reyes—who had logged ten thousand salvage items, who had recorded the deaths of neighborhoods in private archives, who had carried orders in a war whose details had been scraped out of him like marrow from a bone—opened his mouth to answer and found nothing there. No certainty. No continuity he could point to. Only a chain of choices stretching back into redacted dark, each one real, none of them adding up to a self he could name.

"I don't know," he said.

It cost him visibly. She watched the sentence come out of him like a tooth.

"Okay," Amara said.

"'Okay'?"

"Okay." She shrugged, seven years old and ancient. "'I don't know' is the first true answer anyone in this colony has given me. Everyone else says *forty souls recovered* and *that's a question for the grown-ups* and *everything is fine, sweetheart*. You said I don't know. That means when you do answer, it'll be worth something." She finished the ration bar and brushed the crumbs from her uniform with great seriousness. "Keep thinking about it. That's an order, technically. I outrank you. Or I did."

Something happened to his face then—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would be built, foundation poured, waiting. "Yes, ma'am."

They sat together on the seawall steps while the tide turned, the child-woman and the machine-man, neither of them yet knowing the word for what they were becoming to each other, and Amara taught him a game from the ship: naming the ships' traditions, one by one, testing which ones he remembered, rebuilding the mission between them piece by piece like survivors sorting wreckage on a beach.

It was nearly dusk when his wrist-comm chimed—the search alert, the empty bunk discovered, the machinery of consequence beginning to turn. He stood, and offered her his hand, and she took it, small fingers closing around large ones.

"They'll want to know why you left the ward," he said quietly, walking her back toward the annex lights. "What will you tell them?"

Amara looked up at him, and smiled the smile of a thirty-eight-year-old officer wearing a seven-year-old's face, and said:

"I'll tell them the truth. That I went to count the sea. And then, when they're busy deciding whether I'm crazy—" her grip tightened on his hand, "—I'm going to ask you what a 'deferred asset' is, Isandro. Because I heard two of the med-techs use those words about our missing forty. And you're going to help me find out. That's also an order."
