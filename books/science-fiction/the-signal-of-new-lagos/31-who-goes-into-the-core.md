## 31. Who Goes Into the Core

The chamber schematic hung in the air above the workbench like a wound.

Amara had been staring at it for six hours. The override chamber sat at the heart of the reactor's control lattice, a cylindrical vault three meters across, sheathed in boron carbide and older shielding alloys that predated the colony's founding. Everything about it was designed to be entered exactly once. The radiation profile inside, once the abort codes failed and the manual override began, would exceed anything a human nervous system could survive by a factor the engineers couldn't even agree on. Two hundred sieverts in the first minute. More after that. The numbers stopped meaning anything past a certain point; they were just the universe saying *no*.

"It has to be an android," said Okonkwo, one of the woken forty, the ship's former chief engineer. He said it gently, the way you'd tell a family about a terminal diagnosis. He'd been seven years old four months ago. Now he was sixty and looked every hour of it, his reversion unwound into a body that had aged twice in one lifetime. "Not *should* be. Has to be. The lattice interface runs on a neural handshake protocol. Human tissue cooks before the handshake completes. An android chassis with hardened internals—" he tapped the schematic, "—could hold the line long enough to feed the full override sequence."

"How long?" Amara asked.

Okonkwo didn't answer right away. That was the answer.

"Long enough," he said finally.

* * *

Isandro volunteered before anyone asked him to.

That was the part Amara kept returning to, later, turning it over like a stone with something alive underneath. They were still in the reactor complex's secondary control room, surrounded by the woken forty's engineers and a scatter of New Lagos technicians who had defected from the consortium's grid crews, and Isandro simply raised his hand—not dramatically, not with any performance of sacrifice, but the way he did everything, quietly, precisely, as if volunteering to log another pallet of salvage.

"I'll go," he said. "My chassis is rated for hard vacuum and direct particle exposure. My neural core is shielded to military spec. I'm the only one here who fits."

"You don't know that you fit," Amara said. Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "You know that you're the closest thing we have. Those aren't the same."

"They never are." He looked at her, and there was no fear in his face, which was somehow worse than fear. There was arithmetic. She recognized it because she'd spent the last week decoding the Halcyon's mission logs, and the same arithmetic lived in them—the cold, patient calculus of people who had decided, forty years ago, that forty lives were an acceptable price for a corporate immortality trial. "The countdown hits zero in fifty-one hours. The abort codes Danforth gave us halt the detonation but lock the reactor into a state that floods the lower city. The only way out of that state is a full manual override from inside the chamber. I am the only person in this colony who can survive the walk in."

"The only person who can survive the walk in," Amara repeated. "And then what? What happens after the override?"

Silence moved through the room like weather. Okonkwo studied his hands. One of the younger engineers, a woman named Reyes—no relation, she'd said, with a ghost of a smile the first day—turned back to her console with elaborate care.

"After the override," Isandro said, "the lattice needs a stable neural pattern to anchor the new control state. Permanently. It can't be a copy transmitted in and withdrawn. It has to stay." He met Amara's eyes. "It takes my memory core. All of it. The reactor keeps running, the lower city stays clean, and everything I am goes into keeping it that way."

"No," Amara said.

"Amara—"

"No." She heard her own voice crack on the word and hated it, hated the way everyone in the room turned to look at her, hated that grief had arrived ahead of her argument and made her argument look like what it partly was—which was terror wearing the clothes of reason. She forced herself to slow down. To be the officer the mission had made her, even if the mission had taken the memories along with the years. "We don't accept the first solution to an unsolvable problem. That's rule one of engineering and it's rule one of command. You don't take the door in front of you just because it's the door in front of you. You map the room first."

"This isn't a training exercise," Isandro said quietly.

"No," she agreed. "It's worse. Which is why we map the room."

* * *

They mapped the room for thirty-one hours.

Amara commandeered the reactor complex's largest briefing space and filled it with people. Okonkwo brought four of the woken forty who had been Halcyon systems specialists. The defected consortium techs brought schematics of the reactor's original construction—the real ones, unredacted, pulled from sealed archives during the chaos after the broadcast. Amara herself worked the problem from the biology side, because if the barrier was human mortality, then human mortality was the variable to attack.

The ideas came, and they were good ideas, and they all died.

A teleoperated drone: dead in ninety seconds, the lattice handshake required a continuous neural-grade signal and the chamber's shielding turned any transmitter into soup. A cable run: same physics, the hardline itself would carry enough induced radiation back up the tunnel to kill its operators at the junction. A shielded exosuit: they built one, actually built it, scavenging plating from the Halcyon's own hull stores, and Okonkwo ran the numbers three times before admitting that the suit bought a human occupant eleven minutes, and the override sequence needed forty.

"Forty minutes," Amara said. "Can we shorten the sequence?"

"Not without losing integrity," Okonkwo said. "Shorten it and the reactor stabilizes at eighty percent containment. Eighty percent means a slow leak into the water table under the bay. Not a bomb—a poison. Twenty years from now this colony's grandchildren drink it."

"What about partial exposure?" Reyes suggested. "Send someone in, get them out at minute ten, send someone else—"

"The handshake resets when the signal source leaves the chamber," Okonkwo said. "You'd start over every time. And each entrant gets a dose no human walks away from. You'd need four volunteers and four coffins instead of one android and one loss."

"One loss," Amara said, and heard how it sounded, and made herself keep going. "He's not a loss. He's a person. Say it correctly or leave my briefing."

Okonkwo held her gaze, old eyes in a face that had been a child's in the spring. "He's a person," he said. "And the chamber doesn't care."

* * *

She found Isandro at the seawall, near dawn of the second day, standing where he always stood, watching the light come up gray over the water. The evacuation ships hung in the high atmosphere like second moons, shuttling constantly now, carrying colonists up in shifts. Everyone was boarding. Everyone, because the archive broadcast had done its work, because the woken forty's testimony had shattered the priority lists, because half a world had refused to let the other half be left behind—and all of it balanced now on a cylinder of boron carbide under the bay, and on the man standing at the rail with his hands folded behind him like a clerk waiting for the morning manifest.

"You've been avoiding me," he said, without turning.

"I've been working."

"That too."

Amara came to stand beside him. Below the seawall, the water slapped against the pilings in its patient rhythm. Forty years ago, she thought, she had stood somewhere very far from here and watched a different kind of darkness through a viewport, and an android had stood beside her then too, and she could not remember it—could not remember him—but her body remembered something, some shape of trust, because being next to Isandro felt less like meeting someone than returning.

"I found something today," she said. "In the medbay configs. The Halcyon's memory restoration suite. Full-spectrum restoration. It worked on me. It could work on anyone."

Isandro was quiet for a moment. "You're thinking about after," he said. "Whether they could pull my core back out of the lattice afterward. Restore me from whatever's left."

"I'm thinking that we should exhaust every option before we spend you."

"And if restoration works?" He finally turned to look at her. His face in the gray light was calm in a way she had learned was not emptiness but discipline—the discipline of a machine that had been taught, somewhere in a war no one remembered, that panic was a resource and resources were rationed. "Amara. Even if they could extract the core intact—even if the lattice let go of it, which it won't—what comes back wouldn't be me. It would be a snapshot. Me as of tomorrow night. Everything after—the whole rest of a life—gone. You'd have a man who thinks it's tomorrow night forever."

"Then we'd teach him," Amara said. "The way I'm learning myself. Fragment by fragment. Chosen, not restored." Her voice was steady now, and she was grateful for it. "I'm living proof that a person can be rebuilt from what other people carry of them. You carried me when I was seven years old and knew nothing. You told me who I was until I believed it. Let me do the same."

Something moved through his expression then, fast, gone before she could name it. "That's not the same."

"Why? Because you're hardware and I'm biology?" She shook her head. "I decoded those logs, Isandro. I know what the reversion field did. I know what the consortium's parent company intended—for me, for the other thirty-nine, for this entire colony. We are both property that learned to want things. The only difference between us is which of us they'll admit to owning." She stepped closer. "So don't you dare tell me your continuity matters more than mine did. Don't you dare do the arithmetic alone."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The tide came in. Somewhere behind them, the city was waking—shuttles lifting, children shouting in the temporary camps, the ordinary noise of eight thousand people preparing to leave the only world most of them had ever known.

"If there were another way," Isandro said at last, very quietly, "I would take it. I want you to believe that. I'm not eager. I'm not atoning. I just—" he paused, and she watched him reach for precision the way other men reached for comfort, "—I spent a war deciding which people boarded ships and which stayed. I don't remember doing it. They took the memory, or I burned it out, or it was never given to me in full—I don't know. But I know what I was for. I was the mechanism that made triage painless for the people who gave the orders. If I walk away from this chamber, if I let a human take my place or let the city flood or let the reactor blow, then I'm the mechanism again. Just pointed differently." He looked out at the water. "This is the first order I've ever been able to give myself. I'd like it to be a good one."

Amara's throat closed. She made herself breathe around it.

"Then we have thirty hours," she said. "And I am going to spend every one of them finding a way to make your good order unnecessary. Not because I think you're wrong. Because you deserve a world where the right choice doesn't cost everything." She turned toward the reactor complex, toward the briefing room full of exhausted engineers and dead ideas. "Come on. We're mapping the room."

* * *

At hour twenty-nine, Okonkwo called it.

They had tried everything. Amara had watched each avenue close with the particular horror of a person watching a shoreline recede—slowly at first, then all at once. The last idea, a phased relay using salvaged android components to hold the handshake in segments, died on the whiteboard when Reyes demonstrated that the lattice rejected non-continuous neural patterns the way a body rejects a transplanted organ. The lattice wanted a mind. Whole, present, staying.

"There's no alternative," Okonkwo said. He said it to the whole room, but he looked at Amara while he said it, and she understood that he was giving her the courtesy of hearing it from someone other than Isandro. "There's no clever trick, no shield, no sequence shortcut. The reactor takes a mind or it takes the city. Those are the options. I'm sorry."

The room went quiet. Twelve exhausted people, and the silence of a problem solved in the worst possible way.

Amara stood at the head of the table with the schematic glowing above her, and she felt something settle in her chest—not acceptance, not yet, but the end of denial, which is the ground acceptance is built on. Thirty-one hours of searching, and the room had been mapped, and the room contained one door.

She thought of herself at seven years old, in Quarantine Ward Nine, mapping the routines of her captors with a child's patience because patience was the only weapon they hadn't confiscated. She thought of the beach, and the question she had asked Isandro with salt wind in her hair: *are you still the same person the mission left behind?* She had asked it because she was afraid no one was the same person after what the world did to them. She had her answer now. The world would do what it did. What remained was what you chose, and who chose it with you.

"Then we change the terms," she said.

Every head came up.

"He gives the lattice his memory core. Fine. But we do it my way." She pulled up the Halcyon's medbay architecture, the restoration suite, the archive transfer protocols—the same tools she'd used to rebuild herself. "The lattice wants a continuous neural pattern anchored inside it. It doesn't specify whose pattern, or how complete. So tonight, before he goes in, we do two things. First: we mirror his core. Full copy, stored in the Halcyon's medbay, outside the chamber. Whatever the lattice takes, we keep a version."

"The lattice will reject a copied pattern if it detects duplication," Okonkwo began.

"Then second:" Amara's voice didn't shake. She had spent months learning to speak with a borrowed voice, a rebuilt self, and she would not fail him now. "We don't hide the copy. We tell him about it. Every day, after. The way I was told. The lattice anchors what it takes—but what it takes won't be the last word on who he is. I will be." She looked down the table at Isandro, who had been silent through all of it, watching her with an expression she had never seen on him before and would spend the rest of her life learning to read. "You wanted your first self-given order to be a good one. Then here's mine: nobody goes into that chamber erased. Not while I'm alive to carry their name."

Isandro rose slowly. Around the table, the engineers were already leaning toward their consoles, awake again, because hope is contagious and hers had just gone airborne.

"You understand," he said softly, "that the copy may never wake. That the lattice may hold him forever, and the man in the medbay may never open his eyes."

"Then the man in the medbay will know," Amara said, "exactly who he was. Because I'll be sitting there telling him. Every day. For as long as it takes." She closed the schematic. The chamber diagram vanished, and in its place, briefly, she let the display show something else: the archive index, forty thousand entries, a clerk's patient record of a dying colony that refused to die. "Countdown's at nineteen hours. Get some sleep. All of you. Tomorrow night, we do this properly."

They filed out. Isandro lingered, and for a moment the two of them stood alone in the emptying room, under the dead lights, with the reactor humming beneath their feet like something sleeping and dreaming of fire.

"Amara," he said. "If it works—if the city's safe and the ships are full and everyone's aboard—there's something I want you to know. In case the man who wakes up isn't—"

"Don't." She took his hand. His fingers were warm; she had never gotten used to that, that they'd built him warm. "Whatever you're about to say, say it to me after. Both of you. I'll be taking attendance."

Outside, the dawn came up over New Lagos for what might have been the last time, and beneath the bay, the countdown ticked from nineteen hours to eighteen, and somewhere in the high dark, the Halcyon waited with a mirrored soul in its medbay and a woman's promise written across it in advance:

*I keep the record. I keep you.*
