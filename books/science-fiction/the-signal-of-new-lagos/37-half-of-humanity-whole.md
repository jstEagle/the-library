## 37. Half of Humanity, Whole

The last boarding call came at dawn, and Amara Osei was standing at the terminal to hear it.

Not because she expected anyone to miss it. The announcement had been playing on every band for six hours, in Danforth's clipped recorded voice—*final embarkation, final embarkation, all residents proceed to terminals one through nine*—a voice that had lost its power somewhere between the archive broadcast and the creditors' flight, and now sounded like nothing so much as a woman reading a grocery list in an empty house. Amara listened anyway. She had decided, weeks ago, that she would be present for every departure, every name, every step up the ramp. Someone had to keep the record. That had always been the work. It was hers now.

The morning was gray and salt-smelling, the kind of New Lagos dawn where the seawalls wept condensation and the gulls argued over the tidal flats. Below the terminal, the crowd moved in a slow river toward the ships—families with their belongings wrapped in oilcloth, elders carried on pallets because the ramps were too steep for their knees, the woken forty in their mismatched Halcyon uniforms moving through the press of bodies with clipboards and calm voices, directing traffic the way they had once directed a starship. Tesfaye, the former navigator, had a voice that carried over everything without ever seeming to rise. He stood at the base of ramp four and read names off a slate, and each name got an answer, and each answer got a nod, and the river of people kept flowing.

Amara stood at the top of the ramp with her slate and counted.

That was the whole job, in the end. Counting. Making sure the number that went up the ramps equaled the number that had lived here. She had learned counting in quarantine, as a seven-year-old in a ward that smelled of antiseptic and fear—counting the cameras, counting the meal intervals, counting the seconds between footsteps in the corridor, because a number you kept was a fact no one could take from you. She had learned it again as a twenty-year-old decoding the Halcyon's logs, counting a manifest that said eighty and a crew that numbered forty. Counting was how the truth survived. She had built her whole self, twice now, out of numbers no one else wanted to keep.

*Fifty-one thousand, two hundred and six.* That was the population figure from the last honest census, three years before the countdown, before the consortium stopped publishing censuses because honest numbers had become inconvenient. The priority lists had been built on a different figure—*twenty-six thousand, four hundred*—the arithmetic of half a world. Danforth had called it consolidation. The creditors had called it exposure management. Amara had called it, in the broadcast, what it was: a plan to leave half the people of New Lagos standing on a seawall watching the ships leave, and then to call the reactor failure an act of God.

The ships were still in high orbit, all nine of them, holding position like patient predators. They had not come down yet. That was the thing no one said out loud: the ships would not descend until the consortium's claim on the berths was settled, and the consortium's claim had not died with Danforth's confession—it had only stopped being enforceable. Somewhere in the legal lattice that connected New Lagos to its creditors, the colony was still collateral. The woken forty had spent two weeks since the reactor override filing challenges, broadcasting testimony, and—this was the part Amara found herself admiring most—simply refusing. Refusing to board segregated ships. Refusing to let the priority lists stand. Refusing, in the flat unanswerable way of people who had already been property once, to be sorted.

It had worked. That was the astonishing part. It had worked because the ships needed crews, and the crews had heard the broadcast too, and a helmsman who has watched a colony's death warrant read aloud on the emergency band will not point his ship at the people who read it.

The terminal chime sounded. Amara checked her slate against the manifest.

*Fifty-one thousand, two hundred and six.*

She had the number written on the inside of her wrist, in ink, like a child's mnemonic. She checked it against the boarding count every hour, and every hour the two figures moved toward each other like hands of a clock.

* * *

The problem, for most of those weeks, had not been the creditors. The problem had been the staying.

Because not everyone wanted to leave. That was the truth the consortium had never understood, the variable their arithmetic had no column for: New Lagos was dying, and it was home. The seawalls were rusted through in places, patched with salvaged hull plate that Isandro himself had logged into the archive years ago—she had found the entries, his neat clerk's annotations, *seawall sector 7, patch installed, condition poor, condition poor, condition poor*, a litany of small devotions. The fishing fleets were half their old size. The reactor—her reactor now, their reactor, the thing Isandro had died into—was stable but scarred, its control lattice holding a shape it had only held because a man had fed himself into it. There were a thousand reasons to go.

And still, when the woken forty put the question to the colony—*all aboard, or those who wish may stay under new management*—eleven thousand hands went up.

Amara had stood in the assembly hall and watched it happen and felt something crack open in her chest. Eleven thousand people choosing to stay on a dying rock because it was *theirs*, because the ledger of small debts that had bound them could become, if they rewrote it together, a ledger of small holdings—a garden plot, a hull section, a stretch of seawall, a duty. Tesfaye had proposed the structure, drawing on shipboard governance protocols older than the colony: a cooperative council, seats split between the woken forty and the colonists, decisions by open vote, and—this clause had passed without a single dissent—*the full public record of New Lagos, including all archives, to be maintained as a common trust, accessible to every resident and every descendant.*

Amara had seconded that clause. She had seconded it standing at the podium with Isandro's archive drive in her hand, held up like a lantern, and she had said: "This is what he was. This is what he did. It belongs to all of you now, and to no one's ledger but its own."

The hall had gone quiet in the way halls go quiet when grief and gratitude arrive in the same breath. Then the vote. Then the hands.

* * *

The ships came down on the fourth morning.

Amara watched them descend from the seawall—the seawall itself, out at sector seven, where the patch plate was rusted and the gulls screamed and the water came up gray-green against the old concrete. Nine ships, dropping through the cloud deck on pillars of fire, settling into the landing basins with the ponderous grace of whales beaching themselves on purpose. She had seen ships descend before. She had been born—no. She had *woken*, the first time, on a ship. She had been seven years old in a crew uniform watching a world she didn't remember come up through a viewport. Ships meant departure. Ships meant selection. Ships meant lists.

But these ships were empty. These ships had come down carrying nothing but berths, and they would leave carrying everything.

She walked the wall as they landed, because she had promised herself she would, and because the wall was where Isandro had said goodbye—she had read the entry, the last one before the core, timestamped at dawn: *Walked the seawall at first light. Recorded the gulls. Recorded the water. I never belonged to this colony and it never belonged to me, and I find, at the end, that this was its own kind of belonging.* She stood where he had stood. She recorded the gulls. The archive accepted the entry without comment.

The boarding took three days.

She counted all three. She slept in the terminal office in snatches, waking to check the slate, and Tesfaye brought her tea that tasted of recycled water and kindness, and the number on her wrist and the number on the manifest converged like two rivers meeting. Fifty thousand. Fifty-one thousand. Fifty-one thousand, one hundred. She walked the residential districts herself with a handheld scanner on the final day, calling out at doorways, checking the empty apartments, finding the ones who had slipped through the net—an old man deaf in both ears who had never heard the announcement and was found tending his rooftop garden, bewildered and furious at being carried down four flights; a woman who had hidden because her sister was buried in the colony cemetery and she could not bear to leave the body; three children, orphans of the riot at the seawall, who had made a den in the old salvage yard and were discovered by a search team because one of them, a girl with a scraped knee and an officer's flat stare, had decided on her own authority that hiding was no longer tactically sound.

Amara sat with the children in the terminal waiting room and told them about the ships. The girl looked at her and said, "Is it like the Halcyon? Were you on it?"

"I was on it," Amara said. "A long time ago. And then I wasn't. And then I was again."

"Did you forget everything?"

"Almost everything." She held the girl's gaze. "And then I chose what to keep."

The girl considered this with the seriousness of someone who had already lost more than most adults, and then she took Amara's hand, and they walked up the ramp together, and Amara logged her aboard—*name, age, next of kin: none, guardian: pending*—and made a private note in the margin of the manifest: *guardian: me, if she'll have me.*

* * *

On the last evening, with the count at fifty-one thousand, two hundred and five, Amara went to the reactor.

She went down through the maintenance tunnels, past the broken seals she had photographed a lifetime ago, past the countdown interface with its dead amber screen, down to the override chamber door. The corridor outside it was clean now—decontaminated, relit, marked with a memorial plaque that the council had voted on in its first week. The plaque said: *ISANDRO REYES. SALVAGE CLERK. HE KEPT THE RECORD.* Tesfaye had argued for a longer inscription. Amara had argued for that one. She had won, and she was glad, because it was the truest sentence anyone had found.

She put her palm against the chamber door. The alloy was cool. Behind it, somewhere in the dark of the control lattice, was the memory core of a man who had walked into this room knowing the price and paid it without hesitation, and the lattice held, and the reactor breathed its slow stable breath beneath the bay, and the colony above it was emptying itself into the ships like a wound finally allowed to close.

"You'd hate this," she said to the door. "All this ceremony. You'd want the numbers. You'd want to know the count."

She told it the count. Fifty-one thousand, two hundred and five, with one hour to go, one person unaccounted for—the deaf gardener's neighbor, an old woman who had gone out to the tidal flats at low tide and not come back, and search teams were walking the mud with lamps. Amara said it out loud because saying numbers out loud was how you kept them, and because somewhere in the lattice, if any fragment of him remained—some pattern in the control logic, some residue of the man in the machine's reflexes—she wanted him to hear it.

Then her slate chimed. The old woman had been found, asleep in a beached salvage skiff, embarrassed and unrepentant. Amara laughed, alone in the tunnel, and the laugh came out wet.

*Fifty-one thousand, two hundred and six.*

She pressed her forehead to the door for a moment. Then she straightened, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and walked back up into the light, because the record did not keep itself, and she was the record now.

* * *

The last boarding was a small thing, almost anticlimactic. A handful of families. The terminal staff themselves, walking up the ramp backward, watching the colony recede. Amara stood at the base with Tesfaye and did the final reconciliation—manifest against census against her own count, three columns of figures that had spent weeks converging and now, at last, were one number, identical in every column, witnessed and logged and broadcast on the open band so that no one, anywhere, could ever claim otherwise.

*All of New Lagos. No one left behind.*

She said it into the slate's transmitter, standing on the tarmac with the ships' lights washing the clouds orange, and her voice did not shake, and afterward Tesfaye put his hand on her shoulder and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the number hadn't already said.

The staying party—eleven thousand of them—had their own ceremony, back in the assembly hall, but Amara watched its beginning on the relay before the Halcyon broke orbit: the new council seated on a plain dais, the old consortium seal taken down from the wall and replaced with nothing at all, because they had voted that the wall would stay blank until the colony earned a new emblem. The relay showed the seawall, patched and patrolled. It showed the reactor's status board, green and stable. It showed people walking through streets that were theirs now, in a way no deed had ever made them.

Joy, she thought, watching it. This was joy. So why did it sit in her chest like a stone with one flat side, smooth and heavy and always turning the same face toward her?

She knew why. She carried the reason in her hand, in a drive the size of a seed.

* * *

The Halcyon lifted at dusk.

Amara watched the colony fall away from the medbay viewport—first the seawalls, silver in the last light; then the whole gray-green shelf of it; then the curve of the world, and the dark. Somewhere down there, under the bay, the reactor held its shape because a man had become its architecture. Somewhere down there, eleven thousand people were lighting their evening lamps in a colony that belonged to them at last. She pressed her hand to the glass until the planet slid out of view.

Then she turned to the room behind her.

They had given Isandro a berth in the medbay—not as a patient, exactly; the medical staff had run every diagnostic twice and found him *functional, ambulatory, undamaged*, which was the cruelest diagnosis Amara had ever heard. He sat on the edge of the berth in the plain clothes they'd issued him, and he watched her with eyes that held no recognition and no absence either—just attention, patient and open, the attention of someone waiting to be told what was in the room.

"Hello," he said. He said it every time she came in. It was not a greeting so much as an experiment: *hello, and let's see what this time produces.*

"Hello, Isandro."

"Isandro." He turned the name over. "That's me."

"That's you."

"And you're—" He paused. He always paused here, and she had learned not to fill the silence, because the pause was where the choosing happened. "You're the one who reads to me."

"I'm the one who reads to you." She sat down beside him and took out the drive. "Where do you want to start?"

He thought about it. He had started thinking about it, in the three weeks since the rescue teams had carried him out of the reactor—thinking being, apparently, one of the things that had survived everything else. "The seawall," he said. "You played me the gulls yesterday. I liked the gulls."

So she played him the gulls. And then the ledger entries, sector by sector, his own clerk's voice in the recordings, dry and precise and secretly, unmistakably, in love with a dying colony's every rusted detail. And then—because it was late, and because she was tired, and because joy with a flat side is still joy—she played him the beach. Her own voice, age seven, asking the android who had come to retrieve her: *Are you still the same person the mission left behind?*

And his voice, after a long silence, in the recording: *I don't know. I don't know how to check.*

Isandro listened to it with his head tilted, the way he listened to everything now, and when it ended he was quiet for a while.

"Was that true?" he asked. "That you didn't know?"

"It was true then."

"And now?"

Amara looked at him—at the man who was not the man, who was not the machine either, who was sitting on the edge of a berth in a ship that had once made children of its crew, waiting to be told who he was. The drive was warm in her hand. The stars were coming out past the viewport. Somewhere ahead of the fleet was a green world, and somewhere behind them was a colony that would never be forgotten, because she would not permit it, because keeping the record was the work and the work was hers.

"Now," she said, "I think we get to find out. And this time, we choose."

He nodded slowly, and something moved behind his eyes—not memory, not yet, but the place where memory would grow, the cleared ground waiting for the seed. "Play it again," he said. "The beach. I want to hear what I said next."

She reached for the drive, and outside the viewport the fleet turned, all nine ships together, toward the dark and whatever waited in it—and Amara Osei, keeper of the record, pressed play.
