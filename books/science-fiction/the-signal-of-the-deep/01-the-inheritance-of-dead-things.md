## 1. The Inheritance of Dead Things

Hazel Eriksen woke to the sound of a woman dying.

It took her three full seconds to remember that the woman was herself — or had been, once, in a way that mattered to no one but the archivists who had catalogued the memory imprint and sealed it into the gel-cradle behind her skull. Three seconds of raw, animal terror, heart slamming against the wall of her chest, hands scrabbling at the crash webbing before the training kicked in and she lay still, breathing, counting the rivets in the ceiling of the transport *Kestrel* until her pulse agreed to be reasonable again.

Forty-one rivets. She'd counted them every sleep-cycle for six weeks. It was a ritual now, the first thing she did upon waking: count the rivets, name the ship, name herself. *Kestrel. Hazel Eriksen.* The second part still felt like a coat borrowed from a taller woman.

She unstrapped from the bunk and drifted through the zero-g cabin to the observation port. Outside, the stars were doing what stars do on the long haul between the Core and the Outer Reach — nothing, magnificently, for months at a time. Somewhere ahead, past the jump gate they would transit in nine days, hung Meridian Deep: an orbital colony shaped like a spindle and a promise, built thirty years ago when the shipping consortiums still bothered to dress their extraction operations in the language of frontier hope.

The last transmission from Meridian Deep played on loop in the common terminal. It always did. Hazel had asked the ship's AI to stop it twice; both times she had countermanded herself within an hour, because silence was worse. She keyed it on now, out of habit, out of something she refused to call longing.

*"—this is Eriksen, Meridian Deep station log, final entry. If anyone in the Core is receiving this—"* A laugh, dry as regolith dust. *"Of course you're not. You stopped listening eleven years ago."*

Her own voice. Not hers. The timbre was identical — grown from the same genome, tuned by the same vocal coaches in the same vats outside Reykjavik Orbital — but the woman speaking had lived forty-one years and Hazel had lived eight months of consciousness layered over an inheritance. When the original said *I*, it meant something Hazel could only quote.

*"Hydroponics bay three is down. Reactor output is at sixty percent and falling. We have two hundred and fourteen souls aboard, including nine children born on-station who have never seen open sky. I am recording this because someone should record it. That has always been my job here, and it appears it will be my job at the end."*

A pause in the transmission, four seconds long, filled with the hum of failing systems. Hazel knew what came next. She had heard it two hundred times. She braced anyway.

*"If you're hearing this, Core Archive, then the clone is awake. Hello, Hazel. I'm sorry about the mess I've left you. I'm sorry about everything."*

The transmission ended there. It always ended there. Eleven months ago, on Meridian Deep, someone had cut the original Hazel Eriksen's throat in the corridor outside her own quarters, and the station's security system had recorded eleven seconds of it before the cameras were wiped — eleven seconds that existed somewhere in the Core's evidence vaults, which Hazel had been forbidden to access, which she had accessed anyway on the third night after waking, alone in a review booth in Geneva Station with her hands shaking so badly she'd had to grip the armrests.

She remembered dying. That was the thing nobody warned you about, the thing the consent paperwork buried in clause ninety-four of a document written to be unread: the imprint wasn't curated. It ran right up to the end. She remembered the corridor lights strobing as she fell. She remembered thinking, with the strange editorial clarity of the nearly dead, *the archive will want this timestamp*. She remembered a boot entering the frame of her diminishing vision, standard consortium issue, and then nothing — nothing being the last thing the original ever experienced, and therefore the last thing Hazel had inherited.

Eight months old, and she already owned a death.

* * *

Breakfast in the *Kestrel*'s mess was a nutrient brick and reconstituted coffee, shared with a crew that had perfected the art of not looking at her. Captain Idowu nodded from the galley alcove. The two cargo handlers, brothers named Vasquez who had signed on for hazard pay and gotten a passenger instead, ate with the studied indifference of men who had decided she was bad luck and were waiting out the voyage like weather.

Hazel didn't blame them. Clone couriers made people nervous. Not because of what they were — everyone agreed, officially, that an imprinted clone was just a very thorough filing system with a heartbeat — but because of what they implied. That a person could be copied meant a person could be replaced. That a person could be replaced meant a person could be *dispensed with*, and the consortiums had learned long ago that the cheapest employee was the one whose death cost only a vat fee and a grief license.

"You slept loud," Captain Idowu said, not unkindly, refilling her coffee bulb without asking. "Talking."

"What did I say?"

"Names. Dates. Something about hydroponics." He shrugged his heavy shoulders. "You want the playback?"

"No." She'd reviewed enough of her own sleeping mouth to know it mostly recited cargo manifests and station regulations, the original's professional liturgy wearing through the thin fabric of Hazel's dreams. Sometimes it recited other things. Once, she'd woken mid-sentence to find herself saying *not the children, take the records instead* in a voice gone hoarse with urgency, and had sat in the dark for an hour afterward, unable to decide whether she'd dreamed it or inherited it.

Idowu lingered. He was a careful man, gray at the temples, with the patient competence of someone who had hauled freight through the Outer Reach since before the war made it fashionable to call it a war zone. "Nine days to the gate," he said. "You'll want to spend them rehearsing."

"I've spent six weeks rehearsing."

"Six weeks being her." He tapped his temple. "Rehearsing is different. Rehearsing is deciding which parts you keep."

Hazel looked at him. "That's not how it works. The imprint is the imprint. I'm the continuity solution, not an editor."

"Sure." Idowu smiled the smile of a man who had watched a hundred young people leave his ship certain of things. "Gate's in nine days. Decide by then."

He went back to his galley, and Hazel sat with her cooling coffee and the looped transmission whispering from the common terminal — she'd left it on low, a tide sound, the original's voice ebbing and flowing beneath the ship's ambient noise — and tried to understand what he meant.

* * *

The rehearsal, when she finally submitted to it, happened in the *Kestrel*'s cargo bay, because it was the only space large enough to hold the scale of the problem. Two kilometers of freight containers lashed in ranks, and Hazel walking the aisles between them with a hand-light, talking aloud to no one, performing a woman she had never met.

The method was simple and brutal. The Core Archive had given her a mandate: travel to Meridian Deep, assess the colony's condition, formally close the settlement, and compile the final record. The original had been Meridian's recorder-in-residence for nineteen years — its historian, its conscience, its voice to a Core that had stopped answering. Her death had created a gap in the record, and the Core's solution to gaps was always the same: fill them with copies. Hazel was not a daughter or an heir. She was a patch note.

But the mandate assumed the colony was empty. The last census, eleven months old, listed zero permanent residents. The transmission said two hundred and fourteen souls. Someone was lying, and Hazel had spent six weeks turning that contradiction over like a stone, examining what crawled underneath.

If the holdouts were real — if two hundred people had defied the evacuation order and stayed aboard a dying station — then the Core's closure protocol changed entirely. Forced removal. Legal proceedings. Or, if the consortium's shipping charter held the way she suspected it did, simply cutting the station loose and letting the reactor do the work that lawyers found distasteful. And the record — *her* record, the original's life's work — would become evidence in a way neither of them had planned for.

"Station log," she said to the empty cargo bay, practicing. Her voice bounced off the container walls and came back sounding smaller. "Hazel Eriksen, recorder-in-residence, Meridian Deep. Beginning formal assessment."

Wrong. Too crisp. The original had a looseness to her speech, a habit of starting sentences mid-thought as though the listener had been present for the whole conversation. Hazel had the recordings — hundreds of hours of the original's logs, her public addresses, one grainy newsfeed interview from the colony's founding decade where a younger Hazel-Eriksen-Original laughed at a reporter's question and said, *"Sky? Out here? No, we don't miss it. We brought better ones."*

Hazel stopped walking. In the dark between container rows, she closed her eyes and let the memory surface — not her memory, never her memory, but the imprint's, rising like silt in disturbed water.

*A market day on Meridian Deep, ring three. The original is thirty-four. Someone is selling actual oranges, smuggled up from a hydroponics co-op on Ceres, priced like small miracles. The original buys one and doesn't eat it for three days, carries it in her coat pocket, takes it out and looks at it. Finally she eats it standing at a viewport, watching a consortium hauler burn toward the jump gate, and the taste of it makes her cry, briefly, efficiently, the way people cry who have trained themselves to do it without witnesses.*

Hazel opened her eyes. Her face was wet.

That was the treachery of it. Grief arrived pre-installed. She mourned oranges she had never eaten, a sky she had never stood under, friends whose names she knew with the intimacy of a diary and the distance of an obituary. The imprint gave her everything except the living of it, and the living of it was apparently the only part that counted, because every time she reached for one of these inherited moments she found it smooth and complete and utterly uninhabited, like a shell left behind by something that had moved on or been pulled out.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and kept walking.

* * *

On the fourth day before the gate, she found the discrepancy.

She was in her cabin, cross-referencing the original's final station logs against the Core's official incident report — a task she'd assigned herself as due diligence and was performing as penance — when the timeline snagged. The incident report placed the murder at 0347 station time, corridor seven, outside the recorder's quarters. The original's final log entry was timestamped 0302, forty-five minutes earlier: a routine archival note, dictation-quality, the kind of thing recorded while walking.

Hazel played it again. And again. On the fifth pass she stopped listening to the words and started listening to the acoustics.

Corridor seven had a specific echo. She knew it from the imprint — knew the whole station's architecture the way she knew her own hands, every bulkhead and junction box rendered in perfect sensory detail by forty-one years of accumulated memory. Corridor seven was narrow, ribbed with conduit, and it rang. Every footstep, every word, came back doubled off the far bulkhead.

The final log entry had no echo at all. It had been recorded somewhere soft. Somewhere padded and quiet and private.

The original hadn't died walking home. She had recorded her last message from inside her quarters, and then — according to the official report — walked out into corridor seven to meet her death at the hands of persons unknown, in a crime the consortium's security contractors had investigated for exactly nine days before declaring unsolvable.

Hazel sat very still in the cabin's dim light while the *Kestrel*'s engines murmured their endless patience around her. On the common terminal, the looped transmission reached its ending and began again: *"—this is Eriksen, Meridian Deep station log, final entry—"*

"You lied to me," Hazel said quietly, to the dead woman, to herself, to the patch-note copy sitting in the dark with a murdered stranger's memories behind her eyes. "In your last words, you lied."

Or — the thought arrived cold and complete, uninvited — she hadn't lied. She had *edited*. The same way she had once, apparently, laughed at reporters and carried oranges in her pocket and cried without witnesses. The original had known her death was coming — had recorded a farewell addressed to a clone who wouldn't exist for another eleven months — and in her final hours she had chosen what to keep and what to bury, curating her own inheritance, leaving Hazel a house with locked rooms.

Hazel looked down at her hands. They were steady. She noted that with detached interest, the way the original might have, and understood that she had just used the dead woman's habits to survive the dead woman's secrets, and that this was going to keep happening, and that she was no longer sure where the borrowing ended.

Outside the viewport, the jump gate's distant beacon had begun to resolve — a single hard point of light that would swell, over the next four days, into the ring-shaped maw that swallowed ships and spat them out eleven light-years closer to everything she had inherited and nothing she had lived.

Nine days to Meridian Deep. Two hundred and fourteen souls who weren't supposed to exist. A death with the wrong acoustics. And somewhere in the flooded architecture of her own mind, rooms she had never entered, waiting.

Hazel keyed the terminal and, for the first time, turned the looped transmission off.

The silence was enormous. In it, she heard her own breathing — shallow, quick, alive — and beneath it, faint as a signal through deep water, the sense of the original's presence: not a ghost, nothing so dramatic. Just an inheritance. Dead things, passed hand to hand, waiting to find out what the living would make of them.

"Hello, Hazel," she said to the dark, using the dead woman's words, testing the weight of them in her own mouth.

Somewhere ahead, a colony that officially did not exist was waiting to tell her why.
