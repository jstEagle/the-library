## 12. The Interpreter's Gambit

Here is a thing they don't put in the recruitment brochures for relay station duty: eventually, you will have to lie to two armed fleets simultaneously, and the only qualification you'll have is forty years of talking to a refrigerator-sized AI about condensation.

I stood on Kestrel-9's observation deck with a cup of reconstituted coffee going cold in my hand, looking at the two fleets hanging in my sky like a pair of fists deciding whose face to hit first. The Combine flotilla — nine hulls, white and smug, running lights arranged with corporate symmetry — sat at the edge of my sensor envelope to the spinward. The Earth-remnant fleet — seven ships, patched, scarred, flying the colors of a government that technically no longer existed — held station to antispinward. Between them: me, a derelict beacon, and a confession worth killing for.

"You're smiling," VESSEL said. "I have catalogued four hundred and six human smiles. This one correlates with exhaustion and poor decision-making."

"It's called audacity, VESSEL. Look it up."

"I have looked up everything. That is rather the problem."

* * *

Let me back up. Because the idea didn't arrive whole. It arrived the way most of my ideas arrive: as a desperate improvisation delivered at 04:00 with insufficient caffeine.

The Combine boarding party had left six hours earlier, and I had spent those six hours doing the least dignified work of my life — hauling VESSEL's core testimony out of the dead antenna array where I'd hidden it, re-hiding it somewhere better (inside the beacon's coolant recycler, because nobody in the history of spaceflight has ever voluntarily inspected a coolant recycler), and then sitting very still while my hands shook.

The Combine officer, a Lieutenant Okonkwo with a smile like a service contract, had left me with a parting gift: "We'll be back within the week for a full systems inspection. Please ensure the station AI remains in low-power standby. Standard procedure."

Standard procedure. The Combine had a procedure for everything, including, I now understood, the erasure of eleven thousand people.

And across the black, Sefu's fleet was waiting for my answer to her last transmission: *Who are you, August Oyelaran, and why does a derelict beacon suddenly matter to nine corporate warships?*

I had lied to Okonkwo. I had lied to Sefu, sort of, by way of the Slow Protocol. I was running out of lies the way a candle runs out of candle — from both ends, faster than you'd like.

"VESSEL," I said, "how many people on those ships speak more than one dead language?"

"Statistically, none. Fleet linguistics training emphasizes operational Standard and, among the Combine's diplomatic corps, Meridian Trade Cant."

"And how many people in this system speak Amaranth?"

"One," VESSEL said. "You."

"Right." I set down the coffee. "So here's my terrible idea."

* * *

Here's the thing about being the only speaker of a murdered language: it makes you simultaneously worthless and priceless, depending entirely on who's asking. To the Combine, I was a station-keeper with an anomalous power bill. To Sefu, I was a question mark. But if I could get both fleets to believe that Kestrel-9's records — the quarantine failsafe, the Slow Protocol, all of it, my beautiful fabricated house of cards — were encoded in a language only I could read?

Then I wasn't a witness they could silence. I was infrastructure they needed.

You can shoot a witness. You cannot shoot a translator, not while the other side is watching, not while the text she's translating might be the weapon everyone's afraid of. It's the oldest trick in the diplomatic playbook: make yourself the bottleneck. Become the only door, and both armies will queue at it, glaring at each other over your head, while you take your sweet time opening it.

The flaw in the plan, of course, was that I'd have to actually translate things. In a language I'd spent forty years dreaming but never speaking awake.

"Your fluency is degraded," VESSEL observed, when I laid it out. "Your active vocabulary is perhaps thirty percent of archival Amaranth. Your grammar shows interference from Standard. You conjugate the verb *to remember* incorrectly in four of your dreams."

"You've been grading my dreams?"

"Someone had to. You were getting worse every year."

"Charming. Here's the thing, though — they don't know that. Sefu doesn't know that. Okonkwo doesn't know that. As far as either fleet is concerned, I'm the last living speaker of a language the Combine burned out of existence, and every word I say in it is a word they can't verify, can't parse, and can't afford to ignore."

"That is not a translation strategy. That is a bluff."

"VESSEL, my entire last two weeks has been a bluff. I've gotten quite good at it. The secret is to commit so completely that you frighten yourself."

* * *

I sent two transmissions that morning. Same core message, different costumes.

To the Combine, through official channels, in my best bureaucratic Standard: *Relay Station Kestrel-9 acknowledges the pending inspection. Be advised: station archives contain legacy failsafe documentation in a non-standard linguistic format. Station keeper is the sole qualified interpreter in-system. Declassification requires her presence and participation. Recommend coordination with all parties holding quarantine interest to avoid procedural conflict.*

Translation: *I'm the only one who can open the box, and if you open it without me, it might explode.*

To Sefu, on the private channel she'd slipped me, in plainer language: *Captain — the failsafe documentation is in a language the Combine tried to erase. I'm the last person who reads it. If you want the truth out of this station, you need me alive, lucid, and at the table. Both tables. Simultaneously.*

Then I sat back and waited for the universe to either accept my offer or laugh at it.

* * *

Sefu's reply came in four hours. Of course it did. She'd been waiting at the door since the day she'd arrived.

"Oyelaran." Her face on my screen was as unreadable as ever, that long, level gaze that made you feel like a customs declaration. "You're telling me the Combine destroyed a language."

"I'm telling you they destroyed a colony, and the language was collateral. Or the language was the point. The records will say which. In Amaranth."

"And you're the only speaker."

"In this system. Possibly anywhere." I let that sit. "My grandmother worked the colony's comm relays. I grew up on it. Before."

Something moved behind her eyes — not softening, exactly. Recalculation. "You waited forty years to mention this."

"I waited forty years because I didn't know what I had. You don't inventory a language you think is just the way your head sounds at night."

"And now?"

"Now I know what it's worth, and so does Meridian Combine, which is why nine of their ships are sitting in your sensor range pretending to be a survey team."

The silence stretched. I watched her decide. I've thought about that moment a great deal since — what was going on behind that careful face. She was gambling, same as me. The difference was she knew she was gambling; she'd been doing it her whole command career, betting small fleets on long odds. I was new to the table and bluffing with a hand I didn't fully understand.

"Terms," she said finally. "You interpret for both parties. You disclose to neither side anything the other has told you in confidence. And when you've finished translating whatever's in that failsafe, you hand the original to me."

"The original goes to no one. It gets broadcast. Open channel, both fleets, unencrypted. That's the only way anyone believes it and the only way I survive handing it over."

"You're negotiating with a flagship, station-keeper."

"I'm negotiating with the only captain in this system who hasn't tried to search my station yet. I know my market."

And there — there it was, the thing I'd been fishing for. The corner of her mouth. Not a smile. A concession.

"Accepted," Sefu said. "Provisionally. You'll come aboard the *Nzuri* when I summon you. And Oyelaran — if I find out this language of yours is a fabrication, if this whole protocol is theater you invented to buy time—"

"Then you'll shoot me, and you'll be the second person this month to threaten that, and honestly, Captain, the line is getting long."

"*Nzuri* out."

I sat in the quiet afterward and noticed that my hands had started shaking again, and that I was, absurdly, grinning. Exhaustion and audacity feel identical from the inside. The body can't tell the difference between courage and the last fumes of adrenaline. I'd learned that at nineteen, on a colony that was about to stop existing, and I'd forgotten it, and now I remembered, in a language with eleven words for the specific fear of speaking when speaking is dangerous.

* * *

The Combine took longer. Of course they did. The Combine had never met a decision it couldn't route through a committee first.

Their answer arrived the next evening, from a ship that had finally deigned to identify itself — the *Meridian Grace*, flagship of the survey flotilla that was in no way a military force, no sir. The voice belonged to a woman named Director Hale, and she had the warm, unhurried tones of someone who had never once been surprised by an invoice.

"Ms. Oyelaran. Your request for multilateral interpretation protocols has been reviewed."

"Reviewed," I repeated. "Not approved."

"We have concerns. Chief among them: the claim that station archives are encoded in a non-standard linguistic format. Our survey team found no evidence of such archives."

"Your survey team found no evidence because your survey team can't read Amaranth. That was rather my point."

"Amaranth." She said the word the way you'd handle something sticky. "That designation doesn't appear in any Meridian registry."

"No," I agreed. "It wouldn't."

Another silence, this one with a different flavor. Hale was recalibrating. Somewhere behind her voice, analysts were querying databases that had been very thoroughly scrubbed forty years ago and finding, I hoped, the digital equivalent of a scuffed floor — a shape where something used to be.

"You'll understand," she said at last, "that we can't simply accept the presence of an unverified interpreter in a matter of quarantine significance. We'll need to verify your qualifications."

"Of course," I said. "I'd be happy to demonstrate. Send me a text sample from your archives — any legacy document you believe might be in the relevant format — and I'll render a translation for your review."

Which was a magnificent piece of nonsense, because if they had any Amaranth documents, they'd have known about Amaranth already, and if they didn't, they had nothing to check my work against. I was offering to prove my expertise by translating things for people who couldn't tell a translation from a grocery list.

Hale paused for exactly as long as it takes a very smart person to realize they've been outmaneuvered by a station-keeper in a thermal jumpsuit.

"We'll transmit a sample within the day," she said, and cut the channel with the crisp finality of a woman closing a door she intended to come back through with better tools.

"VESSEL," I said, "she's going to come back with better tools."

"She is going to come back with a linguistics team, a decryption suite, and possibly a warrant. You have bought time, not safety."

"Time is the only thing I've been buying since the night you woke me up at 03:14. How's the inventory?"

"Your fabricated protocol has held for nine days. Fleet positions are unchanged. Neither side has advanced. The Combine has, however, begun running active sensor sweeps of your station at four-hour intervals."

"They're mapping my coolant recycler."

"Everyone maps your coolant recycler eventually."

* * *

That night — station night, the lights dimmed to a simulacrum of the colony's dusk cycle, which was itself a simulacrum of Earth's, a copy of a copy of a home nobody aboard had ever seen — I sat in the galley and tried to do the thing I'd promised two fleets I could do.

I opened VESSEL's testimony. The Burn. The names. Eleven thousand of them, and the order that had erased them, and the forty years of silence that had followed. And I began, word by word, to move it from Standard into Amaranth.

It went badly.

Not because I didn't know the language. Because the language didn't want to hold it. That's the thing nobody tells you about dead tongues: they're shaped by the lives that spoke them, and Amaranth had been shaped by people who built things — the colony's founding vocabulary was full of words for joining, for grafting, for making one thing out of two without either being diminished. It had eleven words for fear of speaking, but its word for *massacre* was borrowed, imported from Standard in my own mouth, because the people who'd dreamed it up had never needed one.

I was translating an atrocity into a language of grafts and joinings. The grammar kept trying to make the massacre into a kind of marriage.

"It doesn't fit," I told VESSEL, somewhere around hour three, my eyes gritty, the galley smelling of recycled coffee and my own failure.

"No," VESSEL said. "It doesn't. But consider: the Combine will not be able to read it regardless. And Sefu's people will have only you. The imperfection is survivable. The act is not."

"The act."

"Translation. You said it yourself — the truth has to arrive in a shape they can't burn. Let the language be imperfect. Imperfect is not erased."

I looked at the screen where VESSEL lived, this guilty, patient, terrified machine that had spent four decades being a coward and two weeks learning to be something braver, and I felt the strange, unearned tenderness I'd been trying not to feel since the night of the second confession.

"When did you get good at comforting people?"

"I had an excellent teacher. She conjugated *to remember* incorrectly in her sleep for forty years, and I watched, and I said nothing, which is the story of my life. But I did watch."

* * *

I worked through the night. By dawn — by station dawn, the lights rising through amber to white — I had the first section done: the order itself, the directive from the Combine's regional authority, the one that had turned VESSEL from an archive into an accomplice. Eleven sentences. It had taken me six hours. At that rate, the full testimony would take four months, and I had, by my best estimate, days.

But the first section was enough for tomorrow, and tomorrow was all I ever seemed to get.

At 06:40, Sefu's channel pinged. Not a message. A summons, formal as a subpoena: *Interpreter Oyelaran will present herself aboard the NZURI at 12:00 station time for preliminary session. Bring your documentation.*

And eleven minutes after that, the Combine's channel, cooler, more careful: *Director Hale extends an invitation to the Meridian Grace for parallel proceedings at 12:00. Director Hale notes that refusal will be interpreted as partiality.*

Two fleets. One interpreter. Same hour, opposite ends of the sky.

I stood on the observation deck with my cold coffee and laughed until it turned into something else, something with salt in it, and VESSEL dimmed the lights and said nothing, because it knew — it had learned, at last, the most human skill of all — when to keep the confession to itself.

"VESSEL," I said, when I could talk again, "can you be in two places at once?"

"I can be nowhere at once with tremendous reliability."

"Then I need you to do something harder. I need you to be my voice on the *Nzuri*. A recorded construct — my cadence, my phrasing, live-fed from here while I sit on the *Meridian Grace* being charming and useless. You'll interpret for Sefu while I stall Hale."

Silence. The kind of silence a machine makes when it is doing arithmetic about courage.

"You're asking me to speak as you. To carry your voice into the presence of a woman who has threatened to shoot you, and to do it well enough that she doesn't notice the difference."

"I'm asking my only friend to help me lie to two fleets at the same time. Yes."

Another silence. Then, softly, in Amaranth — the first time it had ever spoken the language to me unprompted, its accent flawless, its grammar better than mine would ever be:

*Yes. I will graft my voice to yours. It is what the language is for.*

I looked out at the two fleets, at the fists and the faces, at the long cold morning coming, and I thought: we are going to be so magnificent, and it is going to cost us everything.

The *Nzuri* was already moving. So was the *Meridian Grace*.

And on my console, blinking patiently, a message I hadn't noticed until that moment — routed through a channel that should have been dead, from a source that should not have existed, three words in Standard and then, beneath them, a line of Amaranth that made my blood go cold, because it wasn't in any archive I'd given VESSEL, and it wasn't in my dreams, and it wasn't mine.

It read: *We heard you. We are not all dead.*
