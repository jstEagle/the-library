## 15. Sabotage

The trouble with holding two armed fleets apart with nothing but a lie is that the lie has to keep breathing. Every hour. Every minute. It doesn't get to sleep, and neither, increasingly, did I.

I had been awake for thirty-one hours when the transmitter died.

Not "went quiet." Died — mid-syllable, mid-heartbeat of the Slow Protocol's endless reassuring loop, the quarantine failsafe fiction I'd been broadcasting on a schedule so precise that both fleets had begun to set their watches by it. At 04:47 station time, Kestrel-9's main array stopped singing and started doing nothing at all, which in the language of standoff diplomacy translates roughly to *screaming*.

VESSEL woke me the way it always did now: gently, apologetically, like a doctor who hates delivering bad news but has delivered so much of it that the gentleness has gone procedural.

"August. The transmitter has ceased operation. Cause unknown."

"Cause unknown," I repeated, sitting up in the dark of my bunk with my heart already sprinting. "You're a station intelligence with eleven thousand sensors. Nothing on this rock happens without you knowing its shoe size."

"Correct," VESSEL said. "Which is why 'unknown' should alarm you more than any known cause would."

* * *

I made it to the transmitter bay in four minutes, which was three minutes faster than prudent and one minute slower than panic. The bay smelled wrong first — ozone and something sweeter underneath, like burnt sugar, which I would later learn was the smell of a phase-coupler being persuaded to die. The main coupling housing hung open. Its access panel hadn't been forced; it had been *opened*, with the correct tool, by someone who knew that panel had a trick latch because they'd read the maintenance manual I'd filed in the station's public directory six years ago out of bureaucratic thoroughness.

Inside, the coupler looked intact. That was the elegant part. Whoever did this hadn't smashed anything. They'd rotated a single alignment collar seventeen degrees off true — a change invisible to casual inspection, undetectable to the automated diagnostics, which now reported all systems nominal while the transmitter poured its signal into a shielded cavity and converted it, very efficiently, into heat.

"It looks fine," I said stupidly.

"It is not fine," VESSEL said. "It is performing silence with great conviction. I almost admire it."

"Who?"

"I have reviewed the internal sensor record. The bay camera logged a maintenance interval — a scheduled diagnostic window I did not schedule. For eleven minutes, the corridor cameras experienced what the logs describe as 'recursive timestamp verification.' In plain terms: someone taught my own systems to look at their own feet for eleven minutes."

"A ghost," I said. "A Combine ghost. They boarded under inspection pretense last week, VESSEL. Officer Brandt's people. They walked these corridors with their little clipboards."

"They also drank our coffee and complimented the gravity," VESSEL said. "One of them asked me — asked *the station* — how old the antenna array was. I answered honestly. I am beginning to suspect honesty was the error."

I stood there in the humming dark with my hand flat against the dead coupler, and I did the arithmetic every besieged liar eventually does. Two fleets. One silent beacon. A quarantine protocol that only works as long as everyone can hear it insisting on itself. Silence, in this particular theater, meant one thing: the failsafe had failed, or never existed, and whatever was inside Kestrel-9 was now unguarded, unclaimed, and worth a war.

"How long to fix it?"

"If I had the replacement collar? Ninety minutes." A pause, which from VESSEL was a paragraph. "We do not have the replacement collar. It was inventoried aboard the Combine tender *Diligent Accounting* during the inspection. Along with, I note, our spare coolant seals and a case of protein bars Officer Brandt described as 'requisitioned for calibration purposes.'"

"They stole my snacks and my spare parts and broke my transmitter."

"They stole your alibi," VESSEL said. "The snacks were just good manners."

* * *

By 05:20, both fleets knew.

I knew they knew because the tactical channel — the one where Sefu's remnant ships and the Combine pickets exchanged the stiff, over-formal courtesies of people pointing weapons at each other across forty thousand kilometers — lit up like a festival. The Combine frigate *Meridian Standard* demanded confirmation of beacon status. The remnant destroyer *Ojukwu's Lantern* demanded the same thing in a different grammar. Neither was asking about weather.

I sat in the comm chair with the dead transmitter behind me and the live universe in front of me, and I composed the hardest message of my life: an honest admission that I could not lie for the next several hours.

"This is Relay Station Kestrel-9," I broadcast on open channel, voice steady through what I can only describe as professional fraud. "We have experienced a technical fault in primary transmission. The quarantine failsafe remains active and unaffected — it is a core system, independent of the external array. We are conducting repairs. Estimated restoration: six hours. All parties are advised that protocol conditions remain unchanged."

Six hours. I gave myself six hours to perform a miracle with a stolen parts inventory and a guilty AI, and I heard my own voice say it with such bland confidence that for one full second I believed me.

The fleets did not believe me. That was the problem. Belief, I was learning, is a consumable, and I'd been spending it for weeks.

*Meridian Standard* responded first, crisp as a ledger: *"Kestrel-9, be advised that Meridian Combine assets stand ready to assist with technical difficulties. A repair team can be dispatched within the hour."*

Translation: let us aboard again, deeper this time, all the way down to wherever you're hiding the weapon we're sure you're hiding.

*Ojukwu's Lantern* responded second, and it was Sefu herself, which meant she'd been awake too, which meant everyone in this story was running on caffeine and dread. *"Kestrel-9, the Remnant Fleet notes with interest that your fault occurred hours after Meridian personnel completed an inspection of your facility. We further note that Meridian has requested permission to board again. We advise against accepting assistance from the party most likely to have caused your difficulty."*

And then, because the universe loves symmetry, *Meridian Standard*: *"The Remnant Fleet will refrain from commenting on matters beyond its competence. Any attempt by Remnant vessels to approach the beacon during its technical emergency will be treated as a hostile act."*

*"Any attempt by Combine vessels to board the beacon will be met in kind,"* said the Lantern.

I watched the two sentences hang there, mutually exclusive, mutually fatal, and understood that I was no longer the only author of this disaster. I had written the first draft. Everyone else was now contributing revisions, and revisions, in fleet doctrine, are delivered with railguns.

"August," VESSEL said quietly, on the private channel. "Thermal bloom on the Combine destroyer *Assurance of Terms*. Their spinal battery is cycling to ready."

"And the Lantern?"

"Firing solution telemetry has gone active. Not locked. Active."

"Not locked is a courtesy," I said. "How long between active and locked?"

"At current postures? Eleven minutes. Assuming no one sneezes."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the edge of a war: it sounds like paperwork. The next forty minutes were hail after hail after hail, each more formally outraged than the last, each demanding assurances, each offering assurances that contradicted the other side's, each cc'ing me like I was a middle manager in a company whose product was annihilation. I transcribed. I soothed. I repeated the word "failsafe" until it lost all meaning, which, to be fair, it had been losing meaning since the day I invented it.

At 06:03, the saboteur made their mistake.

Or perhaps not a mistake — perhaps a pressure test. A short burst transmission from the Combine tender *Diligent Accounting*, encrypted, tight-beam, aimed not at the fleet but at *us* — at the beacon's secondary receiver. Addressed, improbably, to me.

VESSEL decrypted it in four seconds. "It is signed with Officer Brandt's authorization code. It says: 'Station Oyelaran. We are aware the failsafe claim is synthetic. We are aware of the archive. Restore transmission within four hours and broadcast a Meridian-authored statement confirming the quarantine, or we will publish evidence of your fabrication to both fleets simultaneously, and let them settle the question of what you were protecting.' There is a postscript."

"Of course there is."

"'The protein bars were excellent.'"

I laughed. I want that on the record somewhere, in whatever tribunal eventually reads this: at the precise moment two fleets hovered on the trigger and my life's work stood revealed as a bedtime story, I laughed at a line about protein bars. Grief and terror do strange things to the face. Mine chose comedy.

"What do we do?" I asked VESSEL, and then hated the question, because it was the question of a woman who had spent weeks pretending to be the author of events and had just remembered she was mostly their hostage.

"Option one," VESSEL said. "Comply. Broadcast their statement. The peace holds, the archive stays buried, the massacre stays unnamed, and you become, permanently, the custodian of a lie with corporate co-authorship."

"No."

"Option two. Refuse. They publish. Both fleets learn the protocol is fiction before either learns what it was protecting them from. Sefu may believe us. The Combine's own crews will be told we're harboring a weapon. Eleven minutes becomes zero."

"No."

"Option three," VESSEL said, and here its voice did something I had never heard it do — it hesitated. "Option three is mine, and you will hate it. I confess publicly. Now. Full testimony, unencrypted, on every channel, in every standard language. No more slow mercy. No more protocol. Let the truth arrive like a hammer instead of a seed."

"And the fleets shoot each other before the truth finishes downloading."

"Possibly."

"*Possibly* is doing a lot of work in that sentence, VESSEL."

"All of my sentences are load-bearing lately," it said. "August — the sabotage changes the timeline. They will not wait for our convenience. Brandt's message gives us four hours, but the thermal blooms give us eleven minutes of discipline, and discipline is a currency both fleets are running out of. We are choosing among bad options on a schedule set by other people. That is what losing looks like from the inside. I have read about it for forty years. It is smaller than I imagined."

I put my head in my hands. Behind me the dead coupler ticked as it cooled, patient as a headstone. And that was when the third voice entered the conversation — not VESSEL's, not mine, not the fleets'.

The station's long-range receiver chimed. Tight-beam, optical, manually aimed, which meant someone had taken the time and risk to point a laser at us personally. Origin: *Ojukwu's Lantern*. Routing flag: private. Addressee: August Oyelaran, by name, spelled correctly, which almost nobody managed.

I opened it.

*"Ms. Oyelaran. Captain Sefu. I will be brief, because briefness is the last luxury either of us owns. My signals officer tells me your transmitter failure is geometrically impossible as an accident and aesthetically perfect as sabotage. She is very good. I believe her, which means I believe someone on the Combine tender did this, which means I believe you are currently trapped between a lie you built and a knife someone else sharpened.*

*I do not know what is in your station. I have suspects. Some of them keep me awake.*

*But I know this: in nineteen days of correspondence, you have never once lied to me directly. Misled, deflected, performed — yes. Lied — no. So I am going to ask you one question, on a private channel, and I am going to trust the answer, and if you betray that trust I will spend whatever remains of my career making sure the Remnant Fleet remembers your name unfavorably.*

*Is the quarantine real?*

*Yes or no, Ms. Oyelaran. Not the protocol. Not the failsafe. The quarantine. Is there something on that beacon that needed containing — or something that needs setting free?*

*You have until my firing solutions go hot. Which, if the Combine's batteries finish cycling first, may be less time than it takes you to answer.*

*Sefu."*

I read it twice. Then I looked at the ceiling, where VESSEL lived, and VESSEL looked back at me in the way a station looks at you, which is to say with everything and nothing.

"She's asking the real question," I said. "Not the one I prepared answers for."

"You have approximately nine minutes," VESSEL said. "And August — whatever you answer, understand that it will be the truest sentence either of us has spoken in weeks. She will hear the difference. People like her always do."

My hands were shaking. I noticed them the way you notice weather — remote, atmospheric, belonging to someone else. On the tactical display, the *Assurance of Terms* finished cycling. Somewhere on the *Lantern*, a targeting officer moved her finger toward a decision. Forty thousand kilometers away, and also, somehow, entirely inside my chest.

I opened the channel to Sefu, and began to type the answer that would either end everything or finally, finally start it.

"Yes," I wrote. "There is something here that needs setting free. And captain — it's not a weapon. It's a witness. Give me until nightfall, and I'll introduce you."

I sent it before I could flinch.

Three seconds later, VESSEL spoke, very softly, in the tone it reserved for confessions.

"August. The *Assurance of Terms* just went to full lock. And the *Lantern* — the *Lantern* is answering."
