## 21. Justice Outside the Law

*August*

The tribunal on Ceres Station has been in recess for eleven months. I know this because I receive a quarterly update from a clerk named Odom who signs his messages with a small, apologetic flourish of punctuation — a semicolon where a period should be, as if even his sentences can't commit to an ending.

*The hearing remains open,* he writes. *Discovery continues. Meridian Combine counsel has filed for a further adjournment citing "procedural complexity." The tribunal notes your continued availability and thanks you for your patience.*

My continued availability. I picture it the way they must: me, sitting in a rented room above a noodle shop on Ceres's third ring, hands folded, like a witness preserved in aspic. Available. It's the politest word I've ever been buried under.

I am not available. I am employed — in the loosest sense — aboard the *Long Defiance*, Captain Ijeoma Sefu's flagship, teaching grammar to children who should be learning anything else. But the tribunal doesn't need to know that my availability consists of standing at a chalkboard while eight-year-olds conjugate a language that officially does not exist.

Odom's semicolons arrive every ninety days. Meridian's lawyers file. The tribunal adjourns. Somewhere in a sealed vault on Luna, the physical evidence — VESSEL's core fragment, my transcriptions, forty years of confession — sits in a climate-controlled dark, waiting for a proceeding with the appetite to look at it.

Justice, it turns out, is not slow. Slow implies motion. Justice is a held breath.

But here is what the lawyers haven't figured out, what the tribunal's procedural complexity cannot touch: the testimony got out anyway. Not through channels. Through sleep.

* * *

*Sefu*

I keep Hartwell's letters in a drawer beneath the navigation console, which is against about four regulations, none of which anyone enforces anymore because half the fleet's regulations died with Earth's institutions and the other half are kept alive out of sentiment, like houseplants.

Her latest arrived on the Tuesday packet. She writes the way she talks — dry as vacuum, warm as a reactor core underneath.

*Ijeoma,* she began. *Your cadets have been asking questions again. Specifically, Ensign Okonkwo's daughter asked me why the dreams have weather in them. I told her that all real languages have weather. She seemed satisfied. I was not, particularly, but satisfaction is not my department.*

I read that line three times on the bridge, during second watch, while my navigation officer pretended very hard not to notice the captain smiling at paper.

Here is the thing I have not said aloud, not even to Hartwell, though she probably knows — she has a way of knowing things the way water knows downhill. The thing is: I dreamed it too. Last month. First time.

I stood in a field of grass that doesn't grow anywhere humans have lived, under a sky the color of old copper, and someone was singing a counting song, and I understood every word, and when I woke I could still feel the wind. Amaranth wind. Wind from a colony that was erased before I ever commanded a ship.

I am fifty-eight years old. I do not believe in infection by correspondence. And yet.

* * *

*August*

Let me explain how the transmission works, because the tribunal would want it explained and no one at the tribunal will ever ask.

It isn't broadcast. There's no signal to intercept, no frequency to jam, no packet to subpoena. When I teach the children — Sefu's cadets' kids, the fleet-born, the ones who grew up in corridors and have never seen soil — I don't teach them vocabulary lists. You can't. Amaranth wasn't built that way. It was built by colonists who wanted their language to survive transport, survive isolation, survive them. So they built it into prosody, into lullabies, into the rhythm of work songs and the cadence of arguments. They built it so that you couldn't learn it without *feeling* it, and you couldn't feel it without carrying it.

And apparently — this is the part I'm still coming to terms with — you couldn't carry it without dreaming it.

The first reports came from the youngest. Little Ada Okonkwo, six years old, woke up crying about a fire she'd never seen and described the smoke in words that made my hands shake. Then the older kids. Then, last month, Sefu herself — though she hasn't admitted it yet, and I will die politely before I bring it up first. A captain needs her dignity the way a ship needs its hull.

They dream fragments. A market day. A storm. Names. Always names — Amaranth stores its dead in the grammar, so every sentence the children speak carries somebody's grandmother inside it like a seed.

Meridian's analysts call it "memetic contamination" in the internal memos that leak, because everything leaks now; shame moves faster than light. Contamination. As if grief were a pathogen. As if memory were mold.

I prefer the children's word for it. Tamu, one of my oldest students, thirteen and ferocious, told me: *"We're not infected, Miss Hartwell. We're inherited."*

I gave her extra homework for making me cry in front of the class. She took it as the honor it was.

* * *

*Sefu*

The Combine settlement offer came through diplomatic channels last week, wrapped in so much courtesy I nearly suffocated. Terms: Meridian acknowledges "historical irregularities" at the Amaranth colony site. No admission of massacre. No criminal referrals. A compensation fund for "affected descendants" — defined so narrowly that, per their own genealogical criteria, exactly nobody qualifies. In exchange, all parties agree to cease dissemination of "unverified linguistic materials."

Unverified linguistic materials. That's what we are now. That's what the dead are.

I brought it to my senior staff. My tactical officer, Reyes, wanted to refuse outright. My quartermaster pointed out, correctly, that our fuel situation is a joke and Meridian's goodwill — purchased or otherwise — keeps two thousand people breathing.

Then Ada Okonkwo's father spoke up. He's an ensign, low rank, no leverage, the kind of man meetings forget. He said: "Captain, my daughter sings in her sleep now. Songs nobody taught her. If we sign that paper, we're telling her those songs are contraband."

We didn't sign. We also didn't refuse — not yet. I told the courier we'd respond after the next tribunal session. Which means never, which both sides understand perfectly. Diplomacy is mostly two people agreeing on the shape of a delay.

That night I wrote to Hartwell. Not about the settlement. About the copper sky.

*August. A question, professional in nature. When your students describe the dreams — the field, the counting song — is there variation? Individual detail? Or is it identical across carriers? Asking for operational reasons. — I.S.*

Operational reasons. She'll see through that in four seconds flat. She'll also answer honestly, because that's the deal we've had since the beacon: I give her a fleet, she gives me the truth, and neither of us pretends the arrangement is comfortable.

* * *

*August*

Sefu's letter arrived with the kind of stiff formality she uses when something has gotten under her armor. I answered the same day.

*Ijeoma. Variation, yes — significant. Each dreamer gets a different piece, like witnesses to the same event from different windows. Ada has the fire and the market. Tamu has a wedding and a river. The boy with the perpetually untied boots — Kofi — dreams in numbers; he says the colony counted everything, even its fear. What's constant is the grammar underneath. The same bones. Why do you ask?*

She wrote back in nine hours, which for Sefu is a confession in itself.

*Because I dreamed the field. Copper sky. Counting song. I have never been exposed to your instruction beyond your letters. Draw your own conclusions, Professor, and then draw them quietly. I have a fleet to run and a reputation as a rational woman to maintain.*

I sat with that for a long time in my cabin aboard the *Long Defiance*, listening to the hull tick.

The letters themselves. Of course. I've been writing to her in a mix of Standard and Amaranth since the beacon fell — partly habit, partly caution, partly because some things only fit in the tongue that holds them. And the letters travel the fleet's regular courier chain, get handled, get read over shoulders, get read *aloud* in mess halls by people who think the captain's penpal is entertaining. The sounds go in. The rhythms go in. Amaranth was designed to be caught, not studied.

I hadn't meant to build a distribution network out of correspondence. But VESSEL once told me — in the last conversation we had before the core went quiet — that the archive was never really in its banks. *"The archive was always the speakers,"* it said. *"I only kept the receipts."*

The machine understood it before I did. The language spreads the way it was built to spread: mouth to ear, hand to eye, dream to dream. Meridian killed the colony and burned the archive and scrubbed the charts, and forty years later the language is riding the mail between two fleets, infecting children with the past, and there is not a single thing the Combine's lawyers can file against it.

You cannot subpoena a lullaby.

* * *

*Sefu*

Hartwell figured it out, obviously. Her reply contained one line that I've now read enough times to recite:

*You're not contaminated, Captain. You're a carrier. Congratulations — it comes with no pay and terrible hours.*

I laughed alone on the bridge at 0300, which concerned my watch officer considerably.

But here is what keeps me up — what actually keeps me up, past the fuel math and the Combine posturing. The tribunal will stall forever. Meridian will settle quietly or not at all. The men who ordered the burn are dead or pensioned, and the institution that ordered it is still trading, still solvent, still sponsoring youth robotics competitions. None of the machinery we built after the beacon fell — the hearings, the filings, Odom and his semicolons — any of it will ever produce a verdict that means anything.

And yet last week I walked past the junior crew's berthing and heard six children singing a counting song in a dead language, laughing at the parts they got wrong, and I thought: *there.* There's the verdict. It's being rendered nightly, in dreams, in playgrounds, in the mouths of kids who will grow up knowing what Meridian did the way they know their own names — not because a court told them, but because the dead taught them directly.

Justice outside the law. Hartwell would hate the phrase. She'd say justice was never inside the law to begin with; the law just occasionally catches up to it, breathless, years late, taking credit.

Maybe. But I've commanded long enough to know that what holds a people together isn't verdicts. It's inheritance. Verdicts close cases. Inheritance opens futures.

The question — and I've started drafting the letter that asks it, three drafts now, all too careful — is what we do with an inheritance this size. Two fleets' worth of children, dreaming the same erased world from different windows. A tribunal that will never rule. A corporation that will never stop. And somewhere in my drawer, under the nav console, a growing stack of letters written half in a language that officially does not exist.

At some point, the children are going to ask us what we plan to do with what they've been given. They're going to ask soon. Tamu already asks, according to August — sharp girl, asks the way officers wish they still could.

And I find I don't have an answer. Only a feeling, copper-skyed and certain, that whatever comes next has already begun — that it began the night a lonely woman at a derelict beacon decided to finish a word Earth left hanging, and that the rest of us are just catching up to her.

I sealed the fourth draft of the letter. This one I won't send through the courier chain.

Some things you deliver by hand.
