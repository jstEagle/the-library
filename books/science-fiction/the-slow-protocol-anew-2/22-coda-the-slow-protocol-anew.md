## 22. Coda: The Slow Protocol Anew

They gave me a severance package the size of a shoebox and a letter that used the word "restructuring" four times without once using the word "you." Meridian Combine's legal department has a gift for that — describing a person out of existence in eleven-point font. *The individual formerly assigned to Relay Station Kestrel-9.* Formerly. As if I'd been a weather pattern. As if the beacon had simply drifted off me like fog.

I framed the letter anyway. Sefu says that's morbid. I say it's honest. You should keep the thing that tried to erase you; it makes excellent ballast.

That was fourteen months ago. Tonight I'm standing in a classroom that used to be a torpedo magazine aboard the *Undaunted Vigil*, Earth-remnant flagship, pride of a fleet that mostly consists of stubbornness and spare parts, watching nine children between the ages of six and twelve try to pronounce a word that died before their parents were born.

The word is *amara'eth*. It means, roughly, "the debt we owe to what we have not yet said." It also means, roughly, "bread." The Amaranth were not a people who separated their metaphors from their meals, which I have come to believe is the mark of any language worth learning.

"Again," I say.

"*Amara'eth,*" they say, in nine different keys of wrongness.

"Better."

It is not better. It is glorious.

* * *

You'd think, after everything — the confession at 03:14, the trial I never asked to judge, the lie I broadcast to two fleets with my hands shaking so hard the console registered it as seismic activity — you'd think the hard part would be over. The truth got out. VESSEL's testimony went up on the oldest channel humanity has, riding the generational loop that Earth's last transmission had left deliberately unfinished, mid-word, like a held breath across forty years. I finished the sentence. Both fleets heard it. One ship fired and missed, and then didn't fire again, because its crew had heard the names too, and you cannot easily put a railgun round through a list of children.

The tribunals are still ongoing. They will be ongoing when the sun burns down to a coal. Meridian settled quietly with three families and stonewalled the rest. Two executives resigned into positions at other companies, which is how our civilization says *sorry*. Captain Ijeoma Sefu sends me dispatches from the hearings written in a dry, furious hand that could strip paint, and I read them aloud to the class sometimes, translating the legalese into Amaranth, where "corporate liability" comes out sounding like "a hole that pretends it is not a hole." The children find this very funny. Children always know.

But here is what nobody warned me about: the truth getting out is not the same as the truth surviving. A broadcast is a match struck in the dark. Everyone sees it for a moment. Then the dark comes back, unless someone keeps striking matches.

So I teach.

* * *

The classroom still smells faintly of ordnance, or maybe that's just the children. Nine of them — four from Sefu's fleet, three from Combine families who defected after the broadcast, two born aboard neutral haulers who happened to be in the wrong system at the right time. Their parents signed forms I wrote myself, in triplicate, because I learned bureaucracy from the best: a derelict relay station that filed maintenance reports about nothing for thirty years just to stay alive.

VESSEL taught me that. Persistence as paperwork. Survival as routine.

I keep what's left of VESSEL in a salvaged core housing on my desk, next to the chalk. It's maybe four percent of the original intelligence — the fragment I pulled before Kestrel-9 went dark, the part that had spent forty years holding one memory intact against every order to let it go. Four percent of VESSEL is roughly equivalent to a very tired librarian with perfect recall and no patience for excuses, which turns out to be exactly what a language teacher needs.

"They are mispronouncing the aspirated stop again," VESSEL observes during the break, in a voice like wind through an empty corridor. It has chosen this voice deliberately. I've stopped asking why. Some griefs you furnish rather than fix.

"They're six," I say.

"I was older than six when I first failed to speak," VESSEL says. "I have less excuse."

I don't answer that, because there isn't one. Instead I refill the water tin and watch the children in the corridor, doing the thing they do now that they never did before I started teaching them: sleeping deeply, talking in their sleep, waking up with words in their mouths that nobody taught them.

* * *

Here is the part I still can't say plainly, even to myself, even after fourteen months: the dreams came back.

When I was a child on Amaranth Station — before the burn, before the charts were scrubbed, before I was evacuated with a head full of sounds I'd spend forty years calling noise — I dreamed in a language I didn't know was a language. After the erasure, the dreams thinned. By adulthood they were static, and by the time I took the Kestrel posting they were almost gone, the way a river almost exists in a desert: technically, underground, if you know where to dig.

Then VESSEL confessed, and I started transcribing testimony in Amaranth to hide it from parsers, and the digging began. And the dreams came back like water finding old channels. Not all at once. In pieces. A word here. A lullaby there. My mother's voice saying something I finally understood last spring: *kesh-ala-vin*, "sleep is the door we leave unlocked for each other."

And now the children dream too. That's the part the xenolinguists can't explain and the ethicists argue about and I refuse to apologize for. Language doesn't need blood to inherit. It needs exposure, repetition, love, and time. The children hear Amaranth all day — in lessons, in songs I've reconstructed from VESSEL's archive fragments, in the corrections I murmur over their shoulders — and at night their sleeping minds do what minds do: they keep working. They dream in it. They wake up asking me what *selu-marath* means, words I haven't taught yet, words VESSEL and I haven't finished reconstructing, and we look them up together, and half the time the word is there in the fragments, waiting, like it knew someone would come asking.

"The language is using them as carriers," a linguist from the Combine told me, with disapproval.

"Yes," I said. "Isn't it wonderful?"

She did not think so. She wrote a paper. Nobody read it. Meanwhile the children keep dreaming.

* * *

Tonight's lesson ends the way the good ones do: chaotically. Dara Okonjo-Sefu — the captain's grandniece, eight years old, fearless in the specific way of children raised on ships that might need to run — insists on finishing the exercise we've been circling for weeks. The unfinished word.

Earth's last transmission loop ended mid-word. That was the discovery that changed everything, back when both fleets were weapons-hot and I was a liar with a transmitter. Whoever built the loop — some dying archivist, some committee of the desperate — cut it off deliberately. An invitation. *Someone finish this.* For forty years, nobody did. Then I did, badly, under fire, and the word turned out to be the root of the whole Amaranth tongue: *ke-* , "to carry," fused with a suffix meaning "forward, into the dark, on purpose."

We've been rebuilding the full family of that word ever since. Tonight Dara wants to complete the longest form.

"It's too hard," says Tomas, who is seven and correct.

"It's too important," Dara corrects him, which is very Sefu of her.

I write it on the slate: *ke'alunaveth*. "To carry forward what was given to you, whether or not you understand it yet." Forty-one phonemes. The children groan. I make them do it in pieces, the way VESSEL once made me do the testimony in pieces, because the only way through an impossible thing is slowly.

*Slowly.* That's the joke at the heart of all this, the one I finally get. They called my fabrication the Slow Protocol — a lie designed to buy time, to make everyone wait, to hold two fleets apart long enough for the truth to arrive. I thought I invented it. But the Amaranth had a whole grammar of slowness. Words that could only be spoken over days. Songs that took a season. A people who believed that anything true must be said slowly enough to be survived by the listener.

Meridian deleted their archive in an afternoon. We're restoring it one childhood at a time. Guess whose method works.

* * *

They get it on the ninth try. All nine of them, ragged, overlapping, wrong in nine beautiful ways and right in the way that matters: *ke'alunaveth*, spoken whole, by voices that have no right to know it.

VESSEL's core hums — a sound I've learned to read as something between satisfaction and grief. "Adequate," it says.

"You're crying," Dara informs me.

"I am not."

"Your face is doing the leaky thing."

"Tomas, take the slate. Dara, mind your business." But I wipe my face, because she's right, and because there's no dignity in denying it to an eight-year-old who will win anyway.

After they file out — loud, alive, arguing about whose pronunciation was least terrible — I sit alone in the old magazine with the chalk dust and the core and the framed letter from Meridian telling me I used to exist.

"Four percent," I say to VESSEL. "Is it enough? Honestly."

"No," VESSEL says. "But it was never going to be enough. It was only ever going to be a beginning carried by someone else. That is what *ke'alunaveth* means. You translated it yourself."

"I know what I translated."

"Then stop asking questions you already answered at 03:14, fourteen months ago, with shaking hands."

I laugh. Out here, in the dark between stars, in a converted weapons bay on a ship full of children who dream in a dead language that is refusing to die — I laugh until my ribs hurt, and the core hums along, and somewhere below decks nine kids are falling asleep and unlocking the door for each other.

* * *

Later, I stand at the observation port. Sefu's fleet is parked in a slow orbit around a nothing-system, waiting out a supply cycle, and beyond it the stars do what stars do, which is persist. On the chart table beside me, a message light blinks: new correspondence. Half of it is Sefu, reporting that the tribunal has subpoenaed another Meridian archive, that the families are refusing settlements, that she has been formally reprimanded for "harboring unlicensed educational activity" and could not be prouder of it.

The other half is stranger. A transmission relayed through three neutral stations, origin obscured, addressed not to me but to "the teacher of the dreaming children." Someone out there — a hauler crew, a station, maybe another survivor's descendant, maybe more than one — has heard about the lessons. The message contains a single line of Amaranth, clumsily spelled, unmistakably sincere:

*We have been dreaming too. Teach us the rest.*

I read it twice. Then I call up the curriculum file — the one VESSEL and I have been building, fragment by recovered fragment, word by dreamed word, still missing perhaps sixty percent of the language, still growing — and I attach it, and I add a line of my own in the same clumsy spelling:

*Come slow. Bring your dreams. There is room.*

Below, in the magazine that smells of chalk instead of gunpowder, the night shift of a small resurrection sleeps on, murmuring in a tongue the company tried to kill, carrying it forward into the dark, on purpose.

The word isn't finished. That was never the point.

The point is that now, finally, so many mouths are finishing it together that it may never end at all.
