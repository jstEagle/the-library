## 7. The Colony Remembers Differently

The adults had stopped sleeping, and that was the strangest part. Not the dreams themselves — Ilsa had made her peace with the door of light weeks ago, the way you make peace with weather — but the way the grown-ups moved through the days afterward, gray-faced and careful, as if the whole settlement were walking on a frozen lake and listening for cracks.

She noticed it first in the mess hall. Her mother, Devi Renner, who managed the water reclamation plant and had once talked for an entire dinner about membrane filtration without pausing for breath, now sat with her hands wrapped around a cooling cup of tea and said almost nothing. Her eyes kept drifting to the ceiling, as if the answer to something might come through the insulation. Around her, the other parents did the same. Forty-one children had woken at 3:14 in the morning singing the same hum, and the settlement had responded the way it responded to everything: with committees, with airlock-sealed meetings, with a notice on the community board requesting that residents report any *further anomalous auditory experiences* to the council office between the hours of nine and four.

Anomalous auditory experiences. Ilsa had copied the phrase into her notebook because it was so perfectly useless. Nobody had to report anything. The children all knew. The dream came back every night, patient as tide, and every morning they compared notes at school in low voices while the teachers pretended not to hear and the teachers' own faces said they hadn't slept either.

"It's the relay station," said Tomas Okonkwo at lunch, three days after the council notice went up. He was eleven and had a gap in his teeth and a way of stating things as though he'd personally verified them. "My dad says the beacon's been sending out some frequency for years and it's finally scrambled everyone's heads. He wants to shut it down."

"That's stupid," said Priya Chandrasekaran. "It's a relay. It just — repeats things."

"Repeats *what*?" Tomas said, and nobody had an answer, and the silence that followed had a shape to it, like the shape of the door in the dream.

Ilsa didn't say what she knew. She had drawn the antenna array — the whole array, every strut and feedhorn, down to the mounting collar of the secondary dish she'd never seen in any schematic available on the colony network — and she'd mailed it to the relay station because the dream had told her to, in the way dreams tell you things: not in words, but in the certainty that remains when you wake, warm and heavy in the chest, like a stone you've been asked to carry. She hadn't told her mother about the drawing. Some stones you carry alone until you understand their weight.

* * *

The nightmares started with Dev Chandrasekaran, Priya's older brother, on the ninth night.

He was fourteen and had resisted the dream at first — announced at breakfast that he'd simply *not slept*, as if sleep were a civic duty he could decline. Then the dream found the crack in him, the way water finds the crack in anything, and it stopped being a door of light and became something else. He screamed the settlement awake at 2:51 a.m. — twenty-three minutes before the others — and when his parents got to his room he was standing on his bed with his back against the wall, pointing at the corner where the ceiling met, saying *it's under us, it's under the ground, it's been counting*.

After that he wouldn't sleep in his room. After that he wouldn't sleep at all if he could help it, and the medics gave him things that made his eyes swim, and he started saying the number. He said it at school, in the corridor, mid-sentence, the way other people coughed: *nineteen*. Then, a few days later, *sixteen*. Then *twelve*. Always quieter, always with the flat certainty of someone reading off an instrument panel.

The council called an assembly. Ilsa sat in the third row of the community hall between her mother and the Chandrasekarans, and watched First Speaker Alemu stand at the podium with the settlement's founding charter displayed on the screen behind him — the old scanned pages, the careful handwriting of the crossing generation — and try to fold the nightmare back into history.

"Our ancestors crossed nine light-years in the dark," Alemu said. "They endured things we can't imagine. Sometimes memory surfaces in strange ways. The children are not in danger. The children are *remembering*."

"But remembering what?" someone called out, and it was Dev's father, standing up in the back with his voice cracking, and the hall went so quiet Ilsa could hear the ventilation fans. "Speaker, with respect — my son is counting down to something and no one in this room will tell me what he's counting down *to*. You've all seen the archive requests come back sealed. Every one of them. The crossing logs, the beacon commissioning, the first-decade transmissions — sealed, sealed, sealed. You're asking us to calm down while the past sits behind a locked door, and something on the other side of that door is humming to our children."

Alemu's jaw worked. "The seals exist for reasons we—"

"*We* don't know," Dev's father finished. "That's the point. *We* don't know."

Ilsa's mother put a hand on her knee under the seat, a small pressure that said *sit still, don't react*, and Ilsa sat still and didn't react, and inside her the stone in her chest grew heavier. Because she knew something the adults didn't, something she'd never told anyone: the dream wasn't frightening. Even the version that had gotten into Dev and curdled — even that was just the same dream, seen through a crack. Under the dead space there was a door of light, and behind the door something was humming, and the humming wasn't a threat. The humming was a *song*. She was sure of it the way you're sure of your own name. Something under dead space was singing, and it had been singing for a very long time, and it was singing *to* them.

The question that kept her awake now wasn't *what is it*. The question was *why does it know our song*.

* * *

She found out on a Tuesday, in her grandmother's kitchen.

Amma Suri lived at the edge of the settlement in a house that smelled of dried herbs and solder flux, because Amma Suri had been the crossing fleet's junior communications tech before she was anyone's grandmother, before her hands had thickened and slowed. She was the oldest person on the colony now. The council consulted her about water rights and boundary disputes and she answered in a voice like dry paper, and everyone assumed the past was something she kept in a box in the back room with the rest of the family relics.

Ilsa had come to deliver her ration supplements and stayed because the rain had come in sideways off the plain, the kind of rain that turned the paths to clay. She was sitting at the kitchen table doing her schoolwork — orbital mechanics, of all things, the unit on transfer windows — while Amma Suri shelled beans, and the rain made its noise against the roof, and without thinking, without any decision at all, Ilsa started to hum.

The dream-tune. Five notes, then a pause, then five notes again, the way it came in the dream — slow, patient, a signal rather than a melody. She was on the third repetition before she noticed Amma Suri had stopped shelling.

She looked up. Her grandmother had gone pale.

Not startled-pale. Not *you startled me, child* pale. This was the pale of a person who has heard a voice from a locked room. The beans sat forgotten in her lap. Her hands, those thick slow hands that Ilsa had never once seen tremble, were trembling.

"Amma?"

"Where did you hear that." It wasn't a question. It came out flat and fast, the way her grandmother spoke only when something was truly wrong — when the pump had failed, when the storm sirens went.

"Hear what? It's just—"

"*Where did you hear that song.*"

Ilsa's mouth had gone dry. "In a dream," she said. "Everyone's dreaming it. Forty-one of us. It comes with the door of light, under dead space, it—"

"Under dead space," Amma Suri repeated. She said it the way you'd repeat a diagnosis. Then she set the beans aside, very carefully, aligning the bowl with the edge of the table as if the small precision could hold something larger in place, and she looked at Ilsa for a long moment — looked at her the way you look at someone you're measuring for something — and got up and went into the back room.

Ilsa heard boxes being moved. Heard the specific scrape of the metal trunk, the one that was never opened, the one she'd been told since she was small was *Amma's work things, leave it be*. When her grandmother came back she was holding a flat case of the old pre-launch manufacture, dull gray, its seals cracked with age, and inside it, when she laid it on the table and opened the latches, was a stack of data chips and a single sheet of paper — actual paper, fiber and ink, worth more than the table it lay on.

The paper had music on it. Handwritten notation, five lines, and above the staff, in a careful old-fashioned script: *For the crossing. So the children won't be afraid of the dark. — E.V.*

"Sing it," Amma Suri said. Her voice had gone quiet in a different way now. "The whole thing. Not the five notes. The whole song."

"I only know the five notes. That's all the dream—"

"I know," her grandmother said. "That's what frightens me. Sing what you have."

So Ilsa hummed the five notes, the pause, the five notes, and watched her grandmother's face while she did it, and Amma Suri closed her eyes and her lips moved slightly, filling in the rest — the melody continuing past where the dream cut it off, a rising phrase, a falling one, a resolution that landed somewhere warm — the whole shape of a lullaby that Ilsa's five notes were only the opening of, the first bar of, the *call* without the *answer*.

"That's the departure song," Amma Suri said, when she opened her eyes again. "We sang it in the shuttles. Every shift, every watch, for the whole crossing. Eleven years in the dark between the old system and this one, and we sang it so the children born aboard would think of the dark as a place someone was singing to them from, not a place they'd been abandoned in." She touched the paper with one finger, lightly, the way you touch a scar. "Elena Voss wrote it. She was our signal tech — the senior one. I was her apprentice. When we made orbit here and the colony charter was signed, she took a shuttle out to the relay station they'd built on the far side of the system, out past the shipping lanes, in the dead zone. To *commission the beacon*, the record says." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "That's all the record says."

Ilsa's heart was going hard against her ribs. "Amma. The dream comes from out there. From the relay. The five notes are the beginning of the departure song, and something out past dead space is singing them *at us*—"

"Or someone is," Amma Suri said. "Or something that learned it from someone. Or—" She stopped. She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes, and for a moment Ilsa's ancient, granite grandmother looked exactly her age, and then some. "Child, listen to me. There are things sealed in the colony archive that I helped seal. I was young and I was told they were dangerous, and I believed it, and now I am old and I cannot remember whether I believed it because it was true or because believing it was easier." She lowered her hands. Her eyes were wet and fierce. "Elena sent one transmission after she reached the station. One. It came through on the old band, degraded, half of it lost. What we could recover was this: *The beacon works. It was never only a beacon. I have found what we answered, and it has been waiting, and I am going to listen for as long as I last.*"

The rain filled the silence. Ilsa sat very still.

"What did we answer?" she asked. "The crossing generation. What did *we* answer?"

"I don't know." Her grandmother said it like a confession, like the last stone of a wall coming out. "That's the truth, Ilsa. Whatever Elena found out there, she took it with her, and the council sealed the rest, and the ones who knew are dead or have made themselves forget, and now something under dead space is singing our own lullaby to our children and I don't know if it's a welcome or a summons or a *debt*." She reached across the table and took Ilsa's hand, and her grip was still strong, still the grip that had held shuttle handrails in eleven years of night. "The boy who's counting. The number he says. Is it getting smaller?"

"Twelve," Ilsa said. "Last night he said twelve."

Amma Suri closed her eyes again. When she spoke, her voice had dropped to almost nothing, and it was the voice from the old recordings, the apprentice signal tech, the girl who had crossed the dark.

"Then whatever Elena Voss found out there has stopped waiting to be heard," she said, "and started waiting to be *answered*. And Ilsa — Ilsa, look at me — the dream chose you. I can see it chose you. So before the council does something stupid and permanent, before they shut that beacon down or send soldiers or seal one more thing — you find out what the song is *for*. You hear me? You find out, and you tell me, and whatever you decide to do about it—" her grip tightened, "—you will not carry it alone. That's the one thing I know for certain. Nobody carries this alone. Not anymore. Not again."

That night, Ilsa lay in her bed and let the dream come, and this time she paid attention to the edges of it — to the door of light, and the patience behind it, and the way the five notes hung in the dark like a hand held out. *The beacon works. It was never only a beacon.* She thought about the antenna array she had drawn, the component that didn't exist yet, the letter she had mailed into the dark to a woman named Casper Ashgrove whom she had never met and who, the dream assured her with terrible calm, was already asking the same questions.

The transfer-window unit in her schoolwork had given her the numbers that afternoon. Nineteen days until the corridor opened. Twenty-three until it closed.

Ilsa Renner was twelve years old, and she had made up her mind.
