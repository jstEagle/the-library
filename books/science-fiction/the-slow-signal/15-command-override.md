## 15. Command Override

The contact alarm woke Casper from a dream she couldn't remember, which was itself unusual — for nineteen days she had dreamed nothing but the lullaby, and waking to silence felt like surfacing from deep water into a room with the air let out of it.

She was still in the relay's shadow, Perihelion tucked against the structure's flank like a remora. The far relay filled half her forward viewport: a spire of grown material, pale as bone, its surface ridged in patterns that repeated at scales too large and too small to be accidental. It had been transmitting the lullaby on loop since before the colony launched, before Casper was born, before anyone thought to ask what a beacon was *for*.

The contact alarm meant a drive signature. In dead space, that meant one thing.

"Perihelion," she said to the cabin, because talking to herself had been a habit long before it was a necessity, "someone found us."

The ship didn't answer. Ships never did, no matter how many years you spent teaching them your voice.

She pulled up the tactical display with hands that were still stiff and scarred from the coolant repair — the burns had scabbed over into shiny pink ridges across both palms, and every keystroke cost her a small tax of pain she'd stopped noticing and started collecting anyway. The signature resolved slowly, resolving the way things did out here: reluctantly, in pieces, as if the dark had to be persuaded to give up information.

Colony hull profile. Military-grade drive plume running hot — they'd burned hard and fast, which meant they'd left in a hurry, which meant someone back home was frightened enough to spend fuel like water.

Casper sat very still for a long moment, watching the blip crawl across her display, and felt something complicated move through her chest. Relief, first — absurdly, stupidly, relief. Nine years alone had wired her to greet any human signal with joy before her brain caught up. Then dread, arriving late and heavy, like luggage set down behind her.

Because she knew what they'd come to do.

* * *

They hailed her eleven hours later, when their deceleration burn finally brought them inside hailing range. The comm board lit up with a colony authentication code — current, valid, official — and beneath it, a voice channel request flagged PRIORITY ONE, COUNCIL DIRECTIVE.

Casper looked at the flag for a while. She made coffee first. There was maybe a third of her ration tin left, and she decided that if this conversation went the way she suspected it would go, she wanted something warm in her hands. The recycler had been making the coffee taste faintly of coolant for days now, which should have been alarming and instead just tasted like honesty.

She opened the channel.

"This is Relay Station Vesper authority Casper Ashgrove, aboard the courier vessel Perihelion, currently in orbit around—" She paused. Around what? The charts called it nothing. The dream called it the door. "—around the source object. Identify yourself."

Static, then a voice. A woman's voice, low and controlled in the way voices get when they're holding themselves together with procedure.

"Casper Ashgrove. This is Captain Adaeze Okafor, colony rescue vessel *Steadfast*, operating under joint council mandate. I have orders regarding you and regarding that structure." A breath, carefully not audible, but Casper heard it anyway. "First: are you alive? Report your status."

"I'm alive," Casper said. "Ship's marginal. Life support functional. I have a passenger."

Silence. Longer than transmission lag accounted for.

"A passenger," Okafor repeated.

"Ilsa Renner. Age twelve. She stowed away." Casper heard how it sounded and didn't try to soften it. There was no softening it. "I couldn't turn back. The window math wouldn't allow it. I'll take whatever comes of that."

More silence. When Okafor spoke again, her voice had changed temperature — colder, but underneath the cold, something shaking. "You took a child into dead space."

"She took herself. I'm not going to pretend that makes it better. But I'm not going to lie to you about it either."

"No," Okafor said. "You won't have the chance. Ashgrove, listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once. Council Directive 9-Theta, ratified six days ago by emergency session: the anomaly structure is to be shut down permanently. All broadcast capability terminated. The jump window sealed by demolition charge at the corridor mouth. You are ordered to cease all activity aboard or near the structure, return to Steadfast, and surrender yourself and the child to my custody. Forty-one children on our world are dreaming of that thing every night, and the council has voted to make it stop."

Casper closed her eyes. Behind her eyelids, unhelpfully, she saw the relay's inner chamber: the cradle of grown material shaped almost exactly like a human body, waiting all these centuries for someone to lie down in it. She saw Ilsa standing in the airlock light, twelve years old and terrifyingly calm, saying *the dream told me where to hide.*

"The shutdown won't work," Casper said.

"It doesn't need your assessment—"

"Captain." She kept her voice level. She'd had nine years of practice talking to machines; talking to an angry, frightened, competent human was harder. "I've been inside it for three weeks. I've read its archives. I know why it exists and who built it and what was promised. If you plant demolition charges at the corridor mouth, one of two things happens. Either the charges fail, because that structure has survived longer than our species has had written language — or they succeed, and we break a promise our great-grandparents made, and we find out what the other side does when its invitation gets answered with explosives. Do you want to run that experiment?"

"I want to follow my orders," Okafor said. "That's what keeps forty-one children safe. Not archaeology. Not whatever revelation you think you've earned out there."

And there it was — the sentence Casper had known was coming, the one she'd been rehearsing answers to for eleven hours without settling on a single honest one. Because the truth was that the children weren't in danger. The truth was worse and stranger: the children were being *chosen*, and the choosing could be refused, but refusal came with a price tag nobody had read aloud in a hundred years. And the truth above all truths was that Casper had already decided what she intended to do, and every word between here and doing it was going to be a kind of theft.

"Captain," she said, "give me seventy-two hours."

"What?"

"Seventy-two hours before you commit to demolition. That's all I'm asking. Long enough to transmit the archives to you — the full record, unencrypted, everything the first generation wrote down and everyone forgot. Read it. Then decide. If after reading it you still want to put charges on that structure, I'll help you load them myself."

Another pause. Casper could picture her: a broad-shouldered woman in a command chair, jaw set, surrounded by officers who wanted her to be certain. She'd never met Adaeze Okafor. She knew the type intimately — the type was her, nine years ago, arriving at Vesper with a head full of protocol and a heart that hadn't yet learned what solitude does to certainty.

"You're asking me to delay a direct council order based on your word," Okafor said. "Your word, from a woman who abandoned her post, hijacked a colonial asset, and transported a minor through a hazard zone."

"Yes," Casper said. "That's exactly what I'm asking."

"And why would I?"

"Because you checked the dreams." Casper leaned toward the comm panel, as if closeness could carry meaning across the void. "You've talked to those kids, Captain. You've sat with them. You know they're not screaming. You know most of them wake up calm — some of them calmer than they've ever been. The nightmares are real, yes, I know about Tam Voss-Oduya and the others who got the bad version. But you also know, in the part of you that actually listens to children instead of filing reports about them, that whatever is on the other side of that door isn't hunting anybody. So before you blow the door off its hinges, you want to know what's behind it. Any captain worth her ship wants to know. That's why you'll wait."

The silence stretched so long Casper checked the channel integrity twice.

"Sixty hours," Okafor said finally. "Not seventy-two. And you will transmit those archives beginning immediately, in full, unencrypted, to Steadfast directly — not to the colony relay, not anywhere else. And Ashgrove — if I find evidence you've transmitted anything to the structure itself during those sixty hours, the delay ends and my orders resume. Am I understood?"

"Understood," Casper said, and lied to her.

* * *

She sat with the lie for a long time afterward, the way you sit with a stone in your shoe.

It wasn't even a large lie. Okafor had said: don't transmit anything to the structure. And Casper had already transmitted plenty — the renegotiation terms, the counter-offer, the whole architecture of a new bargain, sent up through the cradle's interface in the small hours of the previous watch while Ilsa slept. The relay had accepted provisionally. It was waiting, with its terrible geological patience, for the final answer: a voice, in the cradle, given freely.

But the lie lived in the tense. *During those sixty hours.* Casper had no intention of transmitting anything more to the relay in the next sixty hours. What she intended was to transmit everything to the *colony* — the truth, all of it, unencrypted, to every receiver on the world below — and she had no intention of telling Okafor that either. Not yet. Not until the archives were already falling like snow onto forty-one households and a council chamber full of people who had voted in ignorance.

Loyalty, she thought, turning the coffee tin in her burned hands. Everyone assumed loyalty meant obedience. Nobody warned you that sometimes it meant deceiving the people you served precisely because you served them — because the truth, delivered early, delivered through fear, would get the door blown shut forever, and the children would lose something they'd only just begun to receive.

Or maybe that was just what liars told themselves. She'd had nine years of talking to drones; she was out of practice at knowing the difference.

A knock at the cabin frame — Ilsa's knock, two fingers, always two fingers, a code they'd invented somewhere past the halfway point of dead space for *are you awake, is it safe to come out*. Casper waved her in.

Ilsa Renner had lost weight on the transit and gained something else, some density of presence that made the little cabin feel smaller when she entered it. She looked at Casper's face and then at the comm board, still warm, still logged with Okafor's call sign.

"They came," Ilsa said.

"They came."

"For the shutdown."

"For the shutdown." Casper exhaled. "Sixty hours. Then they decide."

Ilsa nodded slowly, absorbing it the way she absorbed everything — completely, without flinching, which was the most frightening thing about her. Then: "Did you tell her about the choice?"

"Not yet."

"Are you going to?"

Casper looked at her. Twelve years old, and asking the question straight, because that was what Ilsa did — she walked up to the thing everyone else was circling and put her hand on it.

"I'm going to tell everyone," Casper said. "That's the plan. Everything, to everybody, all at once. No council filter, no captain's summary. The first generation hid the truth and it curdled into a hundred years of nightmares. I'm not going to manage the truth. I'm going to hand it over whole and let people choke on it if they have to."

"That's not what the captain ordered."

"No," Casper agreed. "It isn't."

Ilsa considered this with the gravity of a small judge. "Good," she said finally. "The dream says the door closes either way soon. Sixty hours might be too slow."

"How soon?"

"Soon." Ilsa's voice, for the first time, wavered — just slightly, just enough to remind Casper that under all that calm was a child who had ridden a supply crate into the dark because she was afraid to carry something alone. "Casper. When you tell them. Tell them about the good parts too. Not just the terms and the history. Tell them what it sounds like in there. Tell them it sings."

* * *

The transmission took four hours to assemble and eleven minutes to send.

Casper did it from the engineering station, where the antenna gain was best, routing around every restriction Perihelion's systems offered her. She packaged the relay's archives — the first generation's crossing logs, the confession, the bargain, the terms, the succession clause — and appended her own commentary, recorded in one take, voice rough, hands aching on the console:

*"People of the colony. My name is Casper Ashgrove, and I am probably a traitor by the time you hear this. Here is everything. Decide for yourselves. And when you've finished being angry — and you will be angry, you should be angry — go sit with your children and ask them what the door looks like now. Ask them what it says. Then decide whether we close it, or answer it. We have very little time. I'm sorry for the way you're learning this. I'm not sorry that you're learning it."*

She sent it wide. Colony relay, council channels, public bands, the school network, the medical net — everywhere a receiver pointed at the sky. Unencrypted. Undeniable.

Then she sat back, and the cabin was very quiet, and she understood that she had just done the one thing that could not be undone. Somewhere below and behind her, forty-one parents were reading their great-grandparents' handwriting. Somewhere in a command chair, Adaezefor Okafor was receiving an alert that her sixty-hour window had just collapsed into something much shorter.

The comm board lit up within ninety seconds. PRIORITY ONE. COUNCIL DIRECTIVE. Okafor's code.

Casper looked at it. She thought about answering. She thought about explaining, one more time, to one more person, why the truth had to fall like weather instead of arriving like a warrant.

Instead she keyed the channel open, said, "Steadfast, stand by," and cut the line — buying herself another hour, maybe two, before a warship's patience ran out and a countdown began in earnest.

Behind her, through the viewport, the relay turned slowly against the stars, singing its hundred-year-old lullaby, waiting.

The window, Ilsa had said, was closing soon.

Casper stood up, flexed her burned hands, and went to find the girl.
