## 16. What Ilsa Chose

The far relay hung outside the viewport like a thing that had been dreaming for a very long time and had only just been woken. Its surface caught the light of no nearby star and gave it back slowly, as if considering each photon before returning it. Ilsa had been sitting at the observation blister for three hours, her knees pulled up to her chest, watching it turn.

Behind her, Casper Ashgrove was arguing with the ship's coolant telemetry again, muttering numbers under her breath the way other people muttered prayers. The burned skin on Casper's hands had been wrapped in gel dressings that morning, and she'd refused, with great dignity, to let Ilsa tie the knots for her.

Ilsa wasn't watching the relay anymore, really. She was working up her courage, which was harder than any of the math.

She had known this conversation was coming since the night she'd crawled out of the Perihelion's cargo locker and Casper had stared at her with an expression that was equal parts fury and something softer underneath. She had known it even before that — before the launch burn, before the shuttle, before she'd folded herself into the dark among the ration crates and listened to her own heart hammering out the seconds. She had known it in the dream, when the door of light opened and something inside it looked at her with infinite patience and asked its question.

*Will you carry it?*

She had said *not me.* And the door had waited. The door was still waiting.

"Casper." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. She tried again, louder. "Casper, can you stop counting for a minute? I have to tell you something, and if you're doing math while I tell you, I'll know, because your lips move."

The muttering stopped. Casper came around the bulkhead into the blister, moving carefully the way she always did now, favoring her bandaged hands like they were borrowed things. She lowered herself onto the bench across from Ilsa and waited. That was one of the things Ilsa liked best about her: she didn't fill silence just because silence was uncomfortable. She let it sit there until it told the truth.

"I didn't stow away because of an accident," Ilsa said. "Or not only. You think the dream pushed me, and I got scared, and I hid in your ship because hiding is what I do when I'm scared. But that's not what happened."

Casper's eyes narrowed slightly, but she said nothing.

"The dream offered me the choice first." Ilsa made herself hold the grey gaze. "Before you ever found the archives. Before you knew about the successor. Weeks before. Maybe months — time is strange in there. It showed me the relay. It showed me the cradle. It showed me what acceptance means — that whoever answers doesn't come back. And then it asked me if I would be the voice."

The recycler cycled. Somewhere aft, a pump ticked through its rhythm. Ilsa realized her hands were shaking and pressed them flat against her knees.

"And I said no," she went on, and her voice cracked on the word, and she hated that, and she kept going anyway, because stopping would be worse. "I'm twelve years old. I want to grow up. I want to learn how to fix things and see the ocean on Meridian and find out whether I like anyone enough to be embarrassed about it. I said no, and the dream didn't get angry. It just... waited. Like it has all the patience in the universe, because it does. And every night after that, it asked again. Not pushing. Just asking. The way Grandma asks if I've eaten."

"What did your grandmother say," Casper said quietly, "when you told her?"

"I never told her." Ilsa looked down. "That's the worst part. I told nobody. Not Mom, not Dad, not the council, not Doctor Achebe when she ran the sleep studies. Because I knew what they'd do. They'd put me in a room and study me until the window closed, and then they'd close it, and they'd all feel very responsible and very safe, and forty-one children would stop dreaming, and nobody would ever know what we gave up. They'd choose fear. Not because they're bad people. Because fear is easier to organize than hope. Grandma taught me that too, actually, though she didn't mean to."

Casper exhaled slowly through her nose. "So you came to find me."

"I came because you were already going." Ilsa lifted her chin. "You'd done the math. Nineteen days, twenty-three days. One-way. You were going to answer it yourself, and you hadn't told anyone either, so don't look at me like I'm the only coward on this ship. We were both sneaking toward the same door from opposite sides."

Something flickered across Casper's face — the guilt she carried like ballast, the weight of the drones she'd left instructions for, the beacon burning without her. She didn't deny it. Ilsa watched her decide not to.

"But here's the thing I have to tell you," Ilsa said, and now her voice steadied, because this part she had rehearsed in the dark of the cargo hold, over and over, against the sound of the engines. "The choice isn't mine anymore. When Okafor's ship came, when the countdown started, the dream changed. It stopped asking me. It started asking *you*. I can feel it — not hear it exactly, more like knowing which way a compass points. The relay chose you, Casper. It's been choosing you since before I was born. Nine years alone with a beacon that listens. Who else could it possibly pick?"

"That's not—" Casper began.

"It offered me first because I was convenient," Ilsa overrode her, with a firmness that surprised them both. "A child who dreams clearly. A fresh voice. But convenience isn't calling. I was the knock on the wrong door. You're the door." She swallowed. "And I need you to say yes. Or I need you to tell me honestly why you can't, so I can go back to being afraid by myself instead of pretending someone braver than me is handling it."

The words hung in the little blister, in the recycled air, between the two of them and the ancient patient thing turning outside the glass.

Casper was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "You're twelve."

"I'm aware."

"You should be afraid. Being afraid is correct. Anyone who isn't afraid of this hasn't understood it."

"I am afraid," Ilsa said. "All the time. Since 3:14 in the morning when I woke up humming. But Grandma says the lullaby was sung on the crossing ships by people who were terrified, and they sang it anyway, because the babies needed it. Fear isn't the thing that decides. It's just the thing that comes along."

Casper laughed — a short, cracked sound, half wonder. "Who taught you to talk like that?"

"You did. On the transit. You said the math doesn't care about our hesitation, but people do, and the difference between a coward and a brave person is mostly timing." Ilsa unfolded herself and stood, crossing the small space until she stood in front of the seated woman, close enough to see the salt-and-pepper strands escaping her braid, the fine lines around eyes that had watched dead space for nine years. "But I didn't come here just to hand you the bill. There's another option. The dream showed me. It's faint — like a word you can almost read through frosted glass. Acceptance doesn't have to be one voice. The first generation promised *a* successor, but promises are just drafts. You taught me that too. It might be possible to share it. Two voices. One old enough to remember being alone, one young enough to remember being afraid. Teaching each other the song so neither of us ever sings it wrong."

"Sharing it," Casper repeated. Not dismissively. Testing the weight of it.

"I don't know if it will accept that. I don't know anything except that it's waiting to be answered, not obeyed, and you're the one who figured that out." Ilsa took a breath. "So here's my ask. Take the choice from me — all of it, if sharing won't work. I'll stand in the cradle room and watch and I won't fight you. Or take me with you into it. Either way, I need you to stop deciding alone. That's the real reason I stowed away. Not the dream. Me. I was afraid to carry it alone, and then I met you, and you were carrying everything alone too, and I thought — somebody has to break that habit. Might as well be the two people on the smallest ship in the universe."

Outside, the relay turned its slow consideration. Inside, Casper Ashgrove looked at the child for a long, long moment — and Ilsa saw something shift in her face, the way ice shifts on a lake in spring, not breaking but remembering it was once water.

"If I take it with you," Casper said finally, "and it refuses — if it insists on one voice, one life — then I go into the cradle and you go home on Okafor's ship, and you don't argue, and you live your whole long life, and you fix things, and you get embarrassed about someone someday. That's the price of asking me. You promise me that first."

Ilsa's throat worked. "And if it accepts two?"

"Then we renegotiate the oldest contract in human history," Casper said, "with a lullaby and a twelve-year-old's terms. And God help whatever's on the other side of that window, because it has never dealt with anyone like you."

Ilsa laughed wetly and wiped her nose on her sleeve, which was not dignified but was honest.

"It's starting," she whispered suddenly, because she felt it then — the pressure behind her sternum, the compass needle swinging, the dream pressing up through waking like water through floorboards. "Casper. It's asking. Right now. Can you feel it?"

And Casper Ashgrove went very still, and her eyes widened, and Ilsa knew — with the absolute certainty of a child who has dreamed the same dream forty-one times — that for the first time in nine years, the woman who tended the listening beacon could finally hear what it heard.

Through the hull, faint as a heartbeat, patient as everything else out here, the signal began to rise.

* * *

It filled the ship the way dawn fills a valley — not arriving so much as being revealed, everywhere at once. The Perihelion's dead consoles flickered without power. The coolant telemetry forgot itself mid-number. Even the air seemed to lean toward the source.

Casper stood with her bandaged hands half-raised, as if to catch something falling. "It's not words," she breathed. "It's never been words. It's a *question* shaped like a—"

"Lullaby," Ilsa finished. "It's the second verse. The one Grandma wouldn't sing past."

They looked at each other across the trembling cabin — the woman with nine years of silence in her lungs and the girl with a stolen future in her pocket — and somewhere beyond the closing window, Captain Okafor's rescue vessel burned toward them with orders that no longer mattered to anyone aboard the Perihelion.

The relay asked its question.

And together, for the first time, they answered.
