## 20. What Came Home

The *Meridian's Wake* came out of the jump window at 04:17 colony time, and Captain Adaeze Okafor was awake to meet it, because she had not slept in six days and did not expect to sleep again for a long while.

She stood on the observation deck with her hands folded behind her back, the posture of a woman in control of herself, and watched the recovery cradles extend toward the drifting hulk that had once been the courier ship *Perihelion*. The little vessel looked like a moth that had flown through a candle. Its hull was scorched along the port flank where the coolant breach had vented, its antenna array sheared off at the root, one of its two engine bells blackened and cold. It had come home the way a dog comes home after a long hunt — limping, loyal, and carrying something in its teeth.

It carried no Casper Ashgrove.

Okafor had known that before the ship ever crossed back through the window. She had stood on her own bridge, nineteen days ago, and watched the far relay's final transmission resolve into a woman's voice — calm, unhurried, unmistakably alive — saying words that had already begun to tear the colony apart. *I am answering. Not as they planned it. As I choose it.* And then the window had sealed itself like water closing over a stone, smooth and total, and every instrument pointed at the place where Casper Ashgrove had been showed nothing at all.

"Recovery team reports hull integrity stable," said Lieutenant Sefu from the comm station behind her. "They're bringing the black box across now. Also—" he hesitated, and Okafor heard him choose his words the way you choose your footing on ice, "—also personal effects. There weren't many."

"Log everything," Okafor said. "Chain of custody on the box. Nothing gets opened before the council sees it."

"Yes, Captain." A pause. "Ma'am. The children are awake again. All forty-one. They say she told them a story."

Okafor closed her eyes. Behind them she saw the observation deck of the *Wake*, the recovery lights strobing amber against the dark, and beyond all of it, faint as breath on glass, the beacon at Relay Station Vesper still burning its patient light across dead space — except that it wasn't patient anymore. It was talking. Every night at 3:14, station time, it transmitted four minutes of structured signal that the colony's linguists had finally, grudgingly, translated.

*Goodnight,* it said. *Sleep well. I'm here.*

Signed: C. Ashgrove, Voice of Vesper.

* * *

The council chamber smelled of recycled air and old arguments, which was to say it smelled exactly as it had when Okafor had left it, except that now there were forty-one empty chairs arranged along one wall — small chairs, child-sized, set out by some administrator's well-meaning gesture because the children had asked to attend and the council, terrified of what the children knew, had decided that letting them sit silently in the room was safer than refusing them.

Okafor stood at the witness podium and laid the black box on the table before the nine councilors like a surgeon laying out an instrument.

"This is the flight recorder from the *Perihelion*," she said. "Recovered intact. It contains the complete logs of the transit, the malfunction, the arrival at the far relay, and the final ninety-one hours. I have reviewed all of it. I will summarize, and then I will answer questions, and then this body is going to have to decide what kind of lie it wants to live inside — because the era of pretending we don't know is over."

Councilor Brandt, who had signed the order sending Okafor out with shutdown authority, leaned forward. He was a grain-futures man by origin, a committee man by vocation, and he had the look of someone who had spent nineteen days rehearsing outrage. "Captain, you were ordered to abort Ashgrove's departure and secure the relay for decommissioning. You failed in both—"

"I stalled," Okafor said. "Deliberately. And I'd do it again, and if you interrupt me again I'll ask the chair to make you wait your turn, Councilor, because what's on this box doesn't care about your schedule."

Brandt sat back. In the row of small chairs, a girl with braided hair — Renner, twelve years old, the one who had drawn the antenna — watched Okafor with eyes too old for her face, and Okafor found, absurdly, that it was easier to speak to the girl than to the councilors.

"The first generation made a bargain during the crossing," she said. "You'll hear it in their own voices — recorded presences, stored in the relay's archive for a hundred and sixty years. They answered a signal in deep space. The signal offered passage, protection, a corridor through dead space that made this colony possible at all. In exchange, the first generation promised a successor. A human voice. Someone who would go to the far side and carry the connection forward, person to person, so that it would never become — in their words — *a hunger without a mouth*. The promise was never written down. It was sung. As a lullaby. And then it was forgotten, because forgetting was easier than explaining to your children why you'd sold a piece of the future."

She let that sit. Somewhere in the chamber, a woman began quietly to cry.

"For a hundred and sixty years the relay waited. It got tired of waiting politely. So it started choosing candidates — through dreams. Forty-one children, waking at 3:14, humming a tune their grandparents' grandparents sang before launch. That was not an attack. It was a job posting. And Casper Ashgrove answered it."

"She abandoned her post," Brandt said.

"No," Okafor said. "She *was* her post. Nine years alone keeping a light burning for a colony that stopped visiting, and when the truth finally reached her, she didn't run from it. She ran *into* it, alone, on a dying ship, so that none of us would have to. She burned her hands to the bone repairing a coolant line three days from the relay because the alternative was both of them dying in the dark. You'll hear her log entries. You'll hear her laugh about it, actually, which is worse." Okafor's voice caught, and she let it, deliberately, because these people needed to hear that their captain was not made of protocol. "And then, with a rescue vessel bearing down on her and orders to shut the whole thing down permanently, she broadcast everything — unencrypted, to every receiver in the system. The bargain. The terms. The choice. She gave us the truth as her last act, knowing full well half of you would call it treason."

"And the other half?" asked Councilor Mbeke, the eldest, who had not spoken until now.

"The other half," Okafor said, "already hears her every night."

* * *

They played the logs in open session, because Casper would have wanted it that way and because secrets had done enough damage.

The chamber heard the *Perihelion*'s engines coughing through dead space. It heard Ilsa Renner's voice, steady and young, reading coolant pressures while Casper taught her the ship panel by panel. It heard the lullaby — both of them singing it badly, then less badly, then right. It heard the malfunction, the screaming alarms, Casper's hands on a manual shutoff valve hot enough to brand, and her voice going flat and clinical with pain as she talked herself through it: *Third turn. Again. Again. Good girl, keep counting.* It heard the arrival at the far relay, and the silence afterward that lasted eleven seconds — eleven seconds of two human beings breathing in the presence of something grown rather than built, older than the colony, patient past all human patience.

And it heard the end. Casper's final log, recorded forty minutes before the interface cradle took her:

*"If anyone's listening — and I know you are, because I made sure you could — I want the record to show I wasn't brave. I was scared the entire time. I've been scared for nine years; fear and I are old crewmates. But somebody had to answer, and the alternatives were closing the door forever on something our own ancestors opened, or letting it keep knocking on our children's skulls until we went mad trying not to hear. So I renegotiated. That's all leadership is, really — renegotiating other people's bad bargains. The children will dream the connection. They won't be consumed by it. I checked the terms myself. I'm good at reading fine print; I had nine years and nothing else to read.*

*"Tell the council they can court-martial a memory if it makes them feel better. Tell the drones I said thank you. Tell Ilsa—"*

A pause, eleven seconds, the same length as the silence at the relay.

*"Tell Ilsa she was the bravest person on that ship, and I include the ship."*

In the row of small chairs, Ilsa Renner did not cry. She sat with her hands in her lap and her chin up, and when the recording ended she looked directly at the nine councilors, one by one, the way a teacher looks at a class that has disappointed her, and Okafor thought: *there goes the next voice, whether any of you vote for it or not.*

* * *

The vote came at dusk, and it was not the vote anyone had planned.

Shutdown of the relay: defeated, seven to two. Formal censure of Casper Ashgrove posthumously: withdrawn before it could be called, after Councilor Mbeke rose and said only, "I knew her grandmother. We do not get to punish this family twice for being honest." Recognition of Ashgrove's actions, and formal establishment of a standing office — the Office of the Voice — to study, steward, and eventually staff the connection: passed unanimously, which in the history of this council meant that everyone had privately decided the same thing and no one wanted to be the last to admit it.

Okafor walked out of the chamber into the colony's long evening feeling lighter and heavier at once, the way you feel after setting down a weight you've carried so long your body has forgotten how to stand without it.

Sefu fell into step beside her. "Message from Vesper station, Captain. Priority flag. The drones held the beacon per Ashgrove's standing instructions, but tonight's transmission came in early. Four minutes ahead of schedule."

Okafor stopped walking. "Content?"

"That's the thing, ma'am." Sefu handed her the tablet, and his voice dropped, though there was no one near enough to hear. "It's not the goodnight message. It's addressed. By name. To the Office of the Voice — which didn't exist until twenty minutes ago."

Okafor read it standing under the first stars, and the evening air went cold against her skin, and she read it twice because the first time she was sure she had imagined the last line.

The message was brief. It thanked the council for listening. It confirmed the new terms held. It reported, in Casper's dry, precise, infuriatingly calm cadence, that the far relay had detected movement on *its* far side — something older than the relay, something the first generation's partners had always kept beyond the door, beginning for the first time in a hundred and sixty years to pay attention.

And then the last line, the one Okafor read twice:

*We will need more than one voice soon. Ask the girl who drew the antenna. She already knows.*

Okafor lowered the tablet. Down the street, in a lit window of the technicians' dormitory, a twelve-year-old girl sat at a desk doing homework, and even from here Okafor could see that she was smiling.
