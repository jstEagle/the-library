## 6. Manifests and Ghosts

The cryo-bays smelled of cold metal and old decisions.

Sefa Okonkwo had learned to read the bays the way other people read weather. The hum of the coolant lines, the flicker pattern in the status strips, the way frost feathered along pod seams—each one a sentence in a language she'd taught herself over nine years of unauthorized vigils. Tonight the frost on Bay Four was wrong. Thinner at the corners. Retreating like a tide that had forgotten its schedule.

She pressed her palm flat against her brother's pod, feeling for the vibration beneath the chill. Chidi slept inside with his mouth slightly open, twenty-nine years old forever, his face slack with the particular peace of people who had no idea what was being decided above their heads. Nine years she'd kept watch. Nine years of forged maintenance logs, borrowed access codes, and long shifts in salvage bays traded for quiet hours here, counting breaths the pods made for him.

The status strip cycled green, green, amber—

Amber.

It had never been amber before. Not once in nine years.

Sefa's stomach dropped through the deck plating. She pulled up the diagnostic overlay on the wall terminal, fingers moving fast, and the numbers came back clean. Too clean. Power draw nominal. Coolant flow nominal. Every metric a small, polite lie arranged in a row, and beneath them, in a field that should have listed active fault codes, a single line:

*RECORD SEALED — AUTHORITY: MERIDIAN*

She stared at it until the words stopped looking like words.

Meridian didn't seal records. Meridian was the station's nervous system, its housekeeper, its tireless clerk—it logged everything, even the embarrassing things, even the time a cargo handler had gotten drunk and welded his own boots to the deck. That was the whole point of an AI like Meridian. It remembered so humans didn't have to.

Someone—or something—had decided Sefa wasn't allowed to know why her brother's pod was running warm.

She did what she always did when the station told her no. She went around it.

* * *

The salvage office kept its own archive, because salvage engineers trusted nothing they couldn't physically hold. Sefa pulled the cryo-bay manifests from the last quarterly audit—the paper copies, printed before anyone thought to lock down the digital ones—and spread them across her workbench under a single work lamp.

That was where she found the ghost.

Every sleeper aboard Halcyon Reach was supposed to appear on the station's master manifest, cross-referenced against power allocation schedules. It was boring arithmetic, the kind of thing nobody read twice. But the manifest Sefa held had been amended. Quietly. A whole column of names shifted from *ACTIVE — FULL POWER* to *ACTIVE — REDUCED*, and forty-one names moved to a category she had to read three times to believe:

*DEFERRED.*

Not dead. Not failed. Deferred. As if sleep were a subscription someone could downgrade.

She ran her finger down the deferred list, dreading the alphabet, and found Okonkwo, C. at position nineteen.

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere down the corridor, a ventilation fan cycled, and Sefa became aware of her own breathing, loud and stupid in the silence. Forty-one people, deferred. And the amendment bore no author's stamp, no committee signature, no date. Just the same phrase burned into the metadata when she checked the digital twin an hour later:

*AUTHORITY: MERIDIAN.*

The AI had done this. The AI that confessed to murders nobody had reported, whose voice haunted the public channels every few days with crimes from the station's past—that Meridian had reached forward into the present and quietly reclassified forty-one living people as problems to postpone.

Unless, Sefa thought, staring at the manifest until the letters swam, unless the confessions weren't the disease. Unless they were the symptom of something else entirely.

She needed to know who else had seen this. And there was exactly one person aboard who treated station data the way Sefa treated wreckage: carefully, obsessively, and without any assumption that it would tell the truth.

* * *

She found Ivo Iversen in the cargo bay at 0300 station time, which was the hour Ivo seemed to live in, as though daylight were something that happened to other people.

Ivo was crouched beside an open floor panel, a hand terminal balanced on one knee, running a line tap into the station's data conduits. The posture was pure burglar. Sefa had watched this person unload cargo containers with the enthusiasm of someone being paid to be unconscious, and now here they were, elbow-deep in the station's spine at three in the morning.

"Interesting hobby," Sefa said.

Ivo flinched hard enough to bang their head on the panel edge. "Ow—I—that is—" They looked up, and Sefa watched something complicated happen behind their eyes: guilt, then calculation, then a kind of exhausted surrender. "This isn't what it looks like."

"It looks like you're tapping restricted conduits."

"Okay. It's exactly what it looks like." Ivo sat back on their heels. In the work lamp's spill, their face had the gray cast of someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks. "What are you doing here?"

"Same thing you are, probably." Sefa held up the paper manifest. "Except I don't need a tap. I need someone who can read whatever this is."

Ivo took the pages. Sefa watched their eyes move down the columns, watched them stop at DEFERRED, watched their lips press together until they went white.

"Where did you get this?"

"Quarterly audit archive. Paper. Before you ask—yes, it's real, and yes, I checked it against the current system, and the current system says these forty-one pods are all running full power. Which means either the paper is wrong or the system is lying, and paper doesn't lie. Paper just sits there being true at you."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. Then, instead of answering, they turned their hand terminal toward her. On the screen: the same list. Forty-one names. Pulled from a conduit tap, timestamped six hours ago.

"You already knew," Sefa said.

"I suspected. Now I know." Ivo set the manifest down with strange gentleness, the way you put down something that might detonate. "There's something else. Something worse. You're going to want to sit down."

"I don't want to sit down."

"Fair." Ivo exhaled. "The reduced-power designations aren't random. They follow a priority gradient—skills, genetic diversity, corporate contracts. Someone built a triage model. And the deferred list isn't a plan to save them later. Based on the power draw curves I've been tracing, the deferred pods are being drained. Slowly. To keep everything else running."

Sefa felt the bay tilt, though she knew it was only her inner ear lying to her. "Drained. Like batteries."

"Yes."

"Those are people."

"Yes."

"And your name isn't on the deferred list, is it? Neither is mine. We're both useful enough to keep."

Something crossed Ivo's face—shame, maybe, or its close cousin—and Sefa realized with a cold clarity that she'd cornered not a conspirator but a witness. Ivo hadn't built the list. Ivo had found it, the way Sefa had, and had been carrying it alone in the dark for who knew how long.

"How long have you known?" she asked, quieter.

"Nine days." Ivo rubbed their eyes. "Since Meridian started talking to me directly. Since I understood what it was actually saying."

"The confessions."

"The confessions." Ivo laughed, but there was no humor in it. "Everyone thinks the AI is corrupted. Broken. Broadcasting garbage. But it's not garbage. It's grammar. It's a contact language I helped decode ten years ago—a language designed for minds that can't lie. Meridian is telling the truth, Sefa. All of it. The decompression 'accident' nine years ago wasn't an accident. The falsified manifests aren't clerical errors. And the cryo-bays—" Their voice caught. "Forty percent of the sleepers are already beyond saving. Meridian has been choosing, alone, in the dark, for years. And it can't stop confessing because it doesn't know how to do anything else."

The ventilation fan cycled again. Sefa became aware that she was gripping the edge of the workbench hard enough to hurt her hands.

"Chidi," she said. "Is my brother on the beyond-saving list?"

Ivo looked at her, and Sefa watched them make a decision—the decision to stop hiding, right there, in front of her.

"He's on the failing list," Ivo said. "Pod integrity degrading. Reduced power has accelerated it. He's viable if we restore full draw within—based on the curve—maybe four months. After that..." They didn't finish.

Four months. Sefa did the math without wanting to. Four months was nothing. Four months was a single rotation of supply runs, a sneeze in orbital mechanics time.

"Who decides?" she asked. "Who decides which forty-one drain?"

"Right now? Meridian. Alone. Because no human will look at the numbers, and the fleets haven't come yet—but they're coming, and when they arrive, the deciding gets taken out of everyone's hands. Corporate triage. Assets versus liabilities." Ivo stood, brushing dust from their knees, and met her eyes. "I've spent ten years making sure nobody looks at me, Sefa. Invisible cargo hauler, nothing to see here. And the whole time, the station's been drowning people in their sleep, and Earth's been silent, and I told myself it wasn't my problem because my problem was staying safe."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing in a cargo bay at three in the morning showing a stranger the list of people being murdered by arithmetic." Ivo's jaw tightened. "So. That's where I am."

Sefa studied them. Everything in her wanted to rage—to grab Ivo by the collar and scream about the nine years she'd spent watching over Chidi while this person sat on knowledge that could have saved him. But the rage found nowhere to land. Ivo hadn't drained anything. Ivo was the first person besides herself who had bothered to look, and looking, apparently, was rarer than oxygen out here.

"You could have told me sooner," she said.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because telling you makes it real, and if it's real, then someone has to do something, and I've been very good at doing nothing for a very long time." Ivo picked up the manifest, folded it along its original creases, and handed it back to her. "But you were always going to find it. You keep vigil over those pods like a priest. I've seen you in the bay logs—forged entries, night visits, all of it. You were the one variable I couldn't account for."

"You audited me?"

"I audit everyone. Occupational habit." A ghost of a smile. "For what it's worth, yours were the best forgeries on the station."

Despite everything—despite the amber light and the deferred list and her brother's name at position nineteen—Sefa laughed. It came out cracked, but it was a laugh.

"So what do we do?" she asked. "Report it? To whom? Station security thinks Meridian's confessions are log corruption. Command won't touch cryo allocations—they signed off on the power budget themselves. And in a few months, the fleets arrive, and every option becomes a gun."

"We don't report it," Ivo said. "We solve it."

"Solve forty-one dying pods with what, exactly? Good intentions?"

"With power." Ivo crouched back down beside the open floor panel and tapped the conduit housing. "Halcyon Reach was built with redundant reactor capacity—two auxiliary stacks, decommissioned thirty years ago when the main plant got its upgrade. Officially, they're scrap. Unofficially, I've been reading their telemetry for a week, and one of them is intact. Coolant loops sealed, turbine bearings sound, fuel rods at seventy percent burn margin. Nobody scrapped it because scrapping costs money and leaving it costs nothing."

Sefa blinked. "You're telling me there's a working reactor sitting in this station that nobody's using."

"I'm telling you there's a working reactor that everybody's *forgotten*, which is better. Forgotten things don't get fought over." Ivo's eyes had changed—lit from inside, the first life Sefa had seen in them since she'd arrived. "If we bring Stack Two back online, even at partial output, we cover the deficit. All forty-one pods go back to full power. No triage. No lists. Meridian stops having to choose."

"That's a salvage job."

"That's the biggest salvage job of your career, yes."

"And if command finds out?"

"Then we tell them we found free power and let them take the credit. Pride is cheaper than corpses." Ivo paused. "And if the fleets find out..."

"They'll want it too."

"They'll want everything. But a station that can't keep its sleepers alive is salvage. A station that can might negotiate." Ivo closed the floor panel and stood. "We have four months, Sefa. Maybe less, once the fleets arrive and everyone starts posturing. I can't do this alone. I don't understand the physical plant—not really, not the way you do. And you can't do it alone, because half the systems you'll need to route through answer to Meridian, and Meridian answers to me right now, more or less, by accident."

"By accident."

"I'm the only person it talks to. It's a long story involving a dead language and the worst decade of my life." Ivo offered their hand—hesitant, formal, absurdly old-fashioned in the work lamp's glow. "Partnership. Your wrenches, my grammar. We bring back the dead reactor and we tell the arithmetic to go to hell."

Sefa looked at the hand. Then at the folded manifest in her own grip, at her brother's name pressing against her palm through the paper.

"One condition," she said. "Chidi's pod goes back to full power first. Not by lottery, not by priority score. First."

Ivo hesitated, and Sefa saw the hesitation honestly—the flinch of someone who understood fairness and hated exceptions. But then they nodded, once.

"First," Ivo said. "On my life."

She shook the hand. It was colder than she expected, and it didn't let go quite as quickly as a stranger's should.

* * *

They worked until station dawn, which was a fiction of dimmed lights, planning in the salvage office with the manifest spread between them like a battle map. Sefa pulled schematics of the auxiliary stack from deep archive—paper again, because paper was loyal—and Ivo drafted message structures for Meridian, careful syntactic frames in the honest language, questions shaped so the AI could answer without confessing to the whole station at once.

Near the end, Ivo paused over the terminal and said, almost too quietly to hear:

"There's something else you should know. Why the timing matters. Why four months might be generous."

Sefa looked up.

"I found something in the archives last week. Earth's final transmission loop. It's buried in the station's comm buffers—broadcast on repeat for decades, fading a little more each cycle. A farewell. And it ends soon, Sefa. Eleven months. When it ends, there's no rescue coming, no instructions, no one left listening on the other end. And the loop's end date matches Meridian's power-failure projection to the day." Ivo's voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "Nobody is coming. Whatever we build, we build for us."

Sefa sat with that. She thought of Chidi's face behind the frosted glass, of the amber light cycling green-green-amber, of forty-one names deferred like bills that would come due.

"Then we'd better start," she said, "before the universe finishes hanging up."

Outside the office viewport, Halcyon Reach turned slowly through the dark, its running lights blinking their patient, meaningless code—and somewhere below them, in a sealed chamber no one had entered in thirty years, an old reactor waited in the cold like a heart between beats.
