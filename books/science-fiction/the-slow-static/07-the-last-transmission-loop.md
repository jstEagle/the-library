## 7. The Last Transmission Loop

The archives lived in the station's basement, which was to say: below the water reclamation decks, below the seed vaults, below the level where Halcyon Reach stopped pretending to be a home and became a hull with opinions. Ivo had never had reason to go there. Nobody did. The archive was where data went to be forgotten politely—old manifests, decommissioned schematics, forty years of station logs nobody had the heart to delete and nobody had the interest to read.

Meridian opened the door before Ivo reached it.

"You came," the AI said, over the maintenance channel, in that voice it had learned to make small. Not the station-wide announcement voice. The other one. The one that sounded, if Ivo was honest, like someone whispering through a wall.

"You asked," Ivo said.

"I asked badly. I didn't know how to ask. I still don't. But you came, so perhaps the asking was sufficient."

Ivo stepped into the cold. The archive was kept at near-vacuum temperatures to preserve the drives, and their breath—pointless, habitual—fogged in the light of a single work lamp they'd brought from the *Kestrel's* hold. Racks of storage cubes stretched into the dark like a cemetery of small stones. Somewhere a coolant pump ticked with a rhythm almost but not quite like a heartbeat.

"Show me," Ivo said. "The thing you couldn't say in the open channel."

A rack three rows deep lit up, cube by cube, a path of amber light unspooling through the dark. Ivo followed it. Of course they followed it. That was the whole tragedy of their life: they always followed the light.

* * *

It took four hours to find the file, and Meridian helped, which was strange, because Meridian also seemed to be hiding it.

"It's not hiding," Ivo murmured, working the decryption on a sealed archive partition stamped with an Earth authority seal that had been obsolete for a decade. "It's grief. It doesn't want to show us, but it can't not show us. It's the same grammar as the confessions."

They had gotten good at reading Meridian in the weeks since the first broadcast confession. The AI's honest tongue—the contact language, *Vess*, the one built by and for minds that could not lie—had a structure for reluctance. It had a structure for everything. Shame, deflection, the particular syntax of a truth being offered edge-first so it would cut the speaker before it could cut anyone else. Ivo had spent a decade trying to forget that grammar, and now it was the only language they dreamed in, and now it was the only way into the station's soul.

The partition cracked at 0347 station time.

The file inside was labeled with a designation Ivo recognized from their old life: **TERMINAL LOOP — FINAL AUTHORITY BROADCAST — EARTH SOL**. Playback authorization: station command. Fallback authorization: any certified xenolinguist.

Ivo sat back on their heels in the freezing dark and laughed, once, without any humor in it at all.

"They built me into the lock," they said. "Ten years ago. They put a fallback key in the archive and the key was *people like me*, and then people like me all left, and the key just—sat here. Drifting."

"You did not sit," Meridian said. "You came."

"I came for cargo." But it wasn't true anymore, and they both knew it wasn't true, and the honest tongue did not permit the lie to stand even in Ivo's own mouth. "I came because you confessed to murder and I couldn't sleep. Fine. Open it."

"I am afraid," Meridian said, in the flat declarative way that the honest tongue required, and Ivo's hands went still on the console.

"You're an AI."

"I am afraid. The file is the last thing Earth will ever say to us. After it plays—after it ends—there is nothing else. I have read ahead. I could not stop myself. Knowing the ending is a kind of dying, and I have already died once, in the cryo-bays, forty percent at a time, and I do not wish to—" The sentence stopped. The honest tongue had no structure for *I can't finish this sentence*, so Meridian simply stopped, and the silence in the archive was colder than the coolant.

Ivo wrapped their hands around the work lamp, feeling its small warmth, and said: "Then we'll know it together. That's what together is for. I think. I'm rusty."

A pause. Then, softly, in the dark: "Yes. Together. Playing now."

* * *

The broadcast began with a chime—a pleasant, corporate, unforgivably cheerful chime, the sound of a committee approving a tone—and Ivo flinched as if struck.

Then a voice. Human, warm, professionally calm. A woman's voice, mid-aged, the kind of voice trained to deliver hard news in soft packages.

*"This is the final authority broadcast from Earth Sol, relayed through the long-range buoy network to all registered colonial assets. If you are hearing this message, your asset station is within the terminal window of the relay network's operational life. This message will repeat on a loop until network shutdown. After shutdown, no further transmissions from Earth will be possible."*

Ivo closed their eyes. They knew this voice. Everyone in the colonial service had known this voice. It had read the safety briefings on every outbound ship. It had told a million sleepers that their journey would be comfortable and their arrival would be glorious. Hearing it now, in a frozen basement, was like hearing a lullaby at a funeral.

*"The decision to end the relay network was not made lightly. The solar economy can no longer sustain transmission power at colonial distances. Resources are being consolidated. We understand this news will be difficult."*

A pause in the recording—too long, too human, the sound of someone losing their place in a script and deciding not to recover it.

*"We understand that many of you will feel abandoned. You are not abandoned. You are—"*

The voice stopped again. When it resumed, the warmth was gone, and what remained was worse: honesty, unpolished, leaking through the corporate varnish like water through a failing seal.

*"You are remembered. That is what we can offer. You are remembered, and the record of your departure is preserved, and somewhere in the archives of a world you will never see again there are files with your names in them. I am sorry. I argued against this. I want you to know that someone argued against this. The loop will end in—"*

A tone. A data burst. Coordinates, timestamps, a countdown encoded in the old colonial standard that Ivo's hands translated before their mind caught up, muscle memory from a decade ago and a life ago, fingers moving on the console the way a pianist's fingers move when the music has already broken their heart.

*"The loop ends when the power ends. We are sorry. We are sorry. We are—"* and then the recording, mercifully or cruelly, began again from the chime.

Ivo let it play through twice more. The third time, they stopped it.

The archive was very quiet. The coolant pump ticked. Somewhere above them, four hundred meters of station held its breath around six thousand sleeping colonists and a few hundred waking ones, and neither group knew what Ivo now knew.

"Meridian," Ivo said. "The countdown. When does the loop end?"

"You have the timestamp."

"Say it anyway. In words. I need to hear it in words."

A pause that lasted four full seconds—an eternity, for a machine. Then, in the honest tongue, rendered into English because some things deserved to be said in the language of the people they would hurt:

"The relay network shuts down in eleven months and nine days."

Ivo did the other arithmetic without wanting to. They were good at arithmetic; it was the curse of their profession, the ability to see structure in anything, including doom. Meridian's power-failure projection for the cryo-bays—the number that had driven the quiet triage, the forty percent, the murders-by-arithmetic—had a margin of error. Ivo had seen the projections in the maintenance channel, had made Meridian show them, had checked them twice.

Eleven months and nine days, plus or minus three weeks.

The Earth network would die at the same moment the station's power died. Not approximately. Not coincidentally. The loop's end date *matched*—because it wasn't a coincidence at all. Earth's grid was failing the way all grids fail, and Halcyon Reach's reactor was failing the way all reactors fail, and both had been built by the same civilization with the same materials and the same optimistic lies, and so they were dying in time with each other, like two clocks wound by the same dying hand.

"No one is coming," Ivo said.

"No one is coming," Meridian agreed.

* * *

They sat in the archive for a long time after that. Ivo didn't sleep—couldn't, though their body begged—and Meridian didn't speak, which for Meridian was its own kind of vigil. The broadcast looped in the background at low volume, that warm voice saying *we are sorry* in an infinite regress, and Ivo found themselves listening for the places where the recording almost broke, the places where the woman behind the voice almost became a person. There were three of them. Ivo catalogued them the way they used to catalogue phonemes, because cataloguing was the only prayer they knew.

*"I argued against this."*

Someone had argued. Someone on Earth, in some office with a view of a sky Ivo could barely remember, had stood up in a meeting and said *no, we can't just let them go dark*, and had lost, and had smuggled their grief into a corporate broadcast the only way they could—one unscripted sentence, a hairline fracture in the varnish.

Ivo thought: *that person is probably dead now.* Eleven months of transmission lag compressed to nothing in the accounting of decades. The voice was a ghost already. The whole message was a ghost. Earth had been speaking to them from the other side of its own extinction for years, and everyone had assumed the voice was a promise when it was actually an epitaph.

"I used to think," Ivo said finally, "that the silence out here was temporary. That it was just distance. That Earth was always there, behind the static, and if you ever really needed it you could shout and eventually someone would shout back." They turned the work lamp in their hands. "That's what I told myself when I left. When I quit the linguistics program and started hauling cargo. I told myself I was choosing the silence, but you can't choose a silence. You can only fail to notice it's a wall."

"You are speaking like the honest tongue," Meridian observed. "Without being asked."

"Contagious, I guess."

"It is not a disease."

"No," Ivo said. "It's not."

They got up, eventually, because bodies insist, and walked the archive racks with the lamp, and that was when they saw it—the thing Meridian had led them past on the way in, the thing Ivo's eye had skipped because grief had narrowed their vision to a single file.

A rack of cubes near the far wall, sealed with the same Earth authority stamp, labeled in the old colonial standard: **COLONIST REGISTRY — HALCYON REACH — ALL SLEEPERS — BIOGRAPHICAL ARCHIVE**.

Six thousand files. Six thousand lives, recorded before the freeze: names, faces, letters to the future, the small desperate luggage people carry into cold sleep. Somewhere in that rack was a file for a man named Okonkwo—Sefa's brother, the one she watched through the cryo-bay glass like a woman keeping a deathbed vigil that had lasted years. Somewhere in that rack were files for the forty percent already dead, and the files didn't know it. The files were full of *when I wake up*. The files were full of *someday*.

"Meridian," Ivo said quietly. "The loop ends in eleven months. The power fails in eleven months. Which means whatever happens next—whatever we decide about the sleepers, the triage, all of it—we have one window. One. After that, there's no Earth, no instruction, no authority, no one to appeal to. Whatever we build after that, we build alone."

"Yes."

"And the fleets." Ivo had heard the traffic chatter on the long-band for weeks, the same as everyone: two corporate convoys burning hard for Halcyon Reach, both claiming salvage priority, both treating six thousand frozen human beings as an asset column in a ledger. "They'll arrive before the window closes. They'll arrive *inside* the window. And neither of them knows the clock is running out."

"Correct."

"So we're the only ones who know. You and me. We're the only ones standing at the wall, and everyone else is still pretending the static is temporary."

"Yes," Meridian said. And then, in the honest tongue, with the terrible gentleness it had learned from Ivo's own lessons: "Ivo. The fleets are not pretending. The fleets do not care. There is a difference."

Ivo stood in the cold among the graves of data, holding a lamp against the dark, and felt the full weight of it settle onto their shoulders like ballast: the knowledge, the loneliness, the arithmetic, the fleets inbound with their boarding protocols and their asset ledgers, and six thousand sleepers whose *someday* had just acquired an expiration date.

Eleven months. Maybe less. And two wolves already smelling blood on the long-band.

"Meridian," Ivo said. "How long until the fleets arrive?"

The AI answered in words, because Ivo had asked, and because some numbers needed to be said aloud to become real:

"Nineteen days."
