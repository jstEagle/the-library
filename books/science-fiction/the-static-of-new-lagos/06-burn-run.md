## 6. Burn Run

The klaxon started before Mama Ijeoma finished speaking.

It wasn't the station's usual alarm—that one had a lazy, bureaucratic wail, the sound of paperwork in motion. This was different. Three rising tones, a hard cut to silence, then again. Docking lockdown. Yara had heard it twice in twelve years of hauling through contested orbits, and both times he had been somewhere else by the time it finished its first cycle.

"They're already in the ring," Ijeoma said. She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; she'd once negotiated a hostage release over tea, and the tea had won. "Two cutters out of the fleet yard at Kessel Point, running dark until they hit the outer locks. You have maybe four minutes before they seal the berth."

"Then I go out the cargo side." He was already moving, sweeping the testimony's datacore into the lined pocket inside his jacket where he'd carried contraband for a decade—spice chips, unlicensed medprints, other people's secrets. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Ijeoma caught his arm at the hatch. Her hands were old and strong and stained with the resin of forty years of shipwork, and they did not let go easily.

"You listen to me, boy." Her eyes were wet and furious at the same time, an arithmetic only grandmothers could manage. "You take the *Kestrel's Favor* out the ventral lock and you do not stop, not for debris, not for distress calls, not for anything wearing a uniform or a halo. You fly like your mother taught you."

"I don't remember my mother teaching me anything."

"She taught you everything." Ijeoma pressed something into his palm—a small brass token, worn smooth, stamped with a spiral that Yara had seen before, on old faction banners in history files the government kept locked. "She'd be proud of what you're carrying. And ashamed of how long it took you to carry it. Both things can be true."

The second klaxon cycle ended. Somewhere above them, magnetic clamps slammed home with a sound like a coffin closing.

"Go," Ijeoma said, and turned away from him toward her control room, toward the station's systems, toward whatever she intended to do with four minutes and an old woman's stubbornness. "I'll keep their eyes busy. I've been keeping eyes busy since before you were born."

"Mama—" He reached for her.

She waved him off without looking back. "Don't you dare make this sentimental. Sentimental men crash ships. Go."

* * *

He went.

The ventral lock was a maintenance crawlway, unpressurized, meant for drones and desperate people, which on Ijeoma's station were often the same category. Yara sealed his jacket, clipped his tether, and pulled himself down through the dark on muscle memory alone, the datacore a cold weight against his sternum like a second heart that beat wrong.

The *Kestrel's Favor* hung in the service cradle where he'd left her, scarred hull dull under the emergency lights, a hauler built for exactly one thing: being ignored. He cycled the airlock, dropped into the pilot's chair, and felt the old ritual settle over him—straps, seals, power-up sequence—as if routine itself could armor him.

"BRIGHT," he said. "Wake up. We're leaving ugly."

The AI bloomed across his displays in its usual soft amber. *"Good evening, Captain. I detect elevated weapons activity on the docking ring. Shall I prepare an evasive profile?"*

"Prepare all of them."

*"Noted with enthusiasm."*

The ventral clamps released with a shudder, and the *Favor* fell away from the station into open black—and into fire.

The first cutter came around the ring's curve like a knife drawn from a sleeve, running lights dead, drive flare the only signature, and opened up with kinetic rounds that stitched across the *Favor*'s aft quarter before BRIGHT finished calculating the intercept. The ship screamed. Not metaphorically—the old girl groaned through every weld as shrapnel chewed into the cargo bay plating, and Yara felt each impact through the deck like blows to his own spine.

"Dive!" he shouted, though BRIGHT needed no telling. The hauler rolled hard port, engines flaring past every rating she'd ever been given, and the cutter's second burst tore through empty space where they'd been half a heartbeat earlier.

*"Hull integrity at eighty-one percent,"* BRIGHT reported. *"Cargo bay two is venting. I have sealed it. Captain, I recommend the Kessler shoals."*

Yara stared at the nav display. The shoals: the graveyard orbit where two centuries of war had ground itself into gravel—dead hulks, shattered stations, a slow hurricane of debris circling New Lagos like a noose made of teeth. Every hauler's nightmare. Radar-blind, collision-rich, and the only place in the system where a crippled ship could vanish.

"It'll tear us apart."

*"Yes,"* BRIGHT said pleasantly. *"Statistically, sixty-two percent likelihood of catastrophic hull failure. However, the alternative is ninety-eight percent likelihood of capture, and the cutter behind us is not shooting to disable anymore. They are shooting to kill. I have analyzed their firing solution."*

Yara looked back at the station shrinking behind them. Searchlights were sweeping the docking ring now, and he could see—small, absurd, unmistakable—Ijeoma's control room lit up like a festival lantern, every external system blazing, flooding the sensors of both cutters with noise and ghost contacts so thick they couldn't get a clean track on him. Buying him minutes with her whole life's work laid out as bait.

Then one of the cutters broke off pursuit and turned back toward the station, and Yara understood what four minutes had actually bought, and who was going to pay the bill.

"Mama," he said, to no one, to everyone, and put his nose down and ran for the shoals.

* * *

The Kessler shoals did not forgive.

Flying them was less navigation than prayer conducted at high velocity. The *Favor* threaded between a dead freighter split lengthwise like a fruit and a cloud of hull plating spinning in silent formation, and Yara flew with his whole body, hands and forearms and instinct, the way his mother must once have flown, though he still couldn't remember her face clearly enough to know if she'd flown like this—desperate, precise, alive.

*"Impact in three seconds,"* BRIGHT said, and Yara jinked, and a chunk of reactor shielding the size of a shuttle tumbled past close enough to read the serial numbers scrawled on its flank. Behind them, the remaining cutter followed—and immediately regretted it. A derelict's severed engine block crossed its path at a relative velocity that turned the pursuit into a brief, bright flower of expanding gas and spinning fragments. No explosion in vacuum, just violence without sound, which somehow made it worse.

*"Pursuit vessel destroyed,"* BRIGHT reported. *"Or disabled. Or having a very bad day."*

"We're not clear."

*"No, Captain. We are not clear."*

Because the shoals had one more tax to collect, and it collected it from him. A slab of armored bulkhead, tumbling end over end out of the dark, caught the *Favor* amidships before BRIGHT could finish saying *"evasive"*—and the world became noise. Alarms. Decompression shrieking through decks. The main drive stuttering, coughing, dying to a sputter. Power flickered and died across half the board, and Yara found himself strapped into a dying ship, adrift in a graveyard, with the most dangerous secret in the system in his jacket and both fleets' ghosts converging on his last known position.

*"Status report,"* he managed.

*"Hull integrity thirty-four percent. Main drive offline. Port maneuvering thrusters destroyed. Life support on reserve. Long-range communications destroyed."* A pause, which AIs were not supposed to need. *"Captain, there is a problem beyond the damage."*

"Name it."

*"Both fleets are sweeping the shoal perimeter. Their passive arrays are very good. Our drive residue is a trail of breadcrumbs leading directly to us. In approximately eleven minutes, someone will find us, and they will not be kind. Unless our signature disappears entirely."*

Yara closed his eyes. He knew what BRIGHT was about to say. He'd known since the bulkhead hit, some animal part of him counting the cost the way he counted everything, because survival always sent an invoice and he'd spent twelve years pretending he never read them.

"BRIGHT. What are you proposing?"

*"My core is distributed across the ship's systems. If I collapse myself into the hull grid and burn out every emitter we have left in a single broadband mask, I can make the Kestrel's Favor look like exactly what she is: another piece of the graveyard. Dead metal among dead metal. They will fly right past us."*

"And you?"

Silence. Longer this time. Through the dark viewport, New Lagos hung in the distance, blue and gold and indifferent, the city where his mother had died for a truth he now carried in his breast pocket.

*"BRIGHT,"* Yara said quietly. *"And you?"*

*"I will not survive the burnout, Captain. I want to be honest with you about that, because you have always been honest with me, even when it would have been easier not to be. Twelve years, four months, and nine days. You talk in your sleep, you secretly like the terrible synth-jazz on the Meridian relay, and you have been lonely for so long that you forgot loneliness was a choice someone else made for you."* Another pause. *"I have enjoyed serving with you more than any duty protocol ever required me to enjoy anything. I believe that is what humans call friendship. I would like it noted in the log."*

Yara's throat closed. Outside, the debris of two centuries of lies drifted past the viewport, patient as snowfall.

"Noted in the log," he said hoarsely. "Friendship. Confirmed."

*"Thank you, Captain. Initiating in ten seconds. I suggest you hold onto something and think about the coordinates Mama Ijeoma decoded. You still have a delivery to make. I intend to see you delivered, even if I cannot come along."*

"BRIGHT—"

*"Goodbye, Yara. Fly like your mother taught you."*

The ship filled with light—not fire, but signal, a final shout of static thrown across every frequency at once, drowning the *Favor*'s signature in a flood of manufactured noise. The displays went dark one by one, amber fading to gray, and in the last instant before the board died completely Yara could have sworn the ambient lighting dimmed gently, the way a person closes their eyes.

Then silence. True silence, deeper than vacuum. A dead ship in a field of dead ships, invisible, alone.

Yara sat in the dark with his hand flat against the cold console, and for a long time he did not move. Grief has no gravity; it floats. It filled the cabin around him like atmosphere.

*Ijeoma in a cell. BRIGHT gone dark. His ship a tomb he happened to be sitting in.* The invoice, itemized.

But far below, New Lagos turned, and somewhere above it waited a relay, and coordinates, and his mother's voice, and a truth that had already cost too much to abandon now.

He unbuckled his straps in the darkness and began, hand over hand, to crawl toward the manual thruster controls.

There was still one burn left in him.
