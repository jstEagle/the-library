## 7. What the War Was Built On

The corridor outside Commander Vasquez's office smelled of recycled air and old ambition. Sefu had walked it a hundred times in twelve years—first as a junior analyst clutching data slates, later as the officer whose reports shaped strike packages and interdiction grids. The carpet was the same grey. The lighting was the same pale, sourceless white that made everyone look slightly ill. Only she had changed, she thought, standing at the door with her hand raised to knock, feeling like a cadet again.

She knocked.

"Enter."

Vasquez's office overlooked the drydock bays of Fleet Station Meridian-Anchor, where the cruisers *Halcyon* and *Redoubt* hung in their maintenance cradles like sleeping animals. Beyond them, New Lagos turned, its cloud-swirled face beautiful and indifferent, the way it had been beautiful and indifferent through eleven years of war. Vasquez stood with her back to the door, hands clasped behind her, watching the planet as if it owed her money.

"You wanted to see me, Commander."

"I did." Vasquez didn't turn. "Close the door, Adeyemi."

Sefu closed it. The seal hissed, and the room's ambient hum shifted—a privacy field engaging, the kind reserved for briefings where what was said could never be repeated.

"The derelict signal," Vasquez said. "Old Meridian broadcast, dead-man's switch architecture, peace-faction encryption. Your trace on it came back yesterday."

"It did." Sefu kept her voice level. "I've logged the preliminary findings. The source vessel is still being confirmed—"

"Your log says otherwise." Now Vasquez turned, and her eyes were not angry. That was worse. Anger meant surprise; this was something older and more tired. "Your log says you identified the vessel, cross-referenced its docking history, and then sat on the report for six hours before filing an amended version with the confirmation redacted. Six hours, Adeyemi. In intelligence work, six hours is a confession."

Sefu's mouth went dry. She had told herself the delay would be invisible, buried under station traffic, explicable as verification protocol. She had been wrong, or Vasquez had been watching her specifically, and both possibilities landed in her stomach like ballast.

"I was verifying," she said. "The docking record was anomalous. I didn't want to send a strike team after a ghost."

"A ghost named Yara Nystrom." Vasquez said the name without inflection, without mercy. "Cargo hauler. Twelve years clean. Former associate of the Meridian Compact peace faction. And, I'm told, formerly close to you. Very close."

The word *formerly* was a scalpel. Sefu stood very still, the way one stands still when a predator is near—not because stillness helps, but because movement admits fear.

"That's in my personnel file because I disclosed it," Sefu said. "Twelve years ago. Before the faction purge. I disclosed the relationship, I recused myself from Compact-related taskings, and I have served without conflict since."

"I know. Your file is immaculate. That's why you're here instead of in a holding cell." Vasquez crossed to her desk and touched a control. A display bloomed above it: the derelict's signal signature, rendered in the slow, patient waveform of Old Meridian—the language of the first colonial charter, the language the founders had spoken when they dreamed New Lagos would be something better than a garrison. "Do you know why we haven't scrambled every ship in the fleet after that beacon?"

"Because the source is unconfirmed."

"Because the source doesn't matter." Vasquez's voice dropped, and the room seemed to lean in. "Sit down, Adeyemi."

Sefu sat. The chair was cold through her uniform.

* * *

What Vasquez told her took eleven minutes. Sefu counted, because counting was the only thing keeping her from screaming.

The opening attack—the bombardment of Port Cassimir, four thousand dead, the atrocity that had lit the war's fuse eleven years ago—had not come from the Independence fleets. The launch signatures had been forged. The colonial government's own black-ops directorate had built the false trail, dressed colonial missiles in independence transponders, and aimed them at their own civilians, because the war had been cheaper than the alternative: a negotiated settlement with the Compact that would have broken the orbital monopolies, ended the resource levies, and cost the founding families everything they'd spent two generations accumulating.

The Compact had found out. Of course they had—that was why the government had purged them, branded them terrorists, burned their stations with everyone aboard. Not because they were dangerous.

Because they were right.

"We knew," Vasquez said, and her voice was flat as slag. "Not me personally—I learned three years into it, when I made commander and got read into the containment file. But the Admiralty knew. The Governor's office knew. Every officer above a certain clearance has known for years that we are fighting a war built on a lie, and we have kept fighting it because stopping was unthinkable. Do you understand? Four thousand dead at Cassimir, forty thousand since, and the arithmetic only worked if no one ever checked the sum."

Sefu heard herself speak from very far away. "And the derelict. The testimony."

"One of theirs survived the purge. A recorder, seeded into a salvage hulk, waiting for someone small enough to slip through our nets and stupid enough to answer a dead language." Vasquez finally sat, across the desk from her, and for the first time Sefu saw how old the woman looked—older than her service record allowed, worn down to the load-bearing parts. "It contains the full proof. Launch telemetry. The forgery orders. Names."

"Then we recover it. We destroy it."

"No." Vasquez folded her hands. "We let it surface."

The words didn't parse. Sefu stared at her.

"Commander—"

"There are factions, Adeyemi. There always were. Some of us have been waiting eleven years for a lever big enough to move this. If the testimony comes out through official channels, it gets buried—tribunals, sealed records, a scapegoat or two, business as usual. But if it comes out raw, system-wide, uncontrolled—" Vasquez shrugged, a small, terrible gesture. "Then it can't be buried. And the war ends, and we all find out what we are when there's nothing left to hide behind."

"You're telling me to stand down."

"I'm telling you that your six-hour delay was the most honest act in your file." Vasquez slid a slate across the desk. "But the strike team launches in nine hours regardless, because I don't fully control this anymore, and there are people above me who want the testimony recovered and incinerated along with everyone who's touched it. So here is what I'm offering you, Officer Adeyemi, and I suggest you think carefully about the shape of it."

She tapped the slate. It woke to a promotion order, pre-signed. Section Chief. A jump in rank, pay, clearance—and a line item at the bottom, in the small print where careers went to die: *signatory acknowledges continued discretion regarding matter CASSIMIR-PRIME.*

"Sign it, and you're inside the room where decisions get made. You might even steer some of them. Don't sign it, and you're a compromised officer with a personal connection to the target, and you'll spend the next nine hours in a briefing room explaining yourself while someone else runs your trace." Vasquez met her eyes. "Either way, the war is ending soon. The only question is whether you'll be standing when it does."

Sefu looked at the slate. Her name was already typed into the signature field, patient as a trap.

* * *

They gave her a secure terminal and an hour to decide. What she did instead was open the intercepted copy of the testimony—the fragment Vasquez had played for her, the piece the derelict had transmitted on loop into empty space for two hundred days before Yara Nystrom's salvage rig snagged it.

She told herself she was reviewing evidence. She told herself a lot of things, sitting alone in the small room with the privacy field humming, watching the faces of men and women she had saluted for a decade testify calmly, in Old Meridian, to the murder of four thousand people.

The recording was structured as testimony should be—each speaker giving name, role, and account, the Compact's archivists meticulous even in the shadow of the purge. Sefu watched forged telemetry overlays. She watched a logistics officer describe the missile refit at Drydock Nine, the transponder swaps done between midnight shifts. She watched a communications tech explain how the false distress calls had been scripted, word for word, down to the civilian panic in the voices.

And then the seventh speaker stepped into frame, and Sefu stopped breathing.

He was older than she remembered. Greyer. He wore civilian clothes, but he stood the way he had always stood, shoulders squared like a man permanently at inspection, and when he began to speak, Sefu knew the voice before her mind could finish refusing it.

"This is General Tunde Adeyemi, retired, formerly Director of Orbital Defense Integration for the Colonial Authority. I give this testimony freely and under my own name, because my daughter serves in the fleet I am about to indict, and she deserves to hear it from me rather than from history."

Her father.

Her father, who had retired abruptly eleven years ago, citing health. Her father, whose quiet estate on the Lagrange habitats she visited twice a year, whose hands shook now when he poured tea, which she had attributed to age, to grief, to anything but guilt. Her father, who had taught her to shoot, to sail the orbital currents, to believe that order was the highest form of love.

On the screen, he described the meeting in the Governor's residence. He described the vote. He described how he had argued against the attack—not on moral grounds, he said, his voice cracking on the admission, but on strategic ones, and how he had been overruled, and how he had signed the operational authorization anyway, because he was a soldier, and soldiers sign.

"And when they came for the Compact," he said, looking directly into the recorder as if he could see whoever would one day watch this, "I said nothing. I told myself the purge was regrettable necessity. I told myself my silence protected my family. I have watched my daughter put on that uniform every morning for eleven years knowing what it was built on, and I have said nothing, because saying something would cost her everything. That is my testimony. That is my crime. May whoever finds this be kinder to my child than I was to yours."

The recording moved on to the eighth speaker. Sefu didn't hear a word of it.

She sat in the humming dark with her father's voice lodged in her chest like shrapnel, and she understood, finally, the full architecture of the thing that had been built around her life: the war, the promotions, the immaculate file, the careful distance the old man kept whenever she talked about her work. He hadn't been proud of her. He had been hiding behind her.

The terminal chimed. Decision window closing.

Sefu looked at the promotion order glowing on the slate beside her. Signatory acknowledges continued discretion. Stay silent, stay safe, stay inside the machine her father had helped build—the machine that had eaten four thousand people at Cassimir and forty thousand since and called it arithmetic.

Or.

She thought of Yara—of his laugh, low and startled, as if it surprised him every time; of the night before the purge, when he'd begged her to run with him and she had chosen the uniform, chosen the pension, chosen survival, and watched his ship burn away from the station without her. Twelve years. She had spent twelve years being exactly what the system wanted, and the system had repaid her by making her complicit in her own family's crime.

There was no choice. There had never been a choice. There was only the moment of admitting it.

Sefu picked up the slate, and instead of signing, she opened the strike team's mission package—the nine-hour op, the target vessel, the intercept coordinates in the Kessler shoals—and began, quietly, methodically, to corrupt it.

Outside the viewport, New Lagos turned in the light, beautiful and doomed, and somewhere above it a dying relay held a dead woman's message and a cargo hauler ran for his life, and Sefu Adeyemi, section chief of nothing, traitor to everything she'd been, sent her first stolen hour toward the man she'd abandoned—and prayed he was still alive to receive it.
