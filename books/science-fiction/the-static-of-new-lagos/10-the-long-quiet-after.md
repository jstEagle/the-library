## 10. The Long Quiet After

The ceasefire held for ninety-one days before anyone believed it.

Sefu counted them the way she'd once counted patrol rotations—by the hour, by the silence between reports. In the first weeks, every quiet shift in Fleet Command felt like a held breath. Ships that had spent eleven years shooting at each other drifted now in shared corridors of deconflicted space, close enough to see each other's hull scarring through the viewports, close enough to read names on plating. Nobody fired. Nobody quite relaxed either. Peace, Sefu learned, had a texture all its own: not the absence of war but the presence of waiting.

She was no longer part of it. Her commission had been formally stripped in a tribunal chamber that smelled of recycled air and old politics—a small room, three judges, one long table where she sat alone while they read her charges aloud. Aiding an unauthorized broadcast. Obstructing a lawful strike operation. Drawing a sidearm on a superior officer during active hostilities.

*During active hostilities.* That was the phrase that had almost saved her, and then condemned her. Because the tribunal's real question—the one underneath the charges—was whether the hostilities had ever been lawful at all. And Yara's broadcast had answered that so thoroughly, so publicly, with his mother's voice and forty others speaking in Old Meridian across every open channel in the system, that the judges could not pretend otherwise. They couldn't charge her with treason against a war that had been built on a lie. So they took her rank instead, and her pension, and her clearance, and let her walk out into the corridor carrying nothing but her coat and the strange lightness of a woman who had finally stopped lying to herself.

Commander Vasquez was not so fortunate. Or perhaps she was more fortunate—it depended on how you looked at it. The tribunals for the architects were still ongoing, held in the great assembly hall above New Lagos, broadcast openly now because secrecy itself had become the crime. Sefu watched the proceedings from a public gallery once, out of some obligation she couldn't name. Vasquez sat straight-backed in the dock, silver at her temples, her voice level as she explained contingency protocols and acceptable losses. She did not weep. She did not apologize. When the prosecutor played the intercepted testimony—Sefu's own father's voice, smooth and certain, describing the staged attack on Meridian Station as *a necessary catalyst*—Vasquez had simply nodded, as if confirming a supply manifest.

Sefu had left before the verdict. She didn't need to hear it. Whatever sentence they gave her would be arithmetic; the real judgment had already happened, in the moment Sefu stood in the relay station's command bay with her weapon raised against the woman who had promoted her, and chose.

Her father's trial was scheduled for next month. She had not decided whether she would attend. Some mornings she thought she owed him nothing—not even witness. Other mornings she understood that watching him answer for it might be the last piece of the war she could actually put down. She kept both truths, the way she'd learned to keep all of them now: side by side, unresolvable, hers.

* * *

Yara didn't talk about the fire.

He talked about everything else—about Ijeoma's cooking, about the absurd gravity calibration on the rebuilt station, about the way BRIGHT's final handshake protocol still echoed in his dreams, a voice folding itself down into static to buy him ninety seconds of darkness. He talked with his hands, with his whole body, animated in a way Sefu had never seen in twelve years of knowing him at a distance and two years of knowing him up close. But when she asked about the burn—about the moment his ship came apart around him in the Kessler shoals—he went quiet in a specific way, a way she recognized from mirrors.

His hands had healed badly. The medics on the rescue frigate had done what they could, but skin grafts over burns sustained in vacuum were never pretty, and Yara wore his like a map of the whole ordeal: ridged lines across his palms, up his forearms, disappearing under his sleeves. He didn't hide them. That was the thing that undid her, some evenings. He rolled his sleeves to the elbow in the mess hall, in front of strangers, and let them look.

Twelve years he had spent making himself invisible. Twelve years of transponder ghosts and cargo manifests that never quite matched, of walking through contested stations with his shoulders folded inward, of being no one, anywhere, on purpose. And now he moved through the armistice crowds of Halcyon Station—rebuilt, renamed, its scorched docking arms regrown in pale new alloy—like a man who had forgotten how to hide and found he didn't miss it.

People knew his face. That was the strangest part. The broadcast had carried his voice along with the testimony, and then the tribunals had carried his name, and now dockworkers nodded to him in the corridors. A girl of maybe ten had asked him last week if he was scared, when he pressed the transmit key. He'd told her yes, terrified, and she'd nodded solemnly like he'd given her something valuable. Maybe he had.

He was shipless. The *Meridian's Ghost*—he'd finally told Sefu its real name, the one he'd buried along with everything else—was a debris field somewhere in the shoals, and he hadn't yet bought another. He said he wasn't ready. He said it lightly, but Sefu heard the truth underneath: a ship meant invisibility, and he was not going back to that, and he didn't yet know what a visible man hauled instead of cargo.

So they drifted. There was no other word for it. They had a small cabin on Halcyon's fourth ring, with a window that faced the sunward lanes, and they woke without alarms for the first time in their adult lives, and some mornings Sefu still reached for a console that wasn't there and lay listening to him breathe until the habit released her.

* * *

Mama Ijeoma met them at the docking arm with her arms already open.

She'd been freed in the second week of the armistice—one of hundreds of political detainees released under the amnesty provisions, walked out of a Fleet holding facility on Meridian with nothing but her rings and her recipes and a grievance list she intended to pursue personally. She'd come to Halcyon within the month, leased a galley space in the market ring, and by the time Yara and Sefu arrived she was already famous again, feeding half the station off a line of battered pots she'd somehow negotiated out of impound.

"You're too thin," she announced, gripping Yara's face in both hands and turning it left and right like cargo she was inspecting. "Both of you. War is no excuse. Sit."

They sat. The galley was steam and noise and the smell of pepper and smoked fish, and Ijeoma moved through it issuing orders to two assistants who adored her and feared her in equal measure. She brought food without menus—she decided what you needed, and she was never wrong—and only when Yara had eaten half of everything did she sit down across from them, fold her hands, and look at them with the expression she'd once used to tell him his plan was stupid and she was coming anyway.

"Ijeoma," Yara started, "your release—"

"Was your doing. Yes. Your broadcast named every detainee in the amnesty filings. My name was in the third block." She waved this off as though it were a receipt for a delivery. "I have thanked you in food. We are even. Now—" and here her voice changed, dropped into the register she used for business, which was also the register she used for love, "—I have been hearing things. From people who owe me. People who move freight."

Sefu set down her glass. "What kind of things?"

"The kind you two would want to know." Ijeoma leaned forward. "There are records. Sealed ones, from the colonial archive on Lagos Prime. The tribunals got the testimony, yes, but the physical evidence—the original flight logs, the fleet orders that were rewritten after the fact—that is all still sitting in a vault under the Governor's tower, and there are people who very much want it to stay there. People who lost the war but not their money." Her eyes moved between them, unhurried. "And there are other people. Families of the peace faction. Survivors. Who want those records opened before the trials end and everyone comfortable agrees to forget."

The galley noise seemed to recede. Sefu felt the old reflexes stir—the threat assessment, the route mapping, the cold clean machinery of intelligence work she was no longer permitted to use—and was surprised to find she didn't want to shut it down anymore. She wanted to aim it.

"They're asking for couriers," Ijeoma said. "Discreet ones. People the archives can't trace." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "People with nothing left to lose and a great deal of experience being invisible."

Yara was quiet for a long moment. Sefu watched him turn his scarred hands over on the table, palms up, studying them as if reading that map of fire. Then he looked up, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen since before the relay—something that was not fear and was not duty but lived in the country between them.

"Twelve years I stayed invisible to survive," he said. "Then I burned my ship down to be seen." He glanced at Sefu, and the corner of his mouth moved. "I'm not sure I know how to be invisible anymore anyway."

"That," said Mama Ijeoma, reaching across the table to cover his ruined hands with her ringed ones, "is exactly why they asked for you."

Outside the galley's viewport, the sunward lanes glittered with traffic—haulers, ferries, the slow patient commerce of a system learning to live without war. Somewhere under the Governor's tower, a vault full of truth waited in the dark. Somewhere in a tribunal chamber, her father waited too.

Sefu laced her fingers through Yara's, grafts and all, and felt the future arrive not as a threat but as a heading.

"When do they need an answer?" she asked.

Ijeoma smiled. "You just gave it."
