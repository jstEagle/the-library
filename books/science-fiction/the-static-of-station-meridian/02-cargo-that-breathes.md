## 2. Cargo That Breathes

I didn't sleep. That was the first lie I told myself — that I was choosing to stay up, running diagnostics on the *Meridian*'s aging coolant loops because the work demanded it and not because Sefa's name was burning a hole in a hidden partition of my wrist deck like a coal I couldn't stop touching.

The station turned over to night cycle around me. Docking clamps groaned as freighters came and went on the far ring, and the static that gave this place its name hissed through every bulkhead — the old relay array's heartbeat, the sound I'd grown up thinking of as home talking in its sleep.

At 0300 station time, I stopped pretending and pulled up the cargo manifests instead.

The backup containers had been part of our rotation for six years. Standard cryo-freight, the paperwork said: industrial biological stock, cold-chain priority, handle with care, do not power down. They rode in Bay Three, twelve of them, each one the size of an escape pod and twice as heavy, their hulls scarred with the kind of dents you only get from rough transfers across a dozen different ports. We'd hauled them from Meridian to the outer depots and back again, over and over, a circuit so routine I could fly it blindfolded. Oduya never let anyone else touch them. Never let them sit longer than a month at any depot. Never explained why.

I'd asked once, early on, when I was still new enough to ask questions. She'd looked at me with those flat dark eyes and said, "Because they're fragile, Vane, and because nobody pays me enough to explain myself." Then she'd bought me a drink, which was her way of closing a door.

Now I stood in Bay Three with a maintenance light in one hand and my heart going like a failing engine, and I put my palm against the nearest container's access panel.

It should have demanded credentials I didn't have. Instead it blinked amber, thought about it, and opened.

That was the second thing I learned that night: whoever had sealed these containers wanted them openable — just not by everyone. The panel recognized something in my hand. My crew chip. Which meant Oduya had keyed them to the *Meridian* line itself, to us, to the scrappy little outfit nobody in the Concord registry bothered to remember existed.

The lid sighed up on hydraulics older than I was, and frost rolled out over my boots like breath.

Inside, curled under a skin of ice and gel, was a woman.

Not stock. Not samples. A person — mid-thirties maybe, dark hair drifting weightless in the suspension fluid, a thin diagnostic web stitched across her temple, her chest rising in a rhythm so slow I had to watch for a full minute before I believed it. Alive. Cold, but alive. And wired into the container's readout, a status line scrolling patient information in a format I knew, because I'd seen it once before, ten years ago, when they'd let families review the recovery records after the *Halcyon* went down:

**COLD-SLEEP BACKUP — HALCYON COLONY PROGRAM.**

The words swam. I gripped the container's edge and read them again, and again, until they stopped being letters and became a verdict.

Backups. That was the ugly little word the inquiry had used, the one that made the survivors' families scream at the hearing feeds. When the colony program loaded its ships, they hadn't trusted the cryo bays alone. They'd seeded *backup* sleepers through civilian freight — scattered across independent haulers, unlisted, deniable, so that if the *Halcyon* died, some fragment of the colony would survive anyway, asleep in the holds of ships like mine. Ten thousand colonists on the ship. A few hundred more, dispersed like seeds on the wind, in containers nobody was supposed to look inside.

And the *Meridian Line* — three ships, eleven crew, one captain with a temper and a secret — had been carrying them for six years.

I checked the next container. A man, older, frost in his beard. The next: a teenager, impossibly young, her file tagged **PRIORITY: FAMILY REUNIFICATION**, which meant someone aboard the *Halcyon* had been looking for her. Twelve containers. Twelve sleeping people. Six years of circuits, of careful routing, of Oduya quietly making sure they never stayed anywhere long enough for anyone to count them.

They weren't cargo. They were a lifeboat, and we were the oars.

My hands were shaking so badly I nearly dropped the maintenance light. Because here was the arithmetic that cracked me open all over again: if the program had seeded backups through independent freight, then somewhere in this network, in some other hold, in some other year —

Sefa could be in one of these.

Not dead in the wreck. Not a name on a forged manifest. Asleep. Waiting. Being carried, all this time, by hands like mine.

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough for the frost to creep up my sleeves. Long enough that when the voice came from behind me, I flinched hard enough to bang my elbow on the container's rim.

"You weren't supposed to see that yet."

Oduya stood in the bay hatchway, dressed, unhurried, holding two cups of station coffee like she'd been waiting outside the door for an hour, deciding whether to come in. Her face gave away nothing, which from Oduya was itself a kind of message.

"How long," I said. My voice came out scraped raw.

"Six years, four months." She crossed the bay and set one cup on a crate beside me. "Since the day you signed on. You want to know why I hired you? Your references were garbage and your flying was worse."

"Oduya—"

"I hired you because your sister's name was on the *Halcyon* roster, and because you needed a ship more than you needed the truth, and I wasn't sure which one would break you." She took a slow sip of her own coffee. "Turns out neither did. You just got angry. Angry's useful."

"That's—" I laughed, or something near it, and it hurt. "You've known this whole time. Who they are. What they are."

"I know what the Concord inquiry decided they were: lost property. Program assets, legally abandoned when the *Halcyon* was declared a total loss. Do you know what happens to abandoned cryo assets, Vane? Salvage rights. Auction. There are people who'd pay very well for healthy colonist bodies, and worse people who'd pay better." Her eyes flicked to the sleeper, to the slow rise and fall of her chest. "So the Meridian keeps moving them. Quietly. Forever, if we have to. That's the whole job. That's been the whole job since before you."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because last night you found a manifest that shouldn't exist, hid it in a ghost partition, and then spent four hours standing in a freezing cargo bay looking like someone had shot you." She said it flatly, without accusation, and somehow that was worse. "You think I don't watch my own bay feeds? Whatever you found, the fact that you're standing here instead of selling it means you might be worth trusting. So. Show me."

I hesitated. One breath. Two. Then I held out my wrist deck, and watched my captain's face as she read the name I couldn't say out loud.

She didn't gasp. She went very still, the way water goes still right before it freezes, and when she finally spoke her voice was quieter than I'd ever heard it.

"Sefa Vane. On the *Halcyon* master manifest. Ten years after the wreck." She looked up. "Where did this come from?"

"A routine audit. A data packet routed through a dead relay — somebody wanted it found, or wanted it lost, I can't tell which. The metadata's wrong, Oduya. Timestamps don't match. It's a forgery. I'm almost sure it's a forgery."

"Almost sure." She handed the deck back. "And if it isn't?"

"Then she's alive. Asleep somewhere. In a container like this one." I gestured at the woman in the ice, at the slow tide of her breathing. "In a hold like ours."

Oduya set down her coffee. For a long moment she just looked at the sleeper, and something moved behind her eyes that I'd never seen there — not softness exactly, but the weight of six years of nights exactly like this one, carried alone.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "If that manifest stays buried, nothing changes. The Meridian keeps flying its circuit, these people keep sleeping, and in ten years somebody else finds what you found, because secrets on stations have a half-life and yours is already decaying. If it surfaces—" she turned to face me fully, "—the Concord declares the backups state assets and confiscates every container in independent space. The Syndicate declares them salvage and takes the rest by force. And both of them burn through us to get there, because we're the ones holding the boxes."

"So we bury it deeper."

"We can't. It's already loose — you said yourself, someone routed it. Which means someone else has it, or will." She picked her coffee back up. "All we control is the story. Who tells it, when, and how loud."

"And if the story is true?" My throat closed around it. "If she's really out there?"

Oduya looked at me for a long time. Then she reached past me and, gently, closed the container's lid over the sleeping woman, sealing her back into the dark.

"Then we find her before anyone else does," she said. "And God help us, Vane, we'd better be fast."

* * *

We were not fast enough.

The alert came through at 1400 the next day, while I was in the cockpit running pre-flight on the *Meridian*'s short-haul tug, trying to lose myself in checklist liturgy. Oduya's voice crackled over the intercom, stripped of everything but urgency.

"Vane. Get down to the dock office. Now."

The dock office smelled of recycled air and old arguments. Oduya stood behind her desk with her arms folded, and across from her, filling the room the way winter fills a valley, stood a woman in Concord gray.

She was younger than I expected — inspector's tabs crisp at her collar, hair cut regulation-short, a slate case in one hand and the patient, terrible calm of someone who had never once needed to raise her voice. Her badge caught the light: **RHEE, INSPECTORATE OF NAVIGATION AND SALVAGE**.

"Captain Oduya." Rhee's voice was smooth as ballast stone. "Thank you for seeing me. This is a routine audit of independent carrier holdings — though I'll be honest, routine is a generous word for why I'm here." She set the slate case on the desk and opened it. Inside, glowing softly, lay a copy of the manifest. Sefa's name stared up at the ceiling like a ghost at its own funeral.

"Ten days ago," Rhee continued, "a fragment of the *Halcyon*'s master manifest surfaced on a dead relay near your traffic lanes. The Concord declared the wreck a total loss a decade ago. Every soul aboard was listed as recovered or unrecoverable." Her gaze lifted, level and unhurried, and settled first on Oduya — then, deliberately, on me. "And yet here is a name. So my question is simple, Captain. Where did this document pass through? And does the name mean what the Concord fears it means?"

Oduya's face was a wall. "Inspector, documents surface on relays out here the way debris does. Ask the relay operators."

"I intend to." Rhee closed the case, unhurried, and I understood suddenly that the gesture was the threat — the patience of someone who could wait years. "But relays route through carriers. Carriers keep logs. And the Meridian Line, Captain, flies more cold-chain freight through this sector than anyone." She smiled, small and bloodless. "Cold-chain freight. How interesting. I'll want to see your Bay Three."

Every drop of blood in my body went cold, colder than the sleepers, colder than the space between stars. Beside me, Oduya didn't move, didn't blink, and I realized I was watching a woman decide, in real time, how much of the truth to spend.

"The bay's sealed for decontamination," Oduya said. "Regulation. Give me forty-eight hours and full documentation, Inspector, and you can inspect whatever you like."

Rhee considered her. Then me. Then the case on the desk, where a dead girl's name waited in the dark.

"Forty-eight hours," she said. "I'll be aboard the station. Do try to make them interesting."

She left. The hatch sighed shut behind her, and Oduya let out a breath ten years long.

"Vane," she said quietly. "Start the engines. We're moving the cargo tonight."
