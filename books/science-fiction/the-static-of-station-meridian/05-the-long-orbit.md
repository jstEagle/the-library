## 5. The Long Orbit

The Meridian's nav console smelled like burnt sugar and old insulation, the way it always did when Oduya pushed the environmental recyclers past their rated load to keep the cargo bays cold. Ivo had complained about that smell for six years. Tonight she found it comforting — proof that the ship was alive, working, lying about its own temperature just like she was about to lie about everything else.

She had spent eleven hours building a ghost.

On her screen, the Halcyon's reconstructed flight path unspooled in amber thread: departure vector from Port Calloway, the burn profile of a ship running heavy with frozen sleepers, a course correction at the Verge buoy that no surviving log had ever recorded — because Ivo had invented it an hour ago, aged the telemetry with a decay script Oduya pretended not to know she had, and salted it with just enough authentic noise to make a forensic analyst sweat. The forgery didn't have to be perfect. It had to be *believable*, and believable meant flawed in the right places, like something real that had been dragged through ten years of radiation and neglect.

The trail pointed into the Kestrel Deep — four hundred thousand cubic light-seconds of nothing. No stations, no beacons, no reason for anyone to go there except to chase a lie.

"Course is locked," she said over her shoulder.

Oduya stood in the cockpit doorway with two cups of kaf and the expression of a man attending his own funeral out of politeness. "You're sure about the Deep."

"It's empty."

"That's what worries me. Empty space has a way of filling up." He set one cup on her console, close enough that the heat ghosted her wrist. "Rhee's shuttle docked twenty minutes ago. She requested passage on your run. *Requested.* Concord inspectors don't request."

"They subpoena."

"Not this one. She asked nicely, which is worse." He lowered himself into the copilot's chair with the groan of a man twice his age, though he was barely fifty. "Ivo. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong. It'll go badly, or it'll go slowly, but wrong implies there was a right version where we told the truth and everyone held hands." She sipped the kaf. Too sweet. He always made it too sweet, as if sweetness could offset the things he asked her to carry. "The truth gets those colonists — Sefa — turned into an asset. Or a bargaining chip. Or a crater. The lie buys us time, and time is the only currency either fleet respects."

"And if they catch you faking the trail?"

Ivo looked at the amber line stretching into nothing and felt the old grief shift its weight inside her, the way ballast shifts in a turn. "Then I'll be very sorry, posthumously."

* * *

They broke orbit from Station Meridian at 0400 station time, riding a freight window like smugglers through morning traffic. The Meridian Line ran six ships; theirs was the oldest, a hauler named the *Patient Answer* that Oduya had bought salvage-cheap and rebuilt with the stubbornness of a man who refused to let anything he loved be declared dead. Ivo knew the ship the way she knew her own hands: every rattle a syllable, every hum a mood.

Rhee rode in the second seat, silent for the first hour, watching the console readouts with the focused stillness of a heron watching water. Concord navy blue sat wrong on her — the uniform was tailored sharp but she wore it loose, sleeves rolled, like a costume she'd outgrown. When she finally spoke, it wasn't a question.

"You loved her. Your sister."

Ivo kept her eyes on the plot. "That's not in any file I signed off on."

"No. It's in how you built the manifest forgery." Rhee's voice was flat, almost gentle. "You gave the Halcyon a course correction at the Verge buoy. Ships don't correct there unless they're avoiding something. You made her a ship that flinched. That's not data, Vane. That's eulogy."

The cockpit hummed. Somewhere below them, three hundred tons of grain shifted in its cradles, and beneath the grain, in the double-walled bay Oduya had registered as refrigerated produce, the cold-sleep containers breathed their slow chemical breath — forty-one of them, people, woken years ago and ferried moonward one quiet run at a time, except for the handful too fragile to move. Except for everything Oduya had never said until three nights ago, when the word *Sefa* had come out of his mouth like a bullet he'd been holding between his teeth for a decade.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Ivo said.

"I think you do." Rhee turned back to the viewport, where the stars were doing their slow parallax crawl. "And I think you should know that Concord command doesn't want the Halcyon found. They want the Halcyon *claimed*. There's a difference, and the difference is roughly nine hundred sleeping civilians. If your sister is on that ship, the best thing you can do for her is make sure nobody finds anything."

It was the closest thing to an alliance Ivo had heard from anyone in a uniform, and it terrified her more than a threat would have. Threats she could plan around. Kindness from an inspector was a variable with no precedent.

* * *

The trouble came at the third waypoint, out past the last honest beacon, where the dark got thick enough to feel structural.

Kess's escort came out of it like a knife from a sleeve — a Syndicate corvette running dark until it wasn't, its running lights blooming eight kilometers off the *Patient Answer*'s port bow with the theatrical timing of a stage magician. Ivo had maybe four seconds of warning from the passive array: a thermal bloom, a drive signature spooling up, the particular arrogance of a ship that wanted to be seen.

"They're hailing," Rhee said. "Audio only."

"Of course audio only. Video would show me rolling my eyes." Ivo's hands moved without consulting her — throttle down, attitude jets breathing, the *Patient Answer* slewing two degrees to put her mass between the corvette and the faint smudge of the decoy drone she'd launched an hour ago, broadcasting its counterfeit Halcyon transponder into the Deep. "Put it through."

Kess filled the cockpit the way bad news fills a room. He was smaller than his reputation, a compact man with broker's hands and eyes that did arithmetic on everything they touched. His voice came through warm and reasonable, which was how the Syndicate's most dangerous people always sounded.

"Captain Vane. What a coincidence, meeting you out here. This far from anywhere, I mean."

"Space is big, Broker Kess. Coincidences are cheap."

"Mm. And yet here you are, hauling grain along a vector that happens to match a certain reconstructed flight path my analysts have been salivating over." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "You wouldn't know anything about that path, would you? Say, from a manifest that surfaced recently on your station?"

Ivo watched the corvette's position on her tactical display and did not watch Kess's face. "If you have questions about cargo manifests, the Meridian Line has a compliance office. They're very thorough. They'll send you a form."

"My analysts flagged something else," Kess said, conversational now, the way a man is conversational while walking toward you with a weapon behind his back. "Your ship's mass signature. Registered tonnage says grain hauler. Your drive plume says you're carrying something denser. Cold-mass dense." A smile entered his voice like frost entering a seal. "Cold-sleep dense."

Behind her, Ivo heard Rhee go very still.

"The plume's miscalibrated," Ivo said. "She's an old ship. I'll file a maintenance report."

"Do that." Kess's image flickered — he was moving, handing off something off-frame. "Here's my problem, Captain. My employers believe the Halcyon is drifting in this sector, fat with colonists worth more than a war. Concord believes the same thing, which means we're all going to be neighbors out here very soon, and neighbors in contested space have a way of shooting each other over misunderstandings. I'd hate for you to be a misunderstanding." The corvette's drive flare brightened. "So I'm going to escort you. Closely. And if your mass signature turns out to be exactly what I think it is, you and I are going to have a much more profitable conversation."

"We're not altering course for him," Rhee said quietly, once the channel closed.

"He's not asking us to alter course. He's asking us to be slow and predictable so he can keep scanning us." Ivo's mind was running three problems at once: the corvette's active sensor sweeps, ticking against the hull every ninety seconds; the decoy drone, now forty minutes ahead and broadcasting beautifully; and the fact that Kess's scan, if it resolved the bay under the grain, would end everything before the lie had done its work. "He wants to compare our density against the Halcyon's known tonnage. If we match, he doesn't need the drone. He needs *us*."

"So we don't match."

"So we don't match." Ivo pulled up the ballast controls and felt the old familiar clarity descend, the cold arithmetic of a pilot with no good options, only survivable ones. The *Patient Answer* carried emergency water ballast — sixty tons of it, for trim. Dumping it would lighten them, change their plume, wreck the density profile Kess was building. It would also leave them flying a half-empty ship through a corridor that was about to fill with two fleets.

"How much?" Oduya's voice, from the comm panel. He was in the engineering bay. Of course he was. He'd heard everything.

"All of it."

A pause long enough to hold a decade. "Do it," Oduya said. "Ship's mine. I say what she carries."

Ivo keyed the release. The *Patient Answer* shuddered as sixty tons of water vented into the dark in a glittering fan that any decent sensor would read as a ship suddenly, suspiciously lighter — a grain hauler that had lied about its load, embarrassed, correcting the record. Nothing to do with sleepers. Nothing to do with sisters. Just a scrappy line cutting corners, because scrappy lines always cut corners.

Kess's corvette adjusted its sweep pattern within a minute. Recalculating. Doubting.

"Nicely done," Rhee said, and there was something in her voice Ivo couldn't afford to name.

"Don't compliment me yet." Ivo watched the drone's transponder pulse steadily onward into the Kestrel Deep, a lighthouse walking into the sea. "We've got nineteen hours of fake trail left to sell, one Syndicate corvette glued to our flank, and—" The proximity alarm sang, soft and obscene. On the tactical display, a new contact had lit up beyond the corvette: heavy, fast, flying Concord colors, inbound on an intercept that would cross the *Patient Answer*'s bow with less than two kilometers to spare.

Rhee leaned forward and read the contact's designation, and Ivo watched the color leave her face.

"That's not an escort," Rhee said. "That's the *Vigilant Anchor*. That's a fleet flagship, and it's answering to no one I know." She looked at Ivo, and for the first time since Port Calloway the inspector sounded afraid. "Commander, whatever story you're selling — someone just arrived who intends to buy it with guns."

Ahead, the drone pulsed its counterfeit heart into the Deep. Behind, the corvette held its patient, hungry distance. And dead ahead, growing by the second, came the shape of everything Ivo had been trying to buy time against — arriving early, arriving armed, arriving now.
