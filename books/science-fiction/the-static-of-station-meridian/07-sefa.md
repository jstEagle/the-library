## 7. Sefa

The moon didn't have a name on any chart Ivo had ever flown. Rhee's fragment called it only by a catalog string — *KX-447c, unlisted, tidal-locked, atmosphere marginal* — and the fragment had been right about everything else, so Ivo trusted it the way you trust a stranger who has just handed you a knife: carefully, and with your palm wrapped around the blade.

She came in low over the dark side, engines trimmed to a whisper, the Meridian's borrowed skiff hugging the terminator where the light broke across the ridgeline like a slow tide. Below her, the surface was a study in contradictions — black basalt plains veined with frost, and then, blooming out of the frozen waste like something impossible, green.

Not the green of algae mats or lichen crusts. *Agricultural* green. Terraced fields, irrigated by channels that caught the thin perpetual dawn and threw it back in long silver ribbons. Dome clusters strung along a river valley like beads on a wire, their panels dark against the stars, their airlocks glowing warm amber at the seams.

Ivo's hands went still on the controls.

Ten years. Ten years of hauling cold-sleep containers and telling herself they were ballast, spare parts, contraband, anything but what they were. Ten years of carrying a sister's name in her chest like a splinter she couldn't dig out. And all that time, the answer had been sitting here in the dark, growing potatoes.

"You absolute idiots," she said to the valley below, and her voice cracked in the middle of it. "You beautiful, stubborn idiots."

The skiff's comm crackled before she'd even finished her descent vector. A woman's voice, flat and unamused, with the clipped cadence of someone who had rehearsed this greeting.

"Unidentified vessel, you are being tracked by three emplacements. Cut your engines and follow the beacon, or we will follow you with something less polite."

"I'm not armed," Ivo said. "And I'm not Concord."

A pause. Long enough for Ivo to count her own heartbeats — nine of them — before the voice returned, and something in it had shifted, gone quieter, almost afraid.

"Say your name."

"Ivo Vane. Meridian Line."

The silence that followed was worse than any threat. When the beacon finally lit, pulsing amber toward a landing pad carved into the ridge above the first dome, Ivo's hands were shaking so badly she had to run the descent assist twice.

* * *

They met her at the airlock with a dozen people and not one weapon showing, which was its own kind of message. Farmers, mostly — that was the first thing that struck her, how ordinary they looked. Work jackets, sun-worn faces, a teenager with dirt to the elbows and an expression of open curiosity. The Halcyon's sleeping thousands, awake and *gardening*, and the most dangerous thing in sight was a rake leaning against the dome wall.

An older man stepped forward, gray at the temples, hands like a man who had spent a decade learning soil. "Vane," he said. "The navigator. Oduya's people."

"You know about Oduya." It wasn't a question, but he answered it anyway.

"He's pulled us out of three audits and two inspections over the years. We owe him more than we can say." His eyes went over her shoulder to the skiff, then back, and Ivo watched him arrive at the same conclusion everyone on this moon must have arrived at the moment her name came through. "You should talk to Sefa. She's been waiting since the beacon tripped. She's — " he hesitated, choosing the word with visible care — "she's not in a gentle mood."

"Where is she?"

He nodded toward the largest dome, and Ivo walked, and the crowd parted around her the way water parts around something it hasn't decided whether to drown.

* * *

She found Sefa in the hydroponics bay, and the first thing Ivo felt was not joy. It was the vertigo of a map that no longer matched the territory.

Her sister was forty-one years old. Sefa had been thirty-one when the Halcyon broke apart, and Ivo had spent ten years grieving a woman frozen at thirty-one — a woman with the crooked grin and the restless hands and the terrible singing. The woman standing among the bean trellises had the same face, but time had gotten to it here, in the real, in the waking world. Lines around the eyes. Gray threading the black hair she'd tied back with a strip of cargo strap, because apparently some things were eternal. She was taller than Ivo remembered, or Ivo had shrunk, or grief had simply been measuring wrong all along.

"You came in an unarmed skiff," Sefa said, without turning around. "To a moon that three war fleets are about to start drawing lines around."

"Hello to you too."

Sefa turned. For one raw, unguarded second, her face did something complicated — the grin flickering up out of the past, the eyes going bright, the whole architecture of ten years' composure threatening to come down at once. Ivo took a step toward her. Sefa held up a hand, and Ivo stopped like she'd hit glass.

"Don't," Sefa said. "Not yet. Because if you hug me right now I'll forgive you, and I am *not* done being furious, and I have been furious for six days, and I need to finish it."

"Six days?"

"The manifest." Sefa's voice went flat and hard, a blade laid on a table. "The forged manifest with my name on it. The one you hid in a cargo audit file like a teenager hiding a love letter. Do you have any idea what that did?"

Ivo had imagined this reunion a hundred times in the six days since Rhee's fragment had come through — in the cockpit, in the skiff's bunk, in the long hours of the dark-side approach. In every version, there were tears. In some of them there was laughter. In none of them had she imagined standing in a greenhouse smelling of tomato vines and nutrient solution while her dead sister read her the riot act.

"I needed to know," Ivo said. "The manifest was a forgery, Sefa. I made it. I pulled your name off a memorial list and I put it on a ghost ship's manifest because I couldn't — " Her voice caught. She pushed through it. "Because I couldn't stand that the universe had just *deleted* you. No wreck recovered. No pods found. Nothing. I wanted someone to come looking. I wanted *anyone* to come looking, because if nobody was looking, then you were really gone."

"So you made the Concord and the Syndicate look." Sefa's hands were trembling now; she shoved them into her jacket pockets like she was arresting them. "You made two fleets look. Do you know what happens when two fleets look for a colony ship with four thousand sleepers aboard? They don't send a card and flowers, Ivo. They send *marines*. They send claim-jumpers. They send people like the Syndicate broker who's already been asking about salvage rights on a ship that never existed."

"I know."

"You *know*." Sefa laughed, and it was an awful sound, all the terrible singing ground down into gravel. "We built this. Ten years. Do you understand what it took? We woke up in pods that were never meant to open for another century, half of us sick, all of us stranded, and we *built this*. Every terrace. Every seal on every dome. We buried eleven people on that ridge — " her voice finally broke, just once, and she hated it, Ivo could see her hating it — "eleven, Ivo, and we kept going, because there was no one coming. There was never anyone coming. And now, because my little sister couldn't let a ghost rest, there are *two navies* coming, and they're bringing lawyers and missiles, and they both think we're cargo."

The word landed like a blow. *Cargo.* Ivo thought of the containers she'd hauled for years — the ones she'd finally learned were people, the ones Oduya had been protecting, the ones that had led her here. She thought of every manifest she'd ever signed that reduced a human life to a mass and a classification code.

"I didn't know you were alive," Ivo said quietly. "I want you to understand that. I forged that manifest out of grief, not out of hope. Hope would have been kinder. Hope would have meant I thought there was something to find. I did it because I couldn't accept that there wasn't."

Sefa stared at her for a long time. The hydroponics bay hummed around them — pumps, fans, the soft drip of the irrigation lines, the sound of a world that had been kept alive by hand for a decade. Somewhere outside, a child shouted, and the sound of it went through Ivo like a spear, because Sefa had missed ten years of that, and Ivo had missed nothing, and somehow they had both been robbed.

"You look like Mom," Sefa said finally, and her voice came apart on the last word. "You got old. You got old without me, you — I was asleep, and you got *old*."

"You didn't," Ivo said. "That was the problem. I had a sister frozen at thirty-one and I couldn't bury her and I couldn't find her and I couldn't — " She crossed the distance then, fury or forgiveness be damned, and Sefa didn't stop her this time, and the hug was nothing like the versions Ivo had rehearsed. It was desperate and graceless and Sefa was gripping her back hard enough to bruise, and they were both crying into each other's jackets in a greenhouse full of beans on a moon that wasn't on any map.

"I'm sorry," Ivo said into her sister's shoulder. "I'm sorry. I'll fix it. I don't know how yet, but I'll fix it."

"You can't fix it," Sefa said, but she said it into Ivo's hair, and her hands said something else entirely. "You can't unforge a document. You can't unfly two fleets. All you can do is stand between them and us, and Ivo — " she pulled back, and her eyes were dry now, and terrible, and clear — "I need you to understand what standing between them means. Because the beacon tripped forty minutes ago, and my watch officer says the long-range array just picked up drive signatures. Two clusters. Heavy tonnage. Coming out of the deep on intercept."

Ivo felt the floor of the world tilt.

"How long?"

"At their burn rates?" Sefa checked a battered wrist display, and the number on it was small, and getting smaller. "Nineteen hours. Maybe twenty. Concord on one vector. Syndicate on the other."

* * *

They gathered in the colony's command dome — a room that had clearly been a cargo hold once, its walls still stamped with Halcyon shipping codes, now crowded with salvaged consoles and hand-drawn star charts. The older farmer, whose name was Dessen, laid out the situation in the voice of a man who had run disaster drills for a decade. Sefa stood at his shoulder. The colonists filled the room, and their faces were the faces Ivo had seen on the Meridian a thousand times — people doing arithmetic with their lives.

"Options," Dessen said. "We run. We don't have ships that can outrun either fleet. We hide. They'll sweep the moon inside a day. Or we talk."

"Talk to *what*?" someone called from the back. "A missile?"

"Not a missile." Every head turned to Ivo. Her mouth was dry, and her heart was doing something strange and steady, the way it did in the last seconds before a burn. "A broadcast. The Meridian's array is old, but it's old in the good way — it can punch through jamming, it can hit both fleets at once. Everything. The wreck. The pods. The colony. The forgery." She looked at Sefa. "All of it. Including my confession. If they're going to come for you, they're going to have to do it knowing exactly what they're coming for, and exactly what it cost to build this."

Sefa held her gaze for a long, long moment. Then she nodded, once, and turned to Dessen. "Get her to your transmitter."

Outside the dome, far off across the frozen dark, the first stars of the approaching fleets were already rising — two clusters of lights where no lights should be, burning steady and cold, like eyes opening.

Nineteen hours.
