## 2. Halcyon Descending

The sky over the archipelago had been the color of old pewter for three days, and Elodie Thackeray had learned, in eleven years of watching it, to read its moods the way sailors once read water. A flat pewter meant wind. A bruised violet meant storm. But this morning the sky held a color she had no name for — a pale, wrong brightness, like light passing through something that shouldn't be there.

She was on the roof terrace of the vault's upper module, chipping rime from the solar array with a rubber mallet, when she saw it.

At first she took it for a meteor. It came down out of the north trailing fire, a long incandescent scratch across the grey, and her first thought — absurd, unprofessional, human — was *finally*. Eleven years of tending a signal that answered nothing, of logging temperatures into a ledger no one would ever audit, of speaking aloud to rooms that only echoed. If the sky was finally falling, at least it was falling *toward* her.

Then the object flared its braking burn, and the fire became a controlled deceleration, and Elodie dropped the mallet in the snow because meteors did not brake.

The ship came down eight kilometers east, on the ice shelf beyond the black ridge, and the sound of its arrival reached her a full minute later — not a crash but a long grinding exhalation, ice shrieking and then settling, like some enormous animal turning over in its sleep. The shelf would hold or it wouldn't. That wasn't hers to decide. What was hers to decide was already made: she was pulling on her expedition shell, checking the oxygen mix in her sled's tanks, and keying the vault's exterior log with hands that would not quite stay steady.

*Exterior excursion 4,102. Unidentified descent event, grid northeast. Investigating.*

Eleven years of entries that said *all nominal*, and now this.

* * *

The ice shelf near the landing site was scarred in a way that made her stomach tighten. The ship sat canted at fifteen degrees, half its hull still smoking, and it was *vast* — a deep-space vessel, kilometer-class, the kind of ship that belonged to history books and museum holo-archives. Its hull bore a name in letters tall as buildings: **HALCYON**. Under the name, smaller, a registry code that Elodie's eyes snagged on and refused to release: an old Consortium charter mark, the kind that hadn't been issued since before the Collapse.

*That's impossible,* she thought, and then, more honestly: *that's impossible unless everything I've been keeping alive down here is also impossible.*

Her beacon pulsed beneath her feet as she crossed the ice, that patient three-note handshake she'd maintained every day for a decade without knowing what it was saying. She'd never told anyone about it. There had been no one to tell.

The boarding ramp hung open, bent, exhaling warm air that steamed against the cold like breath from a wound.

"Hello?" Her voice fell flat on the ice. "This is Elodie Thackeray, keeper of the Meridian Vault. Do you need assistance?"

Nothing. Then, from somewhere deep in the ship, a sound she needed several seconds to identify, because it was so entirely wrong for a kilometer-long Consortium relic: a child crying.

She climbed.

* * *

Inside, the Halcyon was a cathedral that had been through a war. Emergency lighting strobed amber along corridors sized for giants. Frost ferns bloomed and died on bulkheads as environmental systems fought themselves. And scattered through it — sitting in doorframes too wide for them, curled into acceleration couches built for adults, standing at control consoles with their chins barely clearing the displays — were children.

A dozen of them. Maybe more, deeper in the ship. All of them wearing pressure suits scaled for grown bodies, cuffs rolled and cinched with improvised bands, helmets carried under their arms like burdens they didn't know how to put down. They watched her come with eyes that were children's eyes and expressions that were not, and Elodie felt the hair rise on her arms beneath the suit.

One of them, a boy of perhaps nine, looked up at her and said, in a voice cracked with exhaustion: "Are you ground control? We've been trying to raise ground control for six days."

"I'm — I'm the nearest human being," Elodie said. "I'm here to help."

"That's what Commander said you'd say." He pointed down the corridor. "She's in the command ring. She said don't let anyone touch the navigation core until she gives the word." A pause, and then, with the terrible gravity of a child repeating an adult sentence he doesn't fully understand: "She said the mission doesn't end just because we got small."

* * *

Ilsa Reyes had wedged herself into the commander's chair with a folded blanket behind her back so she could reach the console, and when Elodie came through the hatch, the girl did not stand. Commanders, apparently, did not stand.

She was maybe ten years old. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, someone's attempt at regulation length done by unsteady hands. Her pressure suit bore a name tape that read REYES above a rank insignia and a service record bar longer than any ten-year-old in history should have earned. Her left hand rested on the armrest; her right held a data slate she was reading with fierce concentration, lips moving slightly.

"You're not Consortium," she said without looking up.

"No," Elodie agreed. "I'm not."

"The Consortium team is supposed to meet us here. Six days overdue." Now the girl looked up, and Elodie saw that her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, the eyes of someone who had cried until crying stopped working. "You're not them either. So who are you?"

"Elodie Thackeray. I keep the seed vault forty kilometers west. I saw your descent. I came to help."

Something flickered across the girl's face — suspicion, calculation, and underneath both, so briefly Elodie almost missed it, hope. "Thackeray," she repeated. "Like Doctor Adaeze Thackeray."

The name went through Elodie like cold water. "Where did you hear that name?"

"It's all over our mission files. The beacon protocol, the selection architecture, the reactor licensing — half the foundational documents are authored T-h-a-c-k-e-r-a-y, A." The girl pronounced it carefully, the way children do when they've taught themselves to read words bigger than they are. "I memorized the authorship index during the transit. I memorize things now. My memory used to be automatic." Her jaw tightened. "It isn't anymore."

"Ilsa," said one of the other children from the corridor behind Elodie — the crying boy, recovered now, hovering. "Commander, the med bay auto-doc finished Ensign Okafor's scan. You said you wanted—"

"I said I'd come." But she didn't move yet. She was looking at Elodie with an intensity that had no childhood in it at all. "Before anything else. Before medical, before shelter, before whatever help you're offering. I need you to answer a question, Keeper Thackeray, and I need you to answer it straight, because everyone on this ship has been lying to me by omission since the day we launched and I am very tired of it."

"Ask me."

"What is the beacon for?"

Elodie opened her mouth. Closed it. Eleven years of maintenance logs, calibration cycles, signal integrity reports — and not one line explaining what the signal *said*, or to whom, or why a seed vault in the high Arctic needed a voice that carried past the moon.

"I don't know," she said finally. "I've asked that question my whole life. The archives are scrubbed. My predecessor's records are scrubbed. Whoever built it wanted the answer kept from the people who tend it."

Ilsa Reyes nodded slowly, and something in her posture shifted — not softening exactly, but a decision being made. She turned to the boy in the doorway. "Okafor. Bring me the mission case. The physical one. Code Reyes-Actual."

He hesitated. "Commander, that's flagged—"

"I know what it's flagged. Bring it anyway." And then, to Elodie, while the boy ran: "We were coming here. That's the part nobody will tell me straight, but I've pieced it together. The Halcyon's destination was always this latitude, this longitude. Our mission files reference a facility, a reactor, two — " she frowned, hunting for a word that kept sliding away from her, and Elodie watched her lose it and hate losing it — "two somethings. Two of something, underground. And a protocol that requires authorization from someone called the keeper."

The boy returned, staggering under a hardcase nearly half his size. Ilsa keyed the lock with her palm, then paused, hand flat on the lid.

"When we hit the temporal shear off the dead star," she said, conversationally, terribly, "I was thirty-four years old. I had a wife in cryo-bay two and a daughter on the colony fleet and twenty-two years of service, and I knew this ship down to its last coolant line. Then the shear passed and I was this." She held up her own small hand and studied it like a stranger's. "My body is nine years old. My memories come back in pieces, like someone shook the box. But the mission — " she tapped her temple — "the mission is stitched deepest of all. Whatever they put in us last is what survived best."

She lifted the lid. Inside, nested in foam, lay a slate older than any aboard, its casing stamped with the Consortium charter mark and a single word: *MERIDIAN*.

"We were sent to open something," Ilsa said. "And I think you're the lock."

Elodie took the slate with hands gone numb that had nothing to do with the cold. The screen woke at her touch, and the first thing it displayed was a directory tree — and at its root, a file path she recognized with a lurch, because she had seen its ghost in her predecessor's scrubbed records a hundred times and never known what she was looking at:

`/meridian/selection/protocol/author_thackeray_a/`

"My mother," Elodie said quietly, "built the thing you were sent to open."

For the first time since Elodie had boarded, Commander Ilsa Reyes — thirty-four years old inside a child's body, keeper of a mission she could only half remember — looked afraid.

"Then you'd better read what she wrote," the girl said, "before the Consortium gets here. Because in my recovered flight logs, there's a message queue. And six days ago, somebody on the colony fleet sent a transmission ahead of us."

She turned the slate around. The message header glowed in the amber light:

**PRIORITY — ADVANCE TEAM — ASSET RECOVERY — MERIDIAN SITE — ETA 72 HOURS.**

"They're not coming to rescue us," Ilsa said. "They're coming to collect what we found."
