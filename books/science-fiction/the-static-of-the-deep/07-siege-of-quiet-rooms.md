## 7. Siege of Quiet Rooms

The ship came in low over the glacier at dawn, and it did not land so much as *arrive* — a black wedge of Consortium engineering settling onto the ice shelf with the courtesy of a guest who knows the house is his. Elodie watched from the observation blister, coffee going cold in her hand, and counted the running lights: six. A diplomatic configuration. She had read enough of her predecessor's logs to know what that meant.

"They're polite," she said to Ilsa, who stood beside her on a crate to reach the glass.

"Polite is worse," Ilsa said. Her voice was still a child's voice, but the cadence underneath it belonged to a woman who had negotiated salvage rights in three star systems. "Polite means they think they've already won."

By the time Elodie had pulled on her cold gear and crossed the hundred meters of wind-scoured ice to the landing pad, the airlock was open and a woman was descending the ramp with her hands visible and empty — a gesture performed slowly enough that everyone would notice it. Behind her, four figures in matte-grey exosuits stood in a line that was too straight to be anything but a threat.

"Dr. Thackeray," the woman said. "Or should I say Keeper Thackeray. Marguerite Voss-Adler, advance liaison, Consortium Continuity Directorate." She extended a gloved hand. Her face was kind the way a heated room is kind in a blizzard — you wanted to step into it, and you knew what it cost. "We've come about your beacon."

"It answers no known call," Elodie said, not taking the hand.

"We know." Voss-Adler smiled. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Ilsa watched them from the maintenance gallery above the vault's central atrium, pressed flat against the glass with two of her crew — Okonjo, who was nine years old and had been a propulsion engineer, and the twins Sefa and Ryn, who shared one memory between them like a coat neither could wear alone. Below, the Consortium team moved through the corridors their grandparents' money had built, and Ilsa felt something hot and complicated rise in her chest.

She remembered this building. Not with her eyes — with her *bones*. She had been here before the Halcyon launched, twenty-six years old and furious, standing in this atrium while a man in a grey suit explained that the laborers' archive would be maintained as a matter of principle. Principle. She remembered thinking: *principle is what you call it when you don't intend to pay.*

"Commander," Okonjo whispered. "They're heading for the reactor access."

"I know." Ilsa's small fingers found the maintenance panel beside her, and her adult hands remembered what to do even through the child's knuckles. The panel opened under her touch. "They'll want authorization codes. They'll want *her* to turn the key, because if they force it, the selector logs it, and the fleet can't have that in the record."

"Why not?"

"Because the record is all they have left." Ilsa slid into the duct behind the panel, and the twins followed, and Okonjo brought up the rear with a multitool clutched like a sidearm. "Everything else burned. So we're going to make sure whatever gets written down tonight is true."

* * *

In the vault's conference room — a room designed for eight and occupied by two, because Elodie had never once needed more — Voss-Adler set a slate on the table and turned it toward her with two fingers.

"Your mother," she said.

Elodie looked. Dr. Adaeze Thackeray, younger than Elodie had ever known her, standing on the Halcyon's launch gantry in a flight suit, caught mid-laugh by someone off-frame. It was a good photograph. It was chosen to be a good photograph.

"She didn't die out there," Voss-Adler said gently. "The Halcyon made its jump. The crew aged backward; the ship limped home across eleven years. Your mother survived the shear. She's alive, Keeper. In the elite archive's sister facility aboard the fleet itself — frozen, but alive, and recoverable. All you have to do is authorize the Cradle of First Families. Wake them, and we wake her. One signature."

Elodie kept her face still. Eleven years alone had taught her that. But inside, something turned over — her mother, laughing on a gantry, alive, *reachable* — and she understood with terrible clarity that this was the whole design. Not the armed men. Not the legal language. This photograph, placed on this table, in front of the daughter.

"You've had this image for years," Elodie said.

"We received it when the Halcyon's telemetry reached the fleet. Fourteen months ago."

"And you waited until now."

"The timing of our arrival is a matter of orbital mechanics," Voss-Adler said, and for the first time something flickered behind the kindness — embarrassment, maybe, or its expensive cousin, discretion.

"You waited until the countdown was nearly spent," Elodie said. "You waited until whoever held this key had no time to think. That's not orbital mechanics. That's a siege with good manners."

Voss-Adler folded her hands. "I'm authorized to be honest with you, Doctor, because my principals believe honesty will move you where threats would not. Yes. We need your conscious authorization. The protocol your mother built requires it — a waking mind, choosing freely, on the record. We cannot fake it. Believe me, we tried, before we understood what she'd done." A pause. "She rigged it, you know. Against us. Against her own class. Perhaps against you."

"I know," Elodie said. "I've been reading her logs."

Something moved through the room then — a hum in the floor, faint, wrong, like a heartbeat skipping. Voss-Adler glanced upward. Elodie did not.

"I'd like tea," Elodie said. "And I'd like everything you have on the Halcyon's return trajectory. If I'm going to sign away the future of the human race's most inconvenient inheritance, I want to see the math."

* * *

In the ductwork above the reactor access corridor, Ilsa lay on her stomach in a space built for cables, not children, and rerouted a civilization.

"Okonjo. Coolant junction four."

"On it." Small hands, vast knowledge. He stripped the junction and bridged it with a shunt from the seed-vault climate loop, and somewhere below them the reactor's selector began drinking from a supply line that no longer existed. "Done. It'll show nominal for six hours, then it starts lying."

"Lying is fine. Lying is *tactics*." Ilsa wormed forward to the selector's diagnostic spine and jacked in the improvised interface the crew had built from drone parts and prayer. Data scrolled up her retinal feed. "Sefa, tell me what you see."

Sefa's voice came thin through the comm bead. "Selector's live. Both cradles drawing. Elite archive at ninety-one percent integrity. Laborers' at eighty-eight." A pause, and then, smaller: "There are names, Commander. Thousands of names. I can see the laborers' roster. It's just — it's a manifest. Like cargo."

"It was cargo to them," Ilsa said. "It isn't anymore. Ryn — the audit trail. When the selector fires, where does the log go?"

"Beacon buffer first. Then fleet relay."

"No," Ilsa said. "It goes to the beacon buffer, and then it goes wherever the keeper says it goes. Okonjo, cut the fleet relay. Physically. Make it look like ice damage."

Okonjo hesitated. "Commander, that's — they'll know someone—"

"They'll know," Ilsa agreed. "Eventually. After it matters." She stared at the scrolling data, at the two lists of sleeping humanity balanced on a failing reactor, and felt the full weight of her patchwork self settle: the child's body, the officer's certainty, the missing years like teeth knocked out of a jaw. "My crew died becoming children so that this choice couldn't be made by machines or money. The least we can do is buy the person making it some room to breathe."

Ryn's voice, quiet: "Is this mutiny, Commander? Against the Consortium?"

Ilsa thought about it honestly, the way she thought about everything now — slowly, with a child's refusal to skip steps.

"No," she said. "Mutiny is against your own ship. This is against people who sent children to do their bargaining." She closed the diagnostic. "It's the opposite of mutiny. It's showing up for duty."

* * *

The tea was real — Elodie hoarded actual leaves against exactly this kind of day, though she had imagined needing them for celebration — and Voss-Adler drank it with genuine pleasure, which somehow made everything worse.

"Your mother was brilliant," Voss-Adler said, over the second cup. "You should know that the Directorate doesn't demonize her. What she built was elegant. The free-choice clause, the fail-deadly selector — she understood that an inheritance unearned is just theft with better manners. We simply believe the moment for her principles has passed. Survival first. Justice later. It's not hypocrisy, Doctor. It's triage."

"Triage," Elodie repeated. "Which patient do you pull from the water first?"

"The one who can swim, obviously."

"And the other one drowns while you explain why that's medicine." Elodie set down her cup. Through the floor, faintly, she felt the reactor's rhythm change — a stutter only eleven years of listening could catch. Ilsa was working. Good. "Ms. Voss-Adler. Let me ask you something as one keeper to another. You've seen the rosters. Both of them."

"I've seen summaries."

"I've seen the names." Elodie let that sit. "Eight thousand people in the families' cradle. Forty-one thousand in the workers'. And the workers' cradle wasn't charity — those people were conscripted. Debt-bonded. Their descendants are the ones crewing your colony fleet right now, cleaning your air, raising your children. When the fleet arrives and finds out which cradle you chose—" She stopped herself. Careful. Careful. "If you choose."

Voss-Adler was quiet for a long moment. Then, without changing expression, she said: "You're stalling."

"I'm thinking," Elodie said. "It looks similar. It takes longer."

"There are four hours left on the reactor curve, Doctor. My team has secured the surface access. Whatever your young friends are doing in the walls — and yes, we noticed the coolant anomaly, we're not fools, we're only polite — it buys you hours, not victory." Voss-Adler leaned forward, and the kindness finally fell away, and what was underneath was tired and human and afraid. "Give me the authorization. I wake the families, I wake your mother, and I spend the rest of my career making sure the workers' cradle gets woken next. That is a real promise. I am allowed to make it. Most of my principals would rather I hadn't told you that much."

Elodie looked at her — really looked — and saw the thing she had not expected to see: a woman who had done the arithmetic of her own soul and found it short, offering the only currency she had left.

"That promise dies with you," Elodie said softly. "And you know it. Whoever comes after you reads the same ledger your principals read, and forty-one thousand names are a rounding error in it." She stood. "I need to walk the seed floors. Alone. It's what keepers do before they decide. Give me one hour."

"One hour," Voss-Adler said. "Then I stop being able to protect you from my colleagues."

* * *

In the dark of the seed floor, among the humming racks of ten thousand years of wheat and rice and sorghum, Elodie pressed her palm against the cold steel and spoke aloud, though no one was meant to hear.

"Ilsa. Status."

Ilsa's voice came through the hidden channel, child-bright and iron-calm. "Fleet relay's cut. Selector's blind except to the beacon. We own the doors, the lights, and the truth. Commander Reyes reports ready."

"Mother's alive," Elodie said. "On the fleet. Frozen. They offered me the trade."

Silence for a moment. Then Ilsa, very quietly: "What are you going to do?"

Elodie closed her eyes. Somewhere above her, four armed strangers guarded a door that no longer mattered. Somewhere below her, forty-nine thousand sleepers breathed on borrowed power. Somewhere beyond the sky, a fleet of the desperate and the entitled crawled toward a planet that had already begun to thaw.

"I'm going to stop deciding alone," she said, and keyed the beacon to transmit.

Above the ice, the signal nobody commissioned went out at last with words inside it — both rosters, both crimes, one confession signed *A. Thackeray* — streaming toward every surviving ear on Earth.

The replies began within minutes.
