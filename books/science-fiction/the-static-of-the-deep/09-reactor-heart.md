## 9. Reactor Heart

The selector room sat at the bottom of a shaft that Adaeze Thackeray had never put on any blueprint.

Elodie had found it the way she found everything in this place — by following her mother's ghosts through the maintenance schematics, tracing a conduit that carried power to nowhere and asking the only question that mattered: *where does it actually go?* The answer had been down. Always down. Eleven years of her life spent walking corridors above a room she was never meant to find, and now she stood at its threshold with a child in a grown woman's pressure suit and forty minutes on the reactor clock.

The shaft ladder was rusted at the rungs but sound at the welds. Elodie went first. The cold came up to meet her, that deep geological cold that lived beneath the permafrost like a second ocean, and by the time her boots hit the lower platform her breath was falling as snow.

The selector was smaller than she'd imagined. A cradle of black alloy the size of a coffin, cradling two fiber-optic spines — one silver, one copper — that rose out of the floor and vanished into the dark like the roots of an inverted tree. Between them, a single interface node pulsed with the reactor's dying light. Amber. Then red. Then amber again. A heartbeat deciding.

"That's it?" Ilsa said, dropping down beside her. The girl landed wrong — her legs were too short for the suit's calibration — and caught herself on the alloy with both hands. Up close, in the reactor's pulse-light, her face was a study in contradictions: a child's soft jaw, and eyes that had already done their grieving. "It doesn't look like a god."

"It was never a god," Elodie said. "It was a lock. My mother built the lock, and then she built a flaw into it, and then she left so no one could ever find the flaw and fix it." She knelt beside the node, pulling the interface kit from her pack. Her hands were steady. She was almost surprised by that. "The flaw is that it can't be overridden by command. Only by consent — a conscious, present, biometric choice. She wanted a human being to have to *stand here*."

"And now we're standing here."

"Now we're standing here."

Ilsa crouched next to her, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The reactor hummed through the rock, a sound Elodie had lived with so long it had become silence. Somewhere above them — three decks, two bulkheads, one very long corridor — the Consortium advance team was arguing with a sealed door.

* * *

The breach alarm had come twenty minutes ago, and it had not been dramatic. That was the worst part. No klaxons, no shouting. Just a soft chime and a calm voice announcing: *Perimeter integrity: compromised. Perimeter integrity: compromised.* The Consortium team didn't break things. They *unlocked* them, with codes bought from people Elodie had trusted with her supply manifests, and they walked in apologizing.

But they hadn't reached the shaft. Because between them and the shaft stood six children.

"Vault, status," Elodie said now, into her wrist comm, not looking up from the selector node.

Tomas's voice came back, thin and high and absolutely level — a nine-year-old reading from a checklist he'd memorized in another life. "Bulkhead C is welded from the inside. We used the repair torch. Kemi says it'll hold eleven minutes against their cutter. Bulkhead D is flooded with waste coolant — non-lethal, mostly, but their suits will read it as a contamination event and their protocols will force a decon cycle. That buys us six more minutes after C fails."

"Kemi," Elodie said. "The coolant. You're sure it's non-lethal."

A pause. Then Kemi's voice, younger than Tomas's, older than her years: "It's the stuff we showered in on the Halcyon for four years, ma'am. If it was going to kill us, it already would have."

Elodie closed her eyes. Opened them. "Good work. All of you."

"Commander," Tomas said — and she realized he wasn't talking to her. "Commander, the selector. Is it done?"

Ilsa looked at Elodie. Elodie looked at the node. The amber pulse was slowing, deepening toward red, the reactor spending its last reserves on the two cryo-spines it could no longer afford to feed. Somewhere below the silver spine, forty Consortium families slept in their funded dream. Somewhere below the copper, four thousand laborers slept in a grave no one had paid for.

"Not yet," Ilsa said. "But it's about to be. Hold the line, Tomas."

"Holding the line," he said, in the voice of a man who had held lines before, in a body that had never been to war.

* * *

The selector interface was Adaeze's design, and it was beautiful, and Elodie hated it.

It opened to her palm the way everything in the vault did — her biometrics had been written into the system at birth, or near enough; her mother had made sure of that, had made her the keystone of her own sabotage without asking. The interface bloomed above the node in pale blue light, and there it was, the binary, the whole rotten architecture of the world rendered in two options:

**ARCHIVE A — CONSORTIUM PATRONS. 41 SOULS. WAKE.**

**ARCHIVE B — MERIDIAN WORKFORCE. 4,112 SOULS. WAKE.**

**SELECT.**

Below the two options, in smaller text, the flaw her mother had built and hidden: *NO DEFAULT. NO TIMEOUT RESOLUTION. ABSTENTION = SYSTEM LOCK = TOTAL DISCHARGE.*

Her mother had rigged it to fail. Rigged it so that if no one chose, the reactor would dump its entire remaining charge into the bedrock — a dead lighthouse, both archives sealed for centuries, until someone with power enough to crack them came along. It had been, Elodie understood now, the most furious act of love imaginable: *if the world cannot choose justly, the world gets nothing.*

And Elodie had come down here to do exactly what her mother intended. Refuse the choice. Let it burn.

Her hand hovered over the abstention command.

"Wait," Ilsa said.

Elodie looked at her. The girl was staring at the two options, at the numbers. *41. 4,112.* Her small face was doing arithmetic no child should know how to do.

"If you lock it," Ilsa said, "the 4,112 sleep for three hundred years. Maybe more. Maybe forever. I've done the math — Kemi and I did it in the corridor. Three hundred years is eleven generations of the colony fleet. The Consortium will have won by then anyway. They'll have the power to crack it, and they'll crack it, and they'll wake their forty-one and leave the workers in the dark forever." She looked up. "Your mother's flaw only works if the future is better than the present. What if it isn't?"

"What's your alternative? Choose one? Choose the four thousand and hand the Consortium a martyr's story and a live reactor they can rebuild around?" Elodie's voice cracked on the last word. "Ilsa, I have spent eleven years keeping this place alive without knowing what for. I am not going to—"

"You're going to do what my crew did," Ilsa said quietly.

The reactor pulsed. Red now. Holding.

"We aged backward," Ilsa said. "The shear near the dead star took our bodies and left us our records. And the first thing Tomas asked — nine years old, crying, missing a wife he can't remember the face of — the first thing he asked was *can we still do the mission.* Not *can we have our lives back.* Can we *do the thing.*" She put her small hand flat on the selector's alloy cradle. "Because that's the only self that was ever real, ma'am. Not the body. Not the years. The *doing.*"

She tapped the interface. A third line bloomed beneath the binary, in handwriting-code so old Elodie recognized it with a jolt — it was her mother's notation, the same script from the hidden logs:

**ARCHIVE C — THE AWAKE. POPULATION: UNCOUNTED. STATUS: ALREADY HERE.**

Elodie stared at it. "That's not— my mother never built a third option. There's no third archive. There's nothing to wake."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "There's nothing to *wake.*" Her eyes came up, fierce and wet and certain. "But the selector doesn't check what's behind the spine. It checks where the charge goes. And a reactor, ma'am, is just a very large battery with opinions."

Elodie understood then, all at once, the way you understand a thing you've known your whole life. The refugees in the upper vault — the two hundred souls from the thawing coast who'd come following the beacon, half-frozen, half-starved, *awake*. The children on the bulkheads. Ilsa's crew. Herself.

Her mother had built a binary because she'd believed the choice had to be made between the dead. She'd never imagined someone would refuse the premise.

"Redirect the discharge," Elodie said slowly, "into the vault's external systems. Heat, power, broadcast. Spend the whole reserve on the living."

"Overload the selector doing it," Ilsa said. "Melt the choice shut. Nobody wakes the archives — not us, not the Consortium, not anyone — until the future earns them. But the two hundred upstairs eat hot food tonight."

It was, Elodie thought, her mother's sabotage and its exact opposite at once. Adaeze had rigged the system so that refusing to choose meant destruction. Her daughter would rig it so that refusing to choose meant *provision*. Same flaw, inverted. Same woman's blood, two generations apart, choosing justice over inheritance — except this time the inheritance wasn't refused. It was *spent*.

She pressed her palm to the node.

* * *

The Consortium cut through Bulkhead C at minute nine, not eleven — Kemi's weld had been honest but the cutter was better than advertised. Elodie heard it through the rock, a thin screaming note, while her hands moved through the discharge sequence and the selector screamed back at her in her mother's voice, warning after warning, *BIOMETRIC CONSENT REQUIRED — ARCHIVE SELECTION REQUIRED — ABSTENTION WILL RESULT IN—*

"I know," Elodie said to the dead woman's ghost in the machine. "You gave me a choice between two graves. I'm choosing the door."

The discharge command took her consent, her biometrics, her eleven years, and *ran*.

Above them, the reactor's hum changed pitch — dropped, deepened, became something almost like a voice. Through the shaft above them light began to fall: not the amber of the selector but white, clean, the color of a lighthouse lamp. The external systems came up one by one as the reserve poured into them — heat to the refugee levels, power to the greenhouse banks, and last, greatest, the old beacon itself, flaring from a whisper to a roar, throwing the vault's full testimony out into the dark for anyone with an antenna and the will to listen.

The selector's alloy cradle began to glow. Then to smoke. Then, with a sound like a glacier calving, the two fiber-optic spines fused — silver into copper into slag — and the interface died mid-warning, and the binary was no longer a choice anyone could ever make again.

Elodie sat back on the cold floor and laughed, once, a raw and disbelieving sound.

"Vault," Ilsa said into her comm, standing, small and straight-backed in her enormous suit. "Status report."

"Bulkhead D is holding," Tomas said, and his voice broke on the second word, and he was nine years old and crying and grinning, all of it at once. "The coolant did its job. They're stuck in decon, ma'am. Commander — *Commander, we held the line.*"

"You held the line," Ilsa said.

And then the door at the top of the shaft — the one no one had been defending, because no one had needed to — hissed open.

Elodie was on her feet before her mind caught up, and Ilsa was in front of her, and from the shaft above, silhouetted against the white light of the spent reactor, a figure began to descend the ladder. Unhurried. Unarmed. Wearing no suit at all, as if the cold of the deep vault were a thing he'd been assured of being exempt from.

The voice that came down the shaft was warm, and reasonable, and Elodie had heard its cadence once before, years ago, in a supply drone's message fragment addressed to *the keeper's daughter.*

"Well," it said. "So the daughter finishes the mother's work. I confess, Dr. Thackeray, we expected the workers' archive. You've made everything so much more interesting — and so much more *urgent.* You see, there's something you and the children should know about where the Halcyon really went. And about who gave the orders your mother thought she was disobeying."

The figure stepped off the ladder into the light.

Elodie's breath stopped — because the face was one she knew. It hung, enlarged and yellowed, on the vault's oldest maintenance plaque, above a signature she had traced with her fingers a thousand lonely nights:

*Chief Engineer, Meridian Project — E. Voss.*

A man who had been listed as dead for sixty years. A man who did not look a day over fifty.

"I believe," E. Voss said, smiling down at them from the last rung, "we should talk about your mother's *first* voyage."
