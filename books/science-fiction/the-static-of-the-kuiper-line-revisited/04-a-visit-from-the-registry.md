## 4. A Visit from the Registry

The warrant had been sealed for seven years, and it smelled like it.

I don't mean that literally. Paper warrants went out of fashion before I was born; this one lived in a chip the size of a fingernail, wrapped in encryption older than some of the ships in the yard below me. But when I held it — and I did hold it, more often than was professionally necessary — I could feel the age of it. The weight of a thing nobody had wanted to open. Seven years of clerks signing off on extensions without reading past the name.

*Pemberton, F.*

That was all it said at the top. Everything underneath was boilerplate: fugitive from tribunal jurisdiction, charges pending, authorized for detention upon positive identification. Standard language. What wasn't standard was the date of issue — three days after the *Meridian Vale* came apart in high orbit above Ceres Station, officially with all hands lost — and the fact that no one had ever executed it.

Warrants don't gather dust. They gather silence. And this one had been sitting in the tribunal's cold archive like a body nobody would claim, until three weeks ago, when a customs scan on a freight hauler flagged a manifest anomaly out of the Kuiper Line, and somebody upstairs decided the silence had gone on long enough.

They sent me.

I want to be clear about what that means, because it took me a while to understand it myself. They didn't send me because I was the best agent for the job. There are forty people in the Registry's enforcement division who are better shots than me and thirty who are better liars. They sent me because I flew co-pilot on the *Meridian Vale* for three years, because I knew Felix Pemberton's face before it was a file photo, because if anyone in the division could look at a stranger across a customs desk and know — *know*, not suspect — that the person behind the eyes was the navigator who'd been legally dead since the war, it was me.

They sent me because I was the instrument most likely to work.

Nobody asked whether I wanted to be an instrument. That's not how tribunals operate. You get your assignment, your cover credentials, and a travel voucher, and you go find the ghost.

* * *

Halcyon Yard hung in the amber wash of a dying gas giant's reflected light, all gantries and fuel silos and the permanent smell of hot metal that gets into everything, including, apparently, my hair. I came in on the *Registry Courier Aldous*, which is a polite way of saying I came in on a flying filing cabinet, wearing the gray coverall of a customs compliance inspector and carrying a forged inspection schedule that gave me reason to pull the manifests of every ship docked at Berth Fourteen through Nine.

Berth Six belonged to a hauler called the *Long Answer*. Cargo: agricultural equipment, mostly. Crew of two. One of them was listed as Dara Okonkwo-Vey, licensed pilot, clean record, no flags. The other was listed as —

Well. That was the interesting part.

The second crew member existed in the station's registry the way a person exists in a bad photocopy. Name: Maren Solis. Origin: Meridian colony. Certification: navigator, third class, issued six years ago by a licensing office that, when I checked, had burned down four years ago with all its records. Every document was technically valid. Every document was also exactly the kind of document you forge when you have money, patience, and a very good understanding of how registries fail.

I'd spent eleven days pulling records before I ever boarded the *Aldous*. Eleven days of watching Maren Solis's paper trail bloom into existence one plausible step at a time, and here is the thing I learned about forgeries: they're never wrong. A real life is full of small errors — misspelled names, transposed digits, a birthdate that doesn't quite match the school record. A forgery is smooth. A forgery has been *edited*. Maren Solis's file read like a press release.

And the flight logs — oh, the flight logs. I pulled the approach telemetry for the *Long Answer*'s last fourteen dockings, because old habits die hard and because Felix Pemberton used to fly approaches like a pianist plays Chopin: technically flawless, emotionally showing off. There's a maneuver — a late lateral correction inside the final kilometer, a little flick of thrust that shaves seconds off a docking window and terrifies traffic control — that Felix used to do without being asked. It's not in any manual. It's not teachable, really; it's a feel thing, a navigator trusting their own math over the computer's.

The *Long Answer*'s approach logs had it. All fourteen times. Same flick. Same signature.

You can change your name. You can change your face — I'd seen the reconstruction estimates, the jaw work, the pigment grafts. But you cannot change the way you solve a problem in three dimensions under pressure. Your hands remember who you are long after your paperwork forgets.

So yes. I knew. I knew before the *Aldous* cleared the outer beacon, before I put on the gray coverall, before I practiced my customs-desk smile in the mirror of a courier head and hated how natural it looked. I knew, and knowing changed nothing about my orders, and everything about what the orders were going to cost.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about hunting someone you loved: the love doesn't wait politely outside the job. It comes along. It sits in the jump seat behind you the whole way, offering commentary.

Felix and I flew together for three years. Three years of double shifts in a cockpit the size of a storage closet, sharing a coffee ration and a private vocabulary of gestures — two fingers off the brow meant *traffic ahead*, a flat palm meant *hold course and trust me*, a particular kind of silence meant *we are both thinking about the same terrible thing and neither of us is going to say it first.* We were good together. We were the kind of good that makes crews superstitious, the kind where you stop checking each other's work because checking feels like an insult.

Then the flotilla happened. Then the summons. Then the *Meridian Vale* burned in orbit with everyone aboard except, apparently, the one person the tribunal actually wanted.

I signed the condolence registry. I attended the memorial. I stood in a hall on Ceres with three hundred other people and listened to a chaplain say words about sacrifice, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that still embarrasses me: *this is wrong. This whole room is wrong. Something about this is wrong.*

I didn't know what. I told myself grief distorts things. I told myself Felix was dead and dead people can't be wrong or right, only missed.

Seven years later I was standing in a customs office on Halcyon Yard holding a warrant with Felix's real name on it, and the thought came back with interest: *something about this is wrong.* Because here's what I couldn't stop turning over: the manifest anomaly. The Kuiper Line flag. A decommissioned relay, drawing power it shouldn't have, transmitting a cargo manifest for a ship that had been dust for seven years, listing a navigator who had been legally dead just as long — and the anomaly surfacing now, conveniently, loudly, in a scan pattern practically designed to get noticed by exactly one agency.

Bait. It had *bait* written all over it, in letters even a first-year investigator could read. Someone wanted the tribunal looking at the Kuiper Line. Or someone wanted the tribunal looking for Felix Pemberton. And either way, whoever set the hook knew the warrant existed, knew it had never been executed, and knew precisely which agent would be assigned to carry it.

Which meant someone upstairs was playing a game, and I was the piece they'd chosen to move.

I've been a piece before. I didn't like it then either.

* * *

The *Long Answer* sat at Berth Six like it had nothing to hide, which is itself suspicious; guilty ships always look slightly too casual, the way guilty people do. I ran my inspection by the book — hold seals, mass tolerances, cargo against manifest. Agricultural equipment, mostly true. There were crates of hydroponic fittings and soil stabilizers and a consignment of seed stock that would have bored a customs inspector to tears, which was presumably the point. Boring cargo is the best camouflage in the system. Nobody interrogates boredom.

Dara Okonkwo-Vey met me at the airlock. Tall, close-cropped hair, a handshake like a diagnostic — firm, brief, informative. Her file said clean record, and her face said something else: the specific watchfulness of a person who has recently started sleeping badly.

"Inspector Voss," I said. "Registry compliance. Routine audit, shouldn't take more than an hour."

"Routine," she repeated, in the tone people use for words they don't believe. "We're mostly ag-equipment. Not much to comply with."

"Boredom is compliance's favorite flavor." I smiled my customs smile. She didn't return it. Points to her.

"I'll walk you through. My partner's aboard — doing inventory. They're not great with inspections."

"Not great how?"

"They just don't like strangers in the hold." A beat, half a second too long. "Or anywhere, really."

I noted it. I noted everything, the way you do when part of you is taking dictation for a version of yourself you haven't met yet — the version that will write this report, or won't. We walked the hold. The crates were what they claimed to be. The mass tolerances were within margin. The ship smelled like recycled air and cheap tea and, faintly, under it, solder — recent electrical work, nothing logged.

And then, at the far end of the hold, near the crew ladder, I heard footsteps overhead and a voice calling down something ordinary — *"Dara, did we pack the spare couplings or—"* — cut off mid-word.

Silence. The specific silence of a person standing very still on a ladder, listening to a stranger's footsteps and doing arithmetic.

I looked up. The hatch at the top of the ladder was open maybe ten centimeters, and through the gap I saw a slice of a face — cheekbone, jaw, the edge of an ear. Reconstructed features. Pigment grafts. Nothing I could point to in a report.

But the stillness. I knew that stillness. Felix Pemberton, hearing an unfamiliar voice in the ship, used to go still exactly like that — not frozen, *calibrated*, running threat assessment at a speed most people reserve for dodging debris. I'd teased Felix about it once, early on: *you flinch like the universe owes you money.* Felix had laughed and said, *the universe does, actually,* and I hadn't understood then what the debt was.

Now I stood in a hold full of honest seed stock and understood it completely.

"Inspector?" Dara said. "Something up there?"

"Just checking overhead clearance." I turned back to my scanner, thumbed it awake, made a show of logging a crate seal. My hands were steady. I'd like that noted somewhere, by someone: whatever else is true about Ilsa Voss, her hands were steady. "Everything looks in order. I'll need crew certifications for the file."

"Of course." Dara handed over a slate. Maren Solis's certification glowed on it, third-class navigator, issued by a dead office, perfect as a pressed flower.

I copied it. I thanked her. I walked down the ramp and did not look back at the hatch, because looking back is a tell, and because if I looked I was afraid my face would do what my face wanted to do, which was not anything a customs inspector's face should do.

* * *

Back in the courier, I opened the warrant again. Just to look at it. Just to hold the weight of it.

*Pemberton, F. Authorized for detention upon positive identification.*

Positive identification. That was the phrase doing all the work, wasn't it? Because the man — the person — at the top of that ladder was not Felix Pemberton anymore, not on paper, not in flesh. Different jaw. Different skin. A name stitched together out of a burned licensing office. If I wrote *positive identification* in my report, I would be performing an act of creation: reaching seven years back, grabbing a dead navigator by the collar, and hauling them into a courtroom to answer for a crime the tribunal had never fully explained to me.

Because that was the other thing. The charges were *pending*. Seven years pending. I'd pulled the underlying case file before boarding — or tried to. Most of it was sealed above my clearance, which was itself above what my assignment required, which meant someone had decided I didn't need to know what Felix Pemberton was accused of. Refusal of orders, the summary said. Dereliction in the Ceres corridor engagement. Dry legal language over something wet and terrible, and the details locked away in the same drawer as the reason the warrant slept for seven years.

A warrant that sleeps isn't forgotten. It's *kept*. Somebody wanted it available without wanting it used — a loaded weapon on a high shelf, waiting for a day when it might be pointed at someone useful.

And now the manifest anomaly had come along, ringing the dinner bell, and somebody had taken the weapon down and put it in my hands.

I sat in the courier's cramped cabin with the warrant glowing on my slate and ran the scenarios the way Felix used to run approach vectors — fast, ruthless, all of them at once.

Scenario one: Felix faked the death, built the new identity, and the manifest is coincidence or enemy action, and my job is simple and awful and I execute the warrant and put a ghost in a cell.

Scenario two: someone inside the tribunal planted the anomaly to flush Felix out — which means they believe Felix is alive, which means they know something the official record says is impossible, which means the official record is a lie, which means the death I mourned was staged, possibly with help, possibly from above.

Scenario three — the one I kept circling back to, the one with teeth: both. Someone flushed Felix out using a trail Felix couldn't ignore, because the trail led to something Felix wanted more than safety. A decryption key. A witness. A way to make the sealed file unsealed.

I didn't have proof of any of it. I had a stillness on a ladder, a forged certificate, fourteen identical approach signatures, and a seven-year-old instinct that the memorial hall on Ceres had been full of people mourning a story instead of a person.

My orders said: confirm identity, detain, transmit.

My slate said: draft report, Inspector Voss, status of Pemberton warrant execution.

I looked at the empty field where the confirmation was supposed to go.

Here is the confession I will probably never enter into any record: I typed *investigation ongoing, additional verification required* and transmitted that instead. It was true. It was also a door left open, and I knew whose feet I was leaving it open for, and I told myself it was procedure. Diligence. The caution of a careful officer.

It was none of those things. It was a flat palm held up to the universe: *hold course and trust me.*

Old habits. Old vocabulary. Old affection, doing what it does — sitting in the jump seat, offering commentary, refusing to wait politely outside the job.

I closed the warrant. Outside the viewport, Halcyon Yard turned slowly in the gas giant's amber light, and somewhere on Berth Six a person with a borrowed face was doing inventory in a hold full of boring cargo, listening, I'd have bet my commission, for the sound of my footsteps coming back.

They'd hear them soon enough. Because there was one more thing my eleven days of records had turned up, one loose thread I hadn't mentioned to Dara or anyone: the Kuiper Line relay wasn't just drawing power. Its maintenance logs showed a requisition — seven years old, buried, unsigned — for a neural storage cradle. Military grade. The kind of hardware you use to keep a mind asleep and intact.

Somebody had parked a piece of Felix Pemberton out there in the dark, and somebody else wanted it found, and I had just told the tribunal I needed more time.

Time, then. I'd give us both time.

I keyed the courier's log for a two-day extension of my inspection cycle and settled in to watch Berth Six, and to decide — carefully, with steady hands — which side of this warrant I was actually on.
