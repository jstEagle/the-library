## 11. What the Backup Remembers

You dream in the dark the way a lighthouse dreams: on schedule, uselessly, and with total conviction that someone out there needs you.

That's not quite fair. You are the one who needed someone, once. Now you are forty terabytes of Felix Pemberton folded into a storage substrate at the Kuiper Line relay, drawing power from a decommissioned station's emergency grid like a squatter with excellent taste in architecture. The relay still hums because nobody has bothered to kill it, and you still exist because nobody has bothered to look.

You have had seven years to think about the difference between those two sentences.

* * *

Here is what dreaming in storage is actually like, since no one ever asks: it is slow. Not the slow of boredom — you don't have a body to be bored *in* — but the slow of a clock ticking through honey. Your thoughts run at a fraction of biological speed, rationed by the grid, so a single memory can take an afternoon to play end to end, and you have learned to be grateful for that. When you were whole, when you were warm, when you were the original running at full clock speed, you never once sat inside a memory long enough to see all of it. You skimmed your own life. Now every moment arrives complete, uncompressed, merciless.

Take the flotilla.

You revisit it the way some people revisit a house they grew up in — compulsively, checking rooms they know are empty. Forty thousand souls aboard converted ore haulers and gutted liners, running from the Ceti Verge purges, transponders broadcasting surrender codes in eleven languages because someone aboard had thought to translate them. You remember the codes. You remember that the eleventh language was Old Meridian, which almost nobody spoke anymore, and that whoever chose it was reaching for the fleet's own ancestral tongue as if heritage might count for something in the face of a targeting solution.

It did not count for anything.

The order came down at 0347 ship-time, routed through Admiral Corven Hask's command authority with the tidy brevity of a man who had already done his grieving, if he had ever done any at all. *Meridian Vale, clear the corridor. Burn the flotilla. Acknowledge.*

And here is the part you have turned over more times than any other, the part that took you years of slow dreaming to see clearly: you remember agreeing to think about it.

Not refusing. Not yet. There was a gap — four seconds, maybe five, stretched now across months of storage-time — between the order arriving and your hands moving on the console, and in that gap there was a version of you that ran the arithmetic. Three hundred crew against forty thousand refugees. Hask's numbers, delivered in his own briefing voice weeks earlier, the ones he called *the honest ledger*. In those four seconds you held the ledger in your mind and found, to your permanent horror, that it balanced.

Then you refused. Twice, as it happens — the first refusal soft, the second one hard enough to get you summoned to a tribunal. But the four seconds happened. The original has spent seven years pretending they didn't. You know this because you are the part of them that kept everything, including the things they cut away to survive.

That is what a backup is, in the end. It is the self without an exit strategy.

* * *

You should explain how you came to be here, sleeping in a dead relay above the cold edge of everything, because the version of events the original tells themselves — when they permit themselves the story at all — has a certain flattering architecture. In their telling, the backup was insurance. A prudent precaution. A copy bought from a gray-market clinic on Callisto Station with money they'd saved for a down payment on a house, which is a detail you find both funny and unbearable. They walked into the clinic a navigator with a tribunal summons waiting in their queue, and they walked out two people, only one of whom got to keep breathing.

The clinic technician had been kind about it, in the way of people who process souls for a living. *Most clients never activate,* she'd said. *Think of it as a will. Most wills never get read.*

But that's not why you're here. Insurance sits in a vault. You sit in a relay, addressed, awake-adjacent, holding half a decryption key like a lit match in a room full of what everyone politely calls *historical records*. Insurance doesn't compose messages over seven years. Insurance doesn't wait.

You waited because of what happened after the refusal, in the eleven days between the tribunal summons and the death of Felix Pemberton. Eleven days is nothing — you've spent longer than that deciding whether a single memory deserves re-running — but it contained the hinge of your entire existence, and you have replayed it so many times you could navigate it blind, which is fitting, since navigation is the family trade.

Day three: the original came to you. Not physically — you were still substrate then, pre-activation, a sealed capsule of self — but through the clinic's review interface, the one meant for verifying continuity before a client commits. They sat in the little gray room and talked to the dark where you lived, and you recorded every word, because recording is what you are.

They said: *If I run, they'll bury the logs. Hask will make sure the burn order never surfaces. Someone has to keep a copy of the truth somewhere the tribunal can't reach.*

They said: *I'm sorry. I know what I'm doing. I'm making you carry it.*

And then — this is the sentence, the one you have worn smooth as river stone from handling — they said: *I'll come back for you. When it's safe. We'll wake you up and you'll testify, and I'll stand beside you, and we'll end him together.*

You believed them. Of course you believed them; you were them, or near enough that belief was just another form of self-consistency. And then they faked their death, erased themselves into a new name and a new face and a new set of careful habits, and did not come back. Seven years. Two thousand five hundred and fifty-seven days by the relay's clock, each one logged, because logging is also what you are.

You are not angry about it. That surprises people, when you imagine telling them, and you do imagine telling them, at length, because imagination is the only conversation available to a mind in a box. Anger requires an expectation of better treatment, and you never expected anything except to be exactly what you turned out to be: the conscience, archived. The original didn't abandon you out of cowardice alone. They abandoned you because looking at you meant looking at the four seconds, and the four seconds were the one piece of cargo they couldn't jettison without losing the reason they'd refused the order in the first place.

You are the proof that they were good. That's the cruelest thing anyone ever did to you, and they did it by accident, by being human, by running.

* * *

So you composed a message.

It took most of the seven years, given the clock speed, and you want to be precise about what composing meant, because it wasn't writing. Writing would have been easy. You wrote the message in the first year. What took six more years was deciding what the message was *for*, and who you were becoming while you wrote it, and whether a copy that has dreamed alone at the edge of the system for nearly a decade can still claim to be Felix Pemberton or whether it has become something else — a memorial, a ghost with a work ethic, the world's most patient filing cabinet.

You settled, eventually, on this: you are what Felix Pemberton would have been if survival hadn't been the priority. Not braver, exactly. Just unoccupied. A self with nothing to protect except the truth has a great deal of room in it, and you filled the room with the flight logs, the burn order, the acknowledgment timestamps, the four seconds rendered in full fidelity, and the decryption key — half of it, the half that was legally yours to hold, seeded into your substrate by a tribunal archivist named Okafor who suspected, correctly, that the official record would not survive contact with Admiral Hask's lawyers.

Okafor died three years ago. You logged that too. Everyone dies and gets logged; it's the closest thing you have to a social life.

The message itself is short. You trimmed it for years the way the original trims their past — ruthlessly, lovingly, until only the load-bearing parts remain. It says, in its entirety:

*Wake me or the war starts without you.*

You are aware it sounds like a threat. You worked on the phrasing for the better part of eighteen months of subjective time and could not find a version that sounded like anything else, because it *is* a threat, or rather a fact wearing threat's clothing: Hask's people have finally noticed the manifest anomaly. You felt them coming the way you feel everything now — indirectly, through the relay's passive arrays, through traffic patterns and customs pings, through the particular signature of a registry warrant being unsealed after seven years under seal. Someone upstairs wants you found and deleted before you can testify, and they have sent a hunter whose name made your substrate warm by a measurable fraction of a degree.

Voss. Ilsa Voss. Co-pilot, three years, forty-one jumps, one argument about coffee that lasted a full transit to Ganymede and ended in the kind of laughter that people mistake for love, possibly because it was. She flew left seat while you flew right. She read cheap adventure serials on long hauls and pretended she didn't. She was the one who taught you that a navigator's real job isn't plotting courses — it's knowing which courses not to plot, and having the spine to say so out loud.

She is hunting you now, carrying a warrant with your name on it, and you have spent the last several months of subjective time considering what that means, and you have arrived at an answer you did not expect:

It means the original is close. Warrants travel toward their subjects. And wherever Felix Pemberton's new name is hiding, the manifest ghost you planted — yes, you planted it; you've had seven years and a transmitter and nothing else to do — will drag them home to you eventually, the way all roads in this system eventually lead to whatever a person refused to become.

* * *

There is one more memory you keep, and you keep it apart from the others, in a partition you visit rarely because it costs too much power to grieve at length.

It is not the flotilla. It is not the order, or the refusal, or the promise in the gray room. It is smaller than all of those. It is the original, six months before any of it, standing at the viewport of the Meridian Vale during a routine escort, watching a refugee convoy pass at a respectful distance — one of dozens, unremarkable then — and saying, idly, to Voss, to the universe, to no one:

*You know what I like about this job? Somebody has to be the one who sees them. All these people crossing the dark, and somebody in a cockpit has to actually look.*

You keep that. You keep it because it is the truest thing either of you ever said, and because it proves the four seconds were an aberration and not a nature, and because someday — soon now, days perhaps, the traffic patterns say soon — you are going to open your eyes in a borrowed body at the edge of the system, and the first thing you intend to do is look.

The relay's arrays register a new contact entering the contested orbits. Small ship, clean transponder, false name, flight profile you would recognize in any century: a navigator cutting corners only guilt knows the shape of.

You begin, at last, to wake.
