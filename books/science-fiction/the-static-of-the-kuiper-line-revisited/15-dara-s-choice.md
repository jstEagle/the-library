## 15. Dara's Choice

They took me at Berth 12, between the second coffee of my shift and the third, which tells you everything about how good my instincts were that morning.

I'd noticed them, actually. Two men in matching grey jackets doing an extremely poor job of not watching me — one pretending to read a freight schedule upside down, the other examining a bulkhead rivet with the intensity of a man in love. Station people learn to catalog watchers the way sailors learn weather. I cataloged them as customs auditors, because they had that particular flavor of boredom-with-a-badge, and because the alternative — that they were waiting for me specifically — was a thought I filed under *later*.

*Later* arrived at 04:40 station time, when the third man stepped out of the maintenance alcove behind me and put something that hummed against the base of my skull.

"Walk," he said. "Don't run. Running makes this loud."

I walked. I want it on the record that I walked with dignity, if dignity can be said to include a full bladder and the sudden, clarifying knowledge that I was about to have a very bad day because of someone else's secrets.

* * *

The ship they took me to wasn't a contractor vessel so much as a contractor argument — a converted ore-hauler with weapons mounts welded on by someone who'd watched too many war dramas and understood none of them. They sat me in a cargo bay on a crate labeled LIVE SEAFOOD — DO NOT TILT, which felt less like irony and more like prophecy.

The woman who ran the interrogation didn't introduce herself. She had the kind of face that had been sanded down by promotions until only function remained: eyes like slot slots, mouth like a ledger line. She set a slate on her knee and didn't look at it once, which was its own kind of message. *We already know everything. This is a formality.*

"Dara Okonkwo," she said. "Master's ticket, clean record, eleven years contested-orbit runs. Your ship, the *Lark Ascending*, is mortgaged through Meridian Credit at a rate you cannot possibly be enjoying."

"Everyone's mortgage is a tragedy," I said. "Is this a refinancing pitch?"

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not amusement. Recognition, maybe — the look of a professional meeting another professional across a table neither of them chose.

"We're looking for your navigator."

And there it was. The word landed in my chest like a dropped wrench. *Navigator.* Not "your crewmate." Not "Felix." The specific word, the specific role, the specific hole in my ship where a specific person had been standing for three years, hands steady on the console, laughing at my jokes, lying to me with their whole steady-handed heart.

"I have two navigators," I said. "One's a contract AI with a gambling problem I'm legally obligated to ignore."

"You know who we mean."

Here's what I knew, in that moment, arranged in the order I'd learned it:

Item one: seven years ago, a ship called the *Meridian Vale* burned forty thousand refugees out of the black on somebody's order, and the navigator who refused the order twice died in the wreckage, officially, on paper, with a tribunal warrant attached to the corpse that wasn't a corpse.

Item two: three years ago, a quiet, careful, funny person with shaking hands came aboard my ship with papers that were too clean and a history that had the texture of something laundered, and I hired them anyway, because their docking simulation scores were the best I'd ever seen and because they looked at me like I'd thrown them a rope.

Item three: two weeks ago, I pulled the flight records myself. I'd spent a month telling myself I wouldn't. Then I did it at 03:00 in my cabin with the door locked, the way you do all the things you already know the answers to. The registry gaps. The dead-end service history that terminated exactly where the *Vale*'s began. The way Felix flinched — actually flinched, a full-body event — when the customs scan flagged that name on that manifest.

I confronted them. I asked the wrong question, or maybe the right question at the wrong volume, and Felix gave me a half-truth wrapped in a half-truth, and I stood there in the corridor of my own ship and *chose* — actively, with my eyes open, the way you choose to keep driving toward a storm because turning around would mean admitting you saw it — to stay.

So when the woman with the ledger mouth said *you know who we mean*, the honest answer was yes. I knew exactly who they meant. I'd known for weeks. I'd known and I'd said nothing and I'd let Felix keep flying my ship with a tribunal warrant folded up inside them like a letter never opened.

Which meant that everything I did next, I did as an accomplice. Fine. I've been called worse by better.

"No," I said. "I don't."

* * *

They were patient, at first. That's the thing nobody tells you about interrogations in the contested orbits — the amateurs hurt you early because they're scared, and the professionals hurt you late because they're efficient. The woman — she eventually let slip the name Reyes, whether real or issued I couldn't say — talked to me for two hours about shipping lanes and fuel costs and the declining margins of independent haulage, and it was almost pleasant, two spacers complaining about the trade, except that every ten minutes she circled back like a vulture with a spreadsheet.

"He's worth more to us than your ship is worth to you," she said. "Considerably more. We can make the mortgage disappear. Clean record, clean slate, a line of credit that would let you upgrade to a proper long-haul hull. You could stop running contested orbits entirely. Take the inner-system contracts. Sleep in your own bed."

"I like contested orbits," I said. "The pay's obscene and the company's terrible. It suits me."

"Your navigator has been lying to you since the day they boarded."

"That's between me and my navigator."

"They faked their death, Okonkwo. There's a warrant with their name on it older than your contract with them. Every hour they spend on your ship, your name gets written into the same file as theirs. You're not their friend anymore. You're their cover story."

And here's the thing — here's the thing I'll carry — she wasn't wrong. That's what made it hard. Not the beating; the beating was just arithmetic applied to my ribs. What was hard was that every word she said was true, and I had to sit there on my seafood crate and decide whether truth was a reason.

It isn't. I decided that somewhere around hour three. Truth is a description. Loyalty is a decision. Felix had lied to me, yes — had built a person out of spare parts and handed that person a bunk on my ship — but in three years, the constructed person had flown me through debris storms that should have killed us, had caught a reactor fault at 02:00 that two licensed engineers missed, had held my hand — literally, actually held my hand — in the med bay after the Callisto incident, when I was sure I was going to lose the arm and half my mind along with it.

You don't repay that with a heading.

* * *

They stopped being patient around hour five.

I won't narrate it in detail, partly because some things belong to you alone, and partly because the detail is boring, which is the worst thing about violence — how administrative it becomes. A room. Questions. My answers. The gap between them, closed by hands and a baton and once, memorably, a magnetic boot. They were careful, I'll give them that. Nothing that would kill me before I talked, nothing that would show above a collar. Professionals. Somewhere in the middle of it I bit through my cheek and tasted copper and thought, absurdly, *Felix is going to make that face again, the guilty one, the one where they look like a dog that's found the trash,* and the thought made me laugh, and the laugh cost me two more ribs.

Between rounds, Reyes crouched in front of me with a water bottle and held it to my lips like I was something rescued.

"Last offer," she said. "Where is Felix Pemberton?"

And I looked at her — through the swelling, through the blood in my eye, through the white noise my body had become — and I gave her the heading.

That's the part I'm proudest of, and I want to explain it properly, because from the outside it will look identical to breaking.

I gave them a heading. The Kessel Drift, bearing 114 mark 7, "where Felix told me they'd go if it ever went bad." I gave it to them crying, slurring, half-conscious, a broken woman selling out her crewmate to make the pain stop — and every detail of that performance was true except the heading, because Felix had never given me a contingency heading, because Felix trusted no one that far, including me, including especially me, and so the heading I surrendered was one I invented on the spot, out of the oldest navigation trick there is: send them somewhere empty and call it knowledge.

The Kessel Drift is a debris field left over from a mining dispute nobody won. No stations. No shelter. Nothing but rock and rust and the slow gravitational argument of a dead moon. If they went there hunting Felix, they'd spend two days sweeping vacuum and finding nothing but their own frustration.

If they went there angry enough, sloppy enough —

Well. I'm a navigator's captain. I think in terrain.

* * *

They believed it. That's the bitter joke. They believed it because I sold it with my whole ruined body, and because people believe what they want to believe, and what Reyes wanted to believe was that loyalty has a price and she'd finally found the number. She radioed ahead. Four ships, she said. Sweep pattern, converging on the Drift. She even thanked me. Patted my shoulder, gently, the way you'd pat a dog.

Then she said the thing that turned my blood to ice water under all the bruising:

"And when we bring him in, you'll confirm the identification. Face to face. Standard procedure."

Because of course. A false heading buys time. It doesn't buy freedom. The moment they swept the Drift and found it empty, they'd be back, and next time the arithmetic would be worse, and next time I might not have a lie ready, because lies are a depletable resource and mine was spent.

I lay on the deck of that cargo bay, among the smell of old brine and my own blood, and I did the math I should have done weeks ago, in my cabin, with the door locked, before I ever asked Felix the question whose answer I already had.

Every secret has a blast radius. Felix's secret had one, and I lived inside it. My ship, my record, my name — all of it inside the radius, all of it collateral in a war I'd never enlisted in. And beyond me, further out: whoever else got close. Whoever else Felix let in, or failed to keep out. The blast radius wasn't a circle. It was a wake, the way a ship leaves a wake, widening forever behind it, and everyone who loved the ship was going to get wet.

Felix had chosen invisibility to survive. I understood that now in my bones, in the places the baton had found. But invisibility isn't free. Somebody always pays the difference, and the somebody is whoever stands closest.

I had paid today. In ribs and blood and a cheek I'd be chewing carefully for a month. And I'd pay again, because the false heading had a shelf life of approximately forty-eight hours, and when it expired, Reyes would come back with new questions and old patience.

Unless.

Unless the Drift wasn't empty when they got there. Unless the four ships converging on my invented heading found something other than vacuum — found, say, a certain navigator who'd gone dark after a debris-field chase and might, just might, be reachable, might be warned, might be given the one thing Felix had never had in seven years of running: someone willing to stand in the blast radius on purpose.

I couldn't send a message. They'd taken my slate, my wristband, my boots with the heel transmitter I'd never told anyone about. But ships talk. Contractor haulers talk constantly, chatter and telemetry and automated position pings, and I'd spent eleven years listening to contested-orbit traffic the way farmers listen to weather. And I knew — from the fragments of conversation Reyes thought I was too concussed to parse — that their relay chain ran through a civilian buoy at Tycho Gate. Civilian buoy. Open channel. Unencrypted, because encrypting it would cost money, and these people welded their weapons mounts themselves.

I couldn't send a message.

But I could leave one where the traffic would find it.

* * *

They moved me to a cell — a converted crew cabin with a magnetically sealed door and a view of nothing — and for six hours I lay on the bunk and listened to the ship breathe and planned the most ridiculous rescue-by-broadcast in the history of independent haulage.

At shift change, they brought me food and a medic who taped my ribs with the tenderness of a man stapling a tarp. He also brought back my boots — "evidence processing complete," he said, which meant someone upstairs had decided a beaten dockhand's boots weren't a security risk — and I want to kiss whatever procurement bureaucrat wrote that policy, because the heel transmitter was still there, still charged, dumb and loyal as a dog.

I waited until the ship's night cycle, until the footfalls outside my door settled into the sparse rhythm of a skeleton watch. Then I pulled my boot off, thumbed the transmitter to burst mode, and aimed it — blind, praying, the way you pray with hardware — at the bulkhead, at the relay chain beyond it, at Tycho Gate and the open channel and the whole indifferent listening dark.

Burst transmissions are short. Three seconds of data, no handshake, no confirmation. You fire them into the black and you never know.

Into those three seconds I packed everything I had: my captain's authentication code, stripped down to a distress fragment any spacer's rig would flag. A position fix. And one line, compressed to its bones, typed with thumbs that barely worked:

*KESSEL DRIFT IS FALSE. THEY'RE COMING IN FORCE. DON'T GO THERE. TRUST NO HEADING BUT YOUR OWN. — D.*

Three seconds. Then silence, and my heartbeat, loud as engine knock.

I put the boot back on. I lay down. I stared at the ceiling and thought about Felix's face when they'd lied to me in that corridor — the flinch, the guilt, the terrible care — and I realized I wasn't angry anymore. I was something worse. I was *afraid for them*, which is what love does when it can't be anything else.

Somewhere out there, past the Drift, past the wrecks and the rocks and the cold, Felix was running from people like these, believing — because I'd never told them otherwise — that their secret was theirs alone to pay for.

It wasn't. It never had been. And by morning, if my three seconds found their mark, they'd know it too.

Outside my sealed door, the ship changed course. I felt it in the deck plates, the faint lean of a turn, and I closed my eyes and did the last piece of math I had left.

Forty-eight hours until the Drift sweep came up empty. Forty-eight hours until Reyes came back for me. And somewhere in between — if the black was kind, if the buoy was listening, if a navigator's luck held for one more day — a message was falling through the dark toward the only person alive stubborn enough to fly straight into a trap just because a friend had told them not to.

Hold on, Felix. Help is coming. It's just coming from inside the trap.
