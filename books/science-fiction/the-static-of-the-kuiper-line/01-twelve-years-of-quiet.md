## 1. Twelve Years of Quiet

The trick to being nobody, Tamsin Ishikawa had learned, was to be boring with total commitment.

She'd been running the *Mule's Patience* — a decommissioned ore barge so old it predated the war, so ugly it had been rejected twice by scrappers — through Ceres Gate for eleven years and change. In that time she had perfected the art of the yawn. Her transponder chirped out its forged registry in a flat, unremarkable tone. Her cargo manifest listed agricultural equipment, which was technically true if you considered sealed hydroponic canisters full of heirloom seed stock "agricultural equipment." Her flight plan was filed with the enthusiasm of a woman describing paint drying.

"*Patience*, this is Ceres Gate Traffic. Hold at marker seven for scan queue. Estimated delay forty minutes."

"Copy, Gate Traffic," Tamsin said, and yawned audibly into the mic. She'd practiced that too. A bored voice was an invisible voice. Nobody remembers the sound of boredom.

Forty minutes was generous. The Admiralty had been twitchy all month — something about fleet movements past Jupiter, though the news feeds were careful not to say what. Twelve years of war made everyone careful about everything. The Kuiper Line had been contested since before Tamsin had been decanted, which was a sentence she tried very hard never to think about, because thinking about it led to thinking about *her*, and thinking about *her* was a door Tamsin kept locked, painted over, and stacked furniture against.

She killed the main drive and let the barge drift, then went aft to check on her contraband.

The seed canisters rode in the number three hold, tucked behind a wall of genuinely boring ballast plates. Heirloom stock from the Belt gardens — wheat that remembered Earth, tomatoes bred in low-g before the embargo, beans whose genetic lines hadn't been licensed, taxed, and logged into the Admiralty's Agricultural Registry like conscripts. Officially, moving them was smuggling. Unofficially, half the station families eating real food on Ceres owed their dinners to someone doing exactly what she was doing, and the other half wished they did.

Tamsin ran her palm along the nearest canister. Cold metal, faint hum of the climate seals. Somewhere inside, ten thousand tiny futures waited to be planted.

"Don't get sentimental," she told them. "You're a tax problem."

This was another rule: talk to your cargo like it's cargo. Sentiment was a trail. Trails got people caught.

* * *

The scan came early.

Tamsin felt it before her instruments announced it — that particular prickle along the hull where a deep-pulse sweep kissed the plating, reading density signatures the way a customs officer reads faces. Her console lit amber. *ACTIVE SWEEP — DEEP PULSE — SOURCE: GATE STATION 4.*

"That's new," she murmured.

Deep-pulse wasn't standard for a cargo queue. Deep-pulse was what they used when they were looking for something specific — weapons, people, black boxes. She sat very still for a moment, listening to the sweep crawl across the barge's skin, and did the math she'd done a hundred times before but never casually.

Ore barges were wonderful lies. They were solid, dense, deliberately cluttered with slag ballast and worn machinery, and every cubic meter of them read as exactly what they claimed to be. But a deep pulse could see through walls, and if the operator knew what a pressurized seed canister looked like in cross-section —

The sweep reached hold three.

Tamsin exhaled slowly and keyed the countermeasure she'd installed herself, alone, over six weeks of sleepless nights: a resonance shroud that wrapped the canisters in the density signature of wet ore. It was elegant. It was also, if anyone ever inspected the wiring, extremely illegal, and would convert her from smuggler to spy in the eyes of whatever tribunal got hold of her.

The amber light held. Held. Held.

Then it slid green, and the sweep moved on to the tug behind her, and Tamsin discovered she'd been gripping the armrests hard enough to leave dents in the foam.

"*Patience*, you're cleared for berth twelve. Welcome back."

"Thrilled," she said, with the exact amount of enthusiasm a tired hauler would have, no more, no less.

* * *

Berth twelve smelled like machine oil and old coffee, which were, in Tamsin's opinion, the two great perfumes of human civilization. She docked with the ease of long habit, cycled the locks, and began the ritual of becoming forgettable: logging her arrival with the harbormaster's office under her registered name (T. Ishikawa, independent hauler, no relation to anyone), paying her fees in unremarkable increments, and nodding at the dockworkers who nodded back without curiosity because eleven years of reliable, dull competence had bought her the only currency worth having in a port town: the assumption that she was exactly who she said she was.

"You look tired, Ishi," said Okonkwo from the loading crew, tossing her a bulb of coffee that was mostly chicory and entirely terrible.

"I'm always tired. It's called professionalism."

"Heard Gate Four ran deep pulses today. Somebody upstairs nervous."

"Somebody upstairs is always nervous. That's why they're upstairs." She drank the terrible coffee and didn't wince, which took practice. "Manifest says ag equipment. You want to argue with the manifest?"

Okonkwo grinned and didn't ask again. That was the arrangement. Eleven years of tips paid in quiet favors and never once discussed.

The offload took four hours. Tamsin supervised it with her whole attention split three ways — the loaders, the gate cameras, and the small, cold knot in her stomach that had lived there so long she'd stopped noticing it except when she touched it, the way you stop noticing a scar until you run a finger over it.

Because here was the thing about building a life designed to leave no trace: eventually you noticed that the life itself was the trace. Eleven years of the same berth, the same nods, the same terrible coffee. She had made herself invisible by being consistent, and consistency, over enough years, became a shape. Someone patient enough could trace that shape backward. Someone patient enough could find her.

Which was why the case file stayed encrypted, and why she checked on it now, as she always did after a scan, the way other people checked whether their heart was still beating.

* * *

Under her bunk, bolted into the deck plating behind a false panel, was a data core the size of a fist. Tamsin pulled it out, slotted it into her reader, and entered the passphrase — thirty-one characters, a sentence only one person alive besides her could reconstruct, and that person was dead.

The file opened the way it always opened: with her own face looking back at her.

Not her face, precisely. The original's face. Detective Tamsin Ishikawa, Kuiper Line Constabulary, photographed eight days before she died. Same dark hair cut short for practicality. Same crooked nose, broken once and set slightly wrong. Same eyes that looked at the camera like it was a suspect refusing to answer questions.

Twelve years ago, Tamsin had woken in a vat on a station whose name she'd never learned, with those memories waiting for her like luggage — not memories, exactly, but the shape of them, the imprint. Enough to know how the original thought. Enough to know she'd been investigating something the Admiralty wanted buried. Enough to know she'd been murdered for it, and that the case had died with her, unsolved, until the people who'd grown her decided a clone might finish what the detective started.

They'd been wrong about that. Or maybe they'd just gotten tired of waiting. Either way, Tamsin had walked out of that facility with a forged registry, a stolen ship, and a murder case file she couldn't crack and couldn't put down.

She scrolled the familiar entries. Witness statements from people now dead or scattered. Ballistics reports that contradicted each other in ways that weren't sloppy but *curated*. And at the center of it, the hole: the thing the original had found, the thing that got her killed, referenced obliquely in her own notes — *if I'm right about the Meridian, none of this stays buried* — with nothing after it. No evidence. No coordinates. Just a dead woman's certainty, inherited by a copy who'd spent twelve years hauling beans and pretending that was enough.

It wasn't enough. It had never been enough. But it was hers, in the strangest possible sense — the only inheritance she had, the only proof that the face in the mirror had ever belonged to anyone at all.

Tamsin closed the file, re-encrypted the core, and bolted it back under the bunk.

Then she sat on the bunk's edge in the dim of her cabin and listened to the station breathe around her — coolant pumps, foot traffic, the distant clang of the loading crews — and allowed herself, as she allowed herself once per docking cycle, one minute of honesty.

She was lonely.

Not dramatically. Not in any way that interfered with function. She liked Okonkwo fine. She traded jokes with the harbormaster's clerk. She had a standing arrangement with a noodle stall on Ring Three where the owner knew her order and never asked questions, which was the closest thing to intimacy her security protocols permitted. But liking people at arm's length was a skill, and like all skills it cost something, and the cost compounded quietly over eleven years until some nights the silence in the cabin felt less like safety and more like a hull breach happening very, very slowly.

"One minute's up," she told herself, and stood, and the feeling folded itself away into the compartment where she kept it, next to the case file and the memory of the vat and every other thing she refused to carry in her hands.

* * *

She was locking down the bridge when the alert came through — not on her console, but on the general harbor band, the frequency where ports gossiped.

"All vessels, all berths, this is Ceres Gate Traffic on advisory. We have an unidentified vessel inbound on approach vector nine, no transponder, no registry match, no response to hails. Salvage tugs, be advised: priority contract available for tow assistance. Repeat, we have a ghost coming home."

Tamsin paused with her hand on the lock.

Approach vector nine. That was deep-space trajectory — way out past the Line, the kind of vector you only flew if you'd come from somewhere far and hadn't planned the trip well. No transponder meant either pirates or ghosts, and pirates broadcast false transponders rather than none.

On the harbor band, chatter bloomed. Dockworkers speculating. Someone claiming visual: old hull, freighter class, decades out of date. Someone else saying the thing everyone was already thinking:

"No way that's the *Halcyon Wake*. That ship's been missing twelve years."

Tamsin went very still.

She knew the name. Everyone in the Kuiper Line knew the name. The *Halcyon Wake* had gone missing on a routine deep-haul the same year the war started — the same year the Meridian massacre lit the fuse, the same year a detective named Ishikawa started asking questions that got her killed. Twelve years of rumor had made the ship a ghost story. Sailors swore they'd seen her drifting past Neptune. Conspiracy feeds used her as a thumbnail image.

And now, according to the harbor band, she was drifting toward Ceres Gate with her lights off and her mouth shut.

Tamsin should have stayed in her berth. Every instinct she'd honed over eleven years said *stay small, stay boring, stay invisible* — a ghost ship drawing a salvage crowd was the worst possible place for a woman built out of forged paper.

But the salvage contract was live, and salvage law was older than the war and crueler than the Admiralty: first qualified vessel to render assistance could claim rights. And Tamsin Ishikawa was desperate in the quiet, permanent way of people who've spent twelve years running from a question — and somewhere under the fear, in the locked room where she kept the case file, a dead detective's voice was saying what it always said:

*If I'm right about the Meridian, none of this stays buried.*

"Gate Traffic, this is the *Mule's Patience*," Tamsin heard herself say, keying the band before the smarter part of her could stop it. "I'm fueled and rated for tow work. Put me in the queue for vector nine."

A pause. Then the clerk's voice, dry as ever: "*Patience*, you sure? Ghost ships pay in trouble more than credits."

"They usually do," Tamsin said. "Queue me anyway."

She dropped back into the pilot's seat, brought the old barge's drives up from cold with a rumble like a clearing throat, and pushed away from berth twelve into the traffic stream — one more dull little hauler in a busy port, invisible as ever, heading straight for the brightest light in twelve years of darkness.

Ahead, on the approach scopes, the ghost of the *Halcyon Wake* turned slowly against the stars, dark and patient, as if it had been waiting for someone specific to come out and meet it.

Tamsin had spent her whole life making sure no one was looking for her.

Someone, it seemed, had been anyway.
