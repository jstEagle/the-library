## 10. Intercepted

The warning came as a whisper before it came as a shout.

"Contact," Mio said from the sensor board, her voice pitched carefully flat in the way she'd learned from watching Tamsin. "Three signatures, bearing zero-one-seven. Coming out of the shadow of the K-class rock at the edge of the debris field."

Tamsin was already moving. Twelve years of hauling contraband seed stock past Admiralty checkpoints had taught her the difference between a patrol that happened to be nearby and a patrol that had been waiting for you, and this one had the particular patience of something that had been sitting in the dark of a comet's shadow for hours, engines cold, letting its hull match the temperature of the rock so no thermal eye would find it.

"Hunter-killers," Brask said. He'd come up from the gunnery station without being called, the way he always did when the universe announced itself. His big hands settled on the console like they were greeting an old friend with bad news. "Three of them. That's not a patrol pattern. That's an execution detail."

"Range?"

"Forty thousand klicks and closing. They'll have firing solutions inside six minutes." He paused. "They're hailing us. Fleet encryption, priority channel."

Oyelaran appeared in the hatchway, still wiping her hands on a rag she'd been using on the medbay's water recycler, which was to say still pretending everything aboard the ship was normal. "Should I take the children somewhere?"

"There's nowhere," Tamsin said quietly. She looked at the fold mouth ahead of them — that slow-shimmering lens of distorted space where the Halcyon Wake had gone in young and come out old, or the other way around, depending on how you counted — and then back at the tactical display where three Admiralty warships were unfolding themselves out of ambush like knives from a sleeve.

The *Meridian* hung behind them, ten thousand empty evacuation pods drifting in its hold like a held breath. The truth sat in their cargo bay, and the people who'd built the lie were coming to collect it.

"Answer the hail," Tamsin said. "Audio only."

* * *

The voice that filled the bridge was one Tamsin knew better than she wanted to.

"This is Lieutenant Idris Vale, commanding officer of the Admiralty hunter-kill squadron *Vigilant*, speaking for Captain Reyes of the *Sable Mark* and Captain Oduya of the *Long Watch*. Vessel in the fold exclusion zone, you are ordered to heave to and prepare for boarding. You will surrender all data storage devices, sealed or otherwise, and submit your crew for identity verification."

A pause. Just long enough. Long enough that Tamsin, who had spent twelve years learning to hear what people didn't say, heard it.

He knew whose ship this was. He knew what was in the black box cradle. And he was saying *surrender* instead of *surrender or be destroyed*, which in Admiralty grammar was practically a love letter.

"Tamsin," Brask said under his breath. "Two of those captains. Oduya commanded the picket line at Thessaly Reach. Her wing lost eleven ships covering the retreat there. Reyes lost her whole first command in the opening battle. These aren't Corvane's people. These are war-fighters. People who bled for the story we're carrying proof is a lie."

"I know," Tamsin said.

"You're smiling."

"I'm thinking." She turned it over in her head, the way she used to turn over case evidence in another life, another body, another name. A weapon cuts both ways or it isn't a weapon; it's just a club. And a truth you only show to the enemy is a confession. But a truth you show to everyone —

"Vex," she called down the corridor. "Get up here. Bring the black box playback rig and patch it into the external comm array. Full broadcast band. Not encrypted. Not point-to-point. Everything."

Vex's head appeared around the hatch frame, eyes wide. "Everything? As in, *everything* everything? As in every ship in the system can—"

"As in every ship in the system can hear it. Yes."

Mio looked at her twin. Her twin looked back. Some silent conference occurred between them, conducted in the currency of raised eyebrows, and then Vex said, "Give me four minutes," and vanished.

"Tamsin." Oyelaran's voice was gentle in the way that meant she was about to say something ungentle. "Once you do this, there's no version of your life that comes back. Not the registry names, not the quiet runs, not any of it. You understand that."

Tamsin thought about the encrypted file under her bunk. Twelve years of a dead woman's unsolved case, carried like a stone in a pocket. She thought about Sefa's small hand recognizing her face. She thought about Vale's voice on the open channel, saying *surrender* like a man asking permission.

"I stopped being anyone quiet the moment I opened that box," she said. "I'm just catching the rest of me up."

* * *

They answered the hail on the fourth minute, exactly when Vex gave the thumbs-up from the comm station: broadcast rig live, external array hot, one minute of the Meridian recording cued and ready.

"Lieutenant Vale," Tamsin said. "This is the salvage vessel *Quiet Fortune*. We're claiming emergency sanctuary under Article Nine of the Deep Space Salvage Convention. We are not armed. We are not resisting. And we have something the fleet needs to hear."

"Tamsin." Just her name, on an open channel, in front of two captains and a squadron. She could hear what it cost him. She could also hear that he paid it anyway. "You are harboring stolen Admiralty property. Surrender the black box and I can protect you. I can't protect you if you make this hard."

"That's the thing, Idris." She kept her voice level, almost conversational, the tone of a woman discussing cargo manifests. "Nobody can protect anybody from what's in this box. It's not mine. It never belonged to the Admiralty either. It belongs to ten thousand people who are supposed to be dead."

Silence on the channel. Then, carefully: "What are you talking about?"

"Play it," Tamsin said, and nodded to Vex.

* * *

The recording was ninety seconds long. They broadcast sixty of them.

It began with cabin ambience — the hum of a passenger liner's environmental systems, the distant sound of a child laughing somewhere two decks down. Then a woman's voice, official and calm, the voice of a Meridian evacuation officer running a drill or a departure:

"—all passengers proceed to your assigned pods. This is not an emergency. Repeat, this is not an emergency. Evacuation is proceeding on schedule. All ten thousand pods report green status. Pod bay doors will remain unlocked until final departure—"

And then the part that mattered. The part Tamsin had listened to forty times in the dark of her bunk, the part that had rearranged her understanding of the war the way a fault line rearranges a landscape:

"—final log entry, Meridian, outbound from Ceres Gate under escort. All systems nominal. Passenger manifest confirmed complete. We are departing under our own power, on schedule, with all souls accounted for. There has been no attack. There has been no massacre. Whoever is listening to this after we're gone — we were never bombed. We were *moved*."

Static. Then nothing.

Sixty seconds of a dead woman's voice telling both fleets that the founding atrocity of twelve years of war — the massacre of the Meridian, ten thousand civilians, the burning of the evacuation corridor, the grief that had filled every recruiting office on both sides — had been staged. That the liner had left intact. That its people had been taken somewhere, by someone, for reasons written in someone else's hand.

Tamsin watched the tactical display while it played.

Captain Oduya's *Long Watch* went still first. Engines cutting from burn to drift, the way a person goes still when they've heard something they need to think about. Thirty seconds later, the *Sable Mark* did the same. Two warships hanging motionless in the dark at the edge of a debris field full of older ghosts, their captains presumably listening to the same sixty seconds their crews were hearing, because Vex hadn't patched it to the bridge channels — he'd patched it to *every* channel, including the ones sailors use when they think officers aren't listening.

"They're hesitating," Mio whispered. "Both of them. Why are they hesitating?"

"Because they lost people," Brask said. His voice was rough. His brother had died in the war's first battle, at a place called Halloway's Drift, fighting to avenge the Meridian. "Because every one of them lost somebody to that story, and you don't hesitate over a lie. You hesitate over the thing you were afraid might be true."

On the command channel, Vale spoke again, and his voice had changed. The officer's cadence was gone. What was left was younger and more tired.

"Tamsin. Is it real? Tell me it's fabricated. Tell me it's a clone-deepfake, tell me it's Kuiper Line propaganda, tell me anything — but if you tell me it's real, I have to know."

"It's real," Tamsin said. "We've seen the ship, Idris. She's sitting right behind us, intact, undamaged, holding ten thousand empty pods. Your war has a crime scene, and the crime scene says nobody died."

Another silence. Longer. On the display, the *Vigilant* held its firing solution — she could see it, the ugly geometry of a locked targeting lattice — but the two escorts had drifted out of alignment, just slightly, just enough that a gunner would have to fire *around* his own squadron mates to hit her.

Then a new voice joined the channel. Female, clipped, unfamiliar. Captain Reyes of the *Sable Mark*.

"Lieutenant Vale. Requesting confirmation of orders. My weapons are free per standing doctrine, but I am requesting confirmation." A beat. "For the record."

"For the record," Captain Oduya echoed, and Tamsin heard it clearly now: two captains building a paper trail in real time. Whatever happened next, they wanted history to know they had asked.

Vale was quiet for five full seconds. When he spoke, he was the officer again, but the officer was walking a ledge.

"All units hold fire. Hold fire and maintain position. This is now an intelligence matter and it escalates to fleet command. Nobody fires on anybody until Admiral Corvane's office confirms the engagement order personally. Do you copy, both ships?"

"Copy," said Oduya.

"Copy," said Reyes. And then, off-channel, on a frequency the twins' rig was happily slurping up anyway, she added, very quietly, to no one in particular: "*Moved.* Ten thousand people, moved. To where? By whom? God help us, to *where*?"

* * *

They stood on the bridge in the aftermath of it, the way people stand in a room after a storm has passed through without hitting the house.

"Well," said Oyelaran. "That went well."

Brask made a sound that was half laugh, half something else. "You call that well?"

"I call it 'nobody shot us,' which on my personal scale is a festival."

Tamsin let herself breathe. Out the forward ports, the fold mouth shimmered its slow silver shimmer, indifferent to empires. Behind them, three warships hung in the dark like held fists. Somewhere in the debris field, decades-old wrecks from both fleets drifted in their curated silence, the physical evidence of a history that had been edited, and she understood suddenly that the editing had never stopped — that right now, at this moment, someone in an Admiralty office was already writing the report that would explain why a salvage vessel had broadcast fabricated terrorist propaganda, and someone on the other side was writing the mirror-image report, and the machine that had eaten the Meridian's ten thousand was already digesting this too.

Unless.

"Vex," she said. "How much of the full recording do we have left?"

"The whole thing. Ninety-one minutes. Cabin logs, pod bay telemetry, the escort captain's own voice logs, the works." He patted the black box like it was a temperamental animal he'd befriended. "It's all keyed to her genome, but once it's unlocked, it plays for anyone."

"Keep it unlocked," Tamsin said. "And keep the broadcast rig hot."

Mio frowned. "You're expecting company."

"I'm expecting *more* company." She looked at the tactical plot, at the empty space beyond the three warships, at the deep dark where a fleet flagship would appear if a certain admiral decided this situation required a personal touch. "That hesitation out there? That's not obedience. That's a message going up the chain asking permission. And when the answer comes back, it won't come from anyone who ever loved the Meridian's passengers. It'll come from whoever wrote the lie."

The comm board chimed. Priority channel. Fleet encryption, highest tier — the kind of encryption that meant the sender wasn't bothering to hide anymore.

Vale's voice again, but stripped of everything warm. Recorded, not live. Standard practice for formal orders, and standard practice for a man who couldn't trust his own mouth.

"*Salvage vessel Quiet Fortune. By order of Admiral S. Corvane, commanding, Third Battle Group, you are directed to surrender immediately and without condition. Failure to comply will be met with lethal force. Acknowledge.*"

Behind the recorded order, faintly, the channel stayed open a half-second longer than it should have. Long enough for Tamsin to hear Vale breathe once, quickly, the breath of a man reading words he hates in a room where he can be heard reading them.

She looked at Brask. Brask looked at the fold.

"We can outrun them in there," he said slowly. "Maybe. If the fold doesn't age us into dust, or turn the reactor inside out, or do whatever it did to those kids in reverse."

"If we stay," Mio said, "we die."

"If we run," Vex said, "we die *interesting*."

Tamsin put her hand flat on the console, over the plot, over the little icons of the ships that had hesitated and the empty space where worse things were coming from.

"Get everyone strapped in," she said. "Wake the children's guardian channel and tell Oyelaran to dose them for heavy acceleration. We're going into the fold."

"And if it kills us?" Oyelaran asked.

Tamsin thought of the original — the detective, the ghost, the woman whose face she wore and whose case she carried — and of the recording still waiting in the box, ninety-one minutes of a dead woman who had planned for this exact moment years before Tamsin existed.

"Then it kills us knowing the truth," she said, and lit the drive.
