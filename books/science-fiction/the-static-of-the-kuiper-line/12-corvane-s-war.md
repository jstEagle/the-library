## 12. Corvane's War

Admiral Sera Corvane had not slept well in thirty-one years, and she had stopped pretending it was insomnia.

She stood on the observation deck of the flagship *Vigilant Merit*, watching the fold anomaly shimmer at the edge of sensor range like heat off summer asphalt — a place where light forgot which way it was going. Somewhere inside that shimmer was a passenger liner that should have been a grave. Somewhere behind her, in the wardroom, Lieutenant Vale was drafting a report she would read twice before he finished writing it.

"Ma'am?" Her aide, Commander Reyes, hovered at the door with a slate and the particular expression of a man about to deliver weather news to a farmer.

"Say it."

"The salvage pilot's ship is still holding position at the fold mouth. The hunter-kill squadron has them bracketed. And—" He hesitated, which told her everything before his mouth did. "Two of the escort captains have gone quiet on the command channel. Not refusing orders. Just... quiet."

Corvane turned back to the glass. Thirty-one years ago she had been a young captain with a spotless record and a terrible understanding of arithmetic, and an old woman named First Minister Okonkwo had sat her down in a room with no recording devices and asked her a question that had been eating at both governments for a decade:

*What do you do when peace costs more than war?*

---

People would call her a monster eventually. She had already written most of their speeches for them, in her head, during long watches when the *Merit* drifted through cold dark and the coffee went bitter in the pot. *Monster* was the easy word. Monsters did things for pleasure, or madness, or hunger. Monsters were other.

The truth was more embarrassing. The truth was that Sera Corvane loved people.

Not in the soft way — she'd never been any good at the soft way; ask her ex-wife, who had left with a parting gift of devastating accuracy: *"You love humanity, Sera. It's persons you can't stand."* No, she loved people the way an engineer loves a bridge under load. She could see what they were capable of bearing. She could see what they became when something worth carrying finally appeared.

And twelve years ago, both fleets had nothing worth carrying.

She remembered the armistice talks before the war — the real ones, the ones that never made the histories. Two exhausted governments, the Ceres Combine's merchant council and the Admiralty's high command, staring across a table at each other with the specific despair of institutions that had outlived their purpose. The Kuiper Line had been drawn as a border nobody wanted. Trade was dying. Both fleets were bloated, politicized, half their ships rusting at anchor while their crews' pensions evaporated in budget fights between ministries that had already moved on.

Peace wasn't coming. Peace had *arrived*, quietly, years earlier, and it was killing everyone slowly.

"We need a cause," Okonkwo had said. "Something worth dying for. Because right now we're dying for nothing, and everyone can feel it."

Corvane had thought she was being rhetorical.

She hadn't been.

---

The *Meridian* plan had taken eleven months to build and four hours to execute, and it was, Corvane still maintained, the finest piece of operational work either fleet had ever produced.

A luxury liner, scheduled for scrapping anyway. Ten thousand evacuation pods — real pods, purchased through six shell companies, because Corvane did not believe in cutting corners on the details that mattered. A crew of volunteers who knew they were flying into history, paid triple, sworn to silence, resettled afterward under new names on colonies so distant their mail took two years. The liner itself flew into the fold anomaly's margin — not deep enough to be destroyed, just deep enough to vanish from every chart, every scan, every memory.

Then the recordings. Staged distress calls, spliced and layered until even Corvane's own signals analysts wept reviewing them. The "massacre" of the *Meridian*, ten thousand civilians murdered by the other side's guns, played over every channel in the Kuiper Line on a Tuesday morning in autumn.

She had watched the fleets transform overnight. Ships that couldn't get replacement gaskets in under a year suddenly had full requisition priority. Recruitment stations had lines around the block. Old enemies shook hands in front of cameras and meant it, united by grief, and the grief was fake but the unity was real, and the unity held for twelve years.

Twelve years of purpose. Twelve years of young people enlisting because they believed in something. Twelve years of both societies pouring their idle rage and idle hands into a struggle that gave them shape.

Was it a lie? Of course it was a lie. It was also the only honest thing either government had done in a generation, because at least this lie admitted what everyone else denied: that human beings cannot live without a story, and if you don't give them a good one, they'll find a bad one.

She had visited the resettled *Meridian* volunteers once, incognito, on a colony world where the sunsets lasted four hours. She'd found one of them — the former purser, a round cheerful man named Doss — running a bakery. She'd bought bread from him and asked, carefully, how he felt about the sacrifice.

He'd laughed. "Best decision I ever made. My kids have schools. My wife has a clinic. Somebody had to pay for all this." He'd handed her the loaf like a benediction. "Whoever they were, I hope they know it worked."

He didn't know she was the somebody. She'd walked back to her shuttle crying, and she had never once regretted it since.

That was the thing no tribunal would ever understand. The *Meridian* hadn't cost anything. It had *bought* everything. Every hospital built during the war buildout. Every school. Every shipyard job, every pension restored, every young person who found meaning instead of drift. Ten thousand empty pods against forty million saved lives. She'd do the arithmetic a thousand times in the dark watches and it always came out the same.

The war was the kindest thing anyone had ever done for these people.

And now a clone with a stolen face and a derelict freighter full of children was about to take it away from them.

---

"Admiral." Reyes again, quieter now. "Lieutenant Vale's report is in."

She read it standing up. It was exactly what she expected: precise, dutiful, and gutted. Vale had found the black box cradle empty aboard the salvage ship and covered for its pilot — Corvane noted the omission, noted it the way she noted all cracks in load-bearing structures — and then reported the anomaly anyway, because Idris Vale was constitutionally incapable of doing otherwise. That was why she'd promoted him. That was also why, someday, she might have to break him.

"Pilot's name?" she asked, though she already knew. The genome match had come through intelligence channels three days ago, flagged and re-flagged and finally dumped on her desk with the kind of nervous urgency analysts reserved for things that shouldn't exist.

"Tamsin Ishikawa," Reyes said. "Or — the registry says a dozen names. The biometrics say Ishikawa. The original."

The original. Dead fourteen years, officially killed by pirates in a case file that Corvane herself had signed the closure order on. Cloned, apparently, by a dead detective with better foresight than her entire intelligence service.

There was a poetry to it that Corvane almost appreciated. Almost.

"She found the *Meridian*," she said.

"It appears so, ma'am."

"And she's broadcast a minute of the recording already." Corvane tapped the slate. Two escort captains silent on the channel. By morning it would be four. By the end of the week, unless she acted, it would be the whole line, and then the whole war, and then everything the lie had built would come down in a single afternoon like a bridge with one corroded cable.

"Options," she said.

Reyes laid them out. Interception and seizure — too slow, too public, and the escort captains were already wobbling. Deniable destruction of the salvage vessel — possible, but the evidence existed in more than one place now; killing Ishikawa would only make her a martyr with a paper trail. Which left—

"Scorched earth," Corvane said.

Reyes went pale. "At Ceres Gate? Ma'am, that station holds—"

"I know what it holds, Commander. Forty thousand residents. I signed the evacuation contingency myself nine years ago, which means I also know that a reactor-seeded detonation blamed on a terrorist cell buys us a full investigation cycle — eighteen months minimum — during which the relay array is rubble, the evidence chain is buried, and the story writes itself." She met his eyes. "Ishikawa and her cell destroy the relay trying to flee. Tragic. The war rallies. We rebuild the array with military oversight this time. Life continues."

"And the forty thousand?"

"Evacuation protocols exist. They'll be slow and they'll be ugly and some people will die in the crush, and I will carry every single name." She said it flatly, without drama, because drama was for people who hadn't already done the arithmetic. "But I will not carry the end of the only thing that's kept two civilizations from tearing themselves apart out of sheer boredom. If the choice is a station or a war — a *real* war, this time, with real massacres, fought by people who've forgotten why they're fighting — then the station loses, and I lose sleep, and history gets to call me whatever it likes."

Reyes looked at her for a long moment. She'd seen that look before, on younger officers, on her ex-wife, on her own reflection sometimes: the look of someone realizing the monster has a face, and the face is tired, and the face believes.

"You really think you're saving them," he said. Not accusing. Almost wondering.

"I *know* I am," Corvane said gently. "That's what makes me dangerous, Commander. A monster could be stopped. A true believer has to be *outlasted*."

She turned back to the glass. Out there, past the fold's shimmer, a dying ore barge full of misfits and ghosts was deciding whether to run or fight, and somewhere aboard it a woman wearing a dead detective's face was probably doing arithmetic of her own.

Good, Corvane thought. Let her count. Let her see what the numbers cost. Everyone thinks they're willing to pay until the bill arrives.

"Order the squadron to hold position," she said. "No firing unless fired upon — I want her running, not fighting. And prepare the *Merit* to make for Ceres Gate. Best burn."

"Aye, Admiral." Reyes paused at the door. "And the detonation protocol?"

Corvane closed her eyes. In the dark behind them: a bakery on a far colony, warm bread, a laughing man who thought his sacrifice had been someone else's idea. Forty million lives stacked like sandbags against the flood. One station. One relay. One clone who thought the truth was a gift.

"Arm it," Admiral Sera Corvane said. "Countdown starts the moment we clear the jump gate."

* * *

Later, alone, she poured the last of the coffee and sat with the old case file open on her slate — the original Tamsin Ishikawa's murder investigation, the one that had gotten too close, the one whose closure order bore Corvane's signature fourteen years cold.

The detective had been good. Better than good. In her final logged transmission — recovered, sealed, and locked in Corvane's private archive where no one would ever think to look — she'd written a single line that Corvane had reread perhaps a hundred times over the years:

*You built a beautiful lie, Admiral. But lies are load-bearing, and mine will find the crack.*

Corvane set down the cup and looked out at the stars, at the shimmer of the fold, at the small stubborn light of a freighter that refused to die.

"So find it," she said softly, to no one, to the ghost, to the girl with her face. "But understand what falls when it goes."

The *Vigilant Merit* turned toward Ceres Gate, and the countdown began.
