## 13. Running the Fold

The fold mouth hung ahead of them like a bruise in the dark — a slow-turning lens of light where the stars behind it bent into rings that no geometry Tamsin had ever trusted could explain.

"Tell me again why we're flying *into* the thing," Brask said from the copilot's chair, his big hands gripping the armrests as if he could hold the ship steady by will alone.

"Because Corvane's guns are behind us," Tamsin said, "and physics is ahead of us, and I've spent twelve years learning which of those two I can negotiate with."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." She keyed the intercom. "Vex? Mio? Talk to me about my reactor."

Mio's voice came through, tinny and too cheerful for a woman strapped into an engineering bay that was currently shedding insulation like a molting bird. "Reactor's at ninety-one percent containment integrity, captain, which sounds bad until you remember it was at sixty-eight when we bought her."

"She was a gift," Vex corrected somewhere off-mic.

"She was a bribe."

"She was both. That's what made her special."

Tamsin cut the channel before the twins could finish their liturgy of affection for a ship that had been hauling ore when they were born. On the tactical display, Corvane's squadron spread out behind them in a fan — four hulls, clean and hungry, closing at a burn rate the old barge couldn't match even on her best day, which had been approximately eleven years ago.

"Range to fold threshold?" she asked.

"Four hundred klicks," Brask said. "They'll have firing solution in ninety seconds. Maybe less if their gunnery officer drinks the same coffee mine used to."

"Then we don't give them ninety seconds." Tamsin wrapped her hands around the yoke and felt the ship answer — that familiar shudder, the groan of a hull that had been patched so many times the patches had patches. "Everyone hold onto something that loves you back."

She burned hard.

* * *

The first sheet hit them forty seconds out.

There was no warning, exactly — or rather, there was every warning, all at once, arriving in the wrong order. The forward display flickered and showed them the debris field they'd already passed, whole and unbroken, ships from both fleets drifting in formation as if the decades hadn't touched them. Then it showed the same field rusted and shattered. Then both at once, layered, like two photographs held up to the same light.

"Time shear," Oyelaran called from the medbay station aft, where she'd strapped herself in with three children's worth of spare webbing because nobody else had wanted it. "We're crossing exposure gradients. Instruments are reading our own hull age at — hold on." A pause. "Hold *on*. Captain, the bow plating just registered as thirty years older than it was this morning."

"The whole ship?"

"The whole front half. The stern's still young. We're becoming a metaphor."

Brask barked something that might have been a laugh if it hadn't been so frightened. "A metaphor with a reactor."

"A reactor," Mio announced over the comm, "that has just aged eleven years in nine seconds, and I would like everyone to know that I am taking this personally."

"Patch it," Tamsin said.

"I'm *going* to patch it! Vex, hand me the — no, the other — VEX."

"I'm holding the good welder!"

"You're holding the *sentimental* welder! They're different welders!"

Tamsin tuned them out. There was a particular silence a pilot needed, and she built it around herself now, plank by plank: the hum of the drive, the tick of the yoke under her palms, the bruise-light swelling in the viewport until it filled everything. Behind her, missiles would be launching. She didn't look. Looking backward through a thing that ate time seemed unwise.

The second sheet took the port sensor array and gave it back as something older than the ship itself — readings in formats the nav computer didn't recognize, telemetry from instruments that hadn't been manufactured yet, or wouldn't be. For one vertiginous moment the display showed Tamsin her own position *tomorrow*, a ghost track arcing deeper into the fold, and then it was gone, and she hated how much she wanted it back.

"Captain," Oyelaran said quietly. "The gradient theory. If the anomaly reverses biological age along exposure gradients —"

"I know what you're going to say."

"We're flying deeper than the *Halcyon Wake* ever did. Her crew came out children. If we stay in long enough —"

"How long is long enough?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows. That's rather the point of an anomaly."

"Then we fly fast and we don't stay." Tamsin fed more burn to the drive, and the old barge complained in a register she felt in her teeth. "Brask, anything behind us?"

He checked. His face did something complicated. "No."

"No pursuit?"

"No *anything*. The space behind us is — Tamsin, there's no space behind us. Just fold. It closed."

The cabin went very quiet. Even the twins stopped arguing over the comm, which was how Tamsin knew it was serious; the twins arguing was the ship's heartbeat, and its absence was arrhythmia.

"So we can't go back," Mio said finally.

"We were never going back," Tamsin said. "Corvane made sure of that. Now the universe has agreed with her, which is the first time those two have ever been on the same side, and I resent it."

* * *

The third sheet was the worst, and the best, and Tamsin never found a way to describe it afterward that didn't sound like lying.

Reality stuttered. Not around them — *through* them. For a span of seconds that might have been a breath or a year, the ship existed in several states at once: new, ancient, burning, launched, and not yet built. Tamsin saw the barge's keel laid down in a Ceres yard, saw her own hands on this yoke gray-haired and scarred differently, saw the cargo bay empty and full and full of people who hadn't boarded yet. She heard a voice — her own voice, but pitched the way the original Tamsin's recordings sounded, worn smooth by use — saying something she couldn't catch, and the not-catching hurt worse than any wound she'd taken in twelve careful years.

Then it passed, and the ship was a ship again, and she was a woman again, and her eyes were wet without her permission.

"Captain?" Brask's voice, careful. "You went somewhere."

"Just visiting." She wiped her face with the back of one hand and kept the other on the yoke. "I didn't buy anything."

Ahead, the fold's interior opened up — and this was the wonder part, the part that curdled and sweetened in equal measure. Inside the anomaly, space wasn't black. It was *layered*, sheets of starlight sliding over one another like oil on water, and in the gaps between layers Tamsin could see the debris field from every era of its existence simultaneously: the battle that had scattered these ships, the peace that had preceded it, the launch-day parades, all of it stacked like sediment. History wasn't hidden here. It was *preserved*, pressed flat between moments, waiting for someone with the nerve and the stupidity to come read it.

"Oh," said Mio over the comm, small-voiced. "Oh, that's beautiful."

"Don't get attached," Vex said. "Beautiful things in engineering bays are usually on fire."

"It's not on fire."

"Give it a minute."

It nearly did catch fire. The reactor, having aged a decade in the second sheet, chose the fourth sheet to develop opinions. Alarms cascaded — containment flutter, coolant pressure dropping in a pattern Mio identified over the comm as "the pump remembering being older, which is not a sentence I ever thought I'd say professionally."

"Talk me through it," Tamsin said, holding the ship level along a seam between time-sheets that kept trying to slide sideways.

"Mio's bypassing the secondary coolant loop," Vex reported. "I'm reinforcing the containment jacket with — okay, honestly, with prayer and scrap metal."

"Scrap metal first. Prayer's a backup."

"You'd think that, but the scrap metal's from the *Halcyon Wake*, and I've developed feelings."

The ship shuddered. Somewhere below decks, metal screamed in a voice that sounded genuinely elderly, and Tamsin understood with cold clarity that the hull was dying in fast-forward — that every minute inside the fold was costing the barge a year, maybe five, that they were spending the ship's life to save their own, and that the ledger was not going to balance in their favor.

"How much hull do we have left?" she asked Oyelaran, because the medic had appointed herself keeper of grim statistics somewhere around the second sheet.

Oyelaran checked. "Structurally? Enough. Emotionally? Also enough, but barely, and I include myself in that assessment."

"Good answer. Brask — depth reading. Where's the far side?"

He studied the layered light, jaw working. "Can't tell. Distance doesn't behave in here. But—" He pointed. In the gaps between sheets, far off, something solid: normal space, ordinary black, honest stars. An exit, or the idea of one. "There. Maybe."

"Maybe is my favorite word." Tamsin lined up the burn. "Twins, whatever you're doing to the reactor, keep doing it for six more minutes."

"Six minutes," Mio repeated, in the tone of a woman accepting a dare from God.

* * *

They ran the last stretch with the hull groaning like a shipwreck in slow motion and the viewport full of impossible light. Tamsin flew by feel and by faith, threading seams in the time-sheets that opened and closed like breathing, and twice the ship passed through places where she could see herself — the ghost tracks, the other versions, the woman at this yoke in years that hadn't happened or had happened to someone else. Once, the ghost at the other yoke looked up. Tamsin didn't. Some things you don't check in the rearview.

The exit sheet hit them like surfacing from deep water.

Normal space rushed in — real vacuum, real stars, real silence — and the barge came through trailing sparks and sixty years of sudden age, her hull pitted and gray, her paint gone to powder, her name plate corroded past reading. Every alarm died at once, either from relief or exhaustion.

For a long moment nobody spoke.

Then Mio, over the comm, in a voice thick with tears and triumph: "Reactor's stable. Barely. She's an old lady now, captain, but she's *our* old lady."

"Sixty years in twenty minutes," Oyelaran murmured. "And we're all still —" She paused, and Tamsin heard her running the medical scanner over her own hands. "Still ourselves. The gradient protected the crew compartment. Or we got lucky. I'll determine which later, when my heart rate permits science."

Brask let go of the armrests one finger at a time, like prying open a fist he'd welded shut. "We made it," he said, as if testing whether the words would hold weight. "We actually — Corvane can't follow us in there. Nobody sane follows anyone in there."

"Which means," Tamsin said slowly, "we're safe, and alone, and completely cut off from everyone we love."

The warmth drained out of the cabin in about four seconds.

"The children," Oyelaran said. "The matron. Ceres Gate."

"And the black box." Tamsin turned toward the sealed case bolted beside her station — the box that had carried the original Tamsin's voice across forty subjective years, still locked, still holding most of its secret. "The message isn't finished. Whatever's left in there, we were supposed to find it *deeper*. That's why the coordinates pointed into the fold, not just to the edge."

She looked out the viewport at the honest stars, and then back at the fold mouth behind them, already dimming, already closing like an eye deciding whether to sleep.

Behind them lay Corvane, the blockade, the countdown she could feel ticking somewhere in her chest like a second pulse. Ahead lay the rest of the truth, and a ship too old to make the trip twice.

"Vex," Tamsin said. "Mio. How much life does the old lady have left in her?"

A pause. Then Mio, gently, the way you speak near something dying: "One more run, captain. One good one. Choose it well."

Tamsin put her hand flat against the aged hull plating, feeling the faint tremor of the drive beneath — a heartbeat, old and stubborn and still hers.

"Then we go back in," she said. "All the way this time. Because the box just unlocked another layer, and the message inside it is addressed to me — and it's signed with a date that hasn't happened yet."
