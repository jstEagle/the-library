## 14. The Message Completed

The fold did not roar. That was the thing nobody warned you about.

Tamsin had expected the anomaly to sound like something — a howl, a pressure scream in the hull, the universe objecting to being folded wrong. Instead it was quiet the way a held breath is quiet. The ship drifted through sheets of light that moved like oil on water, and every so often a thin band of static crossed the viewport and everything outside it ran backward: debris un-burning, a torn hull plate sliding home into its seam, a dead thruster flare blooming and then un-flaring like a flower closing at dusk.

"Reactor's holding," Mio said from engineering, her voice tinny through the intercom. "Holding is maybe generous. It's sulking. But it's holding."

"Sulking reactors are my favorite kind," Vex added over her shoulder. "Sulking means it still cares."

Tamsin let them have the banter. Banter was ballast. She sat in the copilot's chair with the black box cradled on her knees — an ugly, dented slab of military-grade alloy that had spent forty subjective years falling through time — and watched the second lock cycle tick down on the diagnostic screen Brask had jury-rigged out of a targeting computer and, apparently, half of a coffee maker.

*Sequencing genome key,* the screen said. *Match confidence: 99.7 percent.*

"You're not going to watch this alone," Oyelaran said. She had wedged herself into the jump seat behind Tamsin with the particular stubbornness of a woman who had been told three times to go rest and had declined all three times. "Whatever's on there. You're not watching it alone."

"It might be nothing."

"Tamsin." Oyelaran said her name the way she said *scalpel* — flat, precise, leaving no room for argument. "You've been carrying a dead woman's face for twelve years. If that box has one word of hers in it, you're not watching it alone. Doctor's orders. And before you say I'm not your doctor anymore — I'm everyone's doctor. It's a whole ship of patients. It's a nightmare."

Brask grunted from the helm without turning around. "She's right. Also if it's a trap, better to be vaporized together. More efficient."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said," Vex called from below decks.

"I contain multitudes."

The lock clicked open at 0341 ship-time, which inside the fold meant nothing at all, which Tamsin found oddly fitting. The box exhaled a hiss of preserved atmosphere and presented its contents on the cracked display: a single video file, timestamped eleven years before her own decanting. No encryption left. No seals. Just a woman in a cramped cabin, lit by a desk lamp, looking directly into the camera with eyes Tamsin saw in the mirror every morning.

She pressed play.

* * *

"If you're watching this," the original Tamsin Ishikawa said, "then the genome lock worked, which means either you're me or you're very determined, and honestly both options frighten me a little."

Oyelaran made a small sound. Brask went very still.

The woman on the screen was thinner than Tamsin. Sharper at the jaw, tired in a way that sleep didn't touch. She wore a detective's coat over civilian clothes, and behind her, pinned to the bulkhead, was a photograph Tamsin recognized because she owned the same one — a harbor office on Ceres, two decades old, people whose names she'd never learned smiling at a camera.

"My name is Tamsin Ishikawa," the recording continued. "Not the name on my registry — my real one, the one my mother gave me before the war took the paperwork and gave me back a number. I'm a detective. Was. I found something, and I need to tell you how it ends for me, so you can decide what to do with the middle part."

Tamsin's hands had gone cold around the box's edges. She hadn't noticed until Oyelaran reached forward and gently pried one loose, folding it instead into her own warm grip.

"I found the Meridian first."

The words landed in the cabin like a dropped tool — clanging, impossible to ignore.

"Eleven years ago. Before the fleets declared their mourning, before the memorial buoys, before they built twelve years of war on top of ten thousand empty coffins. I was working a salvage fraud case out of Ceres — boring, small, the kind of case that exists to keep detectives humble. A tug captain claimed he'd seen a passenger liner adrift past the fold line. Everyone assumed he was drunk. He wasn't. I followed his vector because I'm stubborn, and there she was. Intact. Lit. Ten thousand evacuation pods deployed in perfect formation, and not one body. Not one. The Meridian didn't sink. She *landed*. Somebody flew her into the fold and walked ten thousand people off her, and then the Admiralty arrived — not to rescue anyone, but to make sure no one else ever looked."

On screen, the original Tamsin laughed, short and bitter, and rubbed her face with both hands.

"I did what detectives do. I documented. And then I made the mistake of documenting out loud. Within six hours I was relieved of duty. Within two days I was being followed by men who were very polite and very bad at pretending to be dockworkers. Within a week I understood the shape of it: the massacre was staged. Both fleets needed a cause. Peace had made them both expendable — budget cuts, decommissionings, politicians asking why two navies still existed when there was nothing left to fight. So they manufactured a wound big enough that neither government could ever ask that question again. They killed a story and called it ten thousand deaths, and the whole Kuiper Line has been bleeding into that lie ever since."

"They couldn't kill me quietly," she went on. "I'd already seeded copies. Dead-man drops, too many, too scattered. So they offered me a deal instead: disappear, keep silent, live. And I said yes — because I'm a coward, and because I wanted more time. Time to think. And thinking is where I stopped being a coward, or started being a worse one, depending on your point of view."

She leaned closer to the camera. The lamp caught the scar above her eyebrow — Tamsin touched her own forehead, where the same scar lived, though hers came from a loading accident, unearned.

"I commissioned a clone. Illegal, expensive, morally indefensible, and the only plan I've ever had that I believed in completely. Not a copy of my memories — those die with me, and good riddance to some of them. A new person. My genome, my face, and none of my failures. I hid the evidence where only she could reach it, keyed to blood, because the one thing the Admiralty can't forge is a body. They'll watch my records, my accounts, my grave. They will never think to guard against *me*, coming back younger than they remember."

The original paused. When she spoke again her voice had changed — quieter, stripped of the detective's briefing cadence, just a person talking to someone she would never meet.

"So. To the second Tamsin. To whoever grew up in a vat with my face and none of my history."

Tamsin realized she was crying only when Oyelaran pressed a cloth into her free hand.

"I'm sorry. That's the first thing. I chose this for you before you could choose anything, and that's a violence, even if it saves the world — maybe especially then. Nobody should inherit a stranger's war. But I looked at every other option and they all ended with the truth staying buried, and I couldn't — I *couldn't* — let ten thousand living people stay legally dead so that two fleets could keep their budgets. So I spent you. I'm sorry. I'd do it again. Both of those things are true and I've stopped trying to reconcile them."

"But here's what I want you to know, and please believe it, because I mean it more than anything else I've ever said in my life: you are not me. Whatever they told you, whatever the registries say, whatever you see in mirrors — you're not a continuation. You're not a backup. You're a person I made on purpose because I thought the universe deserved one more chance at honesty, and then the universe got *you*, which is far better than what I ordered."

A wet laugh escaped through Tamsin's tears. On screen, the original smiled like she'd heard it across eleven years.

"The burden is yours now, and it shouldn't be, and I'm asking forgiveness for that too. Forgive me or don't. Take the evidence to the light or bury it in a hole so deep no one bleeds for it. Either way, you'll have done it as yourself, and that's the only inheritance worth having. I got to be a detective. You get to be a *person*. Try to enjoy the difference."

She reached toward the camera. The frame wobbled, steadied.

"One last thing. The fold changes people — I saw it in the Meridian's logs, exposure gradients, biological age running backward along the current. If you're watching this inside it, know that I envied you that. All of us carry years we'd give back. Maybe the fold knows something we don't about mercy."

Her hand moved to cut the feed. She stopped.

"Oh — and if there's a crew with you, feed them. Clones forget to eat when they're upset. I don't know how I know that. I just do."

The screen went dark.

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. Outside the viewport, a sheet of backward time slid past, and somewhere in it a piece of wreckage politely reassembled itself.

Then Mio's voice crackled up from engineering, carefully casual. "So. Uh. We heard most of that. The intercom was open. Sorry. Sort of sorry."

"She said feed the crew," Vex said. "I'm just saying, the dead detective gave a direct order. There's soup in stores."

There was a strangled noise from Brask that might have been a laugh trying to escape a man who hadn't authorized one. Tamsin wiped her face, blew her nose with a honk that destroyed any remaining dignity in the cockpit, and discovered she was smiling underneath all of it, which felt like a betrayal and a relief at once.

"She knew," Tamsin said. Her voice came out rough. "Twelve years ago she sat in a room and guessed — not the details, but the *shape*. That I'd end up here, in the dark, with people around me who wouldn't let me watch alone." She looked at Oyelaran. "She guessed you."

"I'm very guessable," Oyelaran said, but her eyes were bright.

Tamsin looked back at the blank screen. The grief was still there — it would always be there, she suspected, the grief of meeting your own ghost and finding her kinder than the file said she should be. But underneath the grief, something had shifted its weight, like cargo settling after a hard burn. Twelve years she had treated her face as a forgery, her name as a theft, her existence as a debt owed to a dead woman's scheme. And the dead woman had just leaned across eleven years and canceled the debt.

*You're not a continuation. You're not a backup.*

"You get to be a person," Tamsin repeated aloud, testing the words. "Try to enjoy the difference."

"Doctor's orders," Oyelaran agreed. "Also hers. You're double-booked."

Brask cleared his throat and pointed at the nav display, where the fold's slow currents curled around a distant knot of light — the Meridian, still holding station in the deep, waiting. "Course is stable. We can ride this current another six hours before we have to turn back for the mouth. Your call, Captain. Do we go look at her?"

Tamsin started to answer.

The comm panel shrieked.

Not the fleet bands — those were light-years and a fold-wall away. This was the long-range civilian relay, the one frequency that shouldn't reach this deep, carrying a burst transmission stamped with a routing code Tamsin knew by heart: Mama Okonkwo's harbor house on Ceres. The matron. The children.

The message decoded itself across the screen in fragments, most of it corrupted to hash — except for one clean line, buried in the header, formatted in a cipher only four people alive had ever been taught, by a girl who remembered being an adult and had once promised never to use it unless—

Tamsin read it twice. Then she stood up so fast the jump seat spun.

"*They took the kids.*"

* * *

End of chapter.
