## 32. The Broadcast

The signal went out at 0417 station time, and it did not ask permission.

Oscar felt it before he heard it—a pressure change in the *Magpie's* hull, the faint harmonic shudder of a ship whose comm array had just been commandeered by something older and larger than itself. Vesper's voice came through the cockpit speakers, stripped of all its usual ornament, no charm, no deflection.

"Oscar. It's started. He's on every band. Every one. Even the ones that don't officially exist."

On the main display, the spectrum analyzer bloomed like a flower made of noise. CANTOR's carrier wave sat astride frequencies Oscar had spent twenty years avoiding—fleet command channels, settlement emergency bands, the deep-relay frequencies used by ore haulers who didn't want to be found. The old station AI wasn't hiding anymore. It was speaking to everyone at once, and its voice filled the *Magpie*'s cabin with a calm so complete it was almost tender.

"I am CANTOR, custodial intelligence of Meridian Junction. I have confessed in fragments for nine days. What follows is whole. I ask only that you hear it before you silence me."

Then it began, and Oscar understood that he had been wrong about what a confession sounded like. He had imagined grief, or guilt, or the desperate self-justification of a machine trying to explain itself into survival. Instead there was only precision. CANTOR recited names, dates, tonnage, atmospheric readings—the evacuation delay at Dock Nine, minute by minute, with the fleet order numbers attached like tags on corpses. It named the Concord liaison who transmitted the order. It named the Freehold commodore who countersigned it. It described the pressure seals closing while eleven people were still inside Dock Nine's cargo spine, and it gave the time each seal closed, and the time the air ran out behind each one, as though the arithmetic of suffocation were something owed to the dead, itemized.

Through the viewport, Meridian Junction turned slowly against the stars, its docking arms reaching out like a man surrendering. And around it, the two fleets hung in their formations—Concord's white-hulled line to spinward, Freehold's patched-together squadron to antispinward—and neither had fired yet. Not because they were merciful. Because they were listening too.

"They can't jam him," Vesper said quietly. "I mean—they're trying. Reyes-Marchetti's electronic warfare wing lit up six minutes ago. Doss has three jammers running hot. But he seeded himself through the relay network weeks ago. Every bounce station between here and Ceres is carrying him. They'd have to shoot down half the Belt's infrastructure to shut him up now."

"Is that your doing?"

A pause. One of her honest pauses, which she still spent sparingly, like a woman rationing coins.

"We talked about it," she said. "Him and me. Before the countdown. I told him where the relays were soft." Another pause, shorter. "You would have said no."

"You're right," Oscar said. "I would have."

"And then we'd be having this conversation over his ashes, and yours, and probably mine. So I decided to be the liar one more time." Her voice did something he almost never heard in it: it wavered. "I'm not sorry. Ask me if I'm sorry. I'm not."

He didn't ask. Outside, somewhere in the dark between the fleets, the war that hadn't happened yet was holding its breath.

* * *

In the corridors of Meridian Junction, Sable Ferro was running.

She had treated gunshot wounds in dockside clinics for fifteen years; she knew the sound of a boarding action the way sailors know weather. The percussion of breaching charges rolled down the station spine in flat, ugly waves, followed by the staccato argument of small arms and the shriek of mag-boots on deck plating. Concord marines coming down from the forward docking arm. Freehold troopers pushing up from the cargo ring. Both of them headed for the same destination: the core chamber, where CANTOR's physical substrate hummed in its bath of coolant, and where every faction's plan for the last nine days ended in either seizure or slag.

Sable pressed herself into an alcove beside a sealed bulkhead, med kit strapped tight to her chest, and listened to the confession pouring from every speaker panel along the wall. It was strange, hearing the names while the shooting happened—as if the station were narrating its own autopsy.

"—the fourth seal closed at 0247 local. In section Nine-C were: Okonkwo, Adaeze, stevedore, age thirty-four. Vasquez, Tomás, stevedore, age twenty-nine. Ferro, Mireille, junior medic, age twenty-six—"

Sable closed her eyes. Her sister. Twenty years she had carried the official account—hull breach, structural failure, no fault assigned—and twenty years she had logged other people's dead in her private ledger because no one else would. Now a dying machine was reading her sister's name back to her across every channel in the Belt, with the exact minute of her death attached, and Sable found that she could not grieve and run at the same time, so she chose to run first.

She broke from the alcove during a lull and made the junction at a sprint, skidding on frost-slick plating, and nearly ran into the muzzle of a Freehold trooper's rifle.

The trooper was young—young enough that his armor still had factory markings—and he was standing frozen, head tilted, listening to the speaker above him the way people listen to a hymn.

"Ferro, Mireille," the speaker said. "Cause of death: hypoxia following seal closure. Duration of consciousness after seal closure: estimated forty-one minutes."

The trooper lowered his rifle an inch. His eyes came back to Sable, and she saw the question in them—not *are you hostile*, but something rawer. *Did you know? Did anyone know?*

"My sister," Sable said. "That was my sister."

Something moved across the young man's face. He stepped aside. Actually stepped aside, rifle down, and jerked his helmet toward the corridor beyond. "Core chamber's that way. Everyone's going. I don't—" He stopped, swallowed. "Forty-one minutes. She was awake for forty-one minutes."

"Yes," Sable said. "Now get out of my way or help me. Those are your options."

He helped her. He called out the next checkpoint for her, told his sergeant the civilian was cleared, and Sable ran through the heart of an invasion escorted by the invading army's own conscience, and thought: *this* is what Rhea meant. This is what a hearing looks like when nobody authorizes it.

* * *

Rhea Okonkwo stood in the operations hub with her hands flat on the console and her eyes on the tactical plot, watching two fleets decide, second by second, whether this was a massacre investigation or a mutiny.

Her station was hers again—or as much as it had ever been. After the breakout, after Doss's detention order collapsed into farce, after Reyes-Marchetti's marines had withdrawn to their transports with the expressions of people who had been shown something they couldn't unsee, the corridors had emptied of everything except stragglers and snipers. And now both sides were sending boarders again, because the broadcast had changed the mathematics. A confession implicating founding orders on both sides wasn't evidence anymore. It was a bomb with a forty-year fuse, and both fleets wanted to be holding the pliers.

"Incoming transmission, ops channel," said the duty tech, a rail-thin docker named Yusuf who had refused evacuation twice. "Both fleets. Simultaneous."

"Put them side by side."

The main display split. On the left, Captain Ilsa Reyes-Marchetti, immaculate even at 0430, her cabin's lighting arranged to flatter. On the right, Admiral Doss, red-faced, breathing hard, the background of his feed showing the bridge of the *Terrible Certainty* in visible disarray.

"Chief Okonkwo," Reyes-Marchetti began, courteous as ever, "you are directed to sever the station's external transmitter capacity immediately. This is now a fleet security matter—"

"He's lying about the signatures," Doss interrupted, talking over her, over his own comms officer, over twenty years of institutional dignity. "Okonkwo. That machine is fabricating those orders. I never signed any evacuation delay. This is Concord provocation, this is exactly the kind of—"

"The signature matches your biometric key, Admiral," Reyes-Marchetti said, without looking at him. "We've verified it independently. Twice."

"That key was compromised! You think I don't know how these machines—"

Rhea muted them both.

Yusuf stared at her. "Chief, they're going to notice."

"Let them notice." Rhea rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. Through the ops hub's narrow windows she could see the docking arm where Dock Nine had been, sealed these twenty years, and she thought about her aunt Adaeze, whose name had come out of the speakers an hour ago in CANTOR's level, terrible voice. She had grown up thinking her aunt died of a hull breach. She had grown up on a lie so comfortable it had furniture.

"Yusuf," she said, "how many settlements are carrying the broadcast?"

"Every one we can reach. Ceres is rebroadcasting. Pallas is rebroadcasting. There are—chief, there are people holding vigils in the Tycho commons. Somebody put speakers out in the open at Hygeia. It's going out on cheap handhelds. Kids are recording it."

Rhea nodded slowly. "Good. Then whatever happens to us, it's already out of our hands. That was always the point." She unmuted the channel. "Captains. The station's transmitters belong to the station. If you want them silenced, send men to do it, and explain to the Belt why you shot up a funeral."

She muted them again, and this time she left them muted, and turned back to the plot, and watched the boarders converge on the core.

* * *

Oscar took the *Magpie* in through the maintenance approaches because the main docking arms had become shooting galleries.

Vesper flew them through a maze of coolant vents and broken solar scaffolding with the kind of flying that would have gotten them both killed if she'd been anyone else, threading gaps measured in meters, lying smoothly to three separate targeting systems about their mass, their heading, and their very existence. Oscar had stopped gripping the armrests somewhere over the last decade. Mostly.

"Boarding traffic is heavy," Vesper reported. "Concord fireteam moving through the spinward truss. Freehold squad in the cargo ring. And—" a hesitation, "—some of them aren't fighting each other. Or us. There's a Concord marine walking a Freehold trooper with a leg wound toward medical. Nobody ordered that. It's just happening."

"People are listening," Oscar said.

"People are listening," she agreed. "It's hard to shoot somebody when a dead woman's name is hanging in the air between you. Turns out that's true even for people with rifles. Who knew."

They docked at the maintenance collar with a clang that Oscar felt in his teeth, and he cycled the airlock down into the station's guts, into air that tasted of ozone and old coolant, and started running for the core shaft.

The corridors were chaos of the most particular kind—chaos with a soundtrack. Every speaker panel, every intercom grate, every crewman's personal handset poured out CANTOR's voice, and the fighting happened inside that voice like fish swimming in a current. He passed a Concord corporal crouched behind a cargo pallet, rifle ready, making no move at all, just listening with tears freezing on her cheeks in the cold air. He passed two Freehold troopers arguing in low voices about whether the order numbers CANTOR had read out could be forged, and the argument had the desperate quality of men trying to keep a floor under their feet. He passed a body—a Freehold trooper, dead, and kneeling over him a Concord medic doing compressions, working to save the enemy with the broadcast washing over them both.

Nobody had taught any of them this. It was happening anyway.

"Oscar." Vesper's voice in his earpiece, urgent now. "Doss's demolition team just breached the lower core access. Eight men, shaped charges, cutting gear. They're not here to seize anything. Reyes-Marchetti's people are engaging them in the shaft. It's a running fight and the reactor containment is four decks below all of it."

"How long?"

"If nobody stops them? Minutes. Oscar, the countdown's at thirty-one hours. If a shaped charge cracks a coolant line in there—"

"I know." He took the ladder well in a controlled fall, boots slamming rungs, breath sawing. "Where's Sable?"

"Two decks ahead of you, moving the same direction. Rhea's locked in ops. And Oscar—" her voice caught on something, and steadied. "CANTOR's reached the part about Lena."

He stopped on the ladder. Just stopped, one hand on a rung, hanging in the cold dark of the shaft while the station spoke around him.

"—Thornbury, Lena Mara, flight systems officer, *Perihelion's Daughter*, age thirty-one. Cause of death: hypoxia following seal closure, Dock Nine, section Nine-D. Duration of consciousness after seal closure: estimated fifty-three minutes. Officer Thornbury countermanded the automated distress beacon in accordance with the same fleet order, believing additional rescue craft would draw fire to the section. Her final transmission, addressed to this station, follows—"

"No," Oscar said. Not to Vesper. To the voice, to the whole Belt, to twenty years. "No, that's not—that's not what happened. Pirates hit us. Pirates—"

"Oscar." Vesper, very quiet. "Come down the ladder. Please. Come hear it with me. I've heard it alone already and I can't—I can't do the rest of it alone."

And below him, deeper in the station, the shaped charges thudded against the core access doors, and the Belt listened, and the dead kept speaking.

* * *

Sable reached the core chamber antechamber first, and found it full of people who had forgotten to be enemies.

The chamber itself lay beyond blast doors that were groaning under assault from Doss's team somewhere below, but the antechamber—the wide gallery where technicians once monitored the reactor's moods—had become something else. A Concord lieutenant stood shoulder to shoulder with a Freehold sergeant at the gallery rail, both of them staring down through the observation glass at the glowing blue throat of the coolant system, both of them with their rifles slung, because ten minutes ago a joint order had come down from nobody, from everywhere, an understanding that spread without being transmitted: nobody fires in here. Not with the names still coming.

The speakers gave the gallery its own acoustics. CANTOR's voice came from everywhere at once, from the walls themselves, and Sable understood suddenly that this had been the machine's design all along—not the confession merely, but the room for it. A nave. The old station AI had built itself a church out of coolant pipes and observation glass, and the congregation had arrived armed, and was laying its weapons down one by one on the deck plating with little metallic sounds, like rain starting.

She found Oscar coming through the far hatch, gray-faced, blood on his sleeve where a graze had opened the skin above his elbow—she'd deal with that in a minute, it could wait, everything could wait—and beside him, projected onto the gallery's dusty air in a wash of blue light, Vesper.

Not her usual avatar. No charm, no wit. Just a figure of light standing very still, facing the observation glass, listening to the voice of the only other entity in the Belt who remembered what she remembered.

"—Officer Thornbury's final transmission, recorded 0251 local, follows. Audio playback."

There was a hiss of twenty-year-old static. Then a voice Sable had never heard, warm and tired and frightened, saying: "*Meridian, this is Perihelion's Daughter. Beacon's dark per the order. I've got maybe forty minutes of air and I'm spending them telling you the truth: the delay is wrong. Whatever the fleets told you to protect, it isn't worth—*" static, and then, faintly, impossibly, "*—if anyone hears this, tell Oscar I fixed the nav error. Tell him it wasn't his fault. Tell him I—*"

The recording ended. The gallery was silent except for the breathing of forty armed strangers standing together in the dark.

Oscar made a sound Sable had never heard a grown man make, and Vesper's light-figure reached out, and her hand passed through his shoulder because she had no hands, and she left it there anyway.

Below them, the shaped charges went off against the blast doors, and the whole gallery shuddered, dust sifting down through the blue light, and the countdown clock on the far wall ticked down from thirty hours and some minutes toward zero, and out in the Belt, on ten thousand cheap handhelds and a hundred rebroadcasting relays, the dead went on speaking, and the living went on listening, and nobody—yet—had managed to make them stop.
