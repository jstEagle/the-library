## 3. What the Garden Remembers

The garden didn't care about the signal.

That was the first mercy of the morning, and I took it. I took it the way I took most mercies—quietly, with both hands, before it could be revoked. The Vetch's hydroponic bay ran on its own rhythms, indifferent to dead languages and dead grandmothers and the particular quality of dread that settles in a person's chest like ballast. The wheatgrass needed thinning. The dwarf citrus had decided, in its citrus way, that it was dying of neglect, which was a lie, but a productive one, since scolding it with nutrients made it fruit. I did the work. Work is what I do instead of thinking, and I am, I'll admit, very good at it.

The signal sat in the comm buffer like a guest who refuses to leave and also refuses to speak.

I had played it eleven times by then. Twelve. I'd stopped counting at twelve because counting was a form of thinking and I had a strict policy. But the number kept arriving anyway, uninvited, the way numbers do. Twelve plays of a transmission in Pre-Exodus Standard, addressed—by name, in the formal vocative, the grammar of ceremony—to *the Gardener*.

My grandmother used to sing in that language when she thought I was asleep.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about my family, or what I was willing to know, which is a smaller and more carefully pruned thing: we were gardeners. That was the whole story. Nystroms grew things. My mother grew things on Meridian's terraced slopes until she didn't, and then I grew things in a ship's belly, and the line of it was simple enough to hold in one hand. Simple stories are load-bearing. You don't pull on them.

Ilsa Nystrom, my grandmother, taught me three things before she died: how to graft a citrus stock, how to read weather in the smell of recycled air, and fragments of a language she called "the old one," which I assumed meant Old Earth Standard, the way everyone's grandparents claim some connection to the homeworld. She'd murmur it over seedlings like a blessing. *Vesh-kara ilen. Vesh-kara ilen.* I asked her once what it meant and she said, "It means the seeds remember," and when I asked what the seeds remembered, she gave me a look I have spent thirty years trying not to think about and have thought about every day since.

It was not a look that said *the seeds remember the sun.*

It was a look that said *the seeds remember what we did.*

* * *

By second shift I had run out of thinning and scolding, and the buffer was still glowing, and I am not the kind of coward who can outlast a glowing buffer. I pulled the family archive.

Let me describe the family archive, because it deserves description. It was a data core the size of a fist, wrapped in oilcloth, kept in a locker I had not opened since I inherited the Vetch. Inside the oilcloth, inside the core, inside the files: four generations of Nystrom records, and every one of them boring on purpose. Crop yields. Germination logs. Letters from relatives whose names I half-recognized, written in the flat, careful prose of people who know they might be read. My family's archive read like it had been written by an accountant with a guilty conscience, and I had always assumed that was just what family was.

I ran the signal's header against the archive the way you'd run a stranger's face against old photographs—half hoping, half terrified of a match.

The match came back in under a second. That's the thing about dread; it's efficient.

The transmission originated from a vessel registry designation I had never seen: *HALCYON VAULT*, hull class *generation-cryogenic, long-duration*, launch date two hundred and eleven years ago. I checked the Reach's public registry, because I am a thorough coward. The Halcyon Vault did not exist. Not *lost at sea* did-not-exist, not *decommissioned* did-not-exist. It did not exist the way a thing that has been *removed* does not exist. There was no launch record, no crew manifest, no insurance filing, no obituary. Two centuries of paperwork, and the ship had been scrubbed so cleanly that the absence itself had a shape.

But the Nystrom archive had a shape too. It had a hole in it, a gap between my great-grandmother's letters and my grandmother's first crop log—a gap of nineteen years I had never once asked about, because asking about gaps is how you find out what the gaps are for.

And in the archive's deepest directory, in a file with no name and a timestamp older than the Reach itself, there was a crew manifest. Fragmentary. Partially corrupted. But the line was legible, and I read it, and then I read it again, and then I sat down on the deck next to the citrus tree, which took this as further evidence of neglect.

*ILEA NYSTROM — GENETICIST, SEED ARCHIVE — HALCYON VAULT.*

Ilsa. The archive spelled it Ilea, the old way, the way she'd sometimes sign her letters when she was feeling ceremonial. Geneticist. Not gardener—*geneticist*. Seed Archive. And a ship that had never existed, launched the year my grandmother's letters stopped.

I did what any sensible person does with a fact that detonates their entire identity. I made tea.

* * *

The tea did not help. Tea rarely does, but it gives your hands something to do while your mind commits treason against you, and my mind was committing treason at a remarkable rate. Because once I saw *geneticist*, other things rearranged themselves around the word like iron filings around a magnet.

The way she'd talked about seeds. Not the way a gardener talks about seeds—reverent, practical—but the way an engineer talks about a system. "This line will hold," she'd say, pressing a seed into my palm, and she didn't mean *will sprout*. She meant something with a longer horizon. I'd thought it was just her way. Old people talk strangely; that's practically their job.

The way she'd taught me the old language. Not casually, not as heritage, but in fragments, in *doses*, always stopping before I could get fluent. I used to think she was a bad teacher. She was not a bad teacher. She was a careful one. You don't give a child the whole key to a door you're not sure they should open.

And the archive itself. Four generations of records so boring they circled around to suspicious. I had inherited a family and a ship and a garden, and I had never once asked why the garden came with a sealed locker and the family came with a scrubbed nineteen years. I'd built a whole life on top of that gap—the courier route, the silence, the careful non-answering of every message from my past—and I'd told myself the silence was grief, or temperament, or the natural condition of a person who lives between stars.

It was not grief. Grief doesn't have a crew manifest.

*Vesh-kara ilen,* I said to the empty bay, and my accent was better than I'd realized. Thirty years of pretending not to know a language, and it had been waiting in my mouth the whole time, patient as a seed. *The seeds remember.*

The seeds remembered. The question—the one sitting in my chest now like a second heart—was what.

* * *

I want to be honest about the next hour, because honesty is apparently the theme of my life now, whether I subscribed to it or not. The next hour, I considered burning the archive.

Not metaphorically. I stood in the Vetch's tiny galley with the data core in my hand and I genuinely weighed it. There is a version of me—call her the version I'd been building for thirty years—who jettisons the core into Kepler Reach's slow gravitational dark and goes back to the route. Back to the silent colonies and the seed deliveries and the wheatgrass and the citrus and the beautiful, load-bearing nothing. That version of me has a good life. That version of me has never once been shot at, audited, or asked a direct question. That version of me is, when you get down to it, the whole point of the silence: a person so carefully unremarkable that the universe agrees to leave her alone.

The signal was addressed to the Gardener. Not to Tamsin Nystrom. To the Gardener. Which meant whoever sent it knew the family, knew the work, knew the archive, knew the locker with the oilcloth. My silence had been a wall, and someone had walked through it using my grandmother's grammar, and there is no wall you can rebuild after that. The address was the crack. Everything after was just the wall coming down at its own speed.

I put the core back in the locker. I did not burn it. I'd like credit for this.

* * *

Here is what I could reconstruct, working through the night with the signal and the manifest and the nineteen-year gap spread across my console like a dissection:

The Halcyon Vault launched two hundred and eleven years ago, during the Exodus—the great scattering, when the last ships left a dying Earth and argued, all the way out, about what humanity ought to become. I knew the Exodus the way everyone in the Reach knows it: as mythology. The Adapted, who remade their bodies for worlds that never asked to host them. The Pure, who kept the old genome like a covenant. The Reach's founders, whoever they'd been, had papered over the schism with two centuries of cheerful civic history, and I had never looked under the paper, because looking under paper is not a survival trait for a person whose entire strategy is not looking.

The signal—the confession, the countdown, the thing addressed to the Gardener in my grandmother's dead language—came from a ship that officially never existed, crewed by a woman my family archive had been carefully not mentioning for two hundred years.

My grandmother had not been a gardener who happened to know a little science. She had been a geneticist who became a gardener. You don't retire from genetics into seed packets unless you are hiding something in the seed packets. And the look she'd given me—the *seeds remember* look, the look I'd been not-thinking about for three decades—suddenly had a context, and the context was: *one day someone will come asking, and you will be the only one left who knows the old words, and I am sorry, little one, for what I am planting in you.*

She'd planted me. That was the thought that finally made me put my head down on the console, citrus leaves brushing my hair, and laugh the way you laugh when the joke is on you and it isn't funny. All those grafting lessons. All those fragments of language. All those years of her watching me with that particular patience, that particular grief. I had thought I was her grandchild. I had also, apparently, been her contingency plan.

* * *

Near the end of the night shift, I found one more thing, and it is the reason I am telling you all of this instead of keeping it in the locker with the oilcloth where it belongs.

At the bottom of the unnamed file, beneath the manifest, there was a single line of text in Pre-Exodus Standard, untranslated, unannotated—the only sentence in the entire archive my family had not scrubbed boring. I read it slowly, sounding out the old grammar the way my grandmother had taught me, and my mouth knew the shapes before my mind caught up, which is either a testament to her teaching or an indictment of my pretending.

It said: *If you are reading this, the Vault has spoken. Do not go to the Reach. Do not go alone. The garden is not what you think it is.*

I sat with that for a long time. The garden is not what you think it is. My garden—the one I'd tended for thirty years, the one seeded for generations not yet born, the one I'd understood as an inheritance of ordinary hope—was apparently a sentence in an argument I hadn't known was happening. The wheatgrass didn't care about the signal. Fine. But the wheatgrass, I was beginning to suspect, was not the whole garden. The garden was a message, or a weapon, or an ark, or a promise, and I had been its unwitting custodian my entire adult life, and my grandmother had died a hundred years ago knowing exactly what she'd done to me, and had done it anyway, because that is what love looks like in a family built on silence: you bury the truth in your grandchild and call it a graft.

The signal was still in the buffer. Twelve plays. Thirteen, now, because I played it again with new ears and heard what I'd missed every time before: beneath the formal vocative, beneath the ceremony, the voice was not a stranger's. Old, degraded, machine-carried across two centuries of dark—but the cadence, the cadence was a cadence I knew. It was the cadence she used over seedlings. It was *vesh-kara ilen* stretched into a sentence, a blessing and a warning wearing the same clothes.

The signal gave coordinates. Of course it did. Buried in the carrier wave, in the old language's number-forms, which I could read and almost no one else in the Reach could, which was, I understood now, rather the point of me.

The coordinates pointed past the charted dark, past the last relay, into a region the maps called nothing at all.

I am a courier. I know exactly how much trouble a flight plan can cause, and I filed a route extension anyway—routine, boring, the kind of paperwork designed to be waved through—and then I sat in the pilot's chair and looked at the plotted line hanging in the dark in front of me like a thread pulled loose from a hem.

Do not go alone, my grandmother's archive had said. And I had spent thirty years making sure I had no one to go with.

The Vetch turned toward the coordinates, and behind me, in the traffic logs of Kepler Reach, a small anomaly began—unnoticed, for now—to grow.
