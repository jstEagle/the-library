## 5. The Cartographer's Debt

There are exactly three places in the Reach where a person can disappear, and Ruqayya Vance lives in all of them at once.

The first is the drift market at Ceres Hollow, a tumble of welded shipping containers and prayer flags strung between docking arms, where nothing has a price tag and everything has a price. The second is a debt ledger that exists in no database but her own memory, which is why she's still solvent after thirty years of selling maps to people who'd rather she didn't remember them. The third is a room behind the market's eastern bulkhead that smells of ozone and old paper—actual paper, pressed from cellulose grown in vats, because Ruqayya believes some things should be harder to copy than others.

I found her there on the fourth day after the signal, having spent three of them pretending I hadn't received it. I'd run my route. I'd watered the garden. I'd answered two routine traffic pings with the flat courtesy of a courier who has nothing interesting to declare. And then I'd sat in the Vetch's galley with a cup of tea going cold and admitted to myself that I was going to do the one thing I had spent eleven years refusing to do: ask for help.

The favor was old. Older than my license, older than the Vetch, back when I was nobody's courier and just a kid running numbers for a woman who traded in coordinates the way other people traded in contraband—which is to say, openly, but with plausible deniability stitched into every transaction like a lining. I'd once carried a data core for her through a customs sweep at Meridian Gate, tucked inside a seed case among genuine heirloom tomato genetics, and I'd never asked what was on it. She'd said, "Someday I'll owe you more than credits," and I'd said, "Don't," and we'd both let it lie for nine years, the way you leave a loaded thing lying.

Now I needed it to be loaded.

* * *

Her door recognized me before I knocked. That was the first warning sign, though I didn't read it as one yet. Ruqayya's security ran to paranoia the way rivers run downhill—automatically, and without any sense it could be otherwise—and if her systems flagged me as expected rather than merely known, then someone had told her I was coming.

"Courier." She didn't look up from the table where she was working. The room was lit by a single lamp angled low over charts—not projections, not holo-overlays, but printed sheets covered in her own cramped annotations, corrections layered over corrections until some of them were more ink than original. "You look terrible."

"I've been sleeping badly."

"Liar. You don't sleep badly. You sleep like someone who has made peace with every decision they've ever made, which is either admirable or sociopathic, I've never decided which." She finished marking something, set down her pen—a pen, an actual instrument of ink—and looked up. Her eyes did the thing they always did, the quick inventory, cataloguing me the way she catalogued everything: condition, provenance, value, threat. "You're here about the signal."

My face did something. I felt it do it, this small involuntary rearrangement, and hated myself for it, because Ruqayya Vance reads faces the way I read flight plans.

"So you've heard about it," I said.

"I've heard about people asking about it. Which is different, and worse." She stood, joints cracking—she was older than she pretended, older than the Reach, almost—and crossed to a cabinet locked with three separate mechanisms. "Sit down, Tamsin. You're going to want to sit for this."

I didn't sit. It's a small rebellion, staying standing in someone else's room, but I've learned that the body keeps its own counsel even when the mind is busy being brave.

She pulled out a chart. Not a printout—a chart, hand-drawn, the kind of document that doesn't exist anywhere except in the hands that made it. The Kepler Reach spread across it in her precise cartography, colony worlds marked in her system of notation, trade lanes inked in fading blue. And beyond the charted edge, past the last surveyed beacon, a region of blank space annotated in red.

"Four days ago," she said, "a distress transmission went out on a frequency band that hasn't been licensed since before the Reach existed. Pre-Exodus Standard. Dead language, dead protocol, dead everything. Traffic Control logged it as noise, because officially it can't exist. Unofficially—" she tapped the red region, "—three separate parties have come to me in seventy-two hours asking who else knows how to triangulate it."

"Three parties."

"The Meridian Combine sent a man who smelled of money and cologne and thought offering me a bribe was a personality. The Foundationalist enclave on Thessaly sent someone who quoted scripture at me until I threatened to quote freight rates back. And the third—" she paused, and for the first time since I'd entered, she looked genuinely unsettled, which frightened me more than anything else she'd said, "—the third didn't send anyone. The third came themselves. A woman. Inspector-grade clearance, Reach credentials, asked questions in perfect bureaucratic cadence while her eyes did all the real talking."

"Dae-Sun Okonkwo," I said, before I could stop myself.

Ruqayya's eyebrows rose. "So you've met."

"We've corresponded. About my flight logs."

"Ah." Something moved behind her expression—the particular satisfaction of a cartographer watching two lines converge on a point she'd already mapped. "Then you understand your position better than I feared. Because here's what I know, courier, and I'm giving it to you free, which should tell you how much I value our history." She turned the chart toward me. "The signal originated from a point four hundred thousand kilometers beyond the last registered beacon on the outer survey line. There's nothing there. Officially, there has never been anything there. But forty years ago, a survey crew reported gravitational shadowing consistent with a massed object—hull metal, reactor signature, the works—and their report was filed, sealed, and buried so deep that it took me six years and two favors I'll never get back to find it."

"You found it anyway."

"I find things. It's my entire personality." She tapped the red annotation again. "The question isn't whether something is out there. The question is why everyone suddenly cares, and why the moment they started caring, they started looking for whoever else might care too."

"And now they're looking at me."

"They're looking at everyone who might have the language to read it. Do you know how many people in the Reach speak Pre-Exodus Standard? I checked. Eleven. Three are dead, four are children of archivists who learned it as a game, two are institutional and will report directly to whichever faction pays their salary." She looked at me steadily. "And one is a courier who waters tomatoes in zero-g and hasn't spoken to their family in eleven years, whose grandmother—I did mention I check provenances—was listed on no ship that ever existed, and who just walked into my shop asking about a signal in a language only they can read."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the market hummed its endless mechanical heartbeat.

"How long have you known about my grandmother?" I asked.

"Since the day you carried that core through Meridian Gate. You think I let strangers near my cargo? I knew who you were before you knew who you were. It's why I trusted you." She folded the chart with the care of someone handling a relic. "It's also why I'm telling you the part I shouldn't. Those three parties? They're not working independently anymore. As of yesterday, the Combine and the Foundationalists have both filed salvage pre-claims on the coordinates—which means they have coordinates, or think they do, which means someone talked, or someone leaked, or someone wanted them pointed somewhere specific. And the inspector filed a formal request for the Vetch's complete telemetry history this morning."

I did sit down then. Not from weakness—from arithmetic. My legs calculated the situation and concluded that standing was no longer load-bearing.

"If I give you the triangulation," Ruqayya said, quieter now, "you won't be sneaking out to look at something. You'll be racing three factions and a government inquiry to a derelict that may or may not contain whatever it is that makes dead languages worth killing over. And I will have handed you the map. Which makes me complicit. Which makes everything I've built for thirty years—" she gestured at the room, the paper, the paranoia, the whole architecture of her invisible life, "—collateral damage in whatever you're about to do."

"Why tell me all this, then? Why not sell me to the highest bidder?"

For a long moment she looked at me, and I watched her decide something—watched the calculation run and terminate, watched her arrive at an answer she didn't entirely like.

"Because nine years ago a kid carried a sealed core through a customs sweep without asking what was in it, and I've been waiting ever since to find out what kind of person does that." She slid the chart across the table to me. "Either you're the most disciplined smuggler I've ever met, or you're someone who keeps faith with things they don't understand. Both of those are rarer than credits, and I've decided to bet on one of them."

I took the chart. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd expected them to shake.

"There's a condition," she said.

Of course there was. Favors from Ruqayya Vance always came with conditions, the way orbits came with gravity.

"When you find whatever's out there—if you find it—you come back and tell me first. Not the Combine, not the Foundationalists, not your inspector with the honest eyes. Me. Before anyone. I don't need the story. I need to know I wasn't wrong about you."

"That's it?"

"That's it." She smiled, and it didn't reach her eyes, because Ruqayya Vance's smiles are purely decorative, like flags on a diplomatic vessel. "Oh, and Tamsin? One more thing, free of charge. The woman inspector—Okonkwo—she didn't ask me about you specifically. She asked about ships that had filed unusual course corrections in the last month. Yours was one of seven. But yours was the only one whose pilot's lineage records came back..." she searched for the word, "...incomplete. Fabricated, if you want the technical term. Whoever built your identity did good work, but they built it twenty years ago, and the Reach's verification systems have gotten better since. You're not invisible anymore. You're a loose thread, and institutions hate loose threads."

I stood. The chart weighed nothing in my hands and everything in my chest.

"Thank you," I said, and meant it, and hated meaning it, because gratitude is just another form of debt and I had sworn off debts years ago—or thought I had.

"Don't thank me." She was already turning back to her table, already dismissing me, already returning to the careful fiction that this conversation had never happened. "Just come back alive and tell me the truth. That's the whole price. Truth, delivered first, to the person who drew your map."

At the door, I paused. "Ruqayya. The third party—the inspector. When she came here. Did she seem like someone following orders?"

Ruqayya considered the question longer than I expected.

"No," she said finally. "She seemed like someone who suspected her orders were wrong. That's either very good news for you or very bad news, and I genuinely don't know which."

* * *

I walked back through the drift market slowly, letting the crowd carry me, thinking about threads.

That's what I was now. A thread pulled loose from the fabric of my own life, and every step I took toward the signal unraveled me further. The Vetch waited at dock seventeen, patient and small and full of growing things, and I climbed aboard her and sealed the hatch and stood in the garden bay among the tomato vines and the bean trellises and the wheat grasses bending under the grow-lights, and I let myself feel it: the fear, yes, but underneath the fear, something worse.

Recognition.

Because the signal had called for the Gardener, and I had spent eleven years becoming exactly that person, tending seeds for generations I would never meet, keeping silence the way my grandmother must have kept it—deliberately, patiently, as a form of love. Whatever waited out past the last beacon, in the red-annotated dark, it had been waiting for someone like me. Maybe for me specifically. And the people converging on it—the money, the scripture, the institution—they weren't coming for what it was.

They were coming for what it proved.

I laid Ruqayya's chart on the galley table and weighted its corners with seed jars. Four hundred thousand kilometers past the last beacon. Two days' burn at courier thrust, if I pushed the Vetch harder than I liked. Behind me, the Reach's traffic control would log my departure vector within the hour, and Inspector Dae-Sun Okonkwo would see it, and whatever fragile machinery of ignorance I'd built my life upon would begin its final collapse.

I should have felt trapped. Instead, sitting alone in the garden's green light with a dead language humming in my memory and a smuggler's map beneath my hands, I felt something I hadn't felt in eleven years of careful, competent solitude:

Found.

* * *

I filed my departure plan at 0400 station time, listing my destination as a routine seed-delivery run to the agricultural relay at Halley's Fork—a lie so mundane it was nearly poetry. Then I sat in the cockpit and watched the dock lights slide past the canopy, and I did the thing I had trained myself never to do.

I opened the channel to my past.

Not to anyone specific. Just a broadcast, short-range, unencrypted, aimed at nothing: the opening bars of a lullaby in Pre-Exodus Standard, the one my grandmother used to sing while she worked her own garden, decades and a lifetime ago. If anyone was listening—if the inspector's monitoring systems caught it, if Ruqayya's contacts flagged it, if whatever waited beyond the beacon had ears—it would hear a courier saying, in a language two centuries dead:

*I heard you. I'm coming.*

The Vetch turned her nose to the dark, and the burn began.
