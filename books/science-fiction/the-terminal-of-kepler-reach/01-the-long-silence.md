## 1. The Long Silence

The relay between Halcyon and Meridian Station was eleven days of nothing, punctuated by the occasional asteroid, and Theo Zhou had come to think of the asteroids as company.

He'd named them. Not officially—officially they were catalogued as KR-4471 through KR-4479, cold designations issued by a transit authority that believed names were sentimental overhead. But in his head, the big one with the slow tumble was Gerald, because it reminded him of a man he'd never met, and the small pitted one that always seemed to be waiting for him at the halfway mark was Beatrice, because she was reliable, and reliability deserved acknowledgment.

"Morning, Beatrice," he said, on day six, to no one.

The *Long Silence* — his ship, his only conversation partner, and the source of his job title among the dock crews who called him "the hermit of the Reach" — hummed back at him in the key of recycled air and aging thrusters. It was not an answer. It had never once been an answer. But after four years running this route, Theo had learned to hear personality in machinery, the way sailors once heard mermaids in rigging. It was either that or go quietly insane, and the transit authority's medical budget did not cover insanity.

His cargo was sealed messages. That was the whole trade now: two colonies that had stopped trusting each other so thoroughly they'd stopped talking directly, communicating instead through a man who was himself a copy of a dead person, carrying letters neither side would put on an open channel. Diplomatic correspondence, family letters, a surprising quantity of love notes from Halcyon's agrarian districts to Meridian's orbital welders. Humanity, it turned out, could stop trusting each other completely and still find ways to say *I miss you*.

Theo carried all of it. He read none of it. The seals were biometric and punitive.

What he did read, every night in his bunk with the lights dimmed to ember-orange, was the fragment. His fragment.

It came the way weather comes — without warning, without pattern. A smell of ozone and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going bad. A voice saying, *You shouldn't have pulled those records, Inspector.* The sensation of a hand closing on his collar, except it wasn't his collar, it was *the original's* collar, and the memory belonged to a man who had been dead for six years, whose neural pattern the transit authority had purchased from his estate and printed into a body that woke up thinking it was thirty-one years old and terrified of corridors.

Theo had been told the memory transfer was incomplete. Standard clone imprinting, they said. Partial pattern, enough for competence, not enough for continuity. You are your own man, Inspector Zhou. The name was even different — Theo, not Theodore — as if dropping three letters could sever a soul.

But the fragments came anyway. And the worst one, the one that visited him most nights, ended mid-sentence, mid-breath, with a sound like a door closing on a room where someone was still talking.

He kept a log of them, because keeping logs was what inspectors did, and some habits outlive their owners. Entry 1,204: *Ozone. Sweetness. The records again. Whoever killed me — killed him — wanted whatever was in them badly enough to do it in a transit office, where there are cameras.* There were no cameras. He'd checked. Someone had checked very thoroughly, six years before Theo existed, and Theo found that diligence both admirable and chilling.

On day nine, Meridian Station rose out of the dark like a held breath.

It was a beautiful thing, if you liked beautiful things built by people who expected them to be shot at. A spindle of habitat rings around a docking spine, solar sails furled like moth wings, hull plating scarred with the patchwork of forty years of war-adjacent paranoia. Meridian had been the Reach's shipyard once, back when the colonies were one colony. Now it was just the other end of Theo's loneliness.

Docking control crackled through. "*Long Silence*, you're cleared for berth twelve. Cargo manifest says diplomatic seals. Try not to look at them."

"I never do," Theo said. "I'm famously incurious."

A pause, then a snort of static that might have been laughter. Docking control on Meridian was usually a bored voice reading regulations. This one sounded almost human.

Berth twelve took him with the gentle clunk of magnetic collars engaging, and Theo began the ritual: suit up, cycle out, verify seals, sign manifests, avoid eye contact. Four years of practice. On most stations the dock crews looked at him the way people look at a photograph of a dead relative — recognition without welcome. They knew the face. The face had been famous, once, before it died. *That's the Zhou clone,* they'd say, not quietly enough. *The one they printed after the murder.*

He was halfway through verifying seal seven when someone said, "You're bleeding."

Theo looked down. His glove — the left one, the one with the faulty wrist gasket he'd been meaning to replace since Gerald was a smaller asteroid — was weeping a thin line of red into the vacuum-hardened floor. The station's atmosphere hadn't cycled into the berth yet, so the blood beaded and drifted, tiny dark planets orbiting his knuckle.

"So I am," he said.

"You didn't notice?"

"I noticed the glove felt drafty."

The woman who'd spoken was crouched on the hull plating beside a disassembled cargo arm, surrounded by tools arranged with a precision that bordered on devotion. She had grease on her cheekbone like war paint applied by someone with poor aim, and she was looking at him — not at the famous dead face, not at the seals, but at him, at the specific arrangement of tiredness that was uniquely Theo's.

She stood, crossed the distance, took his hand with the brisk authority of someone who fixed things for a living, and inspected the cut. "Wire snag. You'll want to flush it before it infects." She glanced up. "You're the courier."

"I'm the courier."

"The one who talks to asteroids."

Theo felt his ears go warm. "That information is classified."

"It's on three separate dock crews' comm channels. They have a betting pool about which rock is 'Gerald.'" She released his hand and rummaged in her tool kit, producing a dermal patch, which she pressed to his knuckle without asking permission. "I've got money on the big tumbling one."

"Gerald," Theo admitted. "How did you—"

"You talk to it on open channel every run. Day six, 0400 station time. *Morning, Beatrice.*" She smiled, and it rearranged her whole face into something the station's harsh lighting seemed unprepared for. "Mara Voss-Kettering. Dock engineering."

Something in Theo's chest performed a maneuver his flight training had never covered. "Theo Zhou."

"I know," she said, and he braced for it — the flicker, the recalculation, the moment she saw the dead man instead of him. It always came. Even kind people did it.

It didn't come. She just tilted her head. "Do you want coffee? Real coffee, not the station synthesizer's apology for coffee. My grandmother grew actual beans in hydroponics bay four, against approximately nine regulations, and I inherited the crime."

Theo opened his mouth to decline. He had a schedule. He had protocols. He had four years of practiced solitude worn smooth as river stone, and he knew exactly how conversations with strangers went: well, briefly, until they remembered who he was.

"Yes," he heard himself say. "Very much."

* * *

Hydroponics bay four smelled green in a way that made Theo realize how long it had been since he'd smelled anything but metal and recycled air. Rows of leafy things under grow-lights, and tucked shamelessly behind a bank of nutrient tanks, six scraggly coffee plants wearing handwritten labels like *DO NOT REPORT ME, GERALD.*

"You named your plants after my asteroid?" Theo said.

"I named them after *your* asteroid. There's a difference. These are mine; Gerald is public domain." Mara measured grounds with the seriousness of a surgeon. "Sit. You hover like a man who's forgotten how furniture works."

He sat. It was possible he had forgotten how furniture worked.

The coffee was absurdly good. Theo said so, and meant it more than he'd meant most things he'd said in four years.

"My grandmother would be insufferably pleased to hear that, if she weren't dead." Mara settled onto an upturned crate across from him, cup cradled in both hands. Up close he could see she was maybe his age, with calluses in patterns that told a life story in grip surfaces: welding, wiring, wrench work. "So. Eleven days alone with the mail. What do you do with yourself? Besides court-martialing asteroids."

"Logs," Theo said. "Maintenance. I re-read the same four novels until they've achieved a kind of consensus reality. Sometimes I stand at the viewport and experience existential dread, but I try to keep that to weekends."

"What novels?"

"*The Cartographer's Daughter*. *Notes from a Slow War*. Two others you haven't heard of, because nobody has."

"Try me."

He tried her. She'd heard of one, and had opinions — strong ones, wrong ones, delivered with such conviction that Theo found himself arguing, actually arguing, gesturing with his cup, defending a fictional cartographer's honor to a woman with grease on her face, and realizing somewhere around minute ten that he was happy. Not content. Not distracted. Happy, in the specific alarming way of a man who has been cold for years and has just stepped into warm water and can feel himself forgetting why he ever stayed out of it.

"You're staring," Mara said.

"Sorry. I'm — recalibrating."

"Recalibrating what?"

Whether to tell her, he didn't finish. Because here was the thing about the fragments: he'd never told anyone. The transit authority's psych officers got the sanitized version. The dock crews got nothing. And the fragments had been worse lately — louder, closer, the ozone smell so real some mornings he checked the air scrubbers. As if whatever the original had been pulling at, six years dead, was still pulling somewhere, tugging on the thread of copied neurons threaded through Theo's skull.

Instead he said, "Most people, when they meet me, take about ninety seconds to remember they're talking to a copy. You're at twenty minutes. That's a personal record."

Mara considered him over her cup. "My mother used to say a person is what they do next, not what they're made of. By that measure—" she nodded at him, at the patched glove, the empty cup, the argument about cartographers still hanging in the air "—you're doing fine."

Nobody had ever said anything like that to Theo. He examined the feeling carefully, the way he'd examine an unfamiliar seal, turning it over, checking for tampering. It appeared genuine.

"Voss-Kettering! Bay four!" A voice on the intercom, tinny with irritation. "Cargo arm seven is throwing fault codes again, and if you've abandoned it for another coffee conspiracy—"

Mara closed her eyes briefly, a woman communing with patience. "Duty calls." She stood, then paused. "Your return window's in fourteen hours. Berth twelve. If you happen to be near hydroponics bay four around second shift—"

"I happen to be near everything around second shift," Theo said. "Geometrically inevitable."

Her laugh followed her out the door, and Theo sat alone among the green growing things, holding his cooling cup, aware that he was smiling at plants, and unable to stop.

* * *

He spent the intervening hours doing his deliveries — the sealed packets whisked away by Meridian's protocol officers, who handled them with gloved suspicion, as though love letters might detonate — and then, because he couldn't help himself, he pulled the station's public archive and looked up dock personnel. Mara Voss-Kettering. Senior engineer, twelve years' service, commendations for the *Meridian*'s last refit before its deep run.

The *Meridian*. He'd seen the name in his delivery manifests for years — the colony's flagship long-hauler, launched on a decade-long survey run before the colonies finished their estrangement. Due home any cycle now. The whole station buzzed with it; even the protocol officers mentioned it, the first thing both colonies had agreed on in years being a homecoming celebration.

Theo filed the thought away and went back to counting hours until second shift.

He was early. He'd admit that, later, to no one: he was forty minutes early, standing in hydroponics bay four holding a gift — a data chip of the two novels she hadn't heard of, because apparently he was a person who did things like that now — when the station-wide alarm began to sound.

Not the routine chime of traffic alerts. The deep, rolling tone reserved for events outside all schedules.

Over the intercom, the station controller's voice, stripped of boredom, stripped of everything but awe:

"All hands — all hands — the *Meridian* is returning. Repeat, the *Meridian* is coming home. But—" a pause, a breath, ten years of waiting compressed into it "—she's not answering hails. And her crew transponder is reading... reading *wrong*. Everyone to observation decks. Everyone."

Theo stood among the coffee plants, heart hammering a rhythm he didn't yet understand, and watched Mara run past the bay doors toward the docks — toward berth twelve, toward the arriving ship — and after only a heartbeat's hesitation, he ran too.
