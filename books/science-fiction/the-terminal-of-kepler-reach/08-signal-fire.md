## 8. Signal Fire

The children came for him in the dark between shifts, which was fitting, because the dark between shifts was where Theo had lived most of his life.

There were six of them, moving through Voss's flagship like a rumor given legs. The oldest—Pell, who had been a navigator for thirty years and was now perhaps nine—crouched beside his cell door with a lockpick made from a ration fork, working with the patience of someone who had already died once and found it overrated.

"You're slow," Theo whispered through the grate.

"I'm thorough," Pell whispered back. "There's a difference. You'd know that if you'd ever done anything worth doing carefully."

The lock clicked. Theo decided, in that moment, that he liked this child enormously.

They moved through the ship's service corridors, six small ghosts and one grown man trying to make himself smaller. The Meridian children had spent two days aboard the flagship being scanned, sampled, and politely imprisoned, and they had spent those same two days memorizing everything—the patrol rotations, the vent widths, the exact interval at which the galley's autochef emitted its melancholy grinding noise. Children, Theo was learning, were underestimated by everyone, including him, and this turned out to be the single greatest tactical asset in the Kepler Reach.

"The vents are too narrow," he said as they reached the junction where the plan split.

"We know," said Iyo, a girl who had been the Meridian's chief medical officer. She patted his arm with the brisk kindness of a surgeon delivering bad news. "That's why we go through them and you go through the hangar. Try not to get caught. Mara will be upset."

"Will she?"

Iyo looked at him with an expression far too old for her face. "Theo. Everyone on this ship knows you're going to do something stupidly brave together. It's the least surprising thing about either of you."

* * *

The hangar bay was guarded by two ensigns who were, in fairness, watching for adults. What they got instead was Pell walking out into the open, alone, crying with the practiced precision of a child who understood exactly what crying did to people.

"Hey—hey, kid, how'd you—" One of the ensigns knelt. It was reflex, hardwired, older than any protocol. And while he knelt, Theo dropped from the overhead conduit behind him, and the hangar's maintenance override accepted his transit authority codes without complaint, because Director Voss had never quite been able to bring herself to revoke them.

He wondered, briefly, whether that meant something. He decided he could wonder later.

The courier skiff *Long Silence* sat in the third berth, dusty and unloved, exactly as he'd left it. He was through the airlock and cycling engines before the alarms began, and when the hangar doors ground open ahead of him—Pell's work, transmitted on a frequency only children who remembered being engineers would ever think to use—he felt something he hadn't felt since the Meridian first came home.

Hope. Loud, undignified, reckless hope.

"Mara," he said into the comm as the skiff cleared the flagship's shadow. Her voice came back from Halcyon's surface, ragged but alive, broadcasting from the vault station where forty thousand sleepers waited under wiped minds.

"Tell me you have a plan."

"I have a relay array."

"That's not a plan, that's a location."

"It's my location," Theo said, "and it has the largest transmitter in the Reach bolted to it."

A pause. Then, warm despite everything: "That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

* * *

The relay station hung at the system's quiet midpoint, the place where messages went to be passed along and forgotten. Theo had run it alone for eleven years. He knew every cable, every cooling fan, every stubborn panel that required persuasion rather than repair. Docking felt like coming home, which was strange, because home had always been a word other people used.

Mara arrived ninety minutes later aboard a stolen survey tug, flying with the particular aggression of someone whose daughter was currently a nine-year-old hiding in an enemy's ventilation system. She came through the airlock, took one look at him, and hugged him hard enough to rearrange his ribs.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"Barely."

"You're always barely." She pulled back, held his face in her hands, and studied him the way she studied failing machinery—with affection and a mental list of everything wrong. "Voss will come for us. You know that."

"I'm counting on it."

He showed her the memory shard then—the one he'd hidden from the quarantine order, the one holding the vault records, the neural-wipe signatures, the forty thousand names. They jacked it into the relay's core, and the data bloomed across his screens: evidence, patient and damning, of what Halcyon's cryosleep program had really been. Not preservation. Erasure. Half a colony, deleted mid-dream, so that the other half would never have to share the future.

"They'll call it a lie," Mara said quietly. "Fabricated. A clone's delusion."

"Maybe. But lies need silence to survive." Theo laid his hand on the transmit cradle, feeling the old machine hum under his palm like something alive. "This array has spoken to both colonies every day for eleven years. Nobody's ever questioned what it says. Today it says the truth."

"And after today?"

"After today," he said, "there won't be an information blockade anymore. There'll just be information."

She kissed him then, quick and fierce, and stepped back to the monitoring board. "Do it."

Theo opened the channel to both colonies at once—forbidden, impossible, eleven years of protocol burned away in a single command—and pressed transmit.

* * *

It took four hours for the truth to finish crossing the dark.

Four hours during which Theo and Mara sat in the little station's galley, drinking terrible coffee, listening to the acknowledgments trickle in. First the automated relays confirming receipt. Then the colonies' public networks picking up the broadcast, echoing it, multiplying it. Then—this was the part that made Theo's chest ache—the private channels. Ordinary voices. A dockworker on Halcyon asking if his brother's name was on the vault manifest. A teacher on the sister colony reading the wipe signatures aloud to her students because she wanted witnesses. A woman transmitting nothing but a string of numbers over and over: a cryo-bay number, a bed number, a name.

"She's looking for someone," Mara said softly.

"Aren't we all?"

Then the military channels lit up, and the warmth curdled.

"Voss is scrambling," Mara said, watching the intercept traffic bloom across the tactical display. "Three ships out of the flagship's escort wing, burning for the relay. And—" she paused, and Theo watched her face do something complicated, "—both colonies just launched fleets."

"Fleets? Toward us?"

"Toward Halcyon." She looked up. "Everyone saw the coordinates, Theo. Every ship with a grudge and a gun is converging on the vault. Halcyon's garrison thinks it's an invasion. The sister colony thinks it's a rescue. Nobody's talking to anybody except through weapons targeting systems."

Theo stared at the display. Two fleets, arcing toward each other across the dark, with the sleeping dead—and the possibly-not-dead—at the center. The truth was loose. He had set it loose himself, and now it was running wild through the Reach wearing armor and carrying ordnance.

"I thought," he said slowly, "that the truth would fix things."

"The truth doesn't fix things." Mara's voice was gentle. "People fix things. Slowly. If they live long enough to try."

The proximity alarm chimed. Voss's ships, nineteen minutes out, weapons hot.

Theo looked at the relay array—the great skeletal antenna that had been his only companion for a decade, that had carried every sealed message, every lie of omission, every careful silence. It could still speak. But speaking wasn't enough anymore. Someone had to stand between two fleets and say *wait*, and be believed.

"Mara," he said. "How much range does this transmitter have if we stop being polite about power limits?"

She read the board, and smiled the way people smile at the edge of a cliff. "Enough to reach both fleets. Once."

"Once is all I need."

* * *

Nineteen minutes later, as Voss's lead destroyer crossed the threshold of the relay's defensive envelope, Theo Zhou keyed the array to full power and broadcast on every channel in the Reach at once—not the evidence this time, but his own voice, raw and unencrypted:

*"This is Transit Inspector Theo Zhou. Both fleets, hold position. The vault can be woken. Every one of the forty thousand can be woken—but only if nobody fires, and only if you listen to the children of the Meridian, because they remember dying, and they remember waking, and they are the only proof you have that it works. I'm sending you their testimony now. Read it before you shoot. That's all I ask. Read it first."*

He cut the channel. The array's capacitors smoked and died, eleven years of faithful service spent in a single shout.

On the tactical display, the two fleets hung in the dark, forty thousand kilometers apart and closing.

And then—one ship, then three, then a dozen—began to slow.

But Voss's destroyer did not slow. It kept coming, straight and silent, and on the comm band a voice Theo knew better than his own spoke calmly into his ear.

"Copied, Inspector. Now let's talk about what you're willing to burn to be yourself."

The docking clamps of the destroyer locked onto the relay station with a sound like a held breath finally ending.
