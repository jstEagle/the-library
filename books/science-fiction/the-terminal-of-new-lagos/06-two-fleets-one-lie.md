## 6. Two Fleets, One Lie

The message from the Combine arrived at 0400, corridor time, which was to say it arrived in the hour Ansel had long ago claimed as his own — the hour when the Vesper's Lament ran dark and quiet and he could almost pretend the ship was his rather than his employer's, his route rather than theirs.

It came through the diplomatic burst channel, wrapped in three layers of authentication, and it was one sentence long:

*ALL VESSELS IN CORRIDOR: REPORT STATUS AND ANY CONTACT WITH MERIDIAN SURVEY VESSEL HALCYON REACH. COMBINE NAVAL AUTHORITY, LAGOS SPATIAL COMMAND.*

Ansel read it twice, standing in the navigation blister with his coffee going cold in his hand, watching the Reach drift in the external feed like a wound that had finally decided to heal. Eleven years missing. Eleven years of silence, and now both fleets knew she was back, because distress beacons do not distinguish between the people they summon and the governments that own the sky they burn in.

The Meridian message came eleven minutes later, routed through a different channel, phrased with the cold courtesy of a knife wrapped in silk: *MERIDIAN FLEET COMMAND REMINDS ALL LICENSED CARRIERS OF TREATY ARTICLE FOURTEEN. SALVAGE RIGHTS ON MILITARY-DERIVED VESSELS BELONG TO THE FLEET OF ORIGIN. REPORT ANY CONTACT IMMEDIATELY.*

*Immediately.* Both of them. Both of them saying the same word in two accents, like two hands closing around the same throat.

Ansel set down his coffee. He sat in the navigator's chair, which still held the shape of his body the way an old glove holds the shape of a hand, and he thought about what it meant that two armadas had spoken to a single freighter within the same quarter-hour.

It meant they knew. Not everything — not yet — but enough. The beacon had burned for hours before he'd altered course, and beacons are public things, and somewhere in two intelligence services, analysts had pulled the Reach's file, seen the last known position, drawn a circle on a chart, and started asking which ships were inside it.

There was exactly one ship inside it.

He opened the report channel. The cursor blinked at him, patient as a pulse.

Here was what he could say: *I boarded the Halcyon Reach. Her crew is alive. They are children. They remember being adults. There is a phenomenon out there in the deep dark that runs time backward, and it does not kill you, it returns you, and I have a hold full of illegal mind-backups and one of them might be me.*

And here was what would happen if he said it: the corridor would close, the fleets would come, and the treaty — that thin, exhausted membrane holding two civilizations apart — would tear along the oldest seam there is. Not territory. Not honor. *Immortality.* Wars have been started over less than the rumor of a thing like the Fold. Wars had been started over oil, over water, over lines on maps that meant nothing. This would be a war over death itself, and it would not end until one fleet had the Fold and the other had ashes.

Ansel Montrose had spent nine years running this corridor in silence, carrying other people's cargo and his own guilt, and he had never once told a lie that mattered. He had lied the way everyone lies — small, lubricating, forgettable. What he was about to do felt different in his mouth, the way deep water feels different from shallow, the way the pressure changes something in your ears.

He typed: *VESPER'S LAMENT, ANSEL MONTROSE, MASTER. CONTACT WITH HALCYON REACH CONFIRMED, POSITION ATTACHED. VESSEL FOUND DRIFTING, POWER MINIMAL, HULL INTEGRITY COMPROMISED. BOARDING PARTY ENTERED. FINDING: VESSEL STERILE AND UNINHABITED. NO SURVIVORS, NO REMAINS. CAUSE UNKNOWN. CREW OF REACH PRESUMED LOST WITH ALL HANDS. AWAITING INSTRUCTIONS.*

He read it back. *Sterile and uninhabited.* Four words doing the work of a fortress wall.

His thumb hovered over the transmit key for a long moment. In the external feed, the Reach turned slowly, her running lights dead, her name still legible along the bow in flaking paint. Somewhere inside her, a boy of eight years old was sleeping in a captain's bunk that had been built for a man of fifty, and he had saluted Ansel Montrose like it was the most natural thing in the world, because for him it was — he had been doing it for eleven subjective years, saluting ghosts of himself.

Ansel thought of his own face at eight. He couldn't remember it. That was the strange, quiet horror of it — he had a perfect memory of the Fold's coordinates, of the route he'd erased, of the thing he'd lost out there, but his own childhood was a blur of heat and harmattan dust and his mother's voice saying his name in a tone he could no longer reconstruct.

*The boy remembers everything,* he thought. *I remember almost nothing. Which of us is the adult?*

He sent the report.

The acknowledgment came back from Lagos in ninety seconds: *RECEIVED. MAINTAIN POSITION. COMBINE INSPECTION VESSEL EN ROUTE. COOPERATE FULLY.*

And from Meridian, four minutes later, in that same silk-wrapped courtesy: *RECEIVED. MAINTAIN POSITION. FLEET ASSET RECOVERY TEAM EN ROUTE. COOPERATE FULLY.*

Two inspection teams. Two predators, converging on the same carcass, and Ansel Montrose standing in the middle with a ship full of impossible children and a lie already going stale in his mouth.

He looked at the coffee, cold now, the skin forming on its surface like something trying to live. He didn't drink it. He went to find Dara.

* * *

He found her in the engineering bay, sitting on the deck plating with her back against the coolant recycler, and she was not doing anything. That was how he knew the day had gone wrong for her too. Dara Okonjo idle was Dara Okonjo in crisis. Her tools were laid out in their foam case, untouched, in perfect order, and she was staring at the far wall where the ship's schematics glowed in slow rotation.

"They both messaged you," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Both. Inspection teams coming. Combine first, probably. They're closer." He lowered himself to the deck beside her, joints complaining, and for a while they just sat in the hum of the recycler. "I told them the Reach was sterile. Uninhabited. No survivors."

Dara turned her head and looked at him. She had a face built for long silences — broad, patient, the kind of face that made people confess things they hadn't meant to say — and right now it was doing exactly that to him without a word.

"It was the only play," he said. "If I report survivors, both fleets mobilize. If I report *reversed* survivors—"

"There is no if," Dara said. "You reported it. It's done. I'm asking what happens when the Combine inspector walks aboard the Reach and finds thirty-one children with adult bone density and adult dental work."

"The children won't be aboard the Reach."

Dara went very still. "Ansel."

"I can't leave them there. Meridian's recovery team will take the ship. They'll take the children. Whatever the Fold did, whatever made them — Meridian will want to *make more of it*, and the Combine will want to make sure they can't, and the children will be—" He stopped. His hands had made fists without his permission. He opened them deliberately, finger by finger. "They'll be specimens. Both sides. You know that's true."

"I know that's true," Dara said quietly. "I also know you just moved thirty-one children from a derelict onto a licensed Combine freighter carrying contraband stasis pods, which means when the inspector boards, she finds either the children or the pods, and either one of those ends your life and probably starts a war."

"Not if she finds neither."

"Where would you put—"

"The Reach," Ansel said. "The children go back to the Reach. The inspector boards the Reach, finds it exactly as I reported — sterile, dark, empty — and boards my ship, finds nothing but cargo and a crew of two. The Reach stays in the corridor, drifting, until both inspection teams have come and gone. Then we decide what to do with her."

"And if they scan the Reach's life support?"

"It's been dead eleven years. I'll keep it dead. The children live aboard the Lament until the inspections, then transfer back for the duration. Cold ship, cold bodies, no thermal signature, no biosigns if we vent the sections they're in and suit them."

"You're going to put children in vacuum suits and park them in a dead ship so you can lie to two governments."

Ansel heard it laid out in Dara's flat engineer's cadence and heard, for the first time, how it sounded. Not a plan. A descent.

"Yes," he said.

Dara looked at him for a long time. Then she said, "The pods, Ansel. When she boards. The stasis pods in the hold. What do I tell her those are?"

And there it was — the second lie, already waiting, already shaped. Ansel had been carrying it since the moment he'd read the Combine's message, and he hadn't known he was carrying it until it came out of his mouth whole.

"Insurance," he said. "Treaty-sanctioned crew insurance. Deep-route carriers are permitted to maintain cognitive backups under the Long-Haul Safety Protocols — Article Nine. It's in the appendices nobody reads. She's an inspector; she'll know it better than we do. The pods are legal."

"They're not legal. You told me yourself when you hired me — that's why they're in the sealed hold, that's why the manifest doesn't list them—"

"The Combine never ratified the amendment that revoked Article Nine. Lagos kept the old text on the books because revoking it would have meant compensating every carrier that had already invested in the infrastructure. It's a dead letter. Dead letters are the best kind of law — everyone forgot them, but they're still enforceable." He heard himself reciting it, smooth, sourced, and realized with a distant, cold surprise that he had rehearsed this. Somewhere in the nine years of running this corridor, in the nine years of sleeping thirty meters from those pods, some part of him had been preparing this defense the way a man prepares an alibi he hopes never to use.

Dara was staring at him. "You've thought about this."

"I think about everything on this ship. That's the job."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "That's not what I mean, and you know it's not what I mean." She got up, brushed off her coveralls, and stood over him, and for a moment Ansel saw her not as his engineer but as the woman who had signed on to a lonely route because she wanted the silence, and who had gotten instead a captain with secrets and a hold full of sleeping ghosts. "I'll move the children. I'll vent the sections. I'll play my part. But Ansel — the lie you told today is going to need more lies, and each one is bigger, and at the end of that road there's a place where you can't remember which version of the truth you're standing in. I've watched men walk that road. You don't come back from it with your face."

"I know," Ansel said. And he did know. He had a face in his memory — no, not a memory, a *certainty* — of a man who had walked it, out there in the Fold, a man who had come back young and was coming back younger still, and the face was his own.

* * *

The children transferred that night, in ones and twos, ferried across the two hundred meters of vacuum between the Reach and the Lament's ventral lock. They were, most of them, eerily good at it — eleven years of deep-space discipline living inside bodies that had forgotten how to be tall. The youngest, a girl of five who had been the Reach's chief medical officer, cried the whole way across, and Ansel held her helmet against his chest in the lock and told her she was brave, and hated himself for how easily the lie of comfort came, and did it anyway.

By 0300 they were bedded down in the forward cargo bay, in hammocks and improvised bunks among the sealed crates, and Dara had rigged a temporary air curtain so the hold could stay pressurized. Ansel walked the rows of sleeping children the way he had once, in another life, walked the rows of sleeping pods. Same posture. Same silence. Same terrible tenderness.

He found Idris Vane awake, sitting cross-legged on his bunk, watching him with eyes that were too old for the face they lived in.

"Captain," the boy said. Still the salute. Ansel was beginning to understand it wasn't habit — it was armor. As long as Idris was the captain, he was still the man who had made the decision that brought them home. Take away the rank and he was only a child who had been stolen from himself.

"Not your ship, Idris. Just Ansel, for now."

"Captain Montrose." The boy smiled, small and knowing. "You can't help it either."

Ansel crouched beside the bunk. "We have to move you all back to the Reach in a few days. For a little while. There are people coming — inspectors — and they can't find you. If they find you, they'll take you somewhere far from the corridor, far from each other, and they'll study you, and you'll never go home. Do you understand?"

"Because of the Fold." Idris said the word the way sailors say the name of a storm. "Because they'd want to go there. Everyone wants to go there, once they know. That's how it gets you — not the reversing. The *wanting*." He looked past Ansel, at the dark of the cargo bay, at the rows of small sleeping shapes. "My first officer wanted it most. Sefu. He was sixty-two. He used to stand at the viewport and say, *Captain, imagine getting back the years.*" The boy's voice did something complicated — a child's voice carrying a dead man's grief. "He got them back. He's five now. He doesn't say anything anymore."

Ansel's throat was tight. "I'm sorry."

"You're not sorry yet," Idris said. "But you will be. You're the one who's going to have to decide, and deciding is what sorry is made of." He lay down, pulled the blanket up, and closed his eyes — a child's gesture, automatic, and beneath it the unmistakable finality of a captain dismissing his visitor. "Good night, Captain Montrose."

Ansel stood in the dark of the cargo bay for a long time after the boy slept, listening to thirty-one children breathe, and did not sleep himself.

From Dara Okonjo's private log, cycle 4,112, timestamp 0517:

He's going to do it. He's going to walk the whole road. I watched him lie to two governments today and it cost him nothing visible, which is the most frightening thing I've ever seen — not that he lied, but how *well* he lied, how the words came out pre-loaded, sourced, armored. Article Nine. Dead letter. He's had that speech in him for years, which means some part of Ansel Montrose has been planning for the day the truth came knocking, and that part of him is a stranger to me.

So I'm going to do the thing he won't do. I'm going to find the truth myself.

The boy Idris let me draw blood tonight — I told him it was a medical checkup, which was a lie, my first one, and it tasted like copper. He looked at me while I did it and I swear he knew. He said, "Tell me what you find. Not him. Me." Eight years old, and he already understands who lies and who gets lied to.

I ran the first panel against the ship's medical database, the old crew physicals we carry for insurance purposes, and I need to write this down before I talk myself out of believing it: the boy's blood is not the blood of a child. The telomeres are wrong — long, child-length, but *structured* like an old man's, like they'd been shortened and then rebuilt. The methylation patterns read sixty years old in a body of eight. It's not that the Fold made him young. It's that the Fold is *running him backward through what he already was*, and his cells remember every age they've ever been, like rings in a tree, like a recording played in reverse.

He isn't a copy. Copies don't have scars of the future.

I don't know what that means yet. I don't know what it means for the pods in the sealed hold, or for Ansel, or for the boy who salutes. But I know this: when the Combine inspector comes, and she asks me under oath whether there's anything aboard this ship the manifest doesn't show, I am going to have to choose between the man who hired me and the truth I just drew into a vial.

I'm putting the vial in the coolant manifold housing, behind panel forty-one. If something happens to me, it's there.

Panel forty-one. Remember it.

* * *

Ansel stood at the navigation blister at dawn, watching the long-range feed, and saw it: a single bright point detaching from the Lagos side of the corridor, running toward them under diplomatic colors. Fast. Very fast. Combine hull, Combine transponder, and a profile he recognized from the route manuals — an inspection cutter, the kind that carried not marines but something worse: auditors.

The hail came through an hour later, crisp and courteous, in a woman's voice with the measured accent of the Lagos civil service:

*Freighter Vesper's Lament, this is Combine Inspection Vessel Adire's Eye. I am Inspector Yewande Adeyemi, Naval Authority. Prepare to receive boarding party. We have questions about the Halcyon Reach — and, Master Montrose, about your report.*

A pause, exactly long enough to be deliberate.

*We look forward to a thorough conversation.*
