## 7. The Combine's Courtesy

The transmission arrived at 0347, ship's time, which was a lie Ansel maintained the way he maintained all his clocks — faithfully, and for reasons no one else would understand. The Vesper's Lament kept Lagos hours because Lagos had built her keel, though she hadn't seen the lagoon in six years. He read the message twice while the coffee brewed, and by the second reading his hands had gone very still around the cup.

*Combine freighter Vesper's Lament, registry CL-4409. Be advised: diplomatic vessel Amiable Truth will effect rendezvous at your position in fourteen hours for routine corridor inspection under Article Twelve of the Treaty of the Two Bays. Your cooperation is appreciated and expected.*

Routine. The word sat on the screen like a fishhook sits in water — innocent, until you noticed the barb.

Fourteen hours. Ansel stood in the galley with the coffee cooling untouched and did the arithmetic of his ship. Fourteen hours to move eleven reversing children out of the Reach's medbay and into the sealed hold, to scrub the Reach's logs of everything Dara had copied, to coach a ship's company of frightened adults in children's bodies into the performance of not existing. Fourteen hours to make the Halcyon Reach — eleven thousand tonnes of impossible evidence — into a rumor.

Or to make himself into a better liar than he had ever been.

He thought about the message's phrasing. *Appreciated and expected.* The Combine did not waste courtesies on freighters. Somewhere in the bureaucracy of New Lagos, someone had looked at the Vesper's Lament — a mid-sized ore hauler with an unremarkable route and an unremarkable captain — and decided she was worth an inspector's time. Which meant someone had looked at the distress beacon. Someone had noticed that the Lament had answered it when both fleets had ordered it ignored, and that she had spent nine days at the Reach's coordinates doing whatever it was she did there, and that when she finally transmitted her position report, the Reach was described in a single sentence: *derelict, sterile, uninhabited.*

Nine days at a derelict. Ansel had told himself the delay was salvage assessment. He had told himself a great many things in nine days.

He drank the coffee. It tasted like tin and old decisions.

* * *

They came down the docking collar at 1740, precisely when they said they would, and Ansel met them at the airlock with his best face on — the face of a tired hauler with nothing to hide and less to say.

There were four of them. Two security officers in Combine gray, weapons holstered but present the way weather is present. A clerk, thin and forgettable, carrying a slate. And in front, a woman of perhaps fifty with close-cropped silver hair and a Combine inspector's badge worn not on her chest but at her collar, small as a period at the end of a sentence.

"Captain Montrose." She extended her hand. Her grip was dry and brief and entirely without warmth, though her smile was excellent — calibrated, professional, the smile of a woman who had smiled at smugglers and saints and could no longer tell the difference from the outside. "Yewande Adeyemi, Ministry of Corridor Affairs. Thank you for receiving us on short notice."

"Article Twelve doesn't leave much room to decline, Inspector."

"No." She stepped aboard and paused, and Ansel watched her do what he knew she would do: read his ship the way he read a current chart. The scuffed deck plating. The galley's recycled air. The faint mineral smell of a vessel that had hauled ore and worse for a decade. "It doesn't. That's rather the point of it."

The clerk handed her the slate. She glanced at it without appearing to. "Your position report of the ninth. You list the Meridian survey vessel Halcyon Reach as derelict, sterile, uninhabited."

"That's correct."

"Eleven years missing, and when she turns up, she's empty." Yewande looked up from the slate, and her eyes were the color of deep water and about as easy to see into. "You must forgive me, Captain. I've read a great many salvage reports. Sterile is not a word they use. Sterile is a word doctors use. Or priests."

"Her atmosphere was contaminated. We ran the numbers and didn't like them. We boarded in suits, took what readings we could, and got out."

"Nine days."

"Salvage assessment. Her drive was worth cataloguing."

"Mm." She handed the slate back to the clerk and smiled again, and this time the smile reached all the way to her eyes, which was somehow worse. "May I see her?"

* * *

They crossed to the Reach in the Lament's tender, and Ansel spent the eleven minutes of transit rehearsing and hating himself for it. Behind him in the tender's cabin, Yewande sat with her hands folded, watching the Reach grow in the viewport — the long survey spine, the sensor arrays like folded wings, the hull scarred by eleven years of radiation and something else, something that had happened to her from the inside.

"She's beautiful," Yewande said. "For a corpse."

"She held together. That's more than most."

"Eleven years in the deep dark, and her hull integrity is at ninety-two percent." The inspector's voice was light, conversational, a knife being discussed as cutlery. "My office pulled her original specs, Captain. The Reach was rated for forty. Whoever built her built her well."

"Meridian money."

"Meridian money." A pause, exactly long enough. "And yet her crew abandoned nothing. No lifepods missing. No distress logs after the beacon itself. It's as if they simply... stayed."

"They stayed," Ansel said, and the words came out of him dry and even, and somewhere beneath the evenness he could hear his own pulse. "Whatever happened to them, they were here when it happened."

The tender docked. The collar sealed with a sound like a held breath.

* * *

He had done what could be done in fourteen hours, and it was both more and less than enough. The children were in the Lament's hold now, tucked among the backup pods in the dark, and Dara had drilled them on the one rule that mattered: *if anyone comes, you are asleep, you are cargo, you do not breathe loudly.* The Reach's medbay had been stripped and reconfigured into something that looked like a failed hydroponics experiment. The captain's cabin — Idris Vane's cabin, with the boy's drawings still taped to the wall, ships and lighthouses and a stick-figure family rendered in a child's hand by a man's memory — had been sealed with a hull-plate weld that Ansel had cut himself, sweating, at 0600.

He led Yewande through the ship the way a man walks a minefield he laid himself.

She was good. That was the thing he hadn't prepared for — not her thoroughness, which he'd expected, but her patience. She did not march. She wandered. She stopped in the galley and ran a finger along the mess table and asked who cooked. She paused at a bulkhead scuff and asked what had struck it. She stood for a full minute in the corridor outside the sealed captain's cabin and said nothing at all, and the nothing was louder than any question.

"Welded from the inside?" she asked finally.

"Contamination protocol. We sealed the sections we didn't survey."

"Of course." She touched the weld with two fingers, reading it. "Recent work. Your torch or the ship's?"

"Mine."

"You do your own hull work, Captain?"

"I do everything on my own ship. It's a small crew."

"Two. You and Engineer Okonjo." The clerk's slate whispered. "Plus the Reach's captain, Idris Vane, listed as alive at last Meridian contact."

Ansel kept his face where he had put it. "If you find him, Inspector, you'll be the first."

"Yes," Yewande said softly. "I imagine I will."

* * *

It happened in the drive section, near the end. She had finished her circuit, or appeared to, and the security officers had drifted back toward the collar, and Ansel allowed himself the first full breath in six hours. Then Yewande stopped at the base of the reactor housing, turned, and looked at him with the smile put away.

"Captain, may I ask you something off the record?"

"Article Twelve doesn't have an off the record."

"Article Twelve is a door, Captain. I'm asking if we can step through it together." She waited. He said nothing, and she took the silence for the answer it was, and went on anyway, which told him everything about her. "Eleven years ago I processed the paperwork when the Reach went missing. It was my first year in Corridor Affairs. I remember the file because the manifest had an anomaly — a single passenger, unregistered, boarded at New Lagos under a letter and a surname. The file was flagged and then it was unflagged, and the unflagging order came from above my office, and I have wondered about that letter for eleven years." She tilted her head, very slightly, like a bird considering something bright. "You wouldn't know anything about a passenger on the Halcyon Reach."

"No."

"No, you wouldn't know anything. Or no, there was no passenger?"

"Inspector—"

"Because here is what troubles me." She stepped closer, and up close he could see that her eyes were not deep water at all but shallow, clear, and bottomless the way shallow water is bottomless — you could see every stone, and the stones went on forever. "A derelict returns after eleven years. Both fleets order it ignored — which is itself extraordinary; fleets do not ignore things, they race for them. One freighter answers anyway. That freighter spends nine days aboard and reports the ship sterile. And three weeks later, a Combine inspector with my exact portfolio is dispatched to a routine inspection of that freighter, in a contested corridor, at considerable diplomatic expense." She spread her hands, apologetic. "Nothing about that is routine, Captain. I wrote the itinerary myself. I made it boring on purpose. Boring keeps people from looking."

The reactor hummed beneath them. Ansel heard his own voice from a great distance, steady as a buoy.

"If you already know why you're here, Inspector, why ask me anything?"

Yewande Adeyemi looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind her face — not triumph, he realized. Something more tired. Something almost like grief.

"Because I've spent eleven years wondering about that letter," she said, "and I'm hoping, against considerable evidence, that you'll save me the trouble." She turned toward the collar. "I'll want to see your hold before we go. Standard manifest verification. You keep cargo in it, I assume?"

"Ore. Ballast. The usual."

"The usual," she repeated, pleasantly, and walked toward the airlock, and Ansel followed her with the sensation of a man walking behind his own executioner and noticing, absurdly, that he liked her coat.

* * *

The hold of the Vesper's Lament was dark and cold and full of sleeping things.

Ansel had spent nine years telling himself the pods were insurance — that he had built them for a reason he could name if he had to. Standing in the doorway with Yewande's lamp sweeping the racks, he understood at last that he had built them for this. For the day the past came aboard his ship and asked to see the hold.

The pods sat in their rows, frosted, dim, each one carrying a mind folded up small enough to fit in a canister of gel and light. And beneath the floor plating, behind a panel Dara had resealed with weld-marks aged in an acid bath to look a decade old, eleven children lay in the dark among the Reach's salvaged stores, breathing as quietly as they knew how.

Yewande walked the rows. She did not touch the pods, but she read their placards, one by one, and her lamp moved slowly, and the clerk's slate whispered behind her, and the security officers stood by the door with the patience of furniture.

"Mind-state archives," Ansel said. "Treaty-sanctioned. Article Nine, continuity of persons. I hold licenses."

"I know you hold licenses." Her lamp stopped on the nearest pod. "I verified them before I boarded, Captain. That's the courtesy part of the Combine's courtesy." She turned, and the lamp's edge caught her face from below, hollowing it. "The licenses are real. The pods are real. Everything aboard this ship is real, and legal, and in order." A pause. "Which is what troubles me. You understand? In eleven years of corridor work, I have never once inspected a ship where everything was in order. Order is a story, Captain. And stories this tidy are told for a reason."

"Maybe I'm just an honest man."

"Maybe." She clicked the lamp off, and the hold went dark except for the pods' faint green breath of light, and in that light her face was unreadable and very still. "Or maybe you're a man who has practiced."

She walked to the doorway and stopped beside him, close enough that he could smell her — nothing perfumed, only clean fabric and cold air, the smell of institutions.

"Here is what I believe, and I'll say it once, off no record at all." Her voice was quiet, pitched beneath the reactor's hum, for him alone. "I believe the Halcyon Reach came back with her crew aboard. I believe you saw them. I believe you reported them gone because you thought you were protecting something — or someone — or because someone above both our pay grades told you to. I believe you are not a bad man, Ansel Montrose, because bad men don't seal cabins to protect the dead, and bad men don't look at me the way you do, like a man watching a fire he started reach the treeline." She straightened. "And I believe that whatever is under this deck is going to come up eventually, because it always does, and that when it does, the question that will decide a great many lives — mine included — is whether it comes up through a treaty or through a warhead."

"Inspector—"

"Article Twelve is satisfied. Your manifests are in order." She was already moving, brisk again, the period back at the end of her sentence. "You'll receive a certification of compliance within the day. Fly carefully, Captain. The corridor's getting crowded lately. Meridian traffic has doubled this month." At the collar she paused, half through the hatch, and did not look back. "One more thing, off the record. The passenger on the Reach, eleven years ago. The letter and the surname." A beat. "The initial was A."

The collar cycled. The Amiable Truth undocked and fell away toward the Combine lines, small and gray and patient, and Ansel stood in the airlock vestibule with his heart going like a drive core coming out of balance.

Dara found him there ten minutes later. She didn't ask. She looked at his face and got the whole of it, the way she always did, and said only: "The kids heard the whole inspection through the deck. Idris wants to know if the lady believed you."

Ansel looked out the viewport, where the inspector's ship was a dwindling star, already composing her report to New Lagos — a report that would say *in order, certified, nothing here* — and would say it in a tone that would make someone, somewhere, look harder.

"No," he said. "She believed me exactly enough to be dangerous. She knows the initial, Dara. She's had eleven years and a file, and she knows the initial."

He turned from the viewport, and that was when the proximity alarm sounded — soft, apologetic, the Lament's systems almost embarrassed to interrupt. A contact at the corridor's far margin, holding station at the edge of sensor range. Not Combine. Not moving. Not leaving.

Watching.

"Get Idris below," Ansel said. "And get me everything we have on that contact. Now."
