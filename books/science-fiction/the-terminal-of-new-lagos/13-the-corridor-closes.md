## 13. The Corridor Closes

The first sign was the silence.

Not the ordinary silence of the corridor — Ansel had lived inside that silence for eleven years and knew its textures the way a blind man knows the rooms of his house: the hum of the Lament's drive at cruising burn, the tick of thermal expansion in the hull as it swung from Lagos-side sun to Meridian-side dark, the faint wet breathing of the stasis pods in the sealed hold below his feet. That silence had layers. It was a silence he could read like weather.

This was different. This was the silence of everyone else leaving.

He felt it before he saw it on any display — a thinning in the traffic feed, the way the chatter of beacon pings and transponder handshakes went from a river to a trickle to nothing. For six hours he told himself it was a routing outage. Combine nav-arrays failed all the time; Meridian's were worse; the treaty corridor ran on infrastructure both sides maintained grudgingly and repaired never. He told himself this while his hands, without his permission, began pulling up the long-range plots, sweeping the corridor's mouth and tail the way a man checks the locks twice after hearing a footstep on the stair.

At 0400 ship-time, Dara came to the bridge without being called. She carried two cups of tea and set one beside him without a word, which was how she said everything that mattered.

"Tell me I'm imagining it," she said.

"The corridor's closed." He turned the main plot toward her. "Both ends."

The display showed it plainly enough for a child to understand, and there were children aboard now, which made the plainness obscene. At the Lagos terminus, a picket line of Combine destroyers sat in a crescent formation, transponders blazing official, their interdiction fields overlapping like linked fingers. At the Meridian end, the same geometry mirrored back — cruisers this time, heavier, Halvorsen's command among them by its drive signature, the one Ansel had learned to recognize the way you learn the tread of someone who has threatened you. Between the two blockades: eleven light-hours of empty corridor, one aging freighter, one derelict survey vessel under tow, and thirty-one children who remembered being adults.

"Contested zone," Dara read aloud from the advisory scrolling beneath the plot. Both fleets had issued it simultaneously, within minutes of each other, which meant coordination, which meant the thing Ansel had spent three months preventing had already happened somewhere above his head, in rooms he'd never see, between people who had decided the corridor was worth more closed than open. "They're calling it an administrative measure pending resolution of the *Halcyon Reach* question."

"There is no question," Ansel said. "That's what I told them."

"And they believed you right up until it became cheaper not to."

He drank the tea. It was too hot and he drank it anyway, because the burn gave him something true to feel. Outside the viewport, the corridor stretched away in both directions, starless near the plane of the ecliptic dust, and for the first time in eleven years it did not look like a road. It looked like a throat.

* * *

By midday the rationing orders had gone out, and Ansel discovered that a blockade is not a wall but a slow tightening.

The Combine picket transmitted first, courteous as ever, Yewande Adeyemi's office name attached to the footer like a signature on a knife. The Lament was designated a neutral vessel held *in situ* for inspection; resupply would be provided at intervals; deviation from its current station would be interpreted as an attempt to breach the interdiction field, with consequences left politely unspecified. Six hours later, Meridian's version arrived, worded more bluntly, signed by no one, with the added courtesy of a targeting solution painted across the Lament's hull for four seconds — long enough for the sensors to register it, long enough for Ansel to know Halvorsen wanted him to know.

"They're not blockading each other," Dara said, standing at the galley table where she'd spread the manifests. "Look at the positioning. Both lines are angled inward. We're the hinge."

"We're the excuse," Ansel corrected. "The Reach is the reason. We're just what happens to be holding it."

The children were the part the manifests couldn't hold. Thirty-one of them now, plus the twelve still sleeping off the worst of the reversal in the Lament's infirmary bunks, their bodies cycling through years the way fever cycles through hours. The Reach itself drifted in tow behind them, its drive cold, its corridors full of small voices. Captain Vane — eight years old, silver-haired, with a voice like a man reciting from a great distance — had formally requested, in language that made Ansel's skin crawl precisely because it was so adult, that the crew be transferred to the freighter. "Your vessel has gravity and heat," the boy-captain had said. "Ours has neither worth mentioning. A captain does not abandon his ship, but a captain also does not let his crew freeze out of sentiment."

So the Reach's crew lived aboard the Lament now, and the Lament, built for a complement of nine, held sixty-three souls, and every bunk was taken, and the children slept in shifts in the cargo rings among the containers, and the sound of them — laughing, crying, arguing in the cadences of people who had once commanded crews and filed taxes and buried parents — filled the ship like water filling a hull.

Ansel walked the rings that evening. He told himself it was inspection. It was not inspection.

In ring three, a girl of perhaps ten sat cross-legged on a container marked COMBINE AGRI-SULFUR, teaching arithmetic to three smaller children using dried beans. Her hands moved like a surgeon's. When she looked up at him, her eyes were forty years old and tired in a way no child's eyes should be able to manage.

"Navigator," she said, nodding. Word had spread through the little population about who he was — the man who found them, the man who lied for them, depending on which retelling you heard.

"Ma'am," he said, because he could not help it.

"I was a structural engineer for twenty-two years," she said, conversationally, gathering beans into rows. "Now I cry when I drop things. Do you know why? Not the dropping. It's that my hands won't do what I tell them. There's a gap between the wanting and the doing, and every day the gap gets wider or narrower, we can't tell which direction we're going anymore. Some of us are getting younger again. Some are climbing back up. Nobody knows which way they'll wake." She looked at her small hands. "You should understand that better than anyone, I think."

He didn't ask what she meant. He was afraid the answer would be *the boy*, and the boy was the one subject on which Ansel Montrose could not afford to think clearly.

"How are they holding?" he asked instead, nodding at the children.

"As well as anyone trapped in a tin can between two armies." She smiled, and it was a child's smile wearing an adult's grief like an ill-fitting coat. "Better than the adults would, honestly. Children are used to having no power. We keep forgetting we used to have some."

* * *

The dreams started on the third night of the blockade.

Ansel dreamed of the Fold — not the descriptions Idris had given, but the thing itself, the memory he had buried so deep even he sometimes doubted it: the moment his own ship had slid sideways out of time, the stars smearing like wet paint, his own hands growing younger on the yoke while his mind stayed stubbornly, terribly himself. In the dream he always woke at the same instant — the instant he'd chosen to turn back rather than go through — and he always woke with the certainty that the choice had been the coward's choice, and that everything since, the backups, the lies, the eleven years of quiet runs, had been interest accruing on that original debt.

He woke at 0300 to find Idris sitting in the copilot's chair.

The boy had grown again. It was visible now day to day, hour to hour — the jaw squaring, the shoulders broadening, the silver hair darkening at the roots like ink seeping through cloth. He was perhaps fourteen tonight. His feet didn't reach the deck, and he sat with them folded under him anyway, in the posture of a man who had spent decades in that chair, except it wasn't that chair, it had never been that chair, and yet —

"You shouldn't be out of your bunk," Ansel said.

"You dream loudly." Idris didn't look at him. He looked at the blockade plot, still glowing on the main display, the two crescents of warships pinching the corridor shut. "I used to sit watches alone like this. On the Reach, before. I remember hating it. The quiet." A pause. "I remember loving it. Both. Memory's strange when it's been run backward. Everything's true and nothing's settled."

"Idris—"

"Do you know what they're waiting for?" The boy gestured at the plot. "Neither fleet wants to fire first. Firing first means admitting the other side has something worth defending. So they wait, and they tighten, and they starve us by inches, and they call it diplomacy." He turned then, and his eyes in the dim console-light were Ansel's eyes, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said it. "How long do the rations last?"

"Sixty days. Fifty-eight now."

"And after that?"

"After that, someone blinks."

Idris nodded slowly, the way old men nod, and Ansel hated how much he recognized in it. "When I was — when I *will be*, whichever way you say it — I commanded a survey vessel for nine years, and in nine years I never once lied to my crew. I'm starting to understand why you lie. It's heavy work, telling the truth. Someone has to carry the weight so everyone else can sleep." He unfolded himself from the chair, dropped lightly to the deck, and paused at the hatchway. "For what it's worth, navigator — I don't think either fleet is going to blink. I think they're going to squeeze until something breaks, and it won't be them."

Then he was gone, and Ansel sat alone with the plot, watching the two crescents breathe, very slightly, closer.

* * *

On the fifth day, the Combine sent a tender.

It crossed the interdiction line under flag of truce, a small utility craft carrying water, protein stock, and medical supplies — and a single passenger. Ansel watched the transfer on the dockside cameras with his arms crossed and his heart going like a drum in a funeral procession, because of course it was Adeyemi. Of course it was. When the great machines of state want to apply pressure, they don't send soldiers first. They send the polite ones.

She came to the bridge at 1400, accepted tea, declined the chair, and stood looking out the viewport at the Reach hanging in tow behind them like a dead moon.

"Administrative update," she said pleasantly. "The corridor will remain closed for the foreseeable future. Both parties have agreed the *Halcyon Reach* matter requires joint investigation. There will be a combined boarding team assembled within the month — Combine and Meridian inspectors working together, if you can imagine such a thing. History being made, quietly, in your cargo hold."

"That'll be crowded," Ansel said. "I've got children sleeping in the sulfur containers."

"Yes." She sipped her tea. "Thirty-one minors of unknown legal status, recovered from a vessel declared missing eleven years ago, exhibiting medical conditions neither fleet's physicians can explain, aboard a freighter whose captain reported the ship 'sterile and uninhabited' in his initial contact log." She let the silence sit. She was good at letting silences sit; it was her sharpest tool and she used it the way Ansel used a course correction — early, gently, irreversibly. "Sterile and uninhabited. You must forgive me, Navigator Montrose, but I've re-read that report perhaps forty times, and every time I admire it more. It is a masterpiece of the literally true. The ship *was* uninhabited when you boarded it, in the sense that none of its crew were adults. Sterile, in the sense that your swabs found no pathogens. Every word defensible. And the whole of it a lie, arranged like flowers."

"The report stands," Ansel said.

"For now." She set down the cup. "Here is what I actually came to say, off the record, unlogged, and you may believe me or not. The people who ordered this blockade don't want the children, Ansel. Children are complications. They want whatever *made* the children, and they want it before the other side gets it, and they are prepared to keep this corridor sealed for a year, five years, until everyone aboard your ship is starving or grown or dead, whichever comes first. You are not caught between them. You are *inside* one of them — the only territory both armies agree belongs to neither." She moved toward the hatch, then stopped. "I told you once I suspected you. I no longer suspect you. I understand you. That's worse, for both of us. When the joint team comes aboard, whatever you're going to do — do it before they arrive. Afterward will be too late for cleverness."

The hatch sighed shut behind her. Ansel stood a long time looking at the tea she'd half-finished, as if it might contain instructions.

* * *

That night, the Meridian cruiser adjusted its position by four degrees, and the Combine answered with two, and the distance between the blockade lines shrank by eleven thousand kilometers, and every soul aboard the Lament felt the corridor contract around them like a fist learning how to close.

Dara found him in the hold, standing among the stasis pods, the illegal ones, the twelve sleeping backups with their faces lit blue-green from below. She didn't ask why he'd come down here. She never asked anymore. She simply stood beside him, shoulder almost touching his, and looked at the pods with their dreaming occupants.

"Halvorsen pinged me directly," she said. "Private channel. Wants a medical consult on the reversal cases. Officially." A beat. "Unofficially, I think he's mapping who on this ship can be bought."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I'd review his request." She exhaled. "Ansel. Fifty-three days of food. Two fleets closing like a book with us pressed between the pages. A joint inspection team coming in weeks. Even if you keep the lie perfect — even if nobody ever catches you in it — the lie doesn't feed sixty-three people. The truth is the only asset on this ship that either fleet actually wants. You're sitting on the one thing that can buy our way out of here, and you're going to let it rot rather than spend it."

"It wouldn't buy our way out." He kept his eyes on the pods. "It would start a war. That's the price list, Dara. Silence costs fifty-three days. Truth costs however many million people live along the corridor. I did the arithmetic a long time ago, before you ever signed onto this boat, and I've been paying the installments ever since."

"And if the arithmetic is wrong?"

"Then I'll have been wrong carefully," he said, "which is more than most men manage."

She was quiet for a while. In the blue light, the face of the nearest sleeper — a woman who had been a Meridian cartographer in another life, or several — seemed to shift subtly, younger, older, younger, as if time itself couldn't make up its mind about her.

"The children asked me today," Dara said finally, "when they get to go home. All of them, in a group, like a delegation. They chose a spokesperson. The engineer from ring three." Her voice caught slightly. "She said, *tell the navigator we understand he's trying to protect us, but we were somebody once, and being nobody twice is more than a person can survive.*"

Ansel said nothing. There was nothing in him left to say with. Above them, through seven decks of hull, the blockade lines crept closer in the dark, and the corridor — the last open road between two armed worlds, the road he had kept for eleven years, the road that had been his whole identity, his whole exile, his whole penance — finished closing around the *Vesper's Lament* like a terminal around a message no one would ever send.

And in the morning, the general alarm woke him: a launch had broken from the Meridian line, running dark, inbound fast, and it was not flying a flag of truce.
