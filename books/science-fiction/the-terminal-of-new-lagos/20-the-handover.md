## 20. The Handover

The rift was still open when the transfer documents arrived.

They came through three channels at once — Combine diplomatic packet, Meridian fleet courier, and a plain unencrypted burst that Yewande Adeyemi sent personally, as if to say: *this is not a trick, this is only paperwork.* Ansel read them standing in the Lament's navigation bay with the light of the Fold washing through the viewport, a slow aurora of wrong-colored time, and he understood immediately what the documents were.

Not an order. An offer shaped like an order. The fleets had learned, in the last six hours of staring into the rift together, that some things could not be seized — only given.

*Transfer of Identity and Route Rights. Party of the first part: MONTROSE, ANSEL K., navigator, registry 7741-LC. Party of the second part: MONTROSE, IDRIS A., minor in reversion, legal personhood pending certification.*

His own name, typed out in the cold grammar of bureaucracy. He had signed manifests like this a thousand times. He had never been the cargo before.

"You don't have to do it," Dara said from the doorway. She had not slept. None of them had slept. The whole corridor was awake, two armadas holding their breath on either side of a wound in causality, and aboard the Reach the reversed crew were aging forward by the hour, children becoming adults again in fast-forward, their faces cycling through decades like weather.

"Yes, I do." Ansel set the tablet down. "That's the whole point of doing it."

"It's not fair."

He almost laughed. Fair. He thought of the hold below, where forty-one backup pods still slept in their blue light — the life he'd stolen from himself, or given to himself, depending on which direction you ran the clock. "Dara. Nothing about the last eleven years has been fair. That's why it worked. The lie held because nobody wanted it to be true badly enough to check." He touched the tablet's edge. "The truth is out now. So the only thing left worth trading is me."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide not to argue. She'd gotten good at that, these last weeks — deciding, visibly, deliberately, not to fight him. It was its own kind of loyalty, and it cost her more than obedience ever had.

"The pods," she said instead. "What happens to them?"

"Yewande wants them sealed under joint custody. Halvorsen wants them destroyed. I told them both the same thing: they're evidence of a crime nobody can name, committed in a place nobody can reach, against people who are standing right here." He nodded toward the viewport, toward the Reach hanging in the rift-light with her restored crew moving behind her windows. "Let the lawyers drown in it. I'll be gone."

"Will you?"

The question landed softly and went deep. He didn't answer it. There wasn't an answer yet, and there wouldn't be until the name was off him.

* * *

Yewande came aboard at 0600 corridor-standard, alone, which was itself a message. No aides, no marines, no recording suite beyond the one she carried in her breast pocket, openly displayed. She wore civilian gray instead of Combine inspector's black, and when Ansel met her in the airlock she extended her hand rather than her credentials.

"Inspector," he said.

"Retired as of four hours ago. They're calling it a rotation." Her smile was thin and dry as old paper. "I certified a peace treaty this morning, Ansel. Nobody retires after that. They get promoted somewhere far away where they can't talk." She glanced down the Lament's corridor, at the scuffed deck plating, the cable runs Dara had never bothered to hide, the small human mess of a ship that had been home to one tired man and his secrets. "May I see the nav room? I'd like to sign this properly. Where you actually live."

The nav room smelled the way it always had — ozone and old tea and the faint mineral tang of the corridor dust that got into everything this close to the blockade line. Yewande stood in the middle of it and turned slowly, taking inventory of his life: the worn pilot's chair molded to his body over eleven years, the handwritten charts taped above the console because he'd never trusted the digital ones, the photograph — one photograph — of a coastline he had never identified to anyone.

"Lagos," she said, nodding at it. "Before the seawalls. You're older than you look."

"Everyone says that."

"They say it about the boy too." She set her case on the console and opened it. Inside lay the transfer instrument: a single sheet of physical paper, real ink, real seals, because both governments had agreed that something this strange should be done in the oldest medium available. Paper could not be edited backward. After the Fold, everyone had developed feelings about records that couldn't change their minds. "Before we begin, I'm required to ask you one question, and I want you to understand that I am asking it as a private citizen, off any record but mine." She looked up at him. "Do you understand what you're giving away?"

Ansel sat down in his chair. It fit him like a hand.

"I've had eleven years to think about what my name weighs," he said. "Registry 7741-LC. First-class deep-route certification, one of nine left in the Combine. The Montrose corridor rights — the only clean line between your fleet and Halvorsen's. And whatever else comes attached." He paused. "A man who erased evidence once and built a lie on top of it and kept the peace alive by keeping the lie alive. That's the inventory. That's what Idris inherits."

"He'll inherit the enemies too."

"He already has them. He inherited them the day he walked off the Reach with my face getting younger on it every hour." Ansel leaned forward. "Yewande — I've watched him age thirty years in six days. He remembers choosing to erase the Fold. He remembers it better than I do, because he remembers it from the other end, from the place where all of this is already finished. When I hand him my name, I'm not abandoning a child. I'm handing a man his own receipt."

Yewande was quiet for a moment. Then she uncapped the pen — an actual pen, reservoir-fed, absurd and ceremonial — and laid the document flat between them.

"Then let's do it," she said. "Clause by clause, so there's no argument later. Read them aloud. Both fleets insisted. They want it on the record that you were lucid and willing."

"I was never more lucid," Ansel said, and began to read.

* * *

*Clause One: All personal registries, certifications, and biometric identifiers pertaining to MONTROSE, ANSEL K., are transferred irrevocably to MONTROSE, IDRIS A., effective upon signature.*

His voice sounded strange saying his own name aloud in the third person, like reading an obituary for someone who was listening. Outside the viewport, the rift pulsed. Ships flickered along the blockade line — a Combine frigate aging twenty years in a stutter and back, a Meridian destroyer briefly showing hull-scars from battles that hadn't happened yet. Everyone had stopped pretending the phenomenon was localized. Time was leaking, and the only thing holding either fleet steady was the agreement being signed in a cramped nav room over cold tea.

*Clause Two: Route rights to the Montrose Corridor pass wholly to the second party, including toll authorities, beacon maintenance obligations, and priority-of-passage status under the Treaty of Shared Silence.*

There it was. The thing both armadas had actually come for, dressed in legalese. Whoever held the corridor held the only road between the fleets, and for eleven years that road had belonged to a man who used it to keep the two sides from ever quite meeting. Now it would belong to a boy who remembered the future. Ansel wondered if the negotiators understood what they'd done — that they hadn't transferred a route, they'd transferred a prophecy with docking rights.

*Clause Three: The second party assumes all debts, liabilities, and outstanding inquiries attached to the first party's record, including but not limited to the unauthorized deep-route expedition of [date sealed], the matter of the vessel Halcyon Reach, and the disposition of forty-one stasis units currently under joint custodial seal.*

"Including the crime," Yewande said quietly, when he reached that line. "You're making him guilty of what you did."

"I'm making him *responsible* for it. There's a difference, and it's the only inheritance worth leaving anyone." Ansel tapped the clause. "He chose the erasure. In whatever order time actually happened, he chose it. Let him carry the choice forward instead of me carrying it backward forever. That's the trade. That's the whole shape of it."

"And what do you get, Ansel?" She said his name deliberately, savoring it, the way you say a word in a language you're about to stop speaking. "Under Clause Four. Read it."

*Clause Four: Upon full execution of this instrument, the first party shall hold no legal identity within the jurisdiction of the Lagos Combine, the Meridian Fleet, or any signatory power. The first party shall be recorded as: unnamed. Status: free person, unregistered. All claims upon the first party, criminal or civil, are extinguished and cannot be revived.*

Unnamed. Free. Unregistered. He read the words twice and felt them go through him like cold water through a hot engine — not pain exactly, but the shock of a system changing state. For eleven years his name had been a wall he hid behind. Before that, it had been a ladder he climbed. He had never once, in fifty-three years of life lived in whichever direction, simply been a man in a room.

"What do I get," he repeated. "I get to find out."

Yewande studied him for a long moment, and then, for the first time since she'd boarded his ship with polite surgical questions all those weeks ago, she smiled without any iron behind it.

"Sign," she said.

He signed. His hand did not shake. The pen scratched, and the sound was very small for the size of what it did, and when it was done the ink dried in seconds in the dry recycled air, and Ansel K. Montrose ceased, in the eyes of every government between here and the deep routes, to exist.

Yewande countersigned, pressed her seal into the wax Dara had found in the galley stores — plum-colored, meant for preserving jars, doing the work of empires now — and blew on it gently, like blessing it.

"There," she said. "It's done. Halvorsen's withdrawal order is already transmitting; he agreed to stand down the moment the instrument executed. He never wanted the boy, you know. He wanted leverage, and you've just made the boy worthless as leverage by making him legitimate." She closed her case. "And the Combine gets its corridor keeper — young, brilliant, and owing his entire existence to our good faith. My superiors think they won something today."

"Haven't they?"

"Oh, everyone won something. That's how you know it's a real peace and not just a pause." At the hatch she stopped, half-turned. "For what it's worth, Ansel — the question I asked you that first day, the one you lied your way past. I knew the answer the whole time. I always knew. I let the lie stand because the peace was standing on it." She tapped her breast pocket, where the recorder sat. "This recording is blank. Some things shouldn't have witnesses. Goodbye, navigator."

And she was gone down the corridor, gray coat, straight back, carrying nothing.

* * *

Idris came to him at dusk.

Corridor dusk — the artificial dimming cycle the Lament ran because Ansel had never been able to sleep under full light. The boy — no, the man now, nearly; the reversal had carried him through his twenties in the last two days and was slowing at last, settling, like water finding its level — stood in the nav room hatchway wearing clothes that fit him better than they should have, as if the ship had already decided whose he was.

"It's done," Idris said. Not a question. He'd felt it somehow, or remembered it, or both; with him the categories had stopped mattering weeks ago.

"It's done." Ansel gestured to the chair across from him. Idris took it, and for a while neither spoke. Through the viewport the rift burned its slow impossible colors, and along the blockade line the ships of two armadas were turning, withdrawing, folding back into their separate silences like hands unclenching.

"I read the clauses," Idris said finally. "All of them. Including Three." His voice was Ansel's voice, younger by thirty years, rougher by none. "You gave me your crimes."

"I gave you your choices. There's a difference."

"Don't hide inside semantics, old man. I remember being you." But there was no anger in it. There was something worse and better: recognition. "I also remember forgiving you. From the other side. From wherever it is I remember from." He leaned forward. "So here's what I came to say, face to face, in the right order this time. I forgive you. I forgave you before you did it. Whatever you erased out there, whatever you lost, whatever you built those pods to keep — it's mine now, all of it, the guilt and the route and the name. And I'm going to run this corridor better than you did."

Ansel laughed — really laughed, the first time in longer than he could measure. "Better than me? I kept two fleets from a war for eleven years."

"With a lie. I'm going to do it with the truth and a schedule." Idris grinned, and it was Ansel's grin, and it was not, and the difference was the whole miracle. "First convoy goes through in nine days. Combine medical, Meridian observers, joint survey teams to the Fold. Somebody has to navigate between them, and there's exactly one certified pilot alive who knows the corridor and has adult memories of the future." He stood, and extended his hand — the same gesture Yewande had made, the gesture of equals ending something. "Thank you. For the life. Both directions of it."

Ansel took the hand. It was warm. It was his own grip, returned to him from the far end of time, and when they let go he felt lighter than he had since the night a power fluctuation woke him to hear a pod murmuring coordinates only he knew.

"Take care of her," he said, meaning the ship, meaning Dara, meaning both.

"I know," Idris said. "I remember."

* * *

He packed that night, and it took ten minutes.

Eleven years of life fit into one bag: the handwritten charts he left taped above the console — those belonged to the corridor now, and corridors keep their own records — the photograph of the pre-seawall coast, which he took, because some things were his regardless of what the registries said; two changes of clothes, the pen Yewande had left behind on purpose, and nothing else. The pods stayed. The ship stayed. The name stayed, folded into another man like a letter into an envelope.

Dara met him at the airlock. She didn't ask where he was going, because the answer was nowhere, everywhere, the docks of New Lagos, the long quiet places where unnamed men could work with their hands. She just stood there in the yellow airlock light with her engineer's calluses and her unslept eyes, and she said:

"I studied your blood for six weeks, you know. Yours and his. Do you know what I found?"

"Tell me."

"That the Fold doesn't copy and doesn't reverse. It *continues*. Every one of those people on the Reach is the same continuous thread, just tied in a knot and pulled through backward. Which means—" her voice caught, steadied— "which means you're not giving him your life. You're both living it. You're just finally going to live your half forward."

Ansel looked at her for a long time. Then he shouldered the bag.

"Come with me," he said. "Or don't. Either way — walk me out."

She walked him out. Down the Lament's familiar corridor, past the sealed hold where forty-one pods slept their blue sleep under joint seal, past the galley with its plum wax and its old tea smell, to the airlock, where the Reach's shuttle waited to carry him down to the terminal docks of New Lagos, where no one would know his face and no record would confirm it and the war would stay dead and the peace would stay alive and a new navigator named Idris Montrose would take the corridor out in nine days.

At the airlock threshold Ansel stopped, turned, and looked back one last time at the ship that had been his whole world — the Vesper's Lament, scarred and humming, already belonging to someone else's future.

"Goodbye, navigator," he said to the empty corridor, to the man he had been, to the name he had buried alive so that something else could grow from the grave.

Then he stepped through, and the lock cycled behind him, and for the first time in fifty-three years — counted in either direction — Ansel Montrose went forward into a world that had no idea who he was.

The shuttle dropped toward Lagos, toward the lights of the terminal, toward the beginning of whatever came next.
