## 4. Concord Inspection

The notice came through on a Tuesday, which meant nothing out here where Tuesdays were a bureaucratic fiction, but the words on it meant everything: *CONCORD IDENTITY AUDIT — ALL CREW — SECTOR NINE STATION — MANDATORY.*

Jude read it twice in the galley of the *Long Answer*, the screen's cold light pooling in their coffee like something drowned.

"Everyone gets scanned," Okafor said from the doorway, not looking up from the torque wrench she was cleaning. "Standard after an anomaly. They'll wave us through in ninety seconds each."

"Right," Jude said.

"Ninety seconds," she repeated, and this time she did look up, and her eyes held Jude's for one beat longer than casual, before she went back to the wrench as if nothing had passed between them at all.

Ninety seconds was enough. Ninety seconds under a deep-pattern scanner would find what nine seconds of falling had confirmed for Jude alone: that the architecture of their mind had been quietly rearranged, clause by clause, into something the Concord's legal dictionaries had no word for except *defect*. Something that sat, they hoped, one threshold below detection.

They hoped.

* * *

Vess Andrade had been awake for thirty-one hours when the *Long Answer* made its approach vector, and she considered this a professional advantage rather than a hardship. Sleep softened auditors. She had watched it happen to colleagues over eleven years—watched them grow generous, watch them start to believe that most people were mostly honest, and then watched those same colleagues get reassigned to filing duty in the inner system while the liars they'd cleared went on to do whatever liars did with their clean bills of health.

She stood in the inspection bay of Sector Nine Station with her tablet against her hip and watched the freighter dock. It was an old ship, hull scarred with micrometeorite pitting, running lights flickering on the port array in a way that suggested deferred maintenance or deliberate neglect—both, usually, were true of long-haul crews. Two crew listed on the manifest. A xenolinguist named Halloran and a systems engineer named Okafor. Hauling agricultural substrate and medical isotopes between colonies that had stopped answering their comm schedules.

That last part interested Vess more than she let her face show. The anomaly—nine seconds of gravitational failure across every inhabited world, precise as a metronome—had originated somewhere in the outer dark, near coordinates the Concord's public charts labeled only *restricted salvage zone*. And the ships that still ran routes past the edge of the mapped worlds were few, and their crews were the kind of people who saw things and said nothing, because saying something invited exactly the kind of visit Vess was currently making.

The airlock cycled. Two figures came through.

The engineer first—a broad-shouldered woman with gray threading her close-cropped hair, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had spent decades trusting machines more than institutions. She gave Vess a nod that was polite and entirely uninformative.

Behind her came the linguist.

Vess had read the file on the transit out. Halloran, Jude. Age forty-one. Fifteen years of contract decoding work on the alien archive—the dead civilization's signal cache, terabytes of structured transmission that three generations of Concord analysts had failed to fully parse. Clean record. Stipend recipient. No dependents. No political affiliations. The file was so clean it looked laundered, though Vess knew that long-haul lives simply accumulated less grime than station lives. Isolation was its own kind of bleach.

"Halloran," Vess said. "Auditor First Class Andrade. You understand why I'm here."

"The anomaly." Halloran's voice was level. Mid-register, slightly roughened, the voice of someone who talked to comms more than people. "Nine seconds."

"You noticed the duration."

"Everyone noticed the duration. We were floating."

Something in the flatness of it snagged Vess's attention—not evasion, exactly, but a quality of attention underneath the answer, as if Halloran were listening to the question in two registers at once. She filed it. She filed everything; that was the job. A mind was just an archive you hadn't indexed yet.

"Scanner's through the second door," she said. "Deep-pattern. Neural topology, memory-index coherence, identity baseline against your birth registration. Standard post-anomaly protocol." She paused, letting the words sit. "You've done these before?"

"Every five years. License renewal."

"Then you know the drill." She gestured. "After you."

Halloran walked ahead of her, and Vess watched the walk. Not suspicious—she catalogued gaits the way other people collected stamps—but noting. Steady. Hands relaxed. Breathing even. Either genuinely calm or very well rehearsed, and Vess had learned over eleven years that the second category was larger than anyone comfortable wanted to admit.

* * *

The scanner room was small and white and hummed at a frequency designed, Jude suspected, to make lying physiologically difficult. They sat in the cradle chair, and the technician—a station employee with the dead-eyed patience of someone who ran forty scans a day—pressed the crown array down over Jude's skull.

"Cold," the tech said, by way of apology or warning.

It was cold. It was also, Jude thought, the closest thing to being read a last rites that modern bureaucracy offered. The array would map the folding architecture of their cortex, compare it against the baseline recorded at age nineteen, flag any deviation beyond tolerance. Deviations meant reclassification proceedings. Reclassification meant—

Don't think about it in words, Jude told themselves. Think about it the other way. The way the archive thought. Structure without language. Pattern without name.

This was the trick they'd developed over years without ever deciding to, the way a river develops a bed: when the danger of articulation rose, Jude could drop their thoughts below the level of human syntax entirely, into that other grammar that had colonized them one decode session at a time. Not thinking *in* it—they weren't sure anything could think purely in it, not yet—but resting there, the way you rest your weight against a wall. Thoughts became shapes. Relations without subjects. If the scanner flagged linguistic-analytic activity, it would find activity, yes, but none of it would resolve into anything the pattern-matchers could call suspicious. Alien structure didn't look like deception. It didn't look like anything. That was the whole horror and the whole shelter of it.

"Relax your jaw," the tech murmured.

Jude relaxed their jaw and fell inward, down past English, down past the wordless counting that all humans share, into the lattice. Around them the scan light pulsed. Somewhere in the machine, their self was being transcribed and compared against the person they had been twenty-two years ago, and the difference between those two people was a chasm wide enough to fall through forever, if anyone ever measured it correctly.

One threshold below detection. That was the number Jude carried like a stone in the chest. They'd calculated it once, drunk on cheap station whiskey, from leaked technical specs of the older scan arrays: deviation index 0.94, detection threshold 0.95. Five hundredths of a person between citizenship and property.

But the new arrays—

Nobody knew what the new arrays did. That was the thing about audits after anomalies. Nobody knew.

"Thinking about anything?" the tech asked, conversational, eyes on his display.

"Freight schedules," Jude said, which was even true, in a way, because the shape of the thought they were holding—cargo mass against burn windows, load against time—was freight-shaped, whatever grammar wore it.

"Everyone says that."

"Because everyone thinks it."

The tech almost smiled. The scan light pulsed. In the lattice beneath language, Jude held still and waited, the way the arks waited, the way the dead civilization's signals had waited in the dark for someone with ears shaped right.

* * *

Vess watched the biometrics feed on her tablet with the particular pleasure of a woman reading a book whose ending she intended to spoil.

Heart rate: elevated but consistent with scan anxiety. Cortical load: high-normal. Linguistic-analytic centers: active, which was interesting—most people's went quiet under the crown, retreating to rote compliance, but Halloran's were burning like a stove left on in an empty house.

She cross-referenced the live topology against the birth baseline. The overlay rendered in amber and blue, two brains superimposed, and she rotated it slowly, looking for the lie in the geometry.

There. Or—almost there. The left temporal association cortex showed remodeling that the baseline didn't have. Deep white-matter tracts rerouted, density increased, the whole region reorganized around some new center of gravity. Vess had seen remodelings before: musicians, polyglots, trauma survivors, the occasional black-market neural tinkerer's victim. The brain wrote itself around whatever it practiced, and fifteen years of alien signal decoding was certainly practice.

But this wasn't the diffuse thickening of expertise. This was structural. Systematic. As if the region hadn't merely grown but been *rebuilt along foreign lines*.

Her thumb hovered over the flag control.

The deviation index rendered: **0.91**.

Threshold: **0.95**.

Below tolerance. Technically. Legally. The system stamped the scan *CLEARED — WITHIN BASELINE TOLERANCE* in green, and the green looked obscene to Vess, the way a locked door looks obscene when you can hear breathing on the other side.

She didn't flag it. Flagging required cause, and cause required an index above threshold, and Vess had learned the hard way—there had been a case, early on, a dockworker she'd been certain about, certain in her bones, and the bones had been wrong and the man had received a formal apology and Vess had received a notation in her file that still stung—that certainty was not evidence. But she marked the scan for manual review anyway, attached her own annotation—*structural remodeling, L-temporal, etiology unclear, recommend follow-up*—and told herself that follow-up was a neutral act. Follow-up was due diligence. Anyone would be followed up.

She dismissed Halloran with a nod and called in the engineer, and while Okafor settled into the cradle chair with the sigh of a woman who found all of this tedious, Vess kept half her attention on the doorway where Halloran waited, hands folded, face composed, watching her back with an expression of perfect, unreadable courtesy.

*What are you?* Vess thought, and hated the question for being unprofessional, and kept it anyway, because eleven years had taught her that the questions policy forbade were always the ones worth asking.

* * *

In the corridor outside, Jude leaned against the bulkhead and discovered that their hands were shaking.

Not much. A fine tremor, the kind you could pass off as caffeine. But inside, behind the composed face, everything was ringing like a struck bell. The scan had taken four minutes and forty seconds and Jude had spent all of them holding their mind in the lattice, motionless, a swimmer floating to seem like driftwood—and now, released, the human language came flooding back with an urgency that felt almost like grief. Words. Their own words. *I am Jude Halloran. I am a person. I am a person. Say it fast before the machines finish disagreeing.*

Okafor emerged twelve minutes later, cleared in under two minutes flat, exactly as predicted. She fell into step beside Jude without comment until they reached the junction where the station corridor branched toward the commercial docks, and there she stopped, ostensibly checking a cargo manifest on her wrist display.

"Follow-up review," she said quietly. "I heard the tech mention it. Your scan got flagged for manual review."

"It got cleared."

"Cleared and flagged aren't opposites, Jude." She didn't look at them. Her thumb scrolled the manifest that she wasn't reading. "Whatever's sitting in your head that you don't talk about—I'm not asking. I've never asked. But if the Concord starts pulling threads, you tell me before they pull mine. Deal?"

The offer landed in Jude's chest with a weight that was almost unbearable, because it was the first time in six years anyone had named the secret out loud, even sideways, even without knowing its shape. Okafor knew *something*. Had probably known for years. Had chosen, all this time, to let Jude keep it.

"Deal," Jude said.

She nodded once and walked on toward the docks, and Jude stood a moment longer in the junction, under the flickering station lights, feeling the audit's green stamp like a stay of execution—which is exactly what it was, they knew, exactly, a postponement and not a pardon—and thought about the countdown grammar waiting in the archive, ticking toward whatever it was ticking toward, and how the Concord had just shown them, in four minutes and forty seconds, precisely what would happen if it ever learned what they were.

They would become cargo. Property. An asset with a serial number.

Unless they used what they were first.

Across the concourse, on a public news column, the headline cycled past: *CONCORD DENIES ANOMALY LINK TO RESTRICTED ZONE — INVESTIGATION ONGOING.* Beneath it, smaller, easy to miss: *Cryo-Ark Salvage Review Board reconvenes next month.*

Jude stared at that second line until the column refreshed and took it away.

Someone else, it seemed, was asking about the arks too.
