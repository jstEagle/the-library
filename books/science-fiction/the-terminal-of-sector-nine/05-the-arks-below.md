## 5. The Arks Below

The Long Answer hung in the shadow of Vessel-7 like a moth pinned to a dead man's coat, and Jude Halloran spent the nine hours of orbital insertion pretending not to think about the number nine.

Vessel-7 had been a mining platform once, back when the colonies still mined anything worth the cost of lifting it. Now it was a fuel depot and a customs waypoint and a graveyard of smaller ambitions—docked skiffs with their drive cores pulled, a chapel that had been a hydroponics bay, a noodle shop run by a man who charged Concord scrip and never asked questions. The freighter's crew had a six-hour layover before the return leg to Sector Nine, and Jude spent the first two of them in the station's archive annex, which was less a library than a mausoleum of data: racks of cold-storage drives, a terminal with a cracked display, and an archivist drone that moved through the aisles on failing bearings with the patience of something that had outlived its purpose and hadn't been told.

Jude hadn't planned to come here. That was the lie Jude told, and had been telling since the flicker. *You came for coolant filters. You came because Okafor wanted real tea. You did not come because something under your sternum has been counting since the gravity failed.*

The archive terminal accepted Jude's Concord research credential with a bored chirp. Xenolinguistic survey access. Dead signals division. Jude had used it a hundred times to pull the silent screams of extinct civilizations—pulsar lullabies, the radio fossils of colonies that had gone dark before the Concord standardized its records. Jude typed the query the way one might touch a burn to confirm it still hurts.

*Gravitational anomaly. System-wide. Nine-point-zero-zero seconds. Origin vector.*

The drone whirred. The cracked display flickered—Jude's stomach clenched at the flicker, an absurd animal response—and then the answer came back in the flat typography of bureaucracy:

**ANOMALY 9.00s: SOURCE REGION — KUIPER SHELL, SECTOR 11. NEAREST DESIGNATED STRUCTURE: CRYOGENIC ARK CLUSTER "THE HUMBLE CHOIR" (ARKS 1–14). STATUS: SEALED / UNSALVAGEABLE / CONCORD DIRECTIVE 77-C.**

Jude read it four times.

The Humble Choir. Everyone knew the name the way everyone knew the name of a war, or a plague year—the way you know a thing you have agreed not to look at. Half a million people. That was the number that circulated in the freight corridors, in the ration lines, in the whispered arithmetic of the colonies: *half a million souls packed into fourteen arks and launched out past the last useful orbit when the exodus fleet ran out of fuel, out of time, out of mercy.* The Concord's official language was different. *Deferred reactivation pending resource stabilization.* *Unsalvageable* was the word that had leaked, and once it leaked it became true the way words do.

The arks had been out there for sixty-one years.

And nine days ago, something at the edge of the Humble Choir had pulled the gravity out from under every living person in the system, held them all in the air for nine seconds, and set them down again.

Jude sat very still in the archive annex, listening to the drone's failing bearings, and felt the dread from the freighter's cargo bay rise up through them again—not the fear of falling, but the fear of *being noticed*. Because Jude had decoded the alien archive for eleven years now, and the structure of that anomaly, the nine-second held breath, the grammar of it—

*Countdown,* the thought arrived, in no language Jude could name, in a syntax that felt like a hand closing gently around the back of the neck. *Not a warning. A countdown.*

Jude wiped the query from the terminal's buffer, logged out, and walked back through the mausoleum aisles without looking at the drone, without looking at anything, carrying the knowledge the way you carry a live coal in cupped hands—carefully, and knowing you cannot carry it forever.

* * *

Okafor was in the noodle shop when Jude found her, and she was not eating.

That was wrong on its face. Okafor—Chief Okafor, cargo master, the only person aboard the Long Answer who had ever made Jude laugh—treated layovers like religious obligations. She ate the way she worked: thoroughly, loudly, with commentary. She was a broad, gray-templed woman with a laugh that could be heard through two bulkheads and a superstition for every planet in the system, and she was sitting at the counter with a bowl of noodles going cold in front of her, staring at the wall screen where the station news feed cycled its usual litany: ration adjustments, water reclamation milestones, a Concord announcement about the anomaly.

*"...the Concord Office of Civil Stability confirms the nine-second gravimetric event was caused by a localized shear in the deep-field lattice and poses no ongoing risk. Citizens are reminded that speculation regarding unauthorized signals is a violation of Information Discipline Statute 12..."*

"Localized shear," Okafor said, to the screen, to the noodles, to no one. "Nine seconds. Every world at once. Localized."

The shop's owner, wiping down the counter, found somewhere else to be.

Jude sat down beside her. "You okay?"

"No." She said it flatly, without her usual armor of jokes, and that frightened Jude more than the archive readout had. "Jude. Do you know what day it is?"

"Station day, uh—" Jude checked. "Forty-one."

"Forty-one." Okafor turned the bowl a quarter-turn, not eating. "Sixty-one years ago, station day forty-one, they sealed the Choir. Final lockdown. My mother was station-side at Vessel-3 when they did it. She watched the last shuttle come back empty." She looked at Jude then, and her eyes were wet and furious in equal measure. "My brother was on Ark Seven."

The words went through Jude like cold water through a cracked hull.

Okafor had been on the Long Answer for nine years. Nine years of shared watches, shared meals, shared near-misses in the cargo bays. She talked about her mother, dead now, and her garden of illegal herbs in the hydroponics locker, and her endless losing campaign to get the ship's coffee machine replaced. In nine years she had never once said *brother*.

"Tomás," she said. "Tomás Okafor. He was twenty-six. Engineer, second class. He volunteered for the arks because they said—because *everyone* said it would be five years. Ten at most. Wake up, help rebuild, be a hero of the restoration." Her laugh came out broken, one syllable, nothing like her. "Five years. They sealed them and then the colonies started dying and the math changed, and the math kept changing, and the math never changed back."

"Okafor—"

"Sixty-one years, Jude." She finally looked away from the wall. "Do you know what they tell us? When we ask? *Resource stabilization pending.* You know what resource stabilization means? It means never. It means they did the arithmetic sixty years ago and the answer was that half a million sleeping people cost less than half a million awake ones, and they've been doing that same arithmetic every year since, and every year it comes out the same." Her hand closed around the cold bowl. "And now something out there pulls the gravity out from under the whole system, and the Concord says *shear in the lattice*, and everyone nods and goes back to their rations, because that's what we do. We nod. We go back."

The noodle shop's heater cycled. Somewhere in the station's bones, a door closed.

"Okafor," Jude said, very quietly, "why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you're the only person I know who listens to dead things for a living." She turned to face Jude fully. "And because I've been watching you since the flicker. You went quiet. You, who never shut up about syntax and dead civilizations and how the Vossi modals don't map onto anything human. You went quiet like a person holding something." Her voice dropped. "You know something about what happened out there. Don't tell me. I'm not asking you to tell me. I'm asking—" and here her voice finally cracked, the great laughing Okafor, cracked clean through— "I'm asking you to tell me if it's them. If whatever happened, whatever's coming, if it has anything to do with the Choir. Because if it does, I need to know, Jude. I have spent sixty-one years of other people's lives not knowing, and I can't—"

She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. The noodle shop owner had come back and was pretending to inventory condiments with great intensity.

"Tomás used to write me letters," she said into her hands. "Before the seal. Every week, terrible letters, full of engineering jokes I didn't understand. The last one said *see you in five, big sister, keep my ration credits warm.*" She lowered her hands. Her face was composed again, mostly, the way a hull is composed after a micrometeorite strike—intact, and bearing the mark. "I still have the credits. Sixty-one years. I never spent them. Isn't that the stupidest thing you ever heard?"

"No," Jude said. "It's the most human thing I've ever heard."

And the word *human* sat in Jude's own mouth like a stone, because of what Jude had learned in the archive, because of what Jude was becoming, because of the threshold sitting one measurement below Jude's own skull. *Human.* Okafor had spent it on Jude like it was nothing, like it was a fact, like it was a gift.

"I'll tell you what I can," Jude said. "When I can. I promise you that. Not the Concord version. Mine."

Okafor studied them for a long moment. Whatever she saw—the coal in the cupped hands, the dread, the terrible tenderness—she apparently decided it was enough, for now. She picked up the cold noodles and ate them, all of them, methodically, because Okafor believed you did not waste food in front of the hungry dead, and when she finished she said, "Buy me a real tea before we ship out, xenolinguist. The stuff on the ship tastes like coolant."

"It *is* coolant."

"Then I want the expensive kind."

* * *

They walked back through the station's spine as the Long Answer's departure window approached, past the chapel that had been a hydroponics bay, past the docked skiffs with their pulled cores. The station's windows gave onto the dark, and Jude caught Okafor pausing at one of them, her reflection ghosted against the black, looking out and *down*—because down, out past the last charted relay, out in the Kuiper shell where the sun was just the brightest star, fourteen shapes hung in the dark.

Jude had pulled the survey imagery once, years ago, for a decode project. The arks were beautiful in the way that abandoned things are beautiful: long spindle hulls wrapped in frost, radiators folded like sleeping wings, running lights dead for six decades. The Concord's drones passed them on scheduled inspection orbits and reported the same thing every year: *hulls intact. Cryo integrity nominal. No response to hails.* Half a million hearts slowed to a beat a minute, then to a beat an hour, then to whatever a heart becomes when the state decides a heartbeat is a budget line.

*Nominal.* Sixty-one years of nominal. Jude thought of the alien archive, the repeating structure nested in the dead signals like a seed in permafrost, and thought: *nine seconds. Nine-point-zero-zero. Held breath. And then—*

And then what? The grammar had a shape Jude could almost parse, a conditional clause that needed one more iteration to resolve. The countdown was counting. Something in the dark below was learning to speak, or had been speaking all along, and had finally found an ear that trembled.

"Hey." Okafor's voice, beside them. She was looking at Jude's face, not the window. "Whatever you're not telling me. Whatever it is." She put her hand briefly on Jude's shoulder, heavy and warm. "My brother is down there. So if it's good news, hurry. And if it's bad news—" her jaw set— "hurry anyway."

The station chime sounded the fifteen-minute warning for the Long Answer's departure. They walked to the airlock together, and Jude carried two coals now instead of one: the countdown, and the promise. Somewhere below them in the dark, Tomás Okafor slept on, twenty-six years old and sixty-one years asleep, and the thing that had moved the gravity of worlds had done it from his doorstep.

Jude boarded the freighter, sealed their bunk, and opened the decode logs on a screen no one else could see.

The next iteration of the pattern was already arriving. And it was shorter than the last.
