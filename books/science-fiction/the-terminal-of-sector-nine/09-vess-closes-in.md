## 9. Vess Closes In

The auditor's office aboard the *Vigilant Standard* was nine meters long, which Vess Andrade had measured herself on her first day aboard, because she did not trust specifications. Nine meters of grey composite, one desk bolted to the deck, one chair for her and one for whoever sat across from her, and a viewport that looked out onto the slow carousel of Sector Nine Station's docking arms. She had requested the room be stripped of anything soft. Comfort made people loquacious in the wrong directions. She wanted them uncomfortable in the right ones.

She had been awake for thirty-one hours, and she was not tired. Tiredness was a resource allocation problem, and she had allocated against it.

On the desk, three screens burned with the patient light of cross-reference work. The leftmost held the gravity anomaly data: two flickers now, nine seconds and then eight, each one propagating through every inhabited world in the system like a stone dropped into still water. The middle screen held crew manifests—every licensed freighter, tug, and passenger vessel that had been within sensor range of the anomaly epicenter during either event. The rightmost screen held something she had not yet shared with her superiors: her own working theory, written and rewritten eleven times since the second flicker.

The Concord's official position was sabotage. Someone, somewhere, had gotten their hands on gravitic manipulation technology and was holding the colonies hostage—or playing a very elaborate joke, depending on which deputy director you asked. Vess had read the internal bulletins with the flat attention she gave all official positions, which was the attention one gives a child explaining where the missing biscuits went.

Sabotage required equipment. Equipment required procurement. Procurement left trails, and she had walked every trail twice. There were no trails. Whatever had caused the flickers had not been built in any shipyard, workshop, or garage in the system. It had arrived from outside, or it had been here all along, sleeping inside something everyone had agreed to stop looking at.

Her stylus tapped the desk once, twice—a habit she'd never broken, though she'd broken most others—and she pulled up the manifests again.

Cross-referencing was not glamorous work. It was the work of subtraction. Remove the vessels too far away to matter. Remove the vessels whose crews had no relevant certifications. Remove the vessels whose flight paths put solid rock between them and the anomaly's origin point at the moment of the first flicker. Each subtraction narrowed the field, and the field had narrowed the way a noose narrows, until what remained was a shortlist of fourteen vessels, then six, then—

Three.

Three ships had been close enough, positioned correctly, crewed by people with the theoretical knowledge to matter. Two of them were salvage tugs with skeleton crews and records so clean they were almost certainly falsified, because nobody's record was that clean. The third was a long-haul freighter named *Long Answer*.

She said the name aloud, alone in her office, and listened to how it sounded. Names meant things. People chose them or inherited them, and either way they leaked. *Long Answer* suggested someone who expected questions and dreaded them, or someone who had learned that answers take time, or someone being ironic about the whole business of inquiry. She wrote it down anyway. Everything got written down. Her case files were famous in three departments and infamous in a fourth, because she documented hunches alongside evidence and let future-Vess sort out which was which.

*Long Answer*'s manifest listed a crew of four. A captain with a suspended license and an unexplained gap of six years. An engineer with two citations for unauthorized modifications. A cargo handler named Okafor whose file was thin enough to read like a compliment. And a xenolinguist.

Jude Halloran.

Vess had pulled Halloran's file first, because xenolinguists were rare enough to be interesting and employed enough to be traceable. Contract linguist, Concord stipend, assigned to decode dead alien signals from the archive relays. Fourteen years of service. Commendations. No disciplinary flags. The file was beautiful, and beauty in a file was its own kind of warning. Files accumulated scars the way hulls did; a spotless hull meant either a new ship or a careful liar.

She dug deeper. Halloran's neural scan history—required annually for deep-space certification—showed a pattern flagged as "within tolerance" by three successive review boards. Vess had requested the raw scans under her auditor's warrant and spent four hours reading waveforms she was only partially qualified to interpret, which is why she had also requested, quietly, unofficially, a consultation with Dr. Emeth Sarel, a neurocartographer who owed her a favor from a case neither of them discussed anymore.

Sarel's assessment had come back that morning, encrypted, and Vess had read it three times.

*The pattern is anomalous but sub-threshold. Structurally consistent with early-stage syntactic reorganization—the kind seen in subjects exposed to non-human linguistic architectures over prolonged periods. If I were asked to testify, I would say this is within normal variance for the profession. If you were asking me privately, I would say that whoever this is has stopped thinking entirely in human categories, and the scans are catching the edge of it.*

Sub-threshold. One threshold below detection. Vess had sat with those words for a long time, because they described her own situation more precisely than she liked. She suspected everyone. That was the job. But suspicion without confirmation was just paranoia wearing a badge, and the Concord did not pay her to be paranoid—it paid her to be certain, and certainty required evidence, and evidence required proximity.

Which was why Okafor was coming aboard at 1400 hours.

* * *

Adaeze Okafor walked onto the *Vigilant Standard* like a woman entering a room she'd already mapped for exits. Vess watched her come down the corridor from the observation gallery above the airlock bay—the cargo handler moved with the unhurried economy of someone who hauled mass for a living and respected it, shoulders set, eyes doing a slow professional sweep of the deck seams, the bulkhead mounts, the emergency egress markers. Cataloguing. Not nervous. Practiced.

Good, Vess thought. Nervous people lied badly. Practiced people lied well, which would make this interesting.

In the interview room—she'd chosen a different room than her office, smaller, windowless, warm enough to be slightly unpleasant—she offered water and Okafor took it, drank half, set it down with a small precise click that said the offering had been noted and filed.

"Cargo handler, second class, *Long Answer*," Vess began, without preamble. "Fourteen years' service?"

"Eleven." Okafor's voice was level, low, the voice of someone who talked over engine noise for a living. "Before that I ran dockside loading at Meridian Station."

"Your file is thin."

"I do my job and go home."

"Where is home?"

Okafor smiled faintly. "A bunk, mostly."

Vess let the silence sit. Silence was a tool; most people rushed to fill it and filled it with exactly the thing they shouldn't. Okafor did not rush. She looked back at Vess with the patience of someone who had waited out longer silences in darker places, and Vess revised her estimate upward. This was not going to be a woman who cracked on schedule.

"The gravity flicker," Vess said. "Nine seconds, system-wide. Then eight seconds, eleven days later. Where was the *Long Answer* during both?"

"You have our flight logs. You pulled them—I know you pulled them, because my captain got the notice and swore at the galley table for ten minutes."

"I have your flight logs. I want your account."

"My account is the logs." Okafor shifted, and for the first time something moved behind her eyes—not fear, Vess thought. Grief, maybe, or its cousin. "We were hauling ore from Ceres Deep to the Kettle. During the first flicker I was in the mess with our linguist, Jude Halloran, and we both went up off the benches along with the cutlery. Nine seconds floating. Jude didn't scream. I did, a little. Then we came down and cleaned up and finished the shift."

"And the second flicker?"

"Different route. Same result. Things fall, then things don't." She paused. "You're not asking about the flicker, though. Are you."

It wasn't a question, so Vess didn't answer it. She turned her tablet around instead, and on it was displayed a single frame: the *Long Answer*'s external sensor record from the first anomaly, timestamped to the millisecond, showing a data burst—brief, structured, outbound—that the ship's own systems had logged and dismissed as relay chatter.

"Explain this."

Okafor looked at it for four full seconds. Vess counted.

"That's the ship talking to the relay buoy. Standard handshake. Happens every transit." Her voice hadn't changed, but her hands had gone still in her lap, absolutely still, the stillness of someone standing on ice and listening for the crack. "If your people think a handshake burst moved the moon, you should hire better physicists."

"If I thought that, I wouldn't be talking to you. I'd be arresting your captain." Vess leaned forward. "Here is what I know, Ms. Okafor. Three ships were positioned to matter during the anomalies. Two are noise. Your ship is signal. Someone aboard the *Long Answer* has either caused, witnessed, or understood what happened to the gravity of every inhabited world in this system, and I am going to find out which. You can make that easy or hard. Easy takes an hour. Hard takes everything you have, and I have more of everything than you do."

Okafor met her eyes. What lived there was not defiance—defiance was cheap, and Okafor didn't strike Vess as a woman who paid for things she could get free. It was something older and more expensive. Loyalty, maybe. Or love wearing loyalty's coat.

"You want to know about Jude," Okafor said quietly.

"I want to know about everyone. Start wherever you like."

For a long moment Okafor said nothing. Vess watched her decide—not whether to answer, but how much of the truth could be wrapped in truth. Then Okafor laughed, short and dry, without much humor in it.

"You know what I was going to say? I was going to say Jude's the gentlest person on that ship. Reads old signals all day, hums when the recycler's running quiet, taught me to play a card game from a colony that doesn't exist anymore. That's what I was going to say." She shook her head slowly. "But you'd hear it as cover, because everything's cover to you. So here's the other thing. Here's the thing I actually think about at night."

Vess waited.

"Jude doesn't dream out loud anymore. Used to—all that talk, half-asleep, whole languages tumbling out, some of them human, some of them maybe not. Then about five years ago it stopped. Just—stopped. And sometimes I catch Jude looking at me like I'm speaking through water. Like I'm the alien." Okafor's jaw tightened. "That's what I know. That's everything I know. And if you're asking me to hand over my crewmate because their brain works different, then ask it straight, because I've buried family who were declared unsalvageable by people with your paperwork, and I am not doing that arithmetic again."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere in the deck below, a coolant pump cycled, patient as a heartbeat.

Vess closed the tablet. "Thank you for your time."

"That's it?"

"For now. You're free to go. Transport back to the station leaves at 1500." She stood, and Okafor stood, and for a moment the two women regarded each other across the bolted-down table like duelists who had discovered they were fighting the same war from opposite trenches.

At the door, Okafor paused. She didn't turn all the way around—just her head, just enough.

"When you find whatever it is you're looking for," she said, "ask yourself why the Concord sealed half a million people in coffins and called it mercy. Ask yourself who decided the sleepers were cheaper asleep." Her voice didn't rise at all. "Then decide which side of that decision you're on. Because it's coming for you too, Auditor. It comes for everybody eventually."

The door sealed behind her.

* * *

Vess stayed in the room for a while after Okafor left, which was unlike her. She reviewed the interview recording twice, watching Okafor's hands, her breathing, the micro-tells Vess had been trained to read like weather. Nothing. The woman had given nothing away except grief, and grief was admissible everywhere and proved nothing.

But shaken—yes. Vess had seen it at the end, in the doorway, in the way Okafor's composure had cost her visibly, like a weight lifted and immediately regretted. She had said too much, and she knew it, and she would go back to the *Long Answer* and tell them everything, and the net would tighten around a crew that now knew it was being netted.

That was fine. That was even useful. Frightened people moved, and movement revealed shape.

Back in her office, Vess opened the file again. Halloran's face looked back at her from the certification photo—unremarkable, tired around the eyes, the face of someone who had spent fourteen years listening to dead voices and letting them rearrange the furniture of their mind one clause at a time. Sub-threshold. One threshold below detection. Living every day one standard deviation from erasure, and filing annual reports to the institution that would erase them.

And Okafor knew. Not the details—maybe not even the shape of it—but she knew something was different, and she had stood in a Concord interview room and chosen her crewmate over her own safety, and called it nothing, and meant it as everything.

Vess thought about her own file. Thirty years of service. The cases she'd closed and the ones she'd been ordered to close differently. The favors owed and collected. The deputy directors who spoke of the arks in units of budget rather than souls. Half a million sleepers, declared unsalvageable by committee, and a system so rationed it had learned to call abandonment prudence.

*Ask yourself who decided the sleepers were cheaper asleep.*

She pulled up the *Long Answer*'s projected route. Next scheduled port: Sector Nine Station. Departure window: forty hours.

Vess Andrade booked her own transport before she could talk herself out of it. Some suspicions, she had learned, you followed with paperwork. Others you followed in person, alone, before the institution's slowness could turn a living question into a closed case.

She packed one bag. On her way out, she paused at the viewport, looking down at the station's docking arms, at the small blunt shape of a freighter named *Long Answer* nested among the gantries like a seed waiting out a winter.

"One threshold," she said to the glass. "Let's see which side of it you fall on."
