## 14. Breaking the Long Answer

The Long Answer had carried Jude for eleven years, and now they were going to kill her.

They did it at night, or what passed for night in the Terminal's crowded orbit—when Sector Nine's rotation dragged its scarred face away from the sun and the station's windows filled with the cold glitter of the ark graveyard far below. Ilsa Vann had chosen the hour with a smuggler's precision. *Dead shift*, she'd called it. *When the Concord's eyes blink.*

Jude floated through the freighter's spine one last time, hand trailing along the conduit casing the way you'd trail fingers over a sleeping animal's flank. The ship smelled of recycled air and Okafor's cardamom tea and eleven years of small repairs—the particular smell of a home that had never once been called one out loud.

"You're sentimental," Okafor said from the engineering bay, not looking up from the transponder housing. Her voice was gentle with it, though. Teasing the way she teased when something mattered.

"I'm being thorough."

"You're saying goodbye to a machine."

Jude didn't answer, because she was right, and because the truth was worse than that: they were saying goodbye to the only witness of who Jude had been before the alien syntax had begun rewriting them thought by thought. Every log entry, every decode session, every nine-second fall—she had been there for all of it. Ships kept your secrets the way people couldn't.

"Transponder's open." Okafor wiped her hands on her coveralls and stepped back. "You want to do the honors?"

The transponder sat in its cradle like a heart waiting to be stopped. One command and the Long Answer would become a ghost—a dead hull drifting on registry records, unclaimed, untraceable. Vess Andrade would find an empty berth and a vessel that had, officially, suffered a catastrophic systems failure. By the time anyone investigated properly, they would be gone.

Jude entered the command. Somewhere beneath them, a signal that had announced their position to the system for eleven years went silent. It felt less like killing and more like holding your breath.

"There," Okafor said softly. "Now we're nobody."

* * *

The tug was called the *Mercy of Small Hours*, which Ilsa insisted was a joke and refused to explain. She'd won it in a card game nineteen years ago from a man who owed her money, and it had sat in the Terminal's outer berths ever since, slowly becoming part of the station's rust. Decommissioned twice. Recommissioned once by paperwork no one could find anymore. She was ugly as a fist and half the size of the Long Answer, and when Jude first saw her through the observation glass—squat, patched, her hull a patchwork of mismatched plating—they felt something loosen in their chest that they hadn't known was clenched.

"She flies?" Okafor asked doubtfully.

"She flew last year," Ilsa said. "Probably still does. Come on."

Getting her flight-ready took two days of work that left all three of them gray with fatigue. Ilsa rebuilt the drive couplings from parts she cannibalized off the Long Answer's corpse, working with a speed that made her age seem like a rumor. Okafor rewired the life support, cursing continuously in three languages. Jude handled navigation, plotting a course to the ark graveyard that looked, on every public chart, like nothing more than a long, boring haul toward empty space.

It was during those two days that Jude understood, for the first time, what they were actually doing. Not stealing a ship. Amputating a life. The Long Answer's registry was tied to Jude's citizenship file, their crew certifications, their legal existence. When the transponder died, so did the paper trail that said *this person is real*. They were cutting themselves loose from the version of themselves the Concord recognized—and drifting toward the version it would erase on sight.

Okafor caught them staring at the dead freighter through the tug's small viewport.

"Regrets?" she asked.

"I keep doing the math," Jude said. "What I lose if this works. What everyone loses if it doesn't."

"And?"

"The math doesn't balance either way. That's how I know it's the right thing. Clean choices always balance."

Okafor snorted. "That's the most depressing thing you've ever said, and I once heard you describe entropy as 'the universe tidying up badly.'"

* * *

They broke orbit on the third day, during another dead shift, with Ilsa at the helm and the Terminal shrinking behind them like a lantern sinking into dark water. For six hours everything held. The Mercy of Small Hours flew the way old things fly—complaining, rattling, but moving—and Jude began to believe, dangerously, that the hardest part was done.

The coolant breach happened at hour seven.

No warning. No alarm, even—the alarm had been stripped for parts years ago. Just a sound Jude felt through the deck plates before they heard it, a deep wrong note in the ship's throat, and then Ilsa's voice sharp across the intercom: *"Coolant line just let go in the aft ring. We're venting. Somebody suit up NOW."*

The aft ring was already fogging when Jude got there, vapor pouring from a split junction in glittering sheets, temperature plummeting. And beyond the fog, through the breach-side viewport, Okafor was already inside her suit, sealing her helmet, wading into the whiteout toward the rupture.

"Okafor—" Jude keyed the comm. "Wait for patch foam—"

"No time. Line's feeding the drive loop. If pressure drops past critical, we're a brick with opinions." Her voice came calm and clipped, the voice of someone who had done dangerous work her whole life and made peace with its arithmetic. "Hand me the clamp kit when you're suited. Thirty seconds."

Jude suited in twenty-eight. It was the fastest they had ever moved in their life, gloves shaking, helmet seals screaming as they forced them. Through the fog they saw Okafor reach the junction, plant her boots, wrench the first clamp onto the split pipe—

And then the second rupture opened.

It came without sound, the way disasters do in vacuum-adjacent spaces—a seam in the adjacent line simply giving up, and the coolant flash-boiled outward in a jet that caught Okafor across the chest and spun her off the junction like a thrown doll. Her suit's chest readout bloomed red. Frost raced across her visor from the inside.

*"Seal integrity forty percent,"* the suit reported, in its idiot-calm machine voice. *"Thirty-one percent. Twenty-six—"*

Jude crossed the compartment in a way they would never afterward be able to reconstruct. Later, Ilsa would say she'd never seen anyone move that fast outside of emergency drills, and Jude would say nothing at all, because the truth was that they hadn't been thinking in any language at all. Their body had simply understood the geometry of rescue the way it now understood alien grammar—directly, without translation.

They hit the clamp controls with both hands, jammed the seal home, and the jet died to a hiss, then a whisper, then nothing. Silence except for breathing—Jude's own, ragged, and Okafor's, thin and fast inside her ruined suit.

Her visor was iced opaque. Jude cracked it with bare fingers, ignoring the burn, and found her eyes open underneath, tracking them, alive.

"Coolant burns," she rasped. "Chest. Bad?"

"Bad," Jude admitted. The front of her suit was crusted white where the chemical frost had eaten through the outer layer. Beneath it, when they peeled the fabric back in the medbay an hour later, the skin was blistered in a map-shaped burn across her collarbone and shoulder—angry red, deep in places, the kind of wound that would scar in continents.

Ilsa treated her in silence, hands steady, jaw tight. When the dermal patch was finally sealed and Okafor was asleep in the narrow bunk, drugged and breathing easy, Ilsa stood over her a long moment.

"Nineteen years I've flown this hulk," she said quietly, "and nobody's ever bled on her. First day out, and she takes a piece of your friend." She turned to Jude, and her eyes were wet and furious. "This is the cost. I want you to understand that. There's no version of this where nobody pays. Whatever happens at those arks—someone pays."

"I know," Jude said.

"Do you? Because you talk about grammar and protocols and saving half of humanity, and all of it is true, and none of it is *skin*." She touched Okafor's bandaged shoulder, feather-light. "That's skin. That's a woman who followed you into a fog bank because she believes in you. Don't you dare spend her cheaply."

"I won't," Jude said. And meant it with a ferocity that frightened them.

* * *

Okafor woke eight hours later, demanded tea, was refused, demanded it again, and got it. She drank it one-handed, the other arm strapped across the burn, and listened while Jude and Ilsa laid out the final approach plan to the graveyard.

When they finished, she was quiet for a moment, looking down at her bandages.

"My brother used to say," she finally said, "that anything worth doing costs exactly as much as it's worth. No more, no less. He said it right before he boarded the ark." She flexed the fingers of her burned arm, wincing. "So. This much pain means we're close to something worth this much pain."

"That's not how math works," Jude said gently.

"No," she agreed. "It's how faith works. Different notation."

She looked up at them then, and behind the painkillers her eyes were clear and steady and absolutely certain, and Jude understood that whatever happened next—at the arks, with Vess, with the Concord's warships—Okafor had already paid her entry fee and considered it fair.

Outside the viewport, far ahead, the first of the arks had come into view: a pale sliver against the stars, frost-rimed, patient, humming—if instruments were to be believed—with a language only one person aboard could hear.

Jude pressed their hand flat against the cold glass, and in the hybrid silence of their own mind, answered it.
