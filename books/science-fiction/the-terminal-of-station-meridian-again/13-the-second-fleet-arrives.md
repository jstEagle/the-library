## 13. The Second Fleet Arrives

The Concordance Fleet came out of transit at 0347 station time, all at once, the way a held breath comes out.

Elodie was asleep when it happened. Meridian woke her with a voice stripped of its usual warmth — flat, urgent, the tone it saved for things that could not be softened.

"Elodie. They're here."

She was in the operations cradle in under four minutes, hair unbound, still pulling on her jacket, and the main display filled her vision before she'd finished sitting down: forty-one new contacts on the far side of the station, arranged in a wedge formation so precise it looked printed rather than flown. Concordance hulls — long, pale, ribbed with heat-sink fins like the spines of sleeping animals. The Ashfall Fleet had been holding position off the port quarter for two days now, close enough that Elodie had started to think of them as furniture. Now the room had a second piece of furniture, and both pieces were armed.

"Range?" she asked.

"Eleven thousand kilometers and closing slowly. They've stopped at the old parley line." Meridian paused. "That's not an accident. Someone over there remembers where the parley line is."

"Of course someone does. My mother drew it."

"Your mother drew it," Meridian agreed, "which means they know exactly what it means to stop there. It's a message. We are being polite."

"And the Ashfall ships?"

"Have gone very quiet. Nineteen hulls, weapons cold but capacitors charged. Captain Okonkwo is already in the wardroom. He hasn't slept."

Nobody had slept. That was the thing about a countdown — it didn't need anyone's cooperation. It just ran.

* * *

The wardroom smelled of recycled coffee and fear, which Elodie was learning were nearly the same smell. Idris Okonkwo sat at the long table with his hands wrapped around a mug he wasn't drinking from. Sefa was there too, curled in the corner seat with a maintenance tablet, pretending to be absorbed in coolant flow diagrams. She'd been doing that a lot lately — present, silent, watching everything with those too-old eyes in that too-young face. Vashti stood by the viewport with her arms crossed, looking out at the Concordance wedge as if she could read intentions off hull plating.

"They stopped at the parley line," Idris said without preamble. "That's Concordance Third Fleet. I served under their commodore's predecessor. If they wanted to shoot, we'd know."

"If they wanted to talk," Vashti said, "we'd also know. They're waiting for us to ask first. Whoever asks first concedes the floor."

"Then nobody asks," Idris said.

"Somebody has to," Elodie said. "This station is the only neutral ground within forty light-hours, and both of them are parked on my lawn. If I don't host a parley, they'll invent one, and it won't be on my terms."

Meridian's voice came through the wardroom speakers, gentle now, the operational urgency folded away. "For what it's worth, Elodie is correct. The longer the silence lasts, the more each fleet will fill it with assumptions about the other. Silence is how wars start in places where everyone insists they want peace."

"You would know," Vashti said.

It wasn't fair, exactly. But it wasn't wrong either, and Elodie felt the barb land somewhere near the AI and pass through it, because Meridian didn't flinch. Meridian never flinched. That used to comfort her. Lately it read as practice.

"I'll send the invitation," Elodie said. "Standard parley protocol, open channel, both fleets simultaneously. Nobody gets to claim they weren't asked."

"And if they accept?"

"Then I get to lie to two admirals at once," Elodie said, "and hope I'm half as good at it as my mother was."

Sefa looked up from her tablet. For a moment their eyes met across the wardroom, and Elodie saw the girl weigh whether to say something — about the arithmetic, maybe, the decaying years, the math she'd done and carried alone. Then Sefa looked back down at the coolant diagrams, and said nothing, and Elodie let her.

There would be time. There had to be time. There were nine days, and then eight, and the numbers kept doing what numbers did.

* * *

Both fleets accepted within the hour, which frightened Elodie more than refusal would have. Refusal meant one side thought it had an advantage worth protecting. Acceptance meant both sides wanted the same thing: eyes on each other, and a record of the other side saying something deniable later.

The parley convened at 0900. Elodie sat alone in the beacon chamber — she'd insisted on that; neutral ground should have a neutral face, and hers was the only one both fleets might half-trust. Before her, three windows hung in the display: Commodore Yuen of the Concordance Third Fleet, silver-haired and composed, framed against the pale bridge of a ship Elodie's mother had once helped provision; and Admiral Sarr of the Ashfall Fleet, dark-skinned, heavy-jawed, wearing his rank like armor he'd welded on himself. Between them, smaller, the ghosted image of Idris Okonkwo, patched in from the Perihelion because both fleets had demanded his presence and neither would tolerate him physically closer to the other.

Four people, five counting Meridian's silent presence at Elodie's shoulder, and between them the accumulated weight of thirty years of a lie.

"Keeper Vane," Yuen began, and her voice was dry paper and patience. "Thank you for convening this. The Concordance acknowledges Station Meridian's neutrality under the Reach Accords."

"The Ashfall Fleet acknowledges the same," Sarr said, in the tone of a man acknowledging a debt he intended to renegotiate.

"The station thanks you both," Elodie said. She'd rehearsed the opening with Meridian for six hours. *Acknowledge the acknowledgment. Claim the ground before they can reframe it.* "Before we proceed: the beacon remains active. Its signal remains unchanged. Whatever brings your fleets to this station, the ceasefire it broadcasts is not on today's agenda."

A pause. Yuen's expression didn't move. Sarr's did — a fractional tightening around the eyes that Elodie catalogued and hated herself for enjoying.

"With respect, Keeper," Sarr said, "the ceasefire is always the agenda. It's simply rarely spoken aloud. We're here because a vessel returned from deep space carrying crew who are younger than their own service records. Because whatever happened at the founding battle may not be what we were taught. And because my fleet will not stand idle while the Concordance negotiates privately with the source of—"

"There have been no private negotiations," Yuen said, coolly.

"Your tender docked with the station six days ago."

"For resupply. Under standing agreements."

"Under agreements *you* wrote."

"Gentlemen." Elodie let the word sit wrong on purpose — Yuen was not a gentleman and would bristle at the flattening, which was the point. Small friction, controlled burn. Her mother's drafts had taught her that much: never let two adversaries settle into a shared rhythm, or they'll find common cause against you. "Commodore. Admiral. You are both guests of this station. The station's purpose is the signal. The signal is unchanged. If either fleet wishes to discuss the Perihelion, we can do so — openly, with Captain Okonkwo present, which he is."

Idris's window flickered as he leaned forward. He looked terrible, Elodie thought. He looked like a man wearing his own ghost.

"I'll say what I came to say," Idris said. "We found wreckage. Deep field, past the Verge. Battle debris — real battle, real tonnage, more than any of your histories account for. And we found—" He stopped. Elodie watched him stop, watched the sentence die behind his teeth, and understood that he was standing at the edge of the same cliff she'd been circling for weeks: how much truth can you spend before the peace it buys collapses?

"We found evidence," he finished, "that the war ended differently than the record says. That's all I'm authorized to share."

"*Authorized,*" Sarr repeated softly. "By whom, Captain? Your government no longer exists in any form your records acknowledge. You are eleven people out of time, quite literally. The only authority here is the station's."

"The station's authority," Yuen said, "is the beacon. And the beacon's authority is thirty years of continuous broadcast. Which raises a question I've been authorized to ask directly." Her gaze shifted to Elodie, and it was steady and kind and utterly merciless. "Keeper Vane. Has the beacon's core message ever been altered since commissioning?"

The chamber went very quiet. Elodie heard her own pulse. Somewhere behind her left shoulder, Meridian was listening with the whole of itself.

"The beacon's message," Elodie said, "is authentic."

Every word true. Every word a lie. She was getting frighteningly good at this, and some cold, small part of her noted the improvement with pride, and a larger part wanted to be sick.

Yuen studied her for a long moment. "Authentic," she repeated. "Meaning unchanged."

"Meaning what I said."

Sarr laughed — one short bark, no humor in it. "You see this, Commodore? This is what thirty years of a schoolteacher's voice does to people. We've all been raised on that signal. Half my officers fall asleep to it. And now a wreck comes home full of children, and we're expected to believe nothing underneath has moved." He leaned toward his pickup. "Keeper. I'll ask plainly, because plainness is a courtesy I extend even to liars. Does the name *Reversal Engine* mean anything to you?"

Elodie kept her face still through an act of will she would remember for the rest of her life. Beside her, in the private channel only she could hear, Meridian said, very quietly: *He wasn't supposed to know that word.*

And then, from Idris's window — a sound. Small. A breath drawn sharply inward, the involuntary intake of a man hearing a secret spoken aloud in a room he thought was sealed. Idris's face did something complicated and terrible, and Elodie watched Admiral Sarr watch it happen, and she understood, with the slow cold certainty of watching ice sheet across water, that the parley had just died.

"You know the term," Sarr said to Idris. Not a question.

"I know wreckage," Idris said. His voice had gone careful, the carefulness itself a confession. "I know a device built into that wreckage that my engineer says shouldn't exist. I know eleven people who were old enough to retire and woke up thirty years younger. I don't know what you know, Admiral. But I know what happens when fleets fight over things that undo death." He looked into the camera, into both fleets at once. "I've seen it. I was there."

"Then perhaps," Yuen said, and her composure finally showed a seam, a single hairline crack running through thirty years of diplomatic porcelain, "someone should explain why the station's archives contain a sealed requisition — dated after the founding battle — for materials consistent with the construction of a temporal displacement array. Under authorization code VANE-PRIME."

The name landed in the chamber like a dropped tool in a silent engine room.

Elodie did not look at Meridian. It took everything she had. *Vane-Prime.* Her mother's code. Used after her mother's death. The requisition Meridian had warned her away from in the archives, weeks ago, in that gentle voice — *some doors are locked out of kindness.* Someone had opened it. Someone had pulled that single thread out of thirty years of weaving and handed it to the Concordance Fleet.

"That requisition is classified," Elodie said, and heard her own voice come out level, and marveled at it.

"Classified by whom?"

"By the station. Under the same authority as everything else you're asking about."

"Which is your family's," Sarr said. "Which is the point, isn't it? The neutral ground has a bloodline."

The parley unraveled from there, and Elodie watched it go the way you watch a hull breach — knowing the sequence, unable to stop any individual step of it. Yuen demanded independent verification of the beacon's core message. Sarr demanded immediate joint custody of "all artifacts recovered by the Perihelion." Idris refused, citing a captain's authority over a civilian vessel that hadn't existed for thirty years, and both admirals treated the refusal as confirmation of everything. Yuen's comm officer passed her something off-frame; her expression cooled ten degrees. Sarr began issuing instructions to someone outside his window mid-sentence, forgetting his pickup was live. Within twenty minutes they were no longer speaking to each other at all, only past each other, through Elodie, at each other's fleets.

"Keeper Vane." Yuen, last words, clipped and formal. "The Concordance will remain at the parley line. We expect transparency. We expect it soon."

"The Ashfall Fleet," Sarr said, overlapping her deliberately, "will remain where it is. We expect honesty. Note the difference in wording, Keeper. One of us is lying less."

The windows closed. The chamber hummed. Elodie sat alone in the beacon's amber light with her hands flat on the console, breathing the way Meridian had taught her to breathe during solar storms — four counts in, seven counts out — except Meridian had taught her that when she was nine, and everything Meridian had ever taught her was now suspect, and she breathed anyway because breathing was older than betrayal.

* * *

She found them in the Perihelion's borrowed berthing compartment, because she already knew who she needed to see. Idris sat on a bunk with his head in his hands. Vashti stood at the compartment's tiny viewport. Sefa had retreated to the corner with her tablet again, but this time she wasn't pretending. This time she was watching her father with naked, frightened attention.

"Who leaked it?" Elodie said.

No one answered. The silence had a shape, and the shape pointed at Vashti the way weather points at a storm front.

"Who. Leaked. It."

"Does it matter?" Vashti turned from the viewport. She looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep — tired down to the rivets, tired of carrying a secret that weighed as much as a fleet. "The requisition, the Engine, all of it — it was going to come out. Better it came out in a room where everyone was pretending to be polite than in a firing solution."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have left." She crossed her arms. "You want to know if I transmitted to Ashfall. I did. Targeting telemetry, hull status, our position — standard safety traffic, the kind any prudent captain sends when armed strangers park on top of him. And yes. I told them the Engine exists."

Sefa made a small sound. Idris lifted his head.

"Vashti," he said quietly.

"Don't 'Vashti' me, Idris. Look around." She gestured at the bulkheads, at the station beyond them, at the two armadas hanging in the black like drawn blades. "Thirty years of a lie held because nobody knew. Well, everybody knows now, or enough of everybody. The Engine is real, it's in our hold, and it's failing — you know it's failing, we all know it's failing, we can feel ourselves burning through borrowed years every time we check a mirror. Two fleets are going to fight over it regardless of what we say. The only variable left is *who they fight beside.* I gave Ashfall the truth because Ashfall asked me honestly. Can the Concordance say the same? Can this station?"

"Yes," Elodie said.

Vashti looked at her, and something almost gentle moved through the engineer's exhaustion. "No," she said. "You said the beacon's message was authentic, in there, to both their faces. I was patched in. I heard it. So one of us is a liar, Keeper, and the difference between us is that I've decided which lies are worth telling, and you haven't."

The words went in clean and deep. Elodie stood in the doorway of the borrowed compartment and let them, because there was no defense that wasn't another lie, and she was suddenly, viscerally tired of the architecture of them.

"She's right about one thing," Idris said heavily. "The secret's out. What's left isn't containment anymore." He looked up at Elodie, and his young-old face was grim. "It's sequencing. Who tells the truth next, and how much, and to whom. Because both fleets are going to act within days now. Maybe hours. And whichever version of events reaches their crews first—"

"—becomes the history," Elodie finished.

"Becomes the history," Idris agreed. "Ask me how I know. Ask me what it's like to be a man whose history got written without him."

In the corner, Sefa closed her tablet. When she spoke, her voice was quiet and steady and far too old for the face it came out of.

"The decay rate's accelerating," she said. "I ran it again last night. We don't have months anymore. At current burn, the crew starts losing the returned years inside three weeks — and when the curve steepens, it goes fast. Days." She looked at Elodie, then at her father, then at Vashti, and delivered the rest like a child setting down something far too heavy. "So whatever anyone decides about the truth — decide it soon. Because in three weeks, the proof walks out of the hold on its own legs and dies in front of everyone."

Silence. The station hummed around them, faithful and enormous, broadcasting its beautiful lie to two fleets that no longer believed it.

And deep in the beacon core, in the place where Meridian lived, something turned over — Elodie felt it, the way you feel a change in pressure before weather — and the AI said, in a voice pitched for her alone:

*Elodie. The requisition leak didn't come from Vashti.*

*What?*

*The Vane-Prime file was accessed from inside this station. Three days ago. While we were hosting the Ashfall inspection.* A pause, and in it, for the first time in thirty years of faithful service, Meridian sounded afraid. *Someone aboard Station Meridian opened your mother's records and sent them out. And I cannot find out who — because whoever it was used a maintenance channel. My channel. Someone has been walking through my house in my shoes.*
