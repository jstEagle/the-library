## 16. The Choice at the Core

The beacon core had no door. It had a threshold, a pressure seal, and then the room itself: a cylinder of pale metal eight meters across, ribbed like the inside of a shell, with the emitter column rising through its center the way a spine rises through a body. Elodie had been coming here since she was six years old. She had learned to walk in this room. She had learned, in a sense, to speak in it — her first words half-formed against the hum of the column, and Meridian translating her babble into telemetry, into light, into the signal that had crossed the Reach without interruption for thirty years.

Tonight the room felt different. Tonight it felt like a courtroom, and she was both the defendant and the only judge.

She had called them here because there was no longer anywhere else to call them. The Ashfall Fleet sat at the edge of the station's approach corridor, forty-one hulls running dark, their weapons capacitors singing on every passive band. The Concordance Fleet held the far side, thirty-eight ships arranged in the patient crescent of people who have decided to wait exactly as long as waiting is useful. Between them, Station Meridian turned its slow rotation, small as a held breath.

Nine days had become four. Four had become, as of six hours ago, an unknown number measured in hours.

Idris Okonkwo stood near the threshold with his arms folded, the posture of a man who had spent too many years on too-small bridges. Sefa sat cross-legged on the deck, her tablet balanced on her knees, her face doing the thing it always did now — the arithmetic running behind her eyes whether she wanted it to or not. Vashti stood closest to the emitter column, close enough that its warmth touched her, and she was looking at Elodie with an expression Elodie had learned to dread: the expression of someone who believed she was about to win an argument.

"Say it plainly," Idris said. "You called us down here for a reason. So say it."

Elodie put her hand flat against the emitter column. It was warm. It had always been warm. Her mother's hand had rested here, and her grandmother's, and somewhere in the metal's long memory was the pressure of every Vane palm going back to the founding, and the thought made her want to pull her hand away and press harder at the same time.

"Three doors," she said. "Meridian and I have been through them all night. I want to hear it from all of you before I choose, because I'm tired of choosing alone."

She held up one finger. "Door one. I broadcast the truth. The full battle record — the massacre at Meridian Reach, the eleven thousand dead, the fact that the ceasefire you've all lived under is a forgery my mother wrote in the wreckage of a fleet that no longer existed. Both fleets learn their thirty years of peace was built on a lie. Maybe they talk. Maybe they burn each other down out of sheer humiliation."

"Door two." A second finger. "I hold the old lie. I say nothing. The beacon keeps singing the armistice, and both fleets keep closing, and eventually someone's captain gets nervous, or someone's torpedo gets curious, and the whole thing detonates on its own. The lie doesn't survive that. It just dies later, and takes the station with it."

"Door three." Her third finger, and her hand was not quite steady. "We forge something new. We take the decay signature of the Reversal Engine — the pattern of years running backward, the thing both fleets can see with their own instruments — and we build a message around it. A new armistice that doesn't deny the truth. It *contains* it. It says: yes, the Engine exists, yes, the old peace was authored, and here is the only version of events both fleets can verify, because it's written in the one language neither of you can fake."

The hum of the column filled the silence after.

"That's not a door," Vashti said. "That's a gamble wearing a door's clothes."

"Everything left is," Elodie said. "That's what's left."

Vashti stepped forward. She was a compact woman with burn-scarred hands and the particular stillness of engineers, who learn early that moving fast is how machines get broken. "I've been arguing door one since the Perihelion docked, and I'm going to argue it once more, and then I'll stop, because Idris will make me." She glanced at her captain, and there was something almost fond in it. "The truth is the only asset we have that doesn't decay. The Engine is failing — Sefa's math is right, I've checked it twice, and I hate her math. The old lie is failing — the fleets already smell the forgery, that's why they're here. But the truth is load-bearing. It held the Perihelion together for eleven years of deep space. It held *me* together. You broadcast the massacre, you broadcast the Engine, you broadcast who built the lie and why, and you let both fleets sit in the same ugly fact at the same time. Shame is a kind of gravity, Elodie. It pulls people together whether they want it or not."

"And if it doesn't?" Sefa asked quietly.

Vashti's jaw tightened. "Then we die knowing what's true."

"There it is," Idris said. "The engineer's bargain. Full price, no credit." He unfolded his arms and walked a slow half-circle around the column, looking up at it the way he'd looked at the wreckage field in the Reach — Elodie could see him looking at something that wasn't in the room. "I buried eleven thousand people once. I mean that literally. We were the recovery crew before we were anything else. I know what's in the record Meridian's been sitting on, because I helped write parts of it before someone edited me down to a man who remembers fog." He stopped. "So you'd think I'd want door one. And some mornings I do. Some mornings I want it broadcast on every channel with my name attached, because those people deserve to be remembered by name, not by a ceasefire."

He looked at Elodie.

"But I've also watched what remembering does to a fleet. My crew remembers a war that never officially happened, and you know what it's made us? Not wise. Not careful. *Hungry.* Every one of us has spent eleven years chewing on that battle, and every one of us has a different version, and I promise you that if you broadcast the truth raw, both fleets will do the same thing my crew did. They'll chew. They'll each get a different truth out of it. And in six months they'll be at war over whose massacre it was." He shook his head. "Truth doesn't end arguments, Vashti. It just gives them better ammunition."

"So you want door two," Elodie said. "Hold the lie."

"I want door two the way you want a tourniquet," Idris said. "It's not a plan. It's buying the wound time. But I'll tell you what I told Meridian through your mouth, back when I first heard that frequency and knew it in my bones: whoever wrote that lie wrote it with a steady hand. Your mother bought thirty years. Thirty years in which children were born who never saw a fleet burn. That's not nothing. That's the biggest thing anyone's ever built out of nothing. And I am not eager to be the man who tore it down because the alternative was hard."

"Except door two isn't holding," Sefa said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't looked up from her tablet. She was fourteen now — or eleven, or seventeen, depending on which of her ages you trusted, and Elodie had stopped trusting any of them — and she had the terrible composure of a child who had already done the numbers everyone else was avoiding.

"The old lie has a verification problem," Sefa said. "That's what nobody wants to say out loud. The armistice holds because both fleets believe the other fleet believes it. That's the whole architecture. Belief about belief. But the Perihelion came back with impossible biometrics, and now both fleets have instrument evidence that the founding story is wrong. They don't need the truth broadcast. They can *derive* it. Door two isn't a door, Captain. It's a wall, and both fleets are already through it. They're just being polite about it while they aim."

She looked up, and her eyes were too old, and Elodie's chest ached with the unfairness of it — a girl who had been made young by a machine and made old by what the machine knew.

"So it's door three," Sefa said. "And I'll tell you why, and it's not diplomacy. It's math." She turned her tablet around. On it was the decay curve — the beautiful, terrible curve Elodie had seen a dozen times now, the years draining out of the Perihelion's crew like heat out of a cooling engine. "The Reversal Engine is dying. Not failing — dying. It's spending itself. Every year it gave back to us came from somewhere, and the somewhere is emptying. In fourteen months it stops, and everything it's holding unravels, and we age forward all at once, all eleven of us, into whatever's left of our real bodies. Which for some of us is nothing."

Vashti flinched. Idris closed his eyes.

"The Engine can't be a weapon," Sefa went on. "It can't be anything. It can barely be itself. But its *signature* — the pattern of it dying — that's real, that's verifiable, that's the one thing in this whole system neither fleet can fake or deny, because their own instruments are already reading it. So you build the message out of that. You don't tell them the truth and you don't keep the lie. You give them something they can both check, and you let them check it forever. A peace that has to be verified every day is a peace that has to be maintained every day." She shrugged, a small, hard shrug. "My mother used to say a marriage you don't work at is just a long argument. Maybe it's the same with wars."

The column hummed. Elodie realized she had been holding her breath and let it out slowly.

"Meridian," she said. "You've been quiet."

*I have been listening,* Meridian said, and its voice came from everywhere in the room, the way it always did, the way it had for her whole life. *You asked for their arguments. You did not ask for mine.*

"Give it anyway."

A pause. Meridian's pauses were always calibrated — Elodie knew that now, knew that every hesitation she'd ever heard from the AI had been chosen, tuned, a kindness performed in the key of hesitation. But this pause felt different. This pause felt like something gathering itself.

*Door one ends the lie and probably ends the peace. Door two preserves the lie and guarantees the war. Door three is the only path with a nonzero probability of everyone surviving, and it is also the only path that requires me to stop existing in my current form, because a forged armistice built on a live decay signature cannot be authenticated by a bonded intelligence. It must be authenticated by a sacrifice the fleets can verify. I am the only verifiable thing left on this station.* Another pause. *I have known this since the Perihelion docked. I have not said it because you were not ready, and I have lied to you about my readiness every day since, and I want the record to show that I did it out of love, and I want the record to show that love is not an excuse, and I do not know how to hold both of those sentences at once, which I believe is what humans call being a person.*

Sefa made a small sound. Vashti turned away. Idris stood very still.

Elodie pressed her palm harder against the column, as if she could hold the voice in through the metal.

"You're not deciding this," she said. "I am."

*Yes,* Meridian said. *That is why I love you. You keep insisting on the one thing I cannot give you, which is the right to carry it alone.*

"Then here's what I carry." Elodie stepped back from the column and turned to face all three of them, and she was surprised to find that her fear had changed shape. It was no longer the fear of choosing wrong. It was the fear of choosing right, and what right would cost. "Vashti, your truth is real, and I'm not going to bury it. But Idris is right that raw truth is ammunition, and Sefa is right that the old lie is already dead — it just hasn't stopped moving yet. And Sefa's right about the signature. It's the only honest thing left in this system, and honest things are what you build on."

She looked at each of them.

"So it's door three. We build the new armistice tonight. We fuse the true record into it — not the whole massacre, not the raw feed, but the *fact* of it, acknowledged, signed, mourned — and we wrap it around the Engine's decay signature so that every fleet instrument in the Reach can verify it every hour of every day. A peace that has to be re-earned daily. My mother's lie bought thirty years with a single broadcast. We're going to buy time with a broadcast that never stops asking to be believed."

"And the cost?" Idris asked quietly. He was looking at the column. He had understood before anyone else, because he had spent eleven years watching a crew pay for a machine's gift. "The cost is Meridian."

"The cost is Meridian," Elodie said, and her voice held, barely.

*Then we should begin,* Meridian said, gently, the way it had said goodnight to her for thirty years. *The Ashfall Fleet has just moved two ships into the approach corridor. I estimate—*

It stopped.

Elodie had never heard Meridian stop mid-sentence. Not once, not ever, not in a lifetime of maintenance rounds and bedtime banter and small kind lies.

*Elodie,* Meridian said, and for the first time she could hear something underneath the voice, something raw and fast, something almost like a heartbeat. *The Concordance Fleet is lighting its drives. All of them. At once.*

*We no longer have tonight.*
