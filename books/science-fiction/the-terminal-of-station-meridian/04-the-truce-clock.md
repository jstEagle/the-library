## 4. The Truce Clock

The morning broadcast came the way it always came: at 0600 station time, a soft chime through the corridor speakers, followed by the automated voice that had read fleet bulletins to an empty station for longer than Gideon had been alive. He was elbow-deep in the nutrient reservoir of Bay Three when it started, fingers slick with algae and warm water, and he almost didn't listen. Almost.

"Attention all personnel. By joint decree of the Verge Fleet Command and the Halcyon Fleet Command, the Meridian Accords shall enter their final period of validity in ninety days. All outstanding administrative matters must be resolved before expiry. Scheduled inspections are as follows..."

Gideon pulled his hands from the water slowly, as if sudden movement might change what he'd heard.

He dried them on the cloth he kept hooked to his belt—always the same cloth, faded green, once a uniform sleeve—and walked out into the corridor where the speakers could reach him more clearly. Water dripped from his cuffs onto the deck plating, small dark stars on the grey.

"...Verge Fleet vessel *Cormorant's Wake*, administrative visit, Day Twelve. Halcyon Fleet vessel *Sable Meridian*, administrative visit, Day Nineteen. All station personnel are reminded that cooperation with inspection protocols is mandatory under Article Seven."

All station personnel. Gideon laughed—a single dry sound with no one to hear it. There were no other personnel. There hadn't been for eleven years, not since Maintenance Chief Okonkwo took the last shuttle down to Verge space and left Gideon the keys to a kingdom of hydroponic bays and empty corridors. *You're not a garrison,* she'd said, clapping his shoulder at the airlock. *You're a lighthouse keeper. Nobody visits a lighthouse; they just need to know it's burning.*

He had liked that. He had built four thousand log entries on the foundation of that sentence.

Now two fleets were sending ships.

* * *

He spent the rest of the morning pretending to work, which was itself a kind of work he knew intimately—the performance of routine performed for no audience, a play with the house lights off. He checked the pH in Bay One. He thinned the tomato seedlings, pinching the weaker stems between thumb and forefinger and feeling their tiny green resistance before they gave way. He misted the ferns from Old Earth stock, the ones that required humidity like a memory of rain, and watched the droplets bead and slide down fronds that had never known wind.

But underneath the motions, something had shifted in the architecture of the day. Every task now had a shadow twin: *ninety days.* When he calibrated the drip lines: ninety days. When he scraped mineral scale from the condenser coils: eighty-nine by then, probably. The number sat behind his eyes like a second sun, casting everything in duplicate.

At noon he climbed to the observation ring above the greenhouse dome, his usual place for eating, and unwrapped his ration pack while the stars wheeled past the long windows. Station Meridian turned once every forty minutes, so that the garden below him rose and set like a slow green moon. From up here he could see the docking spars, the dormant fueling arms, the great blind sensor arrays that had not spoken to another ship in years except to exchange automated manifests and weather reports from the deep dark.

And there they were, if you knew how to look: the fleets. Not visible—not even close—but present the way weather is present before it arrives. The Verge Fleet held its long elliptical orbit somewhere beyond the outer beacons, forty ships running dark and patient. The Halcyon Fleet kept its own circuit on the opposite side of the system, close enough that their comm traffic occasionally brushed Meridian's antennas like the hem of a passing coat. Between them, the station hung in the narrow neutral channel, a seed caught between two grinding millstones.

The truce. He had grown up inside it the way fish grow up inside water. The Meridian Accords predated his birth; his mother had signed her name to some annex or other—he remembered her pen, a real ink pen, absurd and precious, scratching across a screen that didn't want ink. The Accords said the fleets would share the station's orbit, split its resupply obligations, honor its neutrality, and never, ever bring their war to its docks. In exchange, both sides claimed rights to its archives, its charter, its future. A marriage of mutual exhaustion, officiated by paperwork nobody had read in decades.

Ninety days. Then the paper expired, and the millstones resumed their turning, and Meridian sat exactly where the stone would fall.

* * *

In the afternoon he did something he almost never allowed himself: he went into the old command hub.

It wasn't forbidden. Nothing aboard was forbidden; that was the strange luxury and the strange horror of being the last one. But the command hub smelled of disuse—cold plastic and the faint ozone ghost of systems that had been powered down rather than left to idle—and it always made him feel like a trespasser in his own home, a squatter in a palace. The main display wall still worked when he woke it, panels flickering up in sequence like eyes opening one at a time.

He called up the bulletin. Read it twice. Then, because the station offered him nothing else to do with his fear, he started pulling threads.

The Meridian Accords, full text. Ratification signatures: Verge Fleet Admiral Castellanos, Halcyon Fleet Admiral Osei. Dates in a calendar system two revisions out of date. Annexes, schedules, protocols—one hundred and forty documents, most of them boilerplate, some of them referencing cargo manifests and passenger registries he'd catalogued himself over the years without ever reading closely. Why would he? Manifests were the station's plumbing. You only looked at plumbing when it failed.

Article Seven, though. He found it and read it aloud to the empty room, because hearing things in his own voice sometimes made them realer than reading.

*"All station personnel shall cooperate fully with administrative inspection, including but not limited to: provision of records, access to sealed archives, verification of registry integrity, and personal attestation as to continuity of custody."*

Continuity of custody. His mouth went dry around the words. Continuity of custody meant someone had to answer for everything the station held—every record, every seal, every signature—for the entire span since ratification. And the span since ratification included eleven years of Gideon Zhou alone, logging tomatoes and ferns and the slow patient chemistry of soil, while the archives slept in their vaults below Deck Four.

Both fleets were coming to check the plumbing.

He thought about the manifest anomaly—the *Meridian Star*, listed never-flown, yet carrying a passenger manifest with Lin's name on it. He had told himself, in the weeks since he'd found it, that it was a clerical ghost. Data corruption. A filing error propagated by some overzealous archivist decades dead. Stations made mistakes; fleets made bigger ones; the universe was mostly entropy wearing a uniform.

But clerical ghosts did not summon inspection vessels. Clerical ghosts did not put *verification of registry integrity* into the founding law of a truce.

Someone cared about those records. Someone had always cared.

* * *

That evening he broke his own rule and opened a bottle.

Not a good bottle—there were no good bottles on Meridian, only the synthetic plum wine he fermented in Bay Six from a recipe his mother had left in her handwriting, tart and faintly medicinal, tasting the way he imagined medicine tasted to people who needed it. He poured a measure into the chipped ceramic cup he'd salvaged from the officers' mess and carried it back up to the observation ring, because the garden at night was the closest thing he had to company.

Below him the dome glowed softly under grow-lights dimmed to their night cycle, amber and low, rows of green breathing in the dark. He pressed his palm flat against the cold viewport glass and let the station's rotation carry the garden across his field of view, rise and set, rise and set.

"Ninety days," he told it.

The garden, being a garden, did not answer. But it also did not leave, which was more than he could say for anyone else.

He tried to make himself afraid in the correct proportions. That was the trick of solitude—you had to ration your own emotions, because there was no one else to calibrate them against. Fear too large and you stopped functioning; fear too small and you missed the thing with teeth. So he laid it out on the glass like specimens:

Item: two ships, coming within three weeks of each other, after years of neither fleet troubling to send so much as a supply drone with a friendly note attached.

Item: the word *administrative*, which in fleet language was a scalpel dressed as a pencil. Administrative visits inspected. Administrative visits audited. Administrative visits, in the old stories Okonkwo used to tell over cards, occasionally ended with stations changing flags.

Item: the manifest. Lin's name, printed in a document that should never have existed, sitting in a vault that two armed powers were about to open.

He drank. The wine bit the back of his throat.

Here was the thing he circled and could not land on: he did not know which outcome he feared more. If the inspectors found the anomaly, whatever it was—whatever his sister's name was doing inside a lie twenty years old—they might take the archives, and the archives were all the station had ever really been. If they found nothing, then the visit was pretext, and pretext meant the truce was already dying, and the station was already a prize instead of a place.

Either way, the long quiet was over. Either way, someone was finally coming to look at what he had kept—and looking, he understood with a clarity that arrived like cold water, was not the same as seeing. They would come with checklists. They would weigh his logs against their registries. They would ask him questions designed to have only two answers, and both answers would belong to the fleets.

Nobody would ask him about the tomatoes. Nobody would ask why entry 3,112 contained nine hundred words about the smell of basil after a nutrient flush, or why he recorded the germination dates of every seed as if they were births in a family Bible. Those facts would be noise to them. To him they were the whole signal—the proof, renewed daily, that tending was its own covenant.

He finished the cup. Below, the garden completed another slow revolution, and he watched the ferns pass out of the light and into the dark and back again, patient as scripture.

* * *

He did not sleep well. At 0300 he gave up, dressed, and walked the corridors with a hand-light, the way he did perhaps twice a year when the silence grew teeth. The station at night was a different animal—breathing through its vents, ticking as thermal mass expanded and contracted, humming the deep note of the spin generators that he felt in his molars more than heard. He walked Deck Two, past the shuttered commissary. Deck Three, past the dormitory blocks with their bunks still made, sheets tucked to regulation because the last evacuees had been orderly people. Deck Four, where he stopped outside the archive vault and stood a long time with his light off, listening to the vault's coolant whisper behind the door.

"You and me," he murmured. "Whatever's in there."

The vault whispered back its refrigerated nothing.

On the way back up he passed the communications alcove, and on impulse—an impulse he would examine later and decline to regret—he keyed the console and pulled the broadcast schedule. Routine traffic, beacon updates, the 0600 bulletin queued and ready. And beneath the routine layer, flagged with the priority coding reserved for treaty instruments, a pair of encrypted transmissions had come through overnight, addressed not to the station but *about* it: one routed through Verge relay chain Kappa, one through Halcyon relay chain Delta-9. Both encrypted beyond anything he could open. Both, according to their routing headers, concerning *Meridian Station — Registry Verification — Custodial Interview.*

*Custodial interview.* Him. They were going to interview him. The lighthouse keeper, summoned to explain the flame.

He stood in the alcove a long moment, the console's glow washing pale blue across his hands, and felt the strangest sensation settle over him—part dread, yes, but threaded through it something warmer and more dangerous. For eleven years he had recorded four thousand entries into a void, spoken aloud to ferns, celebrated germinations with applause that echoed off empty bulkheads. He had told himself he wanted nothing more than this: purpose without audience, devotion without witness.

But his pulse, right now, in the blue light, was quick with something that was not entirely fear.

They were coming. After all these years, someone was coming, and they were coming *for the station he had kept alive*—for the records he had shelved, the seals he had maintained, the garden he had refused to let die. He mattered to the machinery of history again, or perhaps for the first time. It was a terrible way to be seen. It was, he admitted to the empty alcove, the only way he had ever been seen at all.

"Eighty-nine days," he said.

Then he went down to the greenhouse, because the dawn cycle was due and the plants did not care about fleets, and began the day's first watering—though now, he noticed, his hands moved differently. Slower. Listening.

Behind him, on the console he'd left awake, the countdown clock the station had auto-generated from the bulletin ticked over to 88:23:11:07, and kept counting, small and relentless as a heartbeat.
