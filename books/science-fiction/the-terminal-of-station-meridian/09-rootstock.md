## 9. Rootstock

The grafting knife had belonged to my mother. That was the first thing I thought of when I took it from its felt sleeve that morning—before the weight of it settled into my palm, before the smell of the greenhouse came up around me like a tide, before I remembered that everything I touched now carried a second meaning underneath its first.

It was a simple tool. Carbon-steel blade worn to a crescent by forty years of use, a handle wrapped in tape so old it had fossilized into something like bone. She'd brought it up from Earth in a padded case sewn into the lining of her coat, or so my father told me once, in one of his rare unguarded sentences. *Your mother smuggled a knife onto a station where every gram was accounted for,* he said, and laughed, and then stopped laughing, because that was how my father talked about her: a sentence begun as joy and ended as grief, with no seam between them.

I set the knife on the potting bench beside a tray of cuttings and did not pick it up again for a long time.

* * *

The greenhouse at 0600 is the best hour Meridian has to offer. The lamps cycle down to their dawn spectrum—amber bleeding into rose—and the condensation on the inner canopy catches the light and lets it fall again in slow, fat drops that tick against the leaves below like a clock made of water. The air is thick enough to taste: wet loam, tomato vine, the green-metallic bite of crushed basil where yesterday's harvest brush had bruised a stem. Out beyond the glass, the station's spine curves away into dark, and beyond that, the slow wheeling of the two fleets in their distant orbits, each one pretending the other isn't there.

I had grown up inside this hour. I knew its sounds the way other people know their mother's heartbeat—which, I realized, standing there with sap drying tacky on my knuckles, was exactly what it had been. My mother's heartbeat and this greenhouse were the same sound. The irrigation pumps cycling at intervals tuned to her breathing. The fans she'd balanced by hand until their hum sat just below hearing. Even the drip rate of the condensate channels—she had adjusted those too, I understood now, adjusted everything, so that the whole room pulsed at the tempo of a resting body. She hadn't built a garden. She'd built a chest, and filled it with living things, and taught it to breathe.

And then she'd left me inside it.

I picked up the knife.

* * *

Grafting is a violence you perform gently. You cut a scion—a young shoot, healthy, full of intention—from one plant, and you cut a matching wound into the rootstock of another, and you press the two raw faces together and bind them until they heal into a single organism. Done well, neither plant remembers being two. The scion keeps its fruit; the rootstock keeps its grip on the soil. What grows afterward belongs to both and neither.

My mother taught me when I was seven. Her hands over mine, the blade guided through green stem with a sound like a whispered word. *You have to match the cambium,* she said. *The living layer. Bark doesn't heal. Only the part that's alive can knit.* I had nodded, seven years old, thinking only of tomatoes.

Now I stood at the bench with a tray of scions cut from the old citrus stock—her stock, grown from seeds she'd carried in that same coat—and a row of hardy hybrid rootstocks bred for low light and thin patience, and I understood that she had been teaching me something else entirely. Something she couldn't say out loud on a station where every corridor listened.

Match the cambium. Only the living layer can knit.

She had grafted forty families onto this station. That was what the manifests meant, once I let myself see it plainly instead of flinching away. Forty families who never flew, listed as passengers aboard a ship that never launched, entered into the legal marrow of Meridian's charter with a forger's precision and a mother's handwriting. And the fleets—the Verge and Halcyon both, sworn enemies circling each other like weather systems—had spent twenty years honoring a truce built on top of those names, because the charter bound them both. Obligated them both. My mother had cut two wounds and pressed them together and bound them, and the fruit had been peace, and the roots had been guilt, and no one—not the fleets, not the arbitrators, not her own son—had known which plant was which.

I made the first cut before I noticed I'd decided to.

* * *

Here is what resentment feels like when it lives in the same body as love: it feels like grafting. Two tissues pressed together, joined at the living layer, growing as one organism that cannot be separated without killing both.

I resented her. I want to write that plainly, because I spent twenty years refusing to, and refusal is its own kind of rot. I resented her for staying behind on Kestrel's Landing while the evacuation lists burned. I resented her for sending me up here with my father and a packet of seeds and a letter I wasn't allowed to read until I was grown—a letter I still hadn't read, because grown is a door you can stand in front of without opening. I resented her for dying in a place I couldn't reach, for a cause I didn't understand, leaving me a garden and a knife and a silence shaped exactly like her.

And now I resented her for this—for the forgery, for the forty families, for the legal architecture of obligation she'd hidden inside passenger manifests like a seed inside a seed coat. Because none of it had been mine to inherit honestly. Every log entry I'd recorded for colonists who would never come—I'd been tending her fiction. Four thousand entries. Four thousand mornings of speaking aloud to an empty room, believing the emptiness was grief, when it was actually design. She had needed someone to keep the garden alive, yes. But she had also needed someone to keep the *story* alive—the fiction of coming colonists, the fiction of Meridian's purpose—because a lie about passengers only holds if the station itself behaves as if it expects people. I was not the keeper of a garden. I was the cambium. The living layer between her cut and the fleets' wound, knitting them together year after year without knowing it.

I set down the knife and pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes until I saw constellations.

* * *

But here is the other tissue. Here is the pride, and I could no more cut it out than the resentment.

Forty families. I kept arriving back at that number the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Forty families on a world going dark—Kestrel's Landing in its last decade, the atmosphere processors failing one by one, the evacuation quotas carved up by fleet politics while people queued in ash-fall to be counted and refused. I'd been a child; I remembered the queues. I remembered my mother's hands on my shoulders, turning me toward the shuttle, and the way she smiled, and how the smile didn't reach anything above her cheekbones.

She couldn't save everyone. No one could. The arithmetic of that decade was written in bodies. But she was a records clerk—junior, invisible, trusted with manifests because no one imagines a manifest is a weapon—and she looked at the machinery of the truce, the mutual obligation clauses, the charter language both fleets had signed to avoid war over a station neither could hold alone, and she saw a cambium layer. A place where two living systems could be joined.

So she invented passengers. Not randomly—never randomly. Each forged name matched a real family left behind: their surnames, their patronymics, their children's ages slotted into the manifest with a clerk's exactness. If the ships ever came, if anyone ever checked, the lie would resolve into truth one generation downstream—because the descendants of those forty families existed, scattered across both fleets as refugees' children, and a name on an old manifest would find its echo in a living face. She hadn't just forged documents. She'd planted a grove. She'd grafted the abandoned onto the legal body of Meridian and bound it tight and walked away from the bench, trusting the healing to time.

And it held. Twenty years of truce. Twenty years in which the Verge and Halcyon fleets, who had come to this system to fight over a terraforming station, instead circled it in armed politeness, each unable to claim it without voiding obligations to the other, each unwilling to test what the other might do. Peace, sustained not by treaties or deterrence but by a junior clerk's steady hand and a stack of paper that never flew.

I should have been proud. I was proud. The pride sat in my chest like heat from the grow-lamps, real and nourishing and impossible to switch off.

And underneath it, feeding on the same light: *she didn't tell me.* She died—no. She stayed. She stayed and was lost with the rest of Kestrel's Landing, and the last thing she gave me was a garden and a duty and a silence, and now the silence had opened up into this, and I was alone on a station with two fleets converging on me and a knife in my hand that had always been a key.

* * *

I worked. It's what the hands are for, when the mind has gone somewhere the body can't follow. Scion after scion: cut, match, seat, bind. The citrus went onto the hybrid stock in pairs, six grafts, twelve, eighteen. The greenhouse sounds closed around me—pumps, fans, the water-clock of the condensate—and I let them hold me the way they'd held me since childhood, at her tempo, at her resting heart rate, and I tried to think the thing through to its bottom.

Because there was a question under all of it, and I had been avoiding it the way you avoid a loose panel in zero-g, knowing what's rattling behind it.

*Did she mean for me to find out?*

The letter I hadn't read. The garden she'd asked me to keep. The timing of it—if the truce simply held forever, the forgery would sleep forever, and I would die a gentle madman talking to tomatoes about colonists who never came. But truces don't hold forever. Truces have expiry dates, and she was a records clerk; she knew better than anyone how documents age, how clauses mature, how the fine print comes due. Ninety days from now, the obligation she wrote into Meridian's bones reaches its term. Both fleets will arrive to collect.

She must have known. She must have done the same arithmetic I was doing now, sitting in the dark of a dying world with a pen in her hand. Somewhere in her letters—letters I had kept sealed for twenty years because opening them felt like exhuming her—somewhere in there was the rest of the graft. The aftercare instructions. What to do when the binding came off.

I finished the last graft—citrus scion number thirty-one, seated against its rootstock, bound in green tape, already looking like it had always been one plant—and I cleaned the knife and slid it back into its felt sleeve, and I stood in the amber light with my hands smelling of sap and steel.

I was angry at her. I would probably be angry at her for the rest of my life, in the way you're angry at weather, at gravity, at things that hold you up whether you consent to or not. She had saved forty families and burdened one son, and both of those facts were true, and they grew from the same cut, and only the living layer could knit.

But I was the living layer. That was the whole of it. That had always been the whole of it.

I crossed the greenhouse to the storage locker where the personal effects lived—the small archive of a dead world I kept the way other people keep ashes—and I took down the box with my father's handwriting on the lid, and inside it, wrapped in the same padded cloth as the knife had traveled in, the letters. Her letters. Unopened. The paper gone soft and tea-colored at the edges, smelling faintly of Kestrel's Landing dust even after all these years, as if the world she'd saved others from had seeped into the fibers and refused to leave.

I held the bundle in both hands. Through the greenhouse glass, far off in the black, a running light blinked twice—the Verge Fleet's inspection tender, still parked at docking ring three, Captain Oyelaran's polite, patient, measuring presence folded inside it like a blade in a sleeve. And beyond her orbit, somewhere past the station's shadow, the Halcyon ships holding their own silent count.

Ninety days. Less, now.

I sat down on the potting bench stool, in the breathing heart of my mother's garden, and I broke the seal on the first letter.

The handwriting inside was small and level and unhurried—the handwriting of a woman forging forty futures in the margins of her workday—and the first line read:

*Gideon. If you are reading this, the binding has come due, and I need you to understand why I cut.*
