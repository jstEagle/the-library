## 11. The Log Nobody Read

The greenhouse at night was a different country.

Gideon had learned this over twenty years of tending it, the way one learns the moods of a coastline—by repetition, by patience, by the slow accumulation of small betrayals and small mercies. By day, under the grow-lamps' full spectrum, the garden was work: rows to prune, nutrient lines to flush, humidity to argue with. But after the lamps dimmed to their amber sleep-cycle glow, when the only sound was the whisper of misters cycling through their programmed dreams, the garden became something else entirely. A held breath. A room where time pooled instead of flowing.

He walked the central aisle now with a mug of barley tea going cold in his hand, past the tomato vines heavy with fruit no one would eat, past the dwarf citrus he'd coaxed into bearing for eleven consecutive seasons, past the rice paddies that murmured under their shallow water like sleeping children. The station's night cycle pressed against the glass panels, and beyond them the two fleets hung in their distant orbits—Verge to port, Halcyon to starboard—like two wolves circling a carcass they hadn't yet decided was dead.

*Four thousand entries,* he thought. *And I've never once listened back.*

The realization had come to him an hour ago, absurdly, while he'd been cataloguing seed stock. He'd opened his personal archive to log a new inventory file and found himself staring at the directory tree of his own voice: four thousand daily recordings, stretching back twenty years, each one timestamped, each one addressed—to whom? To the colonists who never came. To the fleet administrators who never listened. To the station itself, maybe. To no one.

He had made four thousand entries and never played back a single one.

It seemed impossible, now that he thought about it. Twenty years of speaking into the dark without once turning around to look at what he'd said. But there it was: the playback counter on every file read zero. Not because the system didn't allow it. Because he had never wanted to hear the sound of his own hope, day after day, curdling into routine.

*Log entry 1:* he remembered it exactly. He'd been twenty-six years old, three weeks into a posting everyone called a punishment detail, standing in this same aisle with dirt under his fingernails and his mother's last letter folded in his breast pocket. "This is Gideon Zhou, terraforming officer, Station Meridian, day one," he'd said. "The garden is alive. The colonists are coming. I'll keep everything ready."

*The colonists are coming.* He'd believed it then. Somewhere around entry 400, belief had become habit. Around entry 1,500, habit had become liturgy. And somewhere—he couldn't pinpoint the day, and that frightened him more than anything—it had become performance. He wasn't recording for anyone anymore. He was recording because stopping would mean admitting what the recordings already knew.

He set down his tea on the potting bench and pulled up the archive interface on the bench terminal. The list scrolled past like a river seen from above: dates, durations, waveform thumbnails. Four thousand small acts of faithfulness to an audience of zero.

He selected entry 1,000.

His own younger voice filled the greenhouse, startling him—the timbre rounder, less weathered, carrying an optimism that felt almost obscene now.

"—day nine hundred ninety-four. Repaired the east irrigation manifold again; the seals keep failing and I've stopped ordering replacements because requisitions take eleven months and the failure takes eleven days. Tomatoes are producing well. If the colonists arrive next quarter as scheduled, we'll have fresh food for the first harvest festival. I've been saving seeds from the best plants. Lin would have laughed at me for naming them."

Gideon flinched. He'd forgotten he used to say her name out loud.

Lin. Dead twenty years, and resurrected last week in a shipping manifest for a ship that never flew—a passenger aboard a vessel that existed only as ink, listed between strangers, her name sitting in official fleet records like a stone dropped into his chest that hadn't finished sinking.

He let the recording play. His younger self prattled on about pH balances and a broken door sensor and the quality of the light in Sector Four, and Gideon stood in the dark listening to a man who still expected to be relieved, still expected the docking bay to fill one morning with families and luggage and noise. Every entry ended the same way, he realized. Every single one, across twenty years, ended with some version of the same sentence:

*"Everything is ready."*

Everything is ready. Everything is ready. Everything is ready. Four thousand times. A prayer repeated so long it had worn a groove in him, the way water wears stone—not by force but by refusal to stop.

He advanced through the archive in jumps. Entry 2,000: the optimism thinner now, the technical reports longer, the colonists' arrival pushed to "next fiscal cycle" without comment, as if neither he nor the recording wished to look directly at it. Entry 3,000: his voice had gone flat, professional, a maintenance report wearing the costume of a letter. Entry 3,999, recorded yesterday: *"Day 3,999. All systems nominal. Garden at seventy-eight percent capacity. Everything is ready."*

Seventy-eight percent capacity. He'd been quietly letting sections go fallow. Even his body knew what his logs wouldn't say.

Gideon sat down slowly on the potting stool, its legs creaking against the deck plating, and looked at the waveform of yesterday's entry glowing on the screen—a small mountain range of sound, his whole day compressed into peaks and valleys, and not one peak tall enough to be laughter.

*These aren't logs,* he thought. *They're testimony.*

The word arrived with the weight of physical contact. Testimony. An archive of abandonment, recorded in real time by the only witness who stayed. No one had asked him for evidence. No court had subpoenaed his voice. But here it was regardless: twenty years of proof that a station had been kept alive, deliberately, lovingly, for people who were never sent—and that one man had refused, every single day, to let the lie of readiness die quietly. Every entry was a document. Every "everything is ready" was a signature on a contract no one else had signed.

His mother's forged manifests had created a legal fiction binding two fleets to Meridian. But his logs—his stupid, faithful, unread logs—had created something else. A moral record. Evidence of what abandonment actually looked like from inside, measured not in policy documents but in tomatoes grown and eaten alone, in seeds saved for festivals that never happened, in a man talking to empty air for seven thousand days because the alternative was silence, and silence would have meant the station had won.

He thought of Oyelaran's careful questions. Of Ashke's soldiers scanning his archives with hungry eyes. They wanted the manifests, the letters, the paper trail of the forgery. None of them had asked about the logs. To them, four thousand daily recordings were background noise—the hum of a caretaker, beneath notice.

But if the truce broke, if the fleets came to claim Meridian as salvage, those logs would say otherwise. They would say: *this place was tended. This place was promised. You were told, every day, in a voice you never bothered to hear.*

His hands were shaking slightly. He wrapped them around the cold mug and breathed the smell of wet soil and citrus oil, grounding himself in the garden's oldest argument: things grow anyway.

Then another thought arrived, quieter and colder, walking into the room behind the first one like a shadow.

If his own logs were testimony—if twenty years of his voice constituted evidence he'd never examined—then what else was sitting unexamined? What was in his mother's letters?

He had read them all as a child, or thought he had. She'd written forty-one letters from Kestrel's Landing in the final two years before the world went dark, and he'd carried them from posting to posting like relics, rereading favorites, skipping the hard ones. But he'd read them as a son, hungry for her voice, skimming the parts that hurt. He had never read them as an archivist. Never cross-referenced. Never asked what a woman forging passenger lists might hide inside letters to her boy.

And somewhere in that stack—entry 6 of chapter 10 of his own life, the part he'd bookmarked and never finished—was the letter she'd written in the last week, the one that arrived after the transmissions from Kestrel's Landing had gone intermittent, the one whose final page he'd never been able to make himself read. He knew how it began. He'd read the first paragraph a hundred times: *My Gideon, if you're reading this, the ships got out, and whatever happens next, the garden matters more than my words can—*

And then the page break. And then twenty years of not knowing.

He stood up. The greenhouse hummed around him, patient, green, alive. Out in the dark, the fleets turned their slow orbits, counting down their ninety days.

He crossed to the storage locker where he kept the few physical things he owned that weren't tools: a photograph, a dried flower from a world that no longer existed, and the letters, bundled in oilcloth, tied with cord he'd knotted when he was nineteen and had never untied.

His fingers worked the knot now. It resisted, then gave.

Forty-one letters. Forty pages he'd read and one he hadn't. He spread them across the potting bench in order, the paper soft as moth wings, his mother's handwriting slanting across each page like wind through wheat—the same slant, he saw now with an archivist's eye, the same distinctive leftward lean that marked the manifest entries he'd found in the sealed files. Her hand was all over the forgery. It had been all along.

He picked up the final letter. Unfolded it to the last page. Set it down again.

Not yet. Not in the dark, not with his hands shaking, not before he'd earned it. He would read the others first—all of them, properly, in order, the way an archivist reads: looking for what was hidden between the lines. He owed her that much rigor. He owed himself the whole story before he chose which side of it to stand on.

Because that was the choice taking shape in him, clear as transplanting season: the fleets were coming, both of them, each offering to make him a footnote in their version of events. Verge wanted the originals intact. Halcyon wanted them erased. Both wanted him compliant. And the only thing he had that neither of them controlled—not the manifests, not the vault, not the truce itself—was the record no one had ever thought to steal.

His voice. His garden. His four thousand days.

Gideon retied the cord loosely around the letters, leaving the bundle open, accessible, ready. Then he keyed the bench terminal and opened a blank file, and for the first time in twenty years, he recorded an entry addressed to someone specific.

"This is Gideon Zhou," he said, and his voice sounded strange to him—steadier than it had any right to be. "Entry four thousand and one. Correction: the previous four thousand entries were addressed to colonists who were never sent. This one is addressed to whoever is listening—which, as of tonight, I intend to include myself. Tomorrow I start reading my mother's letters from the beginning. All of them. The ones I skipped. The one I couldn't finish. Before either fleet reaches this station, I will know exactly what she did, and why, and what she asked of me. And then I'll decide what kind of keeper I am."

He paused. In the paddy trays, the rice seedlings stirred in the ventilation current, a field the size of a table, feeding no one, growing anyway.

"One more thing," he said. "To whoever forged my sister onto that manifest: I don't know who you are yet. But you used her name to open doors. Fine. I'm going to use these letters to open the truth. We'll see which of us the station remembers."

He stopped the recording. Entry 4,001. The first log in twenty years that wasn't a prayer to the absent.

It was a declaration of intent.

Outside the glass, far off in the black, a navigation light blinked on one of the Halcyon vessels—routine, probably, a course correction, nothing. Probably.

Gideon gathered the letters into his arms, turned off the bench terminal, and carried his mother's voice out of the greenhouse and down the corridor toward the light of his quarters, where forty-one letters waited to finish telling him what he had spent his whole life getting ready for.
