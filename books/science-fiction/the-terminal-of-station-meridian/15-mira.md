## 15. Mira

The credentials had been used again at 0347.

Gideon knew this because he had spent four nights sleeping in the archive antechamber, a cot wedged between server racks that breathed warm air against his back like some vast drowsy animal. He had learned the station's night sounds the way he'd learned the greenhouse's—the tick of coolant lines, the groan of solar panels tracking a sun no one else watched. And now he had learned a new sound: the soft chime of a door opening on credentials that belonged to a dead woman.

Lin Zhou. Dead twenty years. Walking Sector Nine at 0347.

He did not run this time. The first three nights, he had bolted from the cot and reached the corridor in time to see nothing but the amber afterglow of a door seal cycling shut, the faintest chemical trace on the air—something floral, something human, beneath the station's eternal smell of recycled atmosphere and nutrient solution. Tonight he lay still and let the chime pass through him like weather. Then he rose, dressed in the dark, and went to wait.

The vault corridor was a throat of grey composite, lit by strips that dimmed to near-darkness during sleep hours. Gideon stood in that darkness with his back against the cold wall and thought about his sister's hands. He was doing that more lately—not her face, which had softened into approximation over two decades, but her hands: quick, ink-stained from copying their mother's manifests, always cold because she refused to wear gloves even in the unheated sections where they'd played as children. *You have to know what things feel like,* she used to say. *Otherwise you're just guessing at the world.*

Someone down here had been guessing at her world for years.

At 0352, the far door chimed.

A figure entered the corridor, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had done this hundreds of times. Small—or medium, hard to tell in the dark—wearing a maintenance jumpsuit with the sleeves rolled, carrying a slate whose glow lit the lower half of a face from below, turning it into something mask-like. The figure stopped at the vault door, raised the slate, and Gideon stepped out of the darkness.

"Don't," he said quietly. "Please don't run. I've chased you three nights and I'm too tired to chase well."

The figure froze. The slate-light trembled slightly. In the silence that followed, Gideon became aware of breathing—his own, ragged; theirs, controlled but quick, a small animal's rhythm.

"You've been using my sister's name," he said. "Lin Zhou. For—I don't know how long. Years. You opened doors with her name while I was watering tomatoes."

"I can explain." The voice was younger than he expected. Female, low, steady in the way of someone who has rehearsed steadiness. "But not here. Not standing in a dark hallway like criminals."

"We're not criminals?"

The light shifted as the person lowered the slate, and for the first time he saw her whole face—young, mid-twenties perhaps, sharp-boned, with eyes so dark they seemed to hold the corridor's darkness rather than reflect it. There was a scar through one eyebrow, old and thin. She looked at him the way he imagined one might look at a locked door that had unexpectedly opened: wary, calculating, and underneath it all, hungry.

"No," she said. "We're not. But we're both going to wish we were, before this is over. Come on. Not the vault. Somewhere with plants. I want to see if it's real."

* * *

She walked ahead of him through the station's spine with the confidence of long habitation, and that alone unsettled him more than the credentials had. Every junction, every ladder, every shortcut through the service galleries—she took them without hesitation, without the map-flicker of an outsider. This stranger had lived inside Meridian's bones while he had lived inside its skin, and neither of them had known.

The greenhouse doors parted and exhaled their green breath—loam and chlorophyll and the mineral tang of the hydroponic feed—and Gideon watched her stop on the threshold as though she'd struck glass.

"Oh," she said softly.

The rows stretched away under the grow-lights, tier upon tier: tomato vines heavy and split-skinned, beans climbing their strings, the dwarf citrus trees he'd coaxed into fruiting three years ago, the beds of greens in their strict military ranks. It was not beautiful, exactly. It was tended. It was the most tended thing either of them had ever seen, probably.

"It's real," she said, almost accusingly. "I've passed this deck a hundred times. I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought it would be dead by now. Everything on this station is supposed to be dead."

"Everything except me and the garden," Gideon said. "We keep each other company."

She turned to look at him then, really looked, and something moved behind her eyes—recalculation, or recognition, or both. "Log entry four thousand," she said. "I listened to it. All of them, actually. I found the broadcast band your logs ride on when I was fifteen. I used to fall asleep to your voice talking about soil pH to nobody."

The floor seemed to tilt gently. Four thousand entries. Four thousand nights of speaking into a void, and the void had been listening. "You were here," he said. "All that time. On the same station."

"Same station. Different stations, really." She walked slowly down the central aisle, trailing her fingers above the bean leaves without touching them—a gardener's instinctive restraint, he noted, or a thief's caution. "You lived in the official Meridian. I lived in the other one. The one between the walls. My mother came up in a water shipment nineteen years ago. Stowaway. She had me aboard, which made me the first person born on Station Meridian, technically, though there's no record of me anywhere, so *technically* doesn't mean much here."

"What's your name?"

"Mira." She said it simply, without performance. "Just Mira. The rest changes depending on what the door requires."

"And Lin Zhou opens doors."

"Yes." She stopped at the citrus trees, and her reflection ghosted across the leaves. "Your sister's identity is the cleanest thing on this station. Do you understand what that means? A name with fleet clearance attached, a face in the biometric files, a history that checks out because nobody alive remembers checking it. For someone like me—with no name, no origin, no legal existence—your sister isn't a person. She's a key. I'm sorry. I know how that sounds."

"How does she open doors?" Gideon asked, and heard his own voice go flat and careful, grief compressing itself into procedure. "She died twenty years ago on Kestrel's Landing. Her records should have been sealed with everything else from that world."

Mira turned. The grow-lights fell full on her face now, and he saw that she was afraid—not of him, he realized, but of something larger, something she'd been carrying alone for a long time.

"That's the part you're not going to like," she said. "Sit down, Zhou. There's a stool by the nutrient tanks. This takes a while, and you'll want to be sitting."

He sat. Above them, the lights hummed their endless artificial noon.

"The woman who built your sister's new identity wasn't random," Mira began. "She was careful. She chose a name from the manifest—the forged manifest, the one your mother helped write—because a name already inside the system needs less forging. Someone in the archives, decades ago, started maintaining certain identities. Ghosts with clean paperwork. Refugees' children who needed to exist. People who owed the forgery their lives kept it running after the forgers died. It's called the Ledger. It's not a conspiracy, exactly. It's more like—" she searched for the word, fingers moving restlessly, "—a relay. Each runner passes the flame without ever seeing the whole fire."

"Who taught you?"

"My mother. Who was taught by hers. We're all daughters and sons of the same lie, Zhou. That's what nobody wants said out loud: half the people alive in these fleets exist because of paperwork crimes committed out of love. Your mother invented passengers. My mother was one of the invented ones who turned out to be real." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, bright and bitter. "The lie got so good at making people that it started actually making them."

Gideon looked at his hands. They were trembling slightly, and he pressed them flat against his knees. "My sister's name. You've been wearing it. Eating with it. Sleeping in it. While I—" His voice caught. He hated that it caught. "While I recorded four thousand logs about missing her."

"I know." Mira crouched down in front of him, level with his eyes, and he saw that her fear had burned off and left something rawer. "And I need you to hear this, because I've only ever said it to the dark: I didn't take her from you. I kept her. Do you know what happens to names nobody uses? They get purged. Records decay. Clearance lapses. Every year I've worn Lin Zhou, I've renewed her certifications, updated her biometric profile, paid her dues, answered her mail. I have kept your sister more alive than anyone alive has kept anything on this station. Including you and your tomatoes." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she didn't try to hide it. "She was the closest thing I had to a self. Do you know what it's like to be nobody? To walk through a checkpoint and feel yourself not existing? When I put on her name, I existed. I could knock on doors. Doors opened. I'm not proud of it. I'm also not sorry. Both of those are true and I've stopped trying to make them fit together."

The greenhouse hummed around them. Somewhere in the tiers, a drip valve cycled, counting out its patient seconds.

"She would have liked you," Gideon said finally, and was surprised to find it was true. "She was always cold. Refused gloves. She'd have understood keeping a flame alive in the walls."

Mira blinked fast, twice. "That's the cruelest kind thing anyone's ever said to me."

"There's more you're not telling me."

"There's a lot I'm not telling you." She stood, and the wariness returned, sliding back over her like a visor. "But there's one thing you need tonight, because time's short and you strike me as a man who'd rather have a bad truth early than a good lie late. The sabotage in Sector Nine. The vault damage. Everyone thinks it was me using Lin's credentials."

"Wasn't it?"

"No." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "My credentials were copied. Three weeks ago, during a routine systems check I ran under her name, someone skimmed them. I noticed the duplicate access two days before the explosion. I stayed away from the vault afterward—which is why I look guilty, incidentally, absence being the most damning evidence there is. But I didn't do it. And whoever did wanted it to look like the ghost girl did it. Which means someone knows about the Ledger. Someone outside it. Someone who wants the truce broken badly enough to burn the proof and frame a dead woman for it."

Gideon felt the cold arrive in his chest, the same cold he'd felt reading the manifest, the station suddenly vast and watchful around him. "Oyelaran suspects me. Ashke wants the archive. And now there's a third player burning evidence."

"Welcome to the family business," Mira said. "Everyone lies to everyone, and the truth is the only weapon nobody can afford to fire."

"Why tell me any of this? You've survived years down here by telling no one anything."

She looked at him for a long moment. The grow-lights buzzed. Beyond the glass, the stars wheeled in their indifferent slow arc, and somewhere out there, two fleets were counting down ninety days toward each other.

"Because you talked to the dark for four thousand nights," she said, "and the dark turned out to be me. Because when I was fifteen and invisible, your voice was the only one on this station that sounded like it was talking to a person instead of a protocol. Because—" she stopped, jaw tightening, and finished in a smaller voice: "because I'm tired, Zhou. Being nobody is exhausting. Being somebody's ghost is worse. I want to hand the name back before whatever's coming arrives. I want to watch it happen from inside a room instead of inside a wall."

Gideon stood. Up close, he could see how young she was, and how old—two ages living in the same face, the way they must live in every child the lie had made.

"Then here's what happens next," he said. "In nine days, Oyelaran and Ashke will both be aboard. I'm going to put them in this greenhouse, and I'm going to show them everything—the manifest, the letters, the Ledger, all of it. And you're going to stand next to me when I do."

"They'll arrest you. Or worse."

"Probably." He allowed himself a thin smile. "But I've spent twenty years tending a garden for people who never came. I'm done waiting for permission to matter. Are you?"

Mira looked past him at the rows of green, at the impossible living abundance of a tomb, and when she answered, her voice was steady in a way that no longer sounded rehearsed.

"Teach me to graft tomorrow," she said. "If we're going to burn the whole system down, I want to know how to keep something alive first."

They shook on it—her hand cold, his ink-stained now too, two children of the same lie finding each other in the only room on the station that told the truth.

Neither of them saw the maintenance camera above the door, its indicator light burning a small, patient red.
