## 16. Ninety Days, Spent

The greenhouse had never held four people before. Gideon had done the arithmetic of it in the days before, standing among the tomato rows and imagining the geometry: the central aisle was two meters wide, the benches left a shoulder's breadth on either side, and the air recyclers hummed at a volume calibrated for one set of lungs, not four. He had imagined Oyelaran standing with her hands clasped behind her back, the way she did when she was pretending to admire something. He had imagined Ashke filling a doorway. He had imagined Mira at the edge of things, half in shadow, the way she had existed in the station's margins for years without anyone noticing.

What he had not imagined was how the garden would look at them. The grow-lamps turned everyone the same amber, the same color as the wheat he'd harvested for log entry 3,991, the same color as the light in his mother's letters when he read them at night. Under that light, a captain and a commander and a thief all looked like what they were underneath: people who had come a long way to stand in a room full of living things and be judged by them.

He had set the table himself—two crates laid across trestles, covered with a white cloth he'd found in the old commissary stores, starched and yellowed and folded by someone who had expected colonists. On it: a carafe of water, four cups, and the archive.

The archive was a slate, unremarkable, gray as ballast. It held everything. Forty families invented out of thin air and ink. A ship that never flew. A sister who died twice—once in the atmosphere of a burning world, once on paper, resurrected as a passenger so the lie could breathe. His mother's handwriting, looping and patient, forging salvation one name at a time.

He had spent the morning walking the rows, touching leaves, saying nothing to his recorder. Log entry 4,093 had gone out at dawn as always, into the silence that always swallowed it. *Today I have guests,* he'd told the empty channel. *Today I tell the truth to people who may not want it.* It felt like the first honest entry in four thousand.

They arrived within eleven minutes of one another, which Gideon understood to be a kind of choreography. No one wanted to be first. No one wanted to be last.

Oyelaran came through the outer lock in civilian clothes—a plain gray jacket, no rank braid, though she wore her sidearm the way other people wore a wedding ring, without thinking about it. Ashke came with two escorts and dismissed them at the door with a gesture that managed to be both generous and contemptuous, as if even his own soldiers were a concession he was making. Mira came last, through the service corridor she'd been using for years, and Gideon watched Oyelaran's eyes track her and file her away, and watched Ashke pretend he hadn't noticed her at all, which was its own kind of filing.

"Zhou." Ashke looked around the greenhouse the way a man looks at a territory he intends to acquire. "You said you had something to show us. I cleared six hours of my schedule for a garden tour."

"You cleared six hours because you think this station is already yours," Gideon said. "Sit down. Please."

The *please* cost him something. He offered water first, because his mother would have, because the ritual of pouring gave his hands something to do while the three of them arranged themselves—Oyelaran on the crate-bench with her spine straight and her cup untouched, Ashke standing until standing became its own statement and then sitting anyway, Mira perched on the far end, knees drawn up, a girl at a table of adults.

"Before anything else," Gideon said, "I want to say what this is. This is not a negotiation. I'm not selling anything, and I'm not choosing sides. In nine days the truce expires, and both your fleets are going to arrive here with your weapons hot, and you're going to do that because you each believe the other is about to break the truce first. I know why you believe that. I know what the truce is actually made of. And I think you deserve to hear it from me, in this room, before you hear it in the muzzle flash."

"Poetic," Ashke said. "Get on with it."

So he got on with it. He told them about the Meridian Star, listed in the fleet registries as never flown, carrying a passenger manifest of two hundred and six souls. He told them about the forty families who had never existed on any census, invented in a basement office on a world that was coming apart, given names and ages and medical histories so that Meridian's charter would bind both fleets to its protection. He told them about the handwriting—his mother's handwriting—and watched Oyelaran's face when he said it, watched the small stillness there that meant the Verge Fleet had suspected and had never been sure.

"You're telling us," Ashke said slowly, "that the founding obligation of this station is a forgery."

"I'm telling you the founding obligation of this station is a lie that kept two fleets from shooting each other for thirty years."

"And you kept it." Oyelaran's voice was quiet. "You found it, and you kept it, and you waited. Why?"

"Because I didn't understand it yet." He looked at her. "And because I wanted to know if either of you would kill me for it. Now I do know. Commander Ashke offered me sanctuary in exchange for handing it over so Halcyon could void the truce and claim the station before anyone else saw the originals. Captain Oyelaran offered me protection in exchange for keeping them intact—intact and private, which is another word for the same thing. You both wanted the archive. Neither of you wanted the truth. So here it is, unpriced."

The silence that followed had weight. The air recyclers hummed through it. Somewhere in the canopy above them, a drip valve cycled, and the sound of water falling on soil was obscenely peaceful.

Ashke moved first. He stood, and his chair scraped, and the sound went through the greenhouse like a shot. "This is a provocation. Do you understand what you've done? If Halcyon command learns the truce's legal basis is fabricated, the War Council voids it, and we take Meridian under salvage law before the Verge can—"

"Before the Verge can *what*, Commander?" Oyelaran hadn't stood. That was the frightening thing about her; she got calmer the closer things came to breaking. "Say the sentence. Before the Verge can do exactly what you were about to do. You've been planning your administrative visit since before I arrived. Your escorts in the docking bay had boarding kit under their jackets. I counted."

"I counted yours too," Ashke said, "which is why I brought mine."

"Both of you," Gideon said, "shut up and listen to me."

It came out louder than he intended, louder than he had spoken to another human being in years, and it cracked something open in the room. Mira was looking at him with something like awe. Oyelaran's eyebrows had risen a fraction of a millimeter, which from her was a standing ovation.

"The manifest has two hundred and six names," Gideon said, more quietly now. "You've both read the fragments your intelligence services pulled. You've both spent years trying to get the originals because you think they're a weapon. But you haven't read them the way I've read them. You haven't sat with them. So I'm going to read you some."

He picked up the slate. His hands were steady. Four thousand logs had taught him that.

*"Adeyemi, Funmilola, age 34. Agricultural systems technician. Daughter, Adeyemi Kess, age 6."* He looked at Ashke. "Halcyon's third founding generation. Your own fleet histories list an Adeyemi among the charter signatories of your agricultural directorate. Your great-grandmother's generation, Commander, built on a woman who officially never existed."

Ashke had gone very still.

*"Voss, Ilse, age 51. Physician."* He turned to Oyelaran. "The Verge Fleet's medical corps still uses the Voss protocols. You named a training hospital after her."

"You're lying," Ashke said, but there was no force in it. It came out as a question.

"I'm reading," Gideon said. "That's different. Your fleets didn't just tolerate the forgery, gentlemen—captain—commander. Your fleets *descended from it*. The refugees your founders claimed died in the evacuation—the ones your histories mourn, the ones your monuments are carved for—forty of those families didn't die. They were written onto this station. They were *saved*, by a clerk with a pen, and the price of saving them was a lie both your commands agreed never to examine, because examining it would mean admitting that your founding myths—the noble sacrifice, the tragic loss—were partly a story you told yourselves to avoid the shame of having left those people behind in the first place."

"That's not—" Ashke started.

"The truce was never about mutual threat." Gideon set the slate down. "I used to think it was a stalemate. Two fleets, equal strength, nobody willing to pay the cost. That's what the official histories say. But that's not what's in these records. What's in these records is that both your fleets knew. Both your commands signed the original truce with full knowledge of the forged manifest, and the truce holds because the truth would shame you both. It was never a peace between enemies. It was a silence between the guilty."

Mira spoke for the first time. "They didn't leave them behind," she said. Her voice was rough, unpracticed in this company. "That's not—look, I've lived inside these names my whole life. The families on that manifest, they *chose* to vanish. Half of them asked for it. My mother—" She stopped. Started again. "My mother was a stowaway. Real one, not a paper one. She told me the forged passengers weren't victims. They were the payment. Forty families got new names, new histories, new lives in the fleets—and in exchange, both fleets pretended the station owed them nothing and owed the dead everything. It was a trade. Shame on both sides, balanced to the gram."

"And you," Oyelaran said, and her voice had changed, gone softer and more dangerous, "have been spending that inheritance for years. Lin Zhou's credentials. Lin Zhou's access. You've been walking around this station wearing a dead woman's name."

Mira's chin came up. "That name opened doors. You think I don't know what that makes me? I've known since I was nine years old. But that name also *kept* things. I maintained the vault records. I kept the manifest copies rotating so the storage decay wouldn't eat them. If it weren't for me, half of what he's reading right now would be bit-rot." She jerked her chin at Gideon. "Tell them. Tell them who actually preserved the archive for the last fifteen years while he was talking to tomatoes."

"I talk to the tomatoes *and* I preserved the archive," Gideon said, and to his own surprise, to everyone's surprise, Mira laughed—a short, cracked, startled sound—and something in the room loosened by a single degree.

It did not last. Ashke was on his feet again, pacing the aisle between the benches, and his shadow swept across the seedling trays like a cloud. "You're asking me to believe that Halcyon command—three generations of it—has been party to this. That the War Council that signed the original accord knew the manifest was fabricated and signed anyway."

"I'm asking you to believe your own grandfather's signature," Gideon said. "It's on the accord. Page two. Next to Oyelaran's grandmother's. Both fleets, both commands, one secret. That's why the truce has never had an enforcement mechanism, Commander. You've noticed that, haven't you? Thirty years and no inspection regime, no arbitration clause, nothing. Because you can't enforce a truce whose terms are *don't look*."

Oyelaran had picked up her cup at last. She turned it in her fingers without drinking. "There's a version of this," she said, "where you've just started a war. You understand that. Nine days out. Both fleets converging. And you've handed every hardliner on both sides the justification they've been praying for."

"I've handed them the truth," Gideon said. "What they do with it is the part that belongs to them."

"And if what they do with it is burn this station?"

"Then it burns with the record intact, broadcast wide, and every crew deck in both fleets knows what their command chose." He met her eyes. "I've been dead for twenty years, Captain. Professionally speaking. Nobody reads my logs, nobody answers my transmissions, nobody knows I exist. You don't threaten a man like me with dying. You can only threaten him with *mattering*, and both of you already spent that currency."

The drip valve cycled again. Water on soil. Mira had pulled a seedling tray toward her without seeming to notice she'd done it, her fingers in the dirt, an unconscious habit of someone who had grown up hiding in maintenance spaces and had never once been allowed to touch anything growing.

Ashke stopped pacing. When he spoke, the bluster had gone out of him, and what remained was worse—something tired and old. "My grandmother signed that accord," he said. "I've seen her copy of it. There was a marginal note in her hand. I could never read it. It just said *'so that we are not asked.'*" He looked at the slate on the table as if it were a wound. "So that we are not asked. That's what the truce was. An agreement not to ask."

"Now you've been asked," Oyelaran said.

"Yes." He sat down. He picked up his water and drank it all, like a man finishing something. "Now we've been asked."

For a long moment nobody spoke, and the greenhouse held them—the amber light, the green rows, the four of them around a table laid with a cloth folded for strangers who had finally, in a way, arrived.

It was Oyelaran who broke it, and she broke it by standing and walking, slowly, down the central aisle, reading the plant tags the way she'd read the docking bay manifests on her first visit. Tomato. Wheat. Soy. She stopped at the far bench, at the newest cuttings, the rootstock Gideon had grafted in the days after his mother's letters, and she read the tag there, and her shoulders changed.

"What is this one?" she asked.

"Cutting from the original stock," Gideon said. "The seeds my mother carried off the dying world. It's the oldest living thing on this station. Older than the truce. Older than both your fleets' claims." He paused. "It doesn't have a name on the tag yet. I've been waiting to see who it belongs to."

Oyelaran turned back toward the table, and Gideon saw that her eyes were wet, and that she was furious about it, and that she was not going to hide it, and that this—more than anything he had said or read—was the thing that would change the next nine days.

"It belongs to all of them," she said. "Every name on that manifest. And you know it, Zhou, and that's why you called us here instead of selling to whichever of us bid higher."

"I called you here," Gideon said, "because in nine days, someone is going to make a decision about this station, and I wanted the decision made by people who had stood in this room first. That's all. That's the whole of it."

"And if we refuse?" Ashke asked. "If I transmit tonight and Halcyon comes in hot?"

Mira looked up from the seedling tray, dirt on her fingers. "Then you'd better do it soon," she said, "because someone else already knows we're all in this room together—and they know what the archive says—and I don't think they're going to wait nine days."

Gideon felt the temperature of the room change. "What do you mean, someone else?"

Mira's face had gone pale beneath the grow-lamps. "The vault," she said. "I checked the seals this morning, before I came. Sector Nine's outer door was cycled twice last night. And the second cycle used credentials I've never seen before—new ones, clean ones, forged better than mine." She swallowed. "Someone's been copying the manifest. And they finished."
