## 22. Greenhouse Full of Names

The seed trays arrived on a Tuesday, though Tuesdays had long since stopped meaning anything aboard Meridian. Gideon had kept the old station calendar running out of habit, and then out of stubbornness, and now—finally—out of something that felt almost like joy. Tuesday was planting day. He had decided it would be so, and the station, for once, agreed with him.

He stood at the greenhouse airlock at 0600 station time, watching the first of the volunteers come through. They came in ones and twos at first, hesitant, the way people enter a church they were raised in but haven't visited in decades. Verge Fleet techs in their slate-grey jumpsuits, sleeves rolled to the elbow. Halcyon engineers with their insignias freshly unpinned, as if they'd debated the matter in front of their bunks and decided that soil did not care about fleets. A handful of station-born kids—there were more of them than Gideon had ever known, children who had grown up in the lower decks of ships that never docked here—who pressed their palms to the glass of the growing walls and left fog-prints like small signatures.

"Overcrowding," Oyelaran had warned him the night before, standing in the greenhouse doorway with her hands behind her back, studying the seed racks the way she'd once studied his archive manifests. "Half my crew has requested transfer to Meridian. Half of Ashke's too. You're about to become the most popular man in two fleets, and I know how you feel about popularity."

"I feel like a man with four hundred seedlings and a watering schedule," Gideon had said, and she'd laughed—a real laugh, unarmored, the first he'd ever heard from her—and touched his shoulder on her way out, briefly, like a woman sealing a letter.

Now the volunteers filed in, and the greenhouse filled with a sound it had never held before: many voices, overlapping, asking questions. *How deep? How far apart? Is this one a fruit or a flower?* For twenty years the only voice in this room had been Gideon's own, narrating to the air, to the cameras, to the dead. He had learned to make that voice warm enough to keep himself company. Now it caught in his throat, because the room was warm all on its own.

"All right," he said, and had to say it twice before the noise settled. "All right. Listen. Every one of these seedlings gets a name. Not a cultivar number, not a batch code. A name." He held up the first tray—basil, its leaves small and bright as scattered green coins. "These are the names from the manifest. The Meridian Star. Four hundred and twelve passengers who never flew, who never arrived, who were never real—and who were, at the same time, the realest people who ever touched this station. My mother wrote some of those names. Someone's grandmother is on that list. Someone's grandfather. Someone's whole family, invented to save forty other families, and the invention outlived the lie. So we plant them. We give them soil. That's the whole ceremony. That's the whole religion, if you want one."

Mira stood at the record table she'd claimed in the first week—her territory now, marked with a hand-lettered sign reading *REGISTRAR* that Ashke's engineers had found so charming they'd built her a proper ledger stand from salvaged hull plating. She held the manifest copy open, her finger moving down the columns.

"Adeyemi, Folake," she read out. "Who has Adeyemi?"

A Halcyon tech raised her hand. "Here."

"Adeyemi gets tray nine, basil. Write it down." Mira wrote it down, in her careful orphan's hand, the hand that had learned to forge documents in the dark and now wrote only true things. Gideon watched her and felt the old vertigo soften into something steadier. Two children of the same lie, she'd said once, finding each other. But they were not children anymore, and the lie had become, through their hands, a kind of truth.

"Castellanos, Ruy."

"Here." A Verge pilot, young, her hair still smelling of ship's recycled air.

"Castellanos gets the tomatoes. South wall. Full sun, or what passes for it."

* * *

By midmorning the greenhouse was a garden of bent backs and murmured names. Gideon moved through the rows like a slow current, correcting a planting depth here, loosening a root-bound cutting there, and everywhere he went, people told him things.

That was the part he hadn't expected. The names were not just names. They were doors.

"Zhou," said an old Verge quartermaster, squinting at his tag. "Lin Zhou. That's—wait. That's *your* name."

"My sister's," Gideon said. "She died twenty years ago, in the evacuation of Ceres Verge. My mother put her on the manifest afterward. I used to think it was a desecration. Now I think—" He paused, hands in the soil, and found the sentence waiting for him the way the answer to a log entry waits at the end of a long recording. "Now I think my mother understood something I didn't. That a name can be a vessel. That if you pour enough love into an empty name, someone will eventually come to fill it."

The quartermaster nodded slowly. "My grandmother was on this list. Okafor, Adaeze. My family always said she died in the transit. There was no record, no body, nothing. We mourned a ghost for three generations." He turned his tag over in his fingers. "Then your broadcast came through, and I looked her up, and there she was—on a ship that never flew, bound for a station nobody wanted. And I cried like a boy. Because at least she was *somewhere*." He knelt, pressed the seedling in, tamped the soil around it with surprising gentleness for a man with hands like cable anchors. "She's somewhere now."

Gideon heard versions of this all morning. The archive he had broadcast—the whole of it, the garden logs, the letters, the forgery, his mother's confession in her own looping hand—had traveled through both fleets like water through dry ground. People had found their ancestors in the forged manifest and discovered that the forgery was the only honest document their families possessed. *The lie that told the truth*, Mira called it. She had a gift for such phrases. Gideon suspected she'd inherited it from her stowaway mother, who had raised her on invented identities the way other parents raised children on bedtime stories.

At noon, Ashke arrived.

He came quietly, without escort, in civilian clothes that fit him like an apology. Gideon had last seen him signing the Charter of Meridian with the expression of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton. Now the commander of the Halcyon Fleet stood in a greenhouse holding a trowel as if it might detonate.

"I don't know how to do this," Ashke said. No preamble, no rank. That was new too.

"Nobody does," Gideon said. "That's why it's a practice and not a skill. Here." He handed him a seedling—lavender, its roots a pale tangle like something dreaming. "Okafor, Adaeze. Row six."

"Okafor." Ashke turned the name over. "My grandmother's deck chief, on the *Halcyon's* first hull. She used to say the fleet was named after a promise nobody remembered making." He knelt. His hands, so precise on weapons consoles and navigation arrays, were clumsy in the soil, and Gideon did not correct him. Some clumsiness was a kind of honesty. "I spent forty years believing Meridian was a prize. A strategic asset with a legal ambiguity. I built my career on the ambiguity." He tamped the earth down, harder than necessary, then softened. "Your broadcast took forty years off my life and gave me, I think, a different forty. I haven't decided yet if I'm grateful."

"You will be," Gideon said. "Gratitude is like basil. It comes back every season whether you tend it or not."

Ashke looked at him then—really looked, the way he had failed to do in the docking bay all those months ago, when Gideon had been a key, a lock, a liability. "Your sister," he said. "Lin. The name that started everything."

"My sister, and Mira's shield, and the saboteur's ghost, and now a row of lavender." Gideon smiled. "She always wanted to be more than one person. She'd have loved this."

* * *

In the afternoon the children came.

They came in a loose herd from the *Halcyon's* family decks and the *Verge's* education ring, a dozen of them, ages five to fifteen, herded by a teacher who had volunteered for the crossing and never volunteered to leave. Mira took charge of them with the brisk tenderness of someone who had been a child no one accounted for and had decided, long ago, that no child would ever again be unaccounted for in her presence.

"Rule one," Mira announced, standing on a crate so she could be seen over the growing walls. "You do not pull the seedlings up to check if they're growing. They are growing. That is their whole job. Your job is to not annoy them."

A small boy raised his hand. "What if they die?"

"Then we plant another one," Mira said, "and we write down the one that died, because it gets remembered too. Rule two: every plant has a name, and you say the name when you water it. Not because the plant cares. Because *you* should know who you're feeding."

Gideon, working the irrigation line along the north wall, listened to her teach and felt something in his chest that had no name in any of his four thousand log entries. It was close to the feeling of the first sprout breaking soil—something that had been waiting underground for a very long time, choosing its moment.

A girl of maybe seven tugged his sleeve. "Mister Zhou? Is it true you talked to the plants for twenty years and nobody listened?"

"It's true I recorded twenty years of logs," Gideon said, kneeling to her level. "Whether anybody listened—I used to think no. Now I know the station was listening. The seeds were listening. And it turns out that was enough to keep me going until the rest of you showed up."

"Did you get lonely?"

He considered lying to her, and found he no longer had the appetite for it. The whole archive was public now; his loneliness was a matter of historical record, catalogued and cross-referenced. "Yes," he said. "Very. But loneliness is like winter. It feels permanent, and it isn't. Something is always growing under it."

She nodded with the grave authority of the very young, and went off to name a pepper plant after her brother.

* * *

At dusk, when the volunteers had thinned to a few stragglers and the grow-lights cycled down to their evening amber, Oyelaran came in. She no longer wore her captain's coat. She wore work gloves, and she carried a tray of cuttings from the *Verge's* hydroponics bay—clones of plants her own crew had tended for generations without knowing why the tradition mattered.

"Rootstock," she said, setting the tray down. "My grandmother's. She always said we'd plant them 'when we arrived.' We never arrived anywhere. The fleet just kept moving, and the cuttings kept getting cloned, and nobody asked what we were waiting for." She looked around the greenhouse—at the rows of tagged seedlings, at Mira's ledger, at the fog-prints of children's hands still ghosting the glass. "Turns out we were waiting for you."

"I didn't do anything," Gideon said. "I just didn't stop."

Oyelaran smiled. "Yes. That. That's the rarest thing in the fleet, Gideon. Everyone I've ever commanded was very good at starting and very good at stopping. Nobody was good at *continuing*." She picked up a cutting, examined its roots, found a place in the empty bed along the east wall. "What will you log tonight? Four thousand and one was for the arrivals. What's four thousand and two?"

Gideon looked out at the greenhouse. The seedlings stood in their trays and rows, four hundred and twelve of them and then some, each with a paper tag fluttering in the ventilation current like a small flag of a country that had finally been founded. Mira was at her table, closing the day's ledger, her pen moving in the lamplight. Beyond the glass, the docking bay lights showed the shapes of two fleets at rest—not converged for conquest, merely *present*, their crews sleeping in shifts, some of them dreaming, he suspected, of grandmothers they had only just learned they had.

"I don't know yet," he said. "I think I'll just describe the light in here at the end of the day. I've been recording this room for twenty years and I've never once described the light. I was always too busy describing the absence of people to notice what the light was doing to the leaves."

Oyelaran pressed the cutting into the soil and patted it flat. "And what will you call the light?"

Gideon thought about it. The amber glow lay across the rows like a blessing, like the last hour of a day on a world none of them had ever stood on, and it fell on every name equally—the forged and the true, the dead and the living, the sister and the stranger, the mother who had stayed behind and the daughter who had knocked on doors with her name.

"Arrived," he said.

* * *

That night, alone, he made the recording. The greenhouse hummed its low green hum around him. Mira had gone to her quarters—her quarters, a real room with her real name on the door, though she'd told him she sometimes still knocked first, out of habit, before remembering she was allowed in. The seedlings stood in the dark, growing, as was their whole job.

"Station Meridian, log entry four thousand and two," Gideon said. His voice sounded different to him now. It sounded like a voice that had been answered. "Planting season has begun. Four hundred and twelve seedlings in the ground, each bearing a name from the manifest of the Meridian Star. Volunteers from the Verge Fleet and the Halcyon Fleet worked the soil today alongside station staff, and by station staff I mean Mira, who has informed me that she is the registrar, the archivist, and the entire personnel department, and that I am, quote, 'just the guy who knows about dirt.'

"The light in here tonight is amber, and it falls on everything the same.

"My mother asked me to keep the garden alive for whoever finally came. They came. They're still coming—Oyelaran says the transfer requests haven't slowed, and Ashke has proposed expanding the greenhouse into Bay Four, which I have conditionally approved, the condition being that he learns to water things without drowning them.

"Lin, if you're listening—and I've decided that you are, the way the seeds were listening, the way the station was listening—your name is in the ground now. Row six, lavender, tended by a man who commanded half a fleet and couldn't work a trowel. It suits you. You always did collect unlikely devotees.

"End of log. Next entry when there's something new to report. Though—" he paused, and looked down the long rows of small green lives, each one a name, each name a promise kept— "though I suspect there will be something new to report every day now. For the first time in twenty years, I expect tomorrow.

"Meridian out."

He set down the recorder. In the dark of the greenhouse, under the amber afterglow of the lights, four hundred and twelve names stood in their soil and grew, and the station that had been a tomb for twenty years breathed out, slow and green, and began—at last, at last—to live.
