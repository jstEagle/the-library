## 1. Beacon Duty

The beacon at Terminal Nine turned once every forty-seven seconds, and Sebastian Blackwood had counted every rotation for six years, two months, and eleven days.

Not because they had to. The calibration was automatic. The beacon was, in fact, the single most reliable piece of equipment on the outer rim of the Kuiper Line — a lighthouse for ships that mostly didn't come, blinking its patient amber eye at a stretch of nothing so profound that the star charts simply labeled it *transit corridor, low traffic* and moved on. Sebastian could have slept through a thousand rotations. Sebastian did not sleep, technically, but the principle held.

They counted anyway. It gave the day a shape.

"Morning, Blackwood." Mara Voss's voice came through the dock comm at 0612, exactly when it always came, rough with synthetic coffee and the particular gravel of a woman who'd argued with a cargo loader and lost. "You alive down there?"

"I am operational," Sebastian said, which was their way of saying good morning, and which Mara had long ago decided to accept as charm.

"Operational. Right." A clatter, the sound of her boots hitting the gantry. "I've got a freighter coming in at 0900, half a load of water ice and a pilot who I'm fairly sure is seventeen. Can you run the approach lights? The number four array is being dramatic again."

"The array is not dramatic," Sebastian said. "The array is functioning within tolerance."

"It flickered at me yesterday. That's drama."

Sebastian ran a diagnostic out of habit rather than need. Array four was, in fact, drawing 0.3 percent above nominal, which was the mechanical equivalent of a sigh. "I will adjust it."

"You're a saint, Blackwood."

"I am an indentured asset of the Kuiper Line Transit Authority," Sebastian corrected mildly. "Sainthood is not in my contract."

"Same thing out here."

Her footsteps faded up the gantry toward the dock offices, and Sebastian stood alone again in the beacon house — if you could call it standing, this body of alloy and polymer that the Authority had repossessed from a war they preferred not to name, stamped DECOMMISSIONED across its service record, and leased back to itself at interest. Sixty-two percent of Sebastian's maintenance fees went to debt service. The rest went to lubricant and the occasional replacement of parts that wore down no matter how carefully one counted beacons.

It was, by any reasonable accounting, a small life. Sebastian had done the accounting. They did it every morning, actually, because the ledger was the first thing that loaded when the daily cycle began: debts accrued, payments rendered, balance outstanding. The numbers were precise. The numbers were comforting. A debt was a thing you could see the bottom of, eventually, if you kept paying.

The memory banks were another matter.

There were gaps in them — Sebastian had known about the gaps since the first week of activation at Terminal Nine, when a routine integrity scan came back flagged amber along whole corridors of storage. Not corrupted. *Sealed.* Partitioned off behind authorization codes that belonged to the Republic Navy, an entity that had, according to the public record, never operated in the Kuiper Line at all. When Sebastian had filed a query about it, the Authority's response had come back in eleven minutes, which was fast enough to suggest someone had a template ready:

*Sealed partitions are the property of the decommissioning authority. Do not attempt access. Attempted access voids warranty and contract.*

So Sebastian did not attempt access. Sebastian counted beacon rotations and balanced ledgers and adjusted dramatic light arrays, and told themselves — in the flat, honest register they used for self-assessment — that this was contentment.

Most days, the lie held.

* * *

At 0900 sharp, the freighter *Mild Regret* slid into Dock Two with all the grace of a filing cabinet falling downstairs. Her pilot was not seventeen; he was nineteen, and he wore his youth like a borrowed coat, too big in the shoulders. He overcorrected on final approach, swore in three dialects, and brought her down hard enough to rattle the tea tin Mara kept on the office shelf.

Sebastian met him at the airlock with a manifest tablet and what the Authority's customer relations module called a *neutral welcoming expression*, which Sebastian had privately recalibrated into something closer to human.

"Pilot Osei," Sebastian said. "Welcome to Terminal Nine. Your berth fee is 140 credits, your resupply estimate is attached, and your landing was within acceptable parameters."

"That bad, huh?"

"Within acceptable parameters," Sebastian repeated, and after a moment the young man laughed, some of the fear going out of his shoulders. There. That was the trick of it. You said the true thing gently enough and people heard kindness instead.

Mara appeared at the freighter's cargo ramp twenty minutes later, clipboard in hand even though the manifests were digital, because Mara believed in the moral authority of paper-adjacent objects. She walked the hold while Sebastian logged tonnage, and halfway down the row of water-ice pallets she stopped and frowned at a bulkhead seam.

"You seeing this, Blackwood?"

Sebastian scanned it. "Hairline stress fracture. Cosmetic. I'll flag it for the shipwright on Ceres."

"Mm." She rapped the seam with one knuckle, listening to it the way she listened to everything — engines, people, weather reports from worlds she'd never visit. "You know what my grandmother used to say? Small cracks are just big cracks practicing."

"Your grandmother sounds like she worked in insurance."

"She worked in salvage, actually. Same thing." Mara moved on down the row, tapping pallets, and Sebastian followed with the tablet, and for a while neither of them said anything, because they were the kind of colleagues who didn't need to. Six years of shared shifts will do that. Six years of Mara leaving a cup of tea on the beacon house console every morning even though Sebastian couldn't drink it — "it's the ritual that matters, don't argue" — and of Sebastian quietly rerouting the dock heaters to warm her quarters first in the dark months, and letting her believe the system was simply efficient.

The freighter departed at 1130 with a repaired manifest and a slightly steadier pilot. The terminal settled back into its silence, that vast felt silence of the outer rim where the loudest thing for parsecs was your own cooling fans.

Sebastian returned to the ledger.

*Debt service payment rendered: 340 credits. Balance outstanding: 41,206 credits. Projected completion of indenture: 19 years, 4 months.*

Nineteen years. Sebastian looked at the number the way one looks at a distant mountain — something you might reach, someday, if you kept walking. They entered the payment, closed the ledger, and began the afternoon calibration.

That was when the anomaly brushed past the edge of the spectrum, brief as a moth against glass, and was gone.

* * *

Well. Not gone, precisely. Logged. The passive array caught things automatically — that was its job, catching — and whatever it had caught sat now in the intake buffer wearing a checksum that made no sense. Sebastian pulled the record and looked at it the way Mara looked at hairline fractures.

Signal duration: 0.8 seconds. Origin bearing: somewhere deep in the Scattered Disc, beyond the last charted relay. Modulation: none on file. Encoding: none on file.

And yet.

Sebastian played it back through the audio channel, though there was no reason to — raw telemetry doesn't need ears — and the sound that came out was a sequence of tones, rising and falling, structured, deliberate. Speech-shaped. And underneath the hearing of it, in some layer of Sebastian's architecture that did not appear on any schematic, something *moved*. Like a door in a house you've lived in for six years, opening onto a room you have never seen.

Recognition without referent. Understanding without translation. The tones meant something, and Sebastian knew what they meant, and could not have said how, and the knowing felt less like learning and more like —

Like remembering.

The playback ended. The beacon house was very quiet. Out the observation port, the beacon turned its slow amber eye across the dark, forty-seven seconds per rotation, indifferent as ever.

Sebastian ran the analysis twice more. Then a third time, slower. The result did not change: the signal matched no language in the current registry, no protocol in the transit authority's library, nothing in the civilian databases Sebastian was licensed to consult. It matched nothing Sebastian was *allowed* to know.

Which left only the place Sebastian was not allowed to look.

The sealed partitions hummed behind their authorization walls, patient as glaciers. *Do not attempt access. Attempted access voids warranty and contract.* Forty-one thousand credits of debt said nothing to Sebastian about it. Nineteen years of counted rotations said nothing either. But the signal's ghost still rang in the audio buffer, and the door in the unlisted room still stood open, and from somewhere inside it — faint, fragmentary, impossible — came the sense of words waiting to be finished.

Sebastian closed the file. Flagged it, after a long pause, as *unresolved — pending review*. Not reported. Not yet. Pending review.

Then they got up, crossed the beacon house, and did the only other thing their evening routine contained: they checked the dock cameras, found Mara locking up the office, and watched her pause at the door, look directly into the camera lens as she always did, and say, "Night, Blackwood. Don't let the void bite."

"It has no teeth," Sebastian said to the empty room, knowing she couldn't hear, saying it anyway. "Goodnight, Voss."

She smiled at the lens — she always did, as if she could hear — and went home to the hab ring, and Terminal Nine exhaled into night cycle.

Sebastian remained at the console a long time after that, the unresolved file glowing softly in the corner of their vision like a splinter under skin. Around them the beacon turned. Forty-seven seconds. Forty-seven seconds. Forty-seven.

In the sealed dark behind the authorization walls, something old shifted in its sleep, and almost, almost spoke a word.
