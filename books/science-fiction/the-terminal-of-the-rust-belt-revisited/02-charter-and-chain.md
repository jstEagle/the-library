## 2. Charter and Chain

The docking clamps of Meridian Station closed around the *Long Memory*'s hull with a sound Elias Vann had come to think of as a handshake—metallic, formal, and, if you listened closely, faintly glad to see you. He stood on the inspection gantry with his tablet squared against his forearm and his audit sash straightened for the third time in as many minutes, watching the freighter settle into its cradle like an old dog lying down by a fire.

Charter inspectors were not supposed to be glad to see anything. The manual said so, more or less. *The auditor approaches all vessels with impartial rigor.* Elias had underlined that sentence during his certification year, at Ilsa Reyes's insistence, and had privately appended a marginal note he would deny under oath: *rigor is not the same thing as coldness.*

"Inspector Vann," said the dockmaster's voice overhead, warm through the station speakers. "Freighter *Long Memory*, out of Ceres Reach, inbound from the Long Route. Manifest on file. She's carrying—" a pause, the particular pause of a man reading something he didn't fully understand, "—cryo-consignment, class seven. Sealed hold."

"Class seven," Elias repeated. "Sealed."

"Sealed since the incident," the dockmaster said, and then stopped, because everyone on Meridian stopped at that word, *incident*, the way you'd step around a hole in the floor you'd long ago stopped looking down into.

Elias tapped his stylus against the tablet twice—a habit, a metronome—and began the walk down the gantry.

* * *

Rosalind Okonkwo met him at the airlock, which was itself a small irregularity. Protocol said the captain could send a first officer or a cargo master; protocol did not require her to stand there in person, flight jacket zipped to the throat, hair pulled back in a way that suggested she'd done it quickly and then pretended she hadn't thought about it at all.

"Inspector Vann." Her voice was level, official, pitched exactly for any listening recorder.

"Captain Okonkwo." His own voice matched hers, note for note. They had perfected this duet over six years: two people speaking in perfect charter cadence while saying, underneath it, things no charter had words for.

"You'll want the manifest first."

"I'll want the manifest first."

She handed him the slate. Their fingers did not touch, because they were both far too careful for that, but the manifest passed between them along a trajectory so precisely calibrated that it might as well have been a love letter.

He read it standing in the airlock vestibule, because reading it anywhere else would have meant lingering, and lingering was a thing neither of them permitted. Cryo-consignment, class seven: two hundred fourteen units, provenance listed as *colonial insurance program, pre-Contact era*, disposition *return to originating colonies upon charter review*. Every line correct. Every line, he knew, a locked door painted to look like a wall.

"Provenance documentation?" he asked.

"Appendix C."

"Chain of custody?"

"Appendix D, with transfer seals intact. You checked them last cycle."

"I check them every cycle." He looked up. "That's the job."

"That's the job," she agreed, and something moved behind her eyes—not defiance, not guilt, exactly. More like a woman holding very still near a bird she hoped wouldn't startle.

They walked the ship.

* * *

The *Long Memory* smelled the way it always did: recycled air, engine coolant, and beneath both, faintly, the antiseptic sweetness of the cryo systems' coolant bleed—the smell of sleep, Elias thought, though he'd never once said that aloud. Tam Osei was in the engineering corridor with his head inside a coolant manifold, boots sticking out into the walkway like an afterthought.

"Inspector on deck!" Rosalind called.

Tam extracted himself, leaving a smear of blue grease across one cheekbone like war paint applied by a distracted artist. "Inspector Vann. Sir. Captain." He looked between them with the expression of a man who has noticed weather patterns but been raised never to comment on them. "Everything running clean. If you want to inspect the engines, they're boring. That's a compliment. Boring is what we're going for."

"Boring is the highest praise an auditor can give," Elias said gravely, and Tam grinned, and for three seconds the corridor felt less like a procedure and more like a kitchen.

Then they arrived at the sealed hold, and the warmth went out of the air the way heat leaves a room when a door opens onto winter.

* * *

The hold door was charter-gray, seamless, stamped with the class-seven seal and the date of its last authorized verification—Elias's own countersignature, eleven months old. Above the handle, someone had stenciled, in small neat letters, the consignment designation: *CARGO — DO NOT POWER DOWN — DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT CHARTER AUTHORITY.*

"Do you want the seal verified?" Rosalind asked. Her voice had gone flat and professional, and Elias understood why. This was the part of the ritual where they stood on opposite sides of a line drawn in ink, and both of them pretended the line was all there was.

"Seal verified," he said. He ran the scanner along the seam. Intact. Unbroken. Countersigned. He entered the result into his log with two precise taps. *Consignment verified sealed. No irregularities observed.*

"No irregularities observed," Rosalind repeated softly, and for a moment—half a second, no more—she closed her eyes, and Elias saw in the set of her mouth something that was not relief. Relief would have been simpler. It looked closer to grief wearing relief's coat.

"Captain," he said, before he could stop himself, "how are they?"

It was an improper question. There was no field on any form for it. *How are they*—as if cargo could be well or unwell, as if two hundred fourteen sleeping people were a flock someone shepherded across the dark.

Rosalind opened her eyes. "Vitals nominal," she said. "All two hundred fourteen. I run the checks personally. Every cycle."

"I know," he said. "It's in your maintenance logs. You log them at odd hours. 0300, 0415, 0250."

"Somebody has to be awake when the ship is."

"The autopilot could do it."

"The autopilot doesn't know their names."

The words came out of her quietly, without drama, and then hung in the corridor between them like frost. Elias looked at the seal. Rosalind looked at him. Neither of them wrote anything down.

"Their names aren't on the manifest," he said at last, because the manifest was the only ground he had left to stand on.

"No," she agreed. "They aren't."

* * *

They conducted the rest of the inspection the way they always did: thoroughly, correctly, and with a tenderness so deeply buried in procedure that only the two of them could have excavated it. He inspected the galley and she pointed out that the coffee ration was technically misfiled under *medical supplies*, and he wrote a corrective note that said *reclassify* and she said *yes sir* and then made him a cup of the misfiled coffee anyway, in the good mug, the one with the chipped rim that she kept for him specifically and claimed, when Tam asked, was simply the mug nobody else wanted.

He inspected the navigation logs. She stood beside him while he scrolled, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, not quite, a gap measured in millimeters and maintained with the discipline of a woman threading a needle for six years without once letting herself be pricked.

"Your route deviations here and here," he said, tapping the display. "Explain."

"Weather on the relay corridor. Solar activity. Standard avoidance."

"Standard avoidance," he repeated. "Documented."

"Always documented."

He believed her. That was the trouble. He believed her the way you believe a river is deep—you don't need proof, you just need to look at the water. Somewhere in those logged deviations, in the honest little notes about solar weather, was a story she had never told him, and he had never demanded, because demanding it would make him the auditor and nothing else, and some stubborn cellar of himself refused to be nothing else. Not with her. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

"You could ask me," Rosalind said suddenly, quietly, not looking at him. "Anytime you wanted. What's really in the hold. Why I fly the route I fly. You could ask, and I'd—I don't know what I'd do. But you could ask."

"And you'd have to answer, or lie, and either one ends us." He set the tablet down. "So I don't ask. That's my corruption, Captain. Not money. Not favors. Just this: I don't ask."

She turned then, finally, and looked at him full-on, and for a moment the careful architecture of six years trembled like a bridge under too much weight. "Is that love, Elias? Or is it cowardice?"

"It's both," he said. "I've audited myself extensively. Findings inconclusive."

And she laughed—that short, startled laugh she tried to keep filed away, the one that made Tam look up from whatever he was fixing and smile at nothing—and for a moment they were just two tired people in a small warm galley, and the charter was a thing that happened to other people.

Then the moment passed, the way moments did, and she took his empty mug, and he picked up his tablet, and they went back to being Inspector Vann and Captain Okonkwo, who had no irregularities to report.

* * *

At the airlock, the goodbye was conducted as a signature ceremony.

"Inspection complete," Elias said. "Findings: vessel compliant. Consignment sealed and verified. Crew professional. Coffee misfiled."

"Noted for correction," she said.

"Correction noted." He hesitated, stylus hovering. Then, because the recorders were dockside and ambient and he had learned their exact pickup range years ago, he said, in the flattest administrative voice he owned: "Captain. Next cycle, I'll be routing through New Anvil. There's a—situation developing there. Educational sector. Probably nothing. Probably filters doing filter things."

Something changed in her face. Not much. A single degree of temperature.

"Educational sector," she repeated.

"Children's drawings, if you can believe the traffic flags. Being pulled by the automated content screens." He shrugged, a man discussing tariffs. "Forty-one identical images, per the flag summary. Before deletion. Which is routine. Deletion is always routine."

"Of course," Rosalind said. "Routine."

"But I'm an auditor," Elias went on, still flat, still administrative, still signing his life away one syllable at a time. "And I notice when a number repeats. Forty-one children, three settlements apart, drawing the same thing. And I notice, Captain, that your route next cycle passes within relay range of New Anvil. Purely as a matter of logistics."

"Purely," she said.

"Purely." He handed back the signed inspection slate. Their fingers touched this time—one second, deliberate, a signature of a different kind, unwitnessed. "Safe flying, Captain Okonkwo."

"Safe auditing, Inspector Vann."

The airlock cycled. Through the viewport, as he walked the gantry back toward the station core, he watched the *Long Memory* begin her slow departure, and he found himself counting the lit windows along her spine out of pure habit, the way you count beads.

Behind him, his tablet chimed. A priority flag from the colonial net, auto-routed to all charter personnel:

*CONTENT FILTER EVENT — NEW ANVIL EDUCATIONAL SECTOR — 41 DUPLICATE IMAGES PURGED — KEYWORD: FIRE — NO FURTHER ACTION REQUIRED.*

*No further action required.* Elias stood on the gantry and read the sentence four times, and each time, the phrase sounded less like a conclusion and more like an instruction.

He thought of Rosalind's voice in the corridor: *the autopilot doesn't know their names.*

He opened a new file. He titled it, in accordance with regulation, *Preliminary Inquiry — Nonmaterial*. And then, in the notes field, where regulation required only a summary, he typed a single question mark, saved it, encrypted it, and told himself—as forty-one children somewhere slept and dreamed of burning—that it was probably nothing.

Probably.

His stylus tapped twice against the tablet. Once for doubt. Once, though he'd have denied it under oath, for hope.
