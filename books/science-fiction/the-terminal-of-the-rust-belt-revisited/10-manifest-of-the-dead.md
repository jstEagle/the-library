## 10. Manifest of the Dead

*Rosalind*

She heard the shuttle dock before she saw it — that particular graceless clunk of a charter inspection craft mating to a freighter's collar, all of it built by people who had never once worried about fuel margins. Tam looked up from the galley table, where he was eating soup directly from the pot, which was against regulations and also the only way to eat soup.

"That's Vann," Tam said.

"I know."

"You knew he was coming?"

"I know his docking pattern." She took the pot from him and ladled herself a bowl, mostly so her hands would have something honest to do. "He always requests the aft collar. He thinks it's less disruptive. He has never once considered that it means I hear him coming for six full minutes."

"And you let him think it."

"Everyone should get to believe one kind thing about themselves." She blew on the soup. Her hands were steady. That surprised her; inside, everything was doing the arithmetic of panic at once. Nineteen days. The deletion order ticking somewhere in a directorate server farm like a bomb made entirely of paperwork. And now Elias Vann boarding her ship with an auditor's slate and eleven years of careful not-asking behind his eyes.

"What do I do?" Tam asked.

"Finish your soup. Be charming. You're very good at both."

"I'm good at one and adequate at the other."

"Then be adequate loudly."

The airlock cycled. Rosalind set down her bowl, straightened a jacket that did not need straightening, and went to meet the man whose job description, if anyone had ever been cruel enough to write it out plainly, would read: *notice what Rosalind Okonkwo is hiding, and end her.*

* * *

*Elias*

The Long Memory smelled the same as it always did: recycled air, engine coolant, and under it something older, almost botanical, like a greenhouse that had been sealed for years. He had catalogued that smell across nine inspections and never found its source. He suspected Rosalind kept something growing in violation of at least two biosecurity clauses, and he had decided, in a small corruption he permitted himself, never to look for it.

"Inspector Vann," she said, meeting him at the airlock with her hand extended. Formal. Correct. Her palm was warm and dry and held on perhaps half a second longer than procedure required, which was their entire language, really — the half-second where everything they couldn't say lived.

"Auditor Vann," he corrected gently. "Inspectors find things. Auditors confirm them."

"Forgive me. I'd hate to inflate your finding capacity."

"It's a modest capacity. Mostly I confirm that paperwork matches reality." He smiled. "Which is why I'm here. Routine manifest verification. The directorate is doing randomized audits of long-route freighters."

"Randomized," Rosalind said.

"Randomized," Elias confirmed, and they both understood that neither of them believed the word, and that saying it twice was the closest they could come to discussing the thing underneath.

Tam Osei appeared in the corridor with his sleeves rolled up and grease on one forearm, radiating the studied innocence of a man who had recently hidden something large. "Auditor. Good to see you. We've got the cargo bay prepped. Coffee's terrible but it's hot."

"The two best qualities," Elias said, and followed them down.

* * *

*Rosalind*

She watched him work, because watching Elias work was one of the quiet pleasures of her life and she had never trained herself out of it. He moved through the hold with a slate and a stylus, cross-checking seal numbers against the manifest, murmuring serial codes under his breath like a man praying in a language only bureaucrats spoke. He was thorough. He was always thorough. It was the thing she loved about him and the thing that would destroy her, possibly in the same hour.

The backup pods sat in their racks, forty-two of them, cold and silent and labeled as *cryogenic archival stock — agricultural*. A lie she had told so many times it had worn smooth, like a stone in a river. Agricultural stock. As if she were hauling seed corn through the dark instead of the last living testimony of a town that officially had never existed.

Elias paused at rack seven.

Rosalind's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth.

"This pod," he said, "is cycling warm."

"Thermal variance in the coolant loop," she said. "It's on my repair list."

"It's been on your repair list for three audits."

"I'm a slow list-maker."

He looked at her then, and there it was — the question, the real one, rising up through eleven years of coffee and manifests and careful half-seconds. *What are you carrying, Rosalind? What have you been carrying all this time while I signed off on it?*

She could lie again. She was good at it. But she thought about nineteen days, and a deletion order, and Adaeze saying *we burned* in ninety seconds of borrowed life, and she found she was simply too tired to carry the stone one more time.

"Elias," she said. "Run the full manifest query. Not the summary. The deep index."

His stylus stopped moving.

"Rosalind—"

"If you're going to find it, find it properly. I won't have you signing off on a lie. Not this one."

* * *

*Elias*

The deep index took four minutes to compile. He spent those four minutes standing very still in a cargo bay full of cold pods, listening to his own heartbeat, which had gone strange and loud, like someone knocking on a door from the inside.

He knew, of course. Some part of him had known since the second audit, when he'd noticed the mass figures didn't reconcile — forty-two units of agricultural stock weighing exactly what human beings weigh, plus water, plus the particular density of hope. He had filed a note in his own private ledger and then, in the most damning act of his career, had simply never opened the file again. Auditors are paid to notice. He had noticed, and then he had chosen blindness, and blindness purchased year after year of dinners with Rosalind Okonkwo in station canteens where they talked about weather and routing schedules and never once, not once, about what slept in her hold.

The index finished compiling.

**MANIFEST DEEP QUERY — LONG MEMORY — CARGO HOLD 2**

**DECLARED:** Cryogenic archival stock (agricultural), 42 units.

**ACTUAL:** Human backup copies — cryo-preserved neural archives, 42 units.

**ORIGIN COLONY:** Kettle Creek.

**CHARTER STATUS:** Kettle Creek charter issued [date], rescinded [same week]. All associated records purged per Directive 9-C.

**NOTE:** Origin colony does not exist in current registry. Cargo origin field auto-populated from legacy data.

Kettle Creek. The word landed in him like a coin dropped down a well — he heard it falling through every layer of himself, past the auditor, past the loyal charter man, past Director Reyes's hand on his shoulder and her voice saying *there are things the loop cannot survive remembering*, all the way down to whatever he was before any of it. He thought of the file he'd been ordered to close. Eleven years ago. A settlement born and erased in seven days. Children three colonies away dreaming of fire.

He looked up at Rosalind.

She stood by the rack with her arms crossed, not defensive exactly — more like a woman presenting herself for judgment, chin up, eyes level, done with running even though running was the only thing she knew how to do. Behind her, Tam had gone very still by the galley hatch, holding his coffee mug like a shield.

"How long?" Elias asked.

"Since the beginning. Since the first run." Her voice didn't waver. "They're the witnesses, Elias. Everyone who saw Kettle Creek burn is dead except these. And the children, somehow. And me, in a way I can't prove." She unfolded her arms. "You have to report it. I know. I've known for eleven years that someday you'd stand in this hold and have to choose, and I promised myself I wouldn't make it harder for you by asking."

"You're not asking."

"No. I'm telling you the truth, which is different, and worse."

He looked down at the slate. Forty-two names scrolled in the deep index, each one attached to a person who had died once already and was being stored like contraband seed corn. He found the name near the top — **ADAEZE OKONKWO** — and understood, finally, completely, why Rosalind flew the long route. Why she counted her cargo like a rosary. Why she had never once let him closer than a half-second's touch.

"Your sister," he said quietly.

"My sister."

* * *

*Rosalind*

She expected the handcuffs. Honestly, she had rehearsed them — the weight of them, the words she'd say, the way she'd ask Tam to feed the cat-shaped maintenance drone before they towed her in. Eleven years of dread had a shape, and the shape was Elias reading her true manifest and then reaching for his comm.

Instead he stood there for a long moment, thumb resting on the edge of the slate, and she watched something happen in his face that she had no category for. Grief, maybe. Or the specific expression of a man watching his own life divide into *before* and *after*.

Then he did something with the stylus.

Small movements. Precise ones. She stepped closer despite herself and read over his shoulder, and her breath stopped.

He was amending the record. Not deleting — Elias would never delete, deletion was Reyes's language — but amending. Reclassifying the cargo entry. *Cryogenic archival stock, verified against physical count: 42 units, seals intact.* Adding a certification line in his own audit code. Attaching a note that read, in flawless bureaucratic prose:

*Cargo consistent with declared classification. No discrepancy noted. Recommend next full audit cycle: 90 days.*

Ninety days. He had just handed her ninety days, written in ink, countersigned by the charter itself.

"Elias." Her voice came out strange. "You can't—"

"I can," he said. "That's the terrible part, Rosalind. I've known for years that I could, and I told myself the reason I didn't report you was procedural caution. Insufficient evidence. Audit protocol." He laughed, and it wasn't a happy sound. "I just discovered the evidence, complete and damning, and my hand went to the amendment function before I'd finished reading. So much for procedural caution."

"Why?"

He turned to face her fully, and up close she could see how tired he was, how long he'd been carrying his own version of her secret, blind on purpose, loving her in half-seconds.

"Because I closed a file eleven years ago because a director told me to," he said. "Because there are children drawing a burning town none of them have ever seen, and the official answer is a content filter. Because you have been flying your dead sister around the loop for a decade, alone, counting her like prayer beads, and the charter's response to Kettle Creek was to pretend it never happened." His jaw tightened. "And because I love you, and I have never once said it out loud, and I am apparently the kind of coward who says it in amendment code instead."

The hold was very quiet. Somewhere in the racks, coolant whispered through its loops.

"Say it out loud," Rosalind said.

"In a minute. First—" He held up the slate. "You need to understand what this buys you. Ninety days, and then another auditor runs the deep index, and they may not be me, and they may not be mine. Whatever you're going to do — and Rosalind, I have known you eleven years, I know that look, you are going to do something catastrophic and noble — do it inside ninety days."

"Nineteen," she said.

"Nineteen?"

"Nineteen days is when the deletion order executes. The last records of Kettle Creek. After that, even the proof that it happened gets erased." She met his eyes. "I'm going to broadcast the backups' testimony from the old Kettle Creek relay. To every world on the loop."

Elias closed his eyes. She watched him take it in — the scale of it, the treason of it, the fact that the woman he loved had just described, calmly, in a cargo bay, the single largest act of defiance in the history of the colonial charter.

"When Reyes finds out," he said, "she will burn you out of the sky."

"I know."

"She'll burn Tam. She'll burn the ship."

"I know."

"She'll burn me."

Rosalind reached out and touched his hand — not a half-second this time, but fully, deliberately, her fingers over his knuckles and the stylus still warm between them. "That's why I didn't want you to know," she said. "That was the whole point of the last eleven years. Keeping you clean."

"Well." He exhaled. "Too late for that. I amended a manifest to conceal forty-two counts of undeclared human beings. I'm dirty as a fuel filter." He looked down at their hands. "Adaeze Okonkwo. Was she—"

"Older than me by four years. Louder than me by approximately forever. She ran the chapel school at Kettle Creek." Rosalind's voice caught and steadied. "She taught children. That's probably why they dream her. Why they dream all of them."

Elias nodded slowly. Then, with the deliberation of a man setting down something heavy he'd carried too far, he lifted the slate and typed one final line into the audit note, visible to anyone who ever pulled the record:

*Auditor's personal remark: some cargoes are heavier than their mass. This one is carried well.*

"There," he said. "Now it's out loud. In ink."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant." He set the slate down on a crate and took her face in both hands, and the half-second became a minute became however long it was, there among the cold pods and the sleeping witnesses, while Tam Osei very tactfully discovered an urgent need to inspect the galley.

When they broke apart, Rosalind was laughing and crying in roughly equal measure, which she hadn't done since she was a child. "We are both going to prison," she said.

"Probably."

"You amended a federal manifest with a love note."

"A concise love note. Auditors pride ourselves on brevity."

* * *

*Elias*

He left the way he'd come, through the aft collar, walking the dock spine toward his shuttle with the slate tucked under his arm and his career folded up small inside his chest like a letter he'd already mailed. Behind him the Long Memory sat in its berth, dark and patient, full of sleeping witnesses and one woman planning arson with paperwork.

His comm chimed. Directorate priority header. He almost didn't look.

**FROM: DIRECTOR I. REYES**
**SUBJECT: AUDIT SCHEDULE AMENDMENT**

*Vann — cutting your rotation short. You're reassigned effective immediately. New Anvil, direct flight. I'm going planetside myself and I want my best auditor at my side. There's a schoolteacher causing noise, and a freighter captain I've had my eye on for eleven years. Time to close both files personally.*

*Pack warm. — I.R.*

Elias stood on the dock spine and read it twice. *A freighter captain I've had my eye on for eleven years.* She knew. She had always known. The randomized audits, the gentle rotations that kept putting him on the Long Memory's route — not negligence. Leash-length. She had been walking him beside the truth for a decade, waiting to see which way he'd break.

He looked back at the ship. Through the viewport he could see Rosalind at the airlock, watching him go, one hand raised in a small wave that was also, unmistakably, a farewell.

*Nineteen days,* he thought. *New Anvil. Maren. Reyes herself, in the room.*

He keyed his comm and began, with the calm of a man already condemned, drafting his travel requisition — and, buried in the routing preferences where no one ever looked, a single line of code that might, if he was lucky and quick, reach a schoolteacher before the Director's shuttle did.
