## 22. The Terminal of the Rust Belt

The terminal had been called many things over the years—Junction Prime, the Old Splice, the Rust Belt's Front Door—and for eleven years it had been called nothing at all, because no one came here and names require witnesses.

Rosalind Okonkwo stepped off the shuttle with a crate under one arm and her sister beside her, and the first thing she felt was the cold. Not the cold of vacuum, which she knew intimately, but the cold of a place that had been unheated for a decade: a deep, mineral cold that lived in the deck plating and rose through boot soles like a memory of its own. Her breath fogged in the light of their work lamps. Somewhere above them, the great docking arm of the oldest terminal on the loop hung open to the dark, waiting for a ship that had burned two years ago and would never dock again.

"You're smiling," Adaeze said.

"I'm allowed."

"You're smiling *creepily*. Like someone who has finally lost her mind and is enjoying it."

"That's also allowed." Rosalind set the crate down on the customs counter, which was still there, still bolted to the floor, its surface furred with a fine grey dust that had once been somebody's paperwork. "Eleven years I flew past this place. Every loop. I used to tell myself it was just a waypoint. Just geometry. A place where my route bent and I didn't have to look at why."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to make it impossible not to look."

* * *

The plan was Tam's, mostly, though he'd deny it with the modesty of a man who had once held a broadcast array together with his own hands while it tried to kill him. The old relay tower at the terminal's heart—the one that had carried the loop's first traffic back when the colonies still spoke to Earth, back when Kettle Creek was a line item in a settlement ledger—was structurally sound. Its power couplings were dead. Its antenna was crusted with oxide and bird-adjacent organisms nobody could explain this far from any biosphere. But the bones were good.

"Bones are all we ever needed," Tam said, arriving an hour behind them with Maren and a second crate and the particular bounce of a man who had slept badly and woken up happy anyway. He'd cut his hair since the hearing. It made him look younger, which he claimed was the point. "You want to know what I learned from the Long Memory? Ships are just bones with opinions. You keep the bones honest, the opinions take care of themselves."

"The ship is gone, Tam."

"I know what's gone." His voice did something gentle there, quick as a hatch sealing. Then he clapped his hands. "Right. Power coupling first. Adaeze, you're on the lift. Maren, you're on naming duty."

Maren looked up from the crate she was unpacking. "Naming duty?"

"Every wire we replace gets named after a kid who drew the fire. There were forty-one drawings. We've got about three hundred connections to make. So we're going to need more kids' names than we have, which means—" he produced a tablet with the flourish of a street magician, "—we're also doing the classrooms today. Comms window opens at fourteen hundred. The children voted. They want to name things."

"They *voted*," Maren said, in the tone of a woman who had spent two years learning that when children organize, you get out of the way or you get organized yourself.

"Binta led the campaign," Tam said. "There was a platform. There were promises. One of them involved me personally learning to dance, which I have agreed to under duress."

Rosalind laughed, and the laugh went out into the cold dark of the terminal and came back changed, softer, like something the old station had been holding for her all along.

* * *

They worked three days.

It was hard work and it was slow work and Rosalind loved every minute of it in a way that surprised her, because she had expected grief to be the weather of this place and instead it was mostly gaskets. The beacon itself was simple—a transmitter core salvaged from the Long Memory's black box array, which Tam had pulled from the wreck field himself, alone, in a suit, and never talked about except to say "she gave us everything she had, so we're giving it back." Around the core they built a housing out of the terminal's own skin, panels pried from walls that hadn't heard a voice in a decade, riveted into a shape that was part lighthouse and part reliquary and part, if Rosalind was honest, a little bit ugly. Tam insisted the ugliness was structural.

On the second day, Elias arrived on the supply run from New Anvil, carrying coffee that had been roasted by actual colonists in actual small batches, because the loop had developed opinions about coffee now that it had developed opinions about everything else. He had signed onto the *Adaeze* as cargo clerk—lowest rank, freest man, his words—and he wore the rank like a man wearing clothes that finally fit.

He found Rosalind up in the tower, elbow-deep in a coupling, and sat down next to her on the grating without a word, the way he'd learned to. For a long time they just worked. That had always been their language, even in the bad years: proximity conducted as procedure, tenderness hidden inside shared tasks. Except now there was nothing to hide it inside, and it turned out the tenderness did fine on its own.

"I audited this terminal once," Elias said eventually. "Early in my career. Eleven years ago, almost to the week."

Rosalind's hands went still inside the coupling housing.

"It was my first solo inspection. Everything was clean. Too clean—I remember thinking that, and then I remember deciding I was being paranoid, because paranoia doesn't get you promoted." He turned a bolt slowly, uselessly, between his fingers. "There was a manifest flag. Small thing. A settlement charter, issued and rescinded same week. I noted it. My supervisor thanked me and the note vanished and I told myself records get lost all the time." He looked at her. "I could have been the man who asked one more question eleven years ago."

"You were nineteen months old in your career and facing a machine that ate people who asked questions."

"I know. I've had two years of knowing." He set the useless bolt down. "That's why I'm here. Reyes testified, the network collapsed, the record got corrected—and correction isn't restoration, you taught me that, the hearings taught everyone that. Nothing erased comes back whole. So what do you do with the hole where the truth used to be?" He gestured, taking in the tower, the terminal, the dark beyond the docking arm. "You build something in it. Loudly. Permanently. On a loop."

Rosalind wiped her hands on her rag and took his face in both cold-stiffened palms and kissed him, right there on the grating, amid the dust of a decade and the bones of the first relay, and neither of them checked whether anyone saw, because there was no longer anything either of them was required to report.

* * *

The last connection belonged to Binta Ferreira.

The comms window opened at fourteen hundred exactly, and the classroom at New Anvil filled the little screen Tam had rigged on the tower platform—thirty-some faces crowded together, arms waving, someone's pet lizard held aloft like a trophy. Binta stood front and center with the gravity of a child who had been elected to something.

"Beacon crew," she said formally, "this is Classroom Six. Are you ready to receive the name?"

Tam checked his board. "We are ready to receive the name."

"The name is Binta Ferreira, and it goes on the last wire, because I drew the chapel first and Maren says first matters." She paused. "Also the lizard's name is Chairman. Chairman has no official role. He's just here."

"Chairman is welcome," Rosalind said, and meant it, and heard Adaeze snort warmly somewhere behind her.

Binta pressed her palm flat against the screen on her end; on the tower, Rosalind seated the final connector, and the circuit closed with a click so small it shouldn't have mattered and mattered completely.

The beacon lit.

Not dramatically—that was the thing none of them had predicted. No flare, no thunder. Just a soft amber glow rising through the housing seams, steady as a pulse, and then the transmitter waking with a hum that they felt in their teeth before they heard it, and then—

"*Adaeze Okonkwo.*"

Her own name, spoken in her own recorded voice, from the testimony she'd given in the hold of a dying ship. She had recorded all 214 herself, in the weeks after the hearing, sitting in the vault-turned-memorial with the bone fragments and the hand-carved memorial wall behind her, saying each name aloud the way you set a stone. Now the stones spoke.

"*Tomas Ferreira. Yewande Adeyemi. Marcus Bell—*"

Adaeze came to stand beside her at the rail. Below them the terminal's old corridors caught the amber light and threw it back, and the dust in the air turned briefly golden, and the signal went out along the dead relay corridor that Rosalind had flown for eleven years with her eyes half closed—and this time every relay it touched woke and passed it on, world to world to world, the whole rusted loop ringing like a struck bell.

"*Samuel Osei. Grace Nwosu. Peter Halloran—*"

"Do you think they can hear it," Adaeze said quietly, "wherever they are? The originals, I mean. The ones we're copies of. Do you think some part of them knows?"

Rosalind considered lying, or offering comfort, and decided—as she had learned to do in the vault, in the courtroom, in every hard room of the last two years—to offer truth instead. "I don't know. I used to think the hold was a tomb. Then I thought it was a witness box. Now I think maybe it was always a compass. Pointing at what happened so we couldn't fly around it forever." She watched the amber light breathe. "Maybe hearing your own name is like that too. Maybe it just points."

Adaeze put her arm through hers. "Then point."

"*Miriam Castellanos. Dev Anand Rao. Little Ruth Ferreira, aged four—*"

Down on the platform, Maren had brought out paper and the older children visiting with their families were drawing—not the fire, not anymore. They were drawing the beacon. Forty-one drawings had shown a burning settlement; already, on fresh sheets, there were towers with golden seams and stick figures standing beside them holding hands, and Maren pinned each one to the terminal wall as it finished, building a new composite, layer by layer, of a place worth remembering for a better reason.

Elias stood at the edge of the light with his hands in his pockets, watching Rosalind the way he had watched her across a customs counter years ago—like a man auditing something he already believed in. When she caught his eye he didn't look away. He never did anymore.

* * *

They stayed until the loop came around.

That was the thing about a beacon on the oldest terminal of the trade route: it didn't shine once. It shone every time a ship passed, every time a relay cycled, every time a child on any of five worlds opened a classroom window and let the names in. The 214 played on loop, as promised, and would play until someone chose—with witnesses, with ceremony, with the full weight of a corrected record—to stop them, and Rosalind had filed the paperwork ensuring that stopping them would require a public hearing, because she was, at heart, still a woman who respected procedure. She'd simply learned what procedure was *for*.

On the last night, she walked the terminal alone while the others slept in the habitat ring. She stopped at the customs counter and ran her hand over the dusty wood, and she counted—not backups this time, not cargo, not a rosary of dread, but names, all 214, forward and backward, the way you count your way home. The hold of the Long Memory was ash scattered in a wreck field two systems away. But the hold had never really been the hold. It had been her. The sealed room she'd carried for eleven years, the thing she never opened, the reason she flew the long route and told no one why.

She stood in the amber light of the beacon and unlocked it one last time, and found it empty, and found that emptiness was not loss. It was room.

Behind her, boots on the grating—Tam, wrapped in a blanket, hair flattened on one side, carrying two cups of terrible coffee because some traditions survived even the fall of directorates.

"Can't sleep either?" he said.

"Counting."

"Good count?"

"The best one." She took the coffee. Out past the docking arm, a running light appeared on the horizon of the dark—the *Adaeze*, coming in on the morning shuttle run, honest cargo, honest route, her sister's name on the hull and Elias's handwriting on the manifest and nothing anywhere that needed hiding. Rosalind watched her come home and felt the beacon pulse beside her, patient as a heartbeat, and understood at last what she'd been building all these years, on every loop, through every silence:

Not a memorial. A lighthouse. And lighthouses don't mourn the ships that pass. They just make sure the ships see the rocks—and sail on anyway.

"Hey, Rosalind," Tam said, nodding at the growing light. "Incoming traffic wants permission to dock."

She smiled, and keyed the channel, and answered with the oldest words in her repertoire, the ones she'd said ten thousand times in another life to another ship in another dark:

"Long Memory actual. You are cleared to come home."
