## 2. The Language That Forgot Itself

The beacon woke him at 03:14 station time, which was a lie the clocks told to make the dark feel official.

Edmund Dravenmoor had lived aboard Meridian Station long enough to know every sound it made: the groan of thermal plating expanding in the sun-cycle, the arrhythmic tick of the water reclaimer in the next compartment, the distant, patient chewing of rust as the Rust Belt slowly digested itself. He knew which sounds meant trouble and which sounds were just the station talking in its sleep. So when the beacon's alert chimed—a soft, three-note figure he'd never heard before—he lay still for a full minute, listening, half-convinced he'd dreamed it.

It came again. Three notes, rising, like a question asked politely twice.

"Nobody's supposed to be asking questions," he said to the ceiling. "That's the whole arrangement."

He got up anyway. He always got up. It was, he reflected as he pulled on his thermal layer and padded down the corridor toward the beacon chamber, one of the few consistent things about him. He could abandon a name, abandon a manifest, abandon any number of small illegalities—but he couldn't abandon a chime.

The beacon chamber occupied the station's old comms spire, a cathedral of salvaged antenna arrays and cable runs that had been obsolete before Edmund's grandmother was born. At its heart sat the beacon itself: a squat cylinder of pre-Exodus alloy, pitted green with age, humming its low constant hum that Edmund had come to think of as the station's heartbeat—or possibly his own. He'd been tending it for eleven years. Oiling seals that didn't need oil. Replacing components that tested fine. Logging maintenance entries in a hand so careful it looked like devotion, because somewhere deep in his bones he understood that this machine was the only thing on Meridian that had ever needed him without wanting anything back.

Tonight it wanted something.

The main display, usually content to show him the same idle waveform it had shown for a decade, was alive with data. A transmission had arrived—burst format, tight-beam, originating from somewhere out past the Belt's debris shoals where nothing should have been transmitting anything. The signal strength readout made his eyebrows climb. Whoever had sent it had aimed it at Meridian Station specifically. Not at the fleet relays. Not at the customs net. At the beacon.

At *him*, functionally, though nobody alive knew that yet.

"Okay," Edmund said, settling into his chair and pulling up the raw feed. "Let's see who's knocking."

The answer was: no one he knew.

The transmission decoded into text on the second pass, and the text was wrong. Not corrupted-wrong, not static-shredded-wrong, but *old*-wrong. Edmund had spent years around pre-Exodus documents—the beacon's own maintenance manuals were written in them—and he'd learned to read their English the way you learn to read your great-grandmother's handwriting: with patience and occasional guesswork. But this was older than the manuals. This was the dialect from before the Exodus, before the Schism, from back when Earth still had weather worth complaining about. Two centuries dead. The kind of English that existed now only in museum archives and the frozen dreams of two hundred thousand sleepers drifting somewhere beyond the Belt.

*PRIORITY TRANSMISSION — CUSTODIAL AUTHORITY — ACKNOWLEDGE RECEIPT*

And beneath it, rendered in characters that flickered like a candle deciding whether to exist:

*T-90 DAYS AND COUNTING.*

Edmund sat very still.

The sensible thing—the thing any sane smuggler with a clean record and a falsified future would do—was to log the event as signal noise, close the channel, and go back to bed. He even reached for the command. His fingers hovered over it.

Then the display refreshed itself, unprompted, and added a line:

*CUSTODIAN: DRAVENMOOR.*

His grandmother's surname. His surname. Spelled correctly, which was more than fleet intelligence had ever managed.

"Well," he said, after a while. "That's not ideal."

* * *

He did what he always did when the universe misbehaved: he made tea, and he thought about his grandmother.

Odile Dravenmoor had died when Edmund was nineteen, aboard a tug she'd technically owned and legally shouldn't have existed, leaving him three things: her debts, her decoder ring, and a sentence he'd never stopped hearing since. The ring was a battered band of dull metal, too large for any of his fingers, studded with contacts that looked like they'd been gnawed by something patient. She'd called it heirloom junk. "Wear it when the machines talk nonsense," she'd said, "and maybe they'll stop." He'd assumed it was a joke, or a superstition, or the early stages of something sad. He wore it anyway, some nights, the way you keep a dead person's habits alive by performing them.

Now he dug it out of the false bottom of his tool chest, where it lived among genuinely valuable contraband and genuinely worthless souvenirs, and turned it over under the work light.

The contacts were worn smooth in patterns. Patterns, he realized, staring, that matched the interface port on the beacon's maintenance panel—the one he'd never been able to open, the one whose manual page was missing, torn out with surgical precision sometime before Odile had ever touched the machine.

"You knew," he said to the empty workshop. To her, really. "You knew what this thing was, and you gave me an oil can and a shrug."

The ring said nothing, being a ring. But it fit the maintenance port perfectly, and when he seated it and turned it a quarter-turn clockwise—as if opening a jar—the beacon's hum changed pitch for the first time in eleven years. It dropped half a tone, settled, and then, unmistakably, *sighed*.

The display bloomed with translation matrices. Partial ones. The ring wasn't a key so much as a lens: it brought the dead language into rough focus the way squinting brings a street sign into focus, enough to walk by, not enough to read the fine print. Edmund worked through the night, cross-referencing fragments against the beacon's own archived logs, building a dictionary of a language that had forgotten itself, and by the time the station's fake dawn lit the corridor strips amber, he had three solid facts and a headache shaped like a century.

Fact one: the countdown was real. Ninety days, decrementing on schedule, tied to something the fragments called *the truce*—a word the old dialect used the way modern English used *ceasefire*, except heavier, older, load-bearing.

Fact two: the beacon was not a beacon. Or rather, it was a beacon the way a bank vault is a mailbox. Its light was incidental. Its purpose, per the fragments, was *authorization*: the power to wake sleepers, grant docking priority, command fleet assets. A single word kept recurring in the translated headers, and Edmund rendered it, after an hour of wrestling, as *custodian*. Keeper. Warden. The one who holds the keys and answers for them.

Fact three—and this was the one that made him set down his tea with exaggerated care—was a fragment of the original commissioning order, half-legible, signed with a name that had been worn down by time and translation until only its skeleton remained:

*D——N M——R.*

Dravenmoor. Maybe. Possibly. The old script was ambiguous, and Edmund wanted desperately to believe the ambiguity, and equally desperately suspected he wouldn't get to.

"The past has my address," he muttered. He rinsed his mug. He considered, briefly, throwing the ring into the recycler. He did not throw the ring into the recycler. Some inheritances you don't agree to; you just fail to refuse them fast enough.

* * *

By mid-morning he'd done what he could, which was: nothing, thoroughly and repeatedly. He checked the beacon's external profile—it still broadcast the same boring navigation pulse it had broadcast for decades, invisible in plain sight. He scrubbed the intrusion from the local logs, though he suspected the beacon kept its own records somewhere he couldn't reach, the way old houses keep letters in walls. He ate breakfast standing up, which was how he ate everything, and he caught himself reading the countdown off the corner of the display every time he glanced at it, the way you check a wound.

*T-89 DAYS, 11 HOURS.*

A voice at the chamber door nearly took a year off his life.

"Your manifests are late." Mara Voss stood in the doorway in her customs grays, tablet under one arm, expression professionally neutral in the way that only people who know each other far too well can manage. She glanced past him at the beacon, at the display, at the ring still seated in the maintenance port, and Edmund watched her catalog all of it in about a second and a half. She was very good at seeing things. It was, he sometimes thought, the entire problem with both of them.

"Equipment calibration," he said. "Old hardware gets sentimental."

"Mm." She stepped inside, uninvited, and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "There was a burst transmission logged on the outer net last night. Directional. Toward this station. Fleet intelligence flagged it within four minutes." Her eyes found his. "Four minutes, Edmund. They don't move that fast for noise."

"Maybe they're getting better at their jobs."

"Maybe." She held his gaze a moment longer than the joke deserved, then set a data chip on the workbench between them—unmarked, gray, the kind of chip that officially contained shipping schedules and unofficially contained whatever Mara decided shipping schedules should contain. "New manifests. Your hold two inventory has a discrepancy I couldn't help noticing, so I corrected it. You're welcome."

"Voss, you're a poet of paperwork."

"I'm a bureaucrat with a conscience, which is worse." She turned to go, then paused at the door, her back to him. "Whatever that thing just remembered how to say—consider whether you want to hear the rest of it. Some doors are easier closed from the outside."

She left. Edmund looked at the chip, then at the ring, then at the display, where the countdown ticked down another second with the calm of something that had been waiting two hundred years and could certainly wait a little longer.

Below the countdown, faint as breath on glass, a new line had appeared. He hadn't seen it arrive. It was simply there, the way rust is simply there:

*CUSTODIAN ACKNOWLEDGED. PREPARE FOR CONTACT.*

Edmund Dravenmoor, who had built a life on the principle that the best place to stand was slightly outside the light, felt the vertigo of a man watching the light swing toward him across a dark field.

"Ninety days," he told the beacon. It hummed, satisfied, and began, very quietly, to count.
