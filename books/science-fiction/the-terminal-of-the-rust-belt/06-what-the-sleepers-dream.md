## 6. What the Sleepers Dream

The memory-chips arrived the way all my best contraband arrived: mislabeled, under-declared, and smelling faintly of someone else's lunch.

The manifest said *agricultural yeast cultures, 40 units*. The crate said *agricultural yeast cultures, 40 units*. But I had been running the Rust Belt long enough to know that no one ships forty units of yeast in foam-lined shock casing with magnetic shielding, and no legitimate yeast has a serial number etched by the Cryo-Fleet Archive Division. I pried one out in the privacy of my workshop, turned it under the lamp, and felt my heart do something complicated.

Archived dream-fragments. Sleeper stock. Two centuries old, give or take a re-compression.

Now, a man in my line of work develops a certain professional ethics. I don't peek at other people's memories the way I don't peek at other people's mail — not because I'm above it, but because the last time someone peeked at *my* mail, they found the beacon maintenance logs, and I spent a very tense afternoon explaining to a very bored inspector why a navigation beacon needed quarterly lubrication of its "emotional resonance coils." (The inspector had nodded. Boredom, I have found, is the best camouflage in the Belt. Nobody investigates a thing that bores them.)

But these weren't mail. These were dreams. And the beacon — my beacon, my rusted, patient, impossible inheritance — had spent three weeks whispering its countdown at me, and every translation attempt ended in the same wall: *custodian authorization requires empathic verification.* Empathic verification. The old engineers had built a lock that didn't want a password. It wanted *understanding*.

So I told myself it was research. I told myself it was duty. I told myself a lot of things that night, and then I slotted the first chip into the reader, because I am not as disciplined as I like to pretend.

* * *

The first dream was of rain.

Not Rust Belt rain — we don't get rain, we get condensation and the occasional coolant leak — but real rain, falling on real grass, in a dream so vivid I could smell the wet dirt. A woman was standing in it. She was laughing at someone off-frame, and her coat was cut in the style of the Concordance fleet — the blue-and-silver, the *Ascendant Concord*, the faction that currently claimed the northern approach corridors and had, three weeks ago, sent me a letter demanding my station's "cooperation with legitimate custodial authority" in font sizes usually reserved for threats.

She was Concordance. I knew it from the coat. And she was dreaming of a garden.

The dream shifted, the way dreams do, and the garden became a kitchen, and the kitchen became a launch bay, and she was saying goodbye to a man in a different uniform — the deep red of the *Meridian Compact*, the Concordance's estranged sibling, the faction that claimed the southern corridors and had sent me essentially the same letter with more capital letters. The man was her brother. I knew this the way you know things in dreams, without being told. He was boarding a Compact ship. She was staying with the Concordance. Neither of them was happy about it, and neither of them, in the dream, believed they'd see each other again.

The dream ended on a handshake that lasted too long, the way real goodbyes do.

I sat in my workshop with the chip warm in the reader and my eyes stinging, and I thought: *well, that's inconvenient.*

* * *

Here is what the history files say about the Schism War — what's left of them, after two centuries of both factions "correcting" the record the way you'd correct a portrait of an ex-spouse. The fleet split in two over the Waking Question: who gets thawed first, when Earth becomes habitable again, and in what order, and under whose authority. The Concordance said merit. The Compact said equity. Both said *justice*, loudly, until the word wore smooth as river stone. There was a battle. There were several battles. There was a truce, brokered by — if my grandmother's decoder ring and my increasingly suspicious genealogy were to be believed — my own great-great-something, who split the waking authorization in half rather than hand victory to either banner, and then built a beacon to hold the pieces and vanished into the historical record like a man leaving a party through the kitchen window.

Two factions. Two banners. Two hundred years of separate school curricula, separate holidays, separate anthems, separate grudges maintained with the devotion other people reserve for gardens.

And here is what the dreams said: a Concordance woman dreaming of her Compact brother. A Compact engineer dreaming in Concordance hymns because his mother had sung them. A child — God help me, a *child* — whose dream kept splicing between two flags because she'd been frozen during the evacuation and nobody had thought to update her family's faction registration, so the archive couldn't decide which side she belonged to, so it filed her under both, so she dreamed of both, so in her sleep she was simply *everyone's*.

I worked through the crate for six nights. Forty chips. Forty dreams, chopped into fragments by whatever compression algorithm some long-dead archivist had used to fit a whole sleeping population's subconscious into portable storage — and I began to understand why the archivist had bothered. These weren't souvenirs. These were *evidence*. Somebody, two centuries ago, had archived the sleepers' dreams deliberately, knowing — hoping — that someday someone would need to see what the banners had spent two hundred years papering over.

The Concordance sleepers dreamed in Compact dialects. The Compact sleepers dreamed of Concordance weddings they'd attended, Concordance teachers they'd loved, Concordance neighbors whose names they still said with the particular tenderness people reserve for the dead and the far away. A Compact dockworker dreamed of teaching a Concordance girl to weld. A Concordance surgeon dreamed of a Compact patient whose life she'd saved and whose thank-you letter she'd never answered, because the war came, and the letter was from the wrong side, and answering it would have been *inappropriate*.

She still had the letter memorized. Two hundred years frozen, and she had it memorized. I had to stop the playback twice on that one.

* * *

On the eighth night, Mara came over.

She had a way of arriving that was technically a customs inspection. She'd knock, announce herself as "Voss, Meridian Station Revenue and Compliance," and then stand in my doorway holding a clipboard she never wrote on, looking at me with an expression that meant *I am here because I wanted to be here and this is the only legal way I know how to do that.*

"Your yeast shipment," she said. "Forty units. That's a lot of yeast, Dravenmoor."

"I bake."

"You don't bake."

"I'm considering taking it up."

She came in. She looked at the reader, the chips, the forty little foam cradles with their little foam ghosts missing, and she didn't ask. That was the thing about Mara — she had spent years not-asking about my smuggling, my beacon, my grandmother's junk, and her not-asking was so loud some nights I could hardly sleep.

"Dreams," she said finally, picking up a chip. "Sleeper archives. Edmund, do you know what fleet intelligence pays for these?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's the point. Nobody knows. Which means they'd pay *anything*, which means you're sitting on a crate of trouble shaped like yeast." She turned the chip over in her fingers. "Where did these come from?"

"A man who owed me."

"Everyone who ships through you owes you. It's your whole business model." She set the chip down gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping infant, and I thought: *she knows what it is. She's known since the doorway.* "What's on them?"

So I told her. The woman in the rain. The brother on the launch bay. The child with two flags. The surgeon with the memorized letter. I told her all of it, and somewhere in the telling my voice went rough, because I had spent six nights grieving for people I'd never met, grieving for a division that turned out to be a bookkeeping error elevated to a civilization.

Mara listened the way she did everything — completely, and with her hands busy, sorting my tools into the correct drawers without being asked, which is either love or criticism depending on the day.

"You know what this means," she said quietly, when I finished.

"It means the Schism was a lie."

"It means the Schism was a *lie they all agreed to keep telling*, which is worse, because now it's furniture. You don't argue with furniture. You don't even see it." She sat down across from me. "Edmund. If both fleets find out their frozen families are — *mixed* — if the sleepers themselves are the counterargument to two hundred years of doctrine —"

"Then the dreams are worth more than the beacon."

She looked at me for a long moment. "Don't say that where anyone can hear you."

"It's just us."

"That's how it always starts." She stood, straightened her uniform out of habit, and paused at the door. "The Concordance delegation arrives Thursday. The Compact's already docked at Anchor Nine. Whatever you're going to do with that crate, do it fast."

"Mara."

She turned.

"Thank you for inspecting my yeast."

The corner of her mouth did the thing it did — the small, controlled rebellion, the smile she kept filed under *professional misconduct, pending*. "Forty units," she said. "I'm flagging it for follow-up."

She meant: *I'll come back.* She left.

* * *

I sat alone with the chips and the beacon's slow pulse, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

Two factions, converging on my station, ninety days of truce burning down like a fuse. Two banners, each certain of its own righteousness, each carrying a cargo hold full of sleeping people who — if the dreams were any indication — had spent two centuries frozen in a shared dream of *each other*. The Concordance's righteous meritocrats dreamed Compact lullabies. The Compact's righteous egalitarians dreamed Concordance gardens. The war had divided the fleet the way a river divides a country, and the dreams proved the river was two feet wide, and everyone on both banks had spent two hundred years pretending it was an ocean.

My ancestor had refused to choose. I was beginning to understand that it wasn't neutrality. It was that he'd seen this — the dreams, or something like them, the evidence of a humanity that had never actually split — and he'd understood that any choice between the banners was a lie, because the banners themselves were the lie.

The beacon pulsed. The countdown ticked. And the decoder ring, warm on my finger where I'd worn it like a wedding band for three weeks, gave a soft chime I'd never heard before.

A new fragment had decrypted. Not a word this time. A *name* — my family name, in the old pre-Exodus spelling, followed by a string of coordinates and a single line of translation that the ring rendered in my grandmother's careful hand:

*To the custodian: they will come for you before the truce ends. Some will come with warrants. Some will come with kindness. Be more afraid of the kindness.*

I looked at the crate of dreams. I looked at the countdown.

And out my workshop window, past the rust and the traffic lanes, I saw a Concordance shuttle adjusting its approach — not toward the fleet anchorage, not toward station control, but toward *my dock*, running no transponder, flying the kind of quiet, careful, courteous approach that meant someone had decided I was worth being kind to.

My hands were not quite steady when I started logging the approach.
