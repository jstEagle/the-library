## 7. The Offer She Couldn't Refuse

Mara Voss had been lying on official documents for eleven years, and she had gotten very good at pretending it was a small thing.

It was not a small thing. It was four hundred and six falsified manifests, each one a small paper bridge she'd built under Edmund Dravenmoor's boots so he could keep walking across the gap between what he did and what the fleet allowed. She kept a private tally in a leather-bound ledger she'd bought at a junk stall on Meridian's third ring, because if she was going to commit crimes, she was going to commit them with good penmanship.

The ledger lived in a false bottom of her customs desk, beneath a genuine antique: a stamp that read APPROVED, which she had never once used honestly.

She was stamping nothing in particular on the morning the two officers from fleet intelligence walked into her office, and she knew who they were before they showed their credentials, because nobody else in the Rust Belt wore uniforms that clean. Clean uniforms on Meridian Station were like orchids in a coal mine. They meant somebody had paid for them to be there.

"Officer Voss." The taller one had a face like an unmade bed. The shorter one smiled too readily, the way people smile when they've practiced it in a mirror and gotten the angle slightly wrong. "Lieutenant Okafor. This is Specialist Reyes. We'd like a word about the beacon on Pier Nine."

"Which beacon?" Mara said. "There are eleven beacons on Meridian."

"The one that doesn't appear on any commissioning record," Reyes said pleasantly. "The one maintained, without authorization, by a freight hauler named Dravenmoor. The one you've been helping him keep off our screens for—what does your ledger say? Ah. Eleven years."

Mara's hands did not shake. She had trained them not to, the way other people trained for marksmanship. But inside, something cold walked down her spine wearing boots.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said, which was the first lie she'd ever told that she didn't believe.

* * *

They laid it out for her in the back room, away from the junior officers, with the door sealed and the room's recorder formally switched off, which in Mara's experience meant it was recording twice as hard.

Here was the shape of it: fleet intelligence had flagged the beacon six weeks ago. They knew it was old—pre-Schism old, which was a phrase that made the hair on Okafor's arms stand up, though he pretended otherwise. They knew it had received a transmission in a dead language. They knew, or strongly suspected, that Edmund Dravenmoor was something more than a smuggler with a hobby, and they wanted to know what.

"You're going to plant a tracker on it," Okafor said. "A passive one. It doesn't transmit, it doesn't interfere, it just listens. You'll tell him it's a routine emissions compliance tag. He won't question it. He trusts you."

The word *trusts* sat in the air between them like something spilled.

"And if I refuse?" Mara asked.

Reyes produced a folder. It was not a thick folder, but it didn't need to be. Four hundred and six pages, or the digital equivalent—every falsified manifest, cross-referenced, annotated, dated. Eleven years of small paper bridges, catalogued by someone patient enough to be frightening.

"Then Dravenmoor goes to a fleet disciplinary habitat for twenty to thirty, and you go for longer, because you did it from inside." Reyes's smile stayed at that slightly wrong angle. "Or—option two—the tracker goes on, the manifests quietly become real, and everyone's paperwork stays boring. You know we don't actually care about the memories he smuggles. Nostalgia chips. Half of them are just recordings of rain. We care about the beacon."

Mara looked at the folder. She thought about Edmund's hands, which were always faintly greased, always careful, the hands of a man who fixed things other people had forgotten had names. She thought about the look he gave her at customs every run—not gratitude, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who knew exactly what she was doing for him and had decided, in his quiet way, to be worthy of it.

He trusted her. That was the whole problem with love, Mara thought. It gave people leverage over you, and the only defense was to have no one.

"Routine emissions compliance tag," she repeated.

"Standard issue. Here's a dozen." Okafor slid a small case across the table. "Plant it during his next inspection. You have six days."

* * *

She spent three days not doing it, which was its own kind of torture.

She went through the motions of her life. She stamped manifests. She drank terrible coffee in the officers' canteen and listened to a junior customs agent complain about his love life with the desperate intimacy of a man who had mistaken her silence for wisdom. She walked past Pier Nine twice and didn't go in.

On the third night she found herself in her quarters, holding the compliance tag under a magnifier, taking it apart with the small jeweler's tools she used for repairing her ledger's hinges. Passive, they'd said. She found the second antenna coiled inside like a tapeworm. Passive the way a spider is furniture.

She put it back together. She was good at putting things back together looking innocent. It was, she reflected, the entire architecture of her life.

And that was when she understood the trap wasn't the folder, or the threat, or even the tracker. The trap was that she could do this. She could plant it, Edmund would never know, the fleet would get whatever they wanted, and her life would continue in its comfortable dishonest groove until the day it didn't. The trap was that betrayal, for her, was easy. She had eleven years of practice at exactly this: smiling at a man while altering his paperwork.

The only hard thing available to her was the truth.

* * *

Edmund was under the beacon when she came to Pier Nine, which was where he always was, half inside a maintenance panel, talking to the machine in a low voice the way some men talk to horses.

"I know you're there," he said, without turning around. "You walk like someone who's about to deliver bad news. You've always walked like that. I used to think it was the uniform."

"It is the uniform," Mara said. "The uniform is bad news with epaulets."

He backed out of the panel, wiping his hands on a rag that had been black since before she met him. He looked tired in the specific way of a man who had been translating a dead language in his head for weeks. There was a decoder ring on the workbench—his grandmother's heirloom junk, he called it, though he handled it like a relic—and beside it a slate covered in his cramped handwriting, half of it crossed out.

"Mara." He said her name the way he said it every time: like a man checking that a load-bearing wall was still there. "You've got your customs face on."

"I've got several faces on. It's a layered situation." She set the case of compliance tags on the workbench. "I'm going to tell you something, and then you're going to have every right to hate me, and I'd like to get through the telling before the hating starts, if that's all right."

Edmund looked at the case. Then at her. He was, she realized, not a man who surprised easily anymore—the beacon had burned the surprise out of him—but this landed anyway. She watched it land.

"Talk," he said.

She told him all of it. Okafor and Reyes and their orchid-clean uniforms. The folder with four hundred and six pages. The tracker with its coiled tapeworm antenna. She told him the part she was most ashamed of last, and she made herself say it plainly: *I could have done it. You'd never have known. That's what I am, Edmund. I'm a person who could have done it.*

The beacon hummed behind him, that low patient tone it made, the one she'd privately thought of for years as the sound of the station breathing.

When she finished, Edmund was quiet for a long moment. Then he picked up the case of compliance tags, turned one over in his greased fingers, and said:

"Eleven years of falsified manifests and this is the first time you've ever handed me contraband honestly."

"That's not forgiveness."

"No. It's not." He set the tag down. "But it's something. Mara—do you know what the beacon told me? The real message, under the countdown? It can wake half the sleepers. Only half. And it takes the choice of whoever's holding it when the truce expires." He laughed, short and unamused. "Two centuries ago my family was handed the job of not choosing. Turns out the job was always going to land on someone. It landed on me. I've been invisible my whole life so I'd be ready to make the most visible decision in human history. That's either a plan or a joke, and I can't tell which."

"Both fleets are coming," Mara said. "I've seen the traffic projections. Ninety days."

"Eighty-six now." He looked at the tracker in her hand, and something moved behind his eyes—she'd seen him look at broken machinery that way, right before he did something clever to it. "You said it's passive. Listens only."

"Listens only. I checked. I took it apart."

"Of course you did." And there it was—the ghost of a smile, tired but real. "Then here's what we're going to do. You're going to plant it. Exactly where they told you to."

Mara stared at him. "Edmund—"

"Because in eighty-six days, two fleets are going to arrive and demand codes from a machine that only answers to me, and every one of them is going to assume the other side is cheating. And when they do—" he tapped the tracker, gently, the way he tapped anything he was about to repurpose— "I want them listening. I want fleet intelligence to hear exactly what I broadcast. I want it on their channels, clean and undeniable, because the only thing both factions trust is their own spies." He looked up at her. "You didn't bring me a betrayal, Mara. You brought me a broadcast antenna with a really good cover story."

She sat down on an ammunition crate that probably predated the Schism, because her knees had opinions.

"I was ready to lose you," she said. "I had it planned. I'd confess, you'd hate me, I'd request transfer to the outer ring, and I'd spend the rest of my life stamping manifests for people who never lie to me because they never tell me anything."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the best plan I've ever had. All my other plans involved paperwork."

Edmund crouched in front of her, and for a moment the great weight he'd been carrying—the countdown, the bloodline, the half of humanity asleep in two angry halves—lifted just enough that what was underneath it was visible: a man who fixed things, looking at the one thing he'd never had to fix because it had never broken.

"Mara Voss," he said, "you falsified four hundred and six manifests to keep one stubborn man supplied in nostalgia chips, and then you confessed to treason to save him from a lesser crime. If that's not love, it's the closest thing the fleet's bureaucracy has ever produced, and I'll take it."

"It's love," she said. "Filed in triplicate."

He laughed then, really laughed, and the beacon hummed its patient note, and somewhere out in the dark between stations, two fleets were already burning fuel toward them.

"Eighty-six days," Edmund said, standing, pulling the workbench light closer, already reaching for his tools. "Then we'd better get to work. You know their inspection protocols better than they do. I need to know exactly where Okafor thinks you'll hide a tracker—because that's exactly where we're going to hide the truth."

Mara picked up her ledger on the way out of Pier Nine, and for the first time in eleven years, she left a page blank.

She had a feeling she'd want to write down what happened next.
