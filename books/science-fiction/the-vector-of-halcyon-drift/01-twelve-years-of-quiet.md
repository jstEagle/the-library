## 1. Twelve Years of Quiet

The vault woke Odile the way it always did: not with sound but with absence. No alarm, no hum of life support, no neighbor's engine coughing through a bulkhead. Just the long silence of Halcyon Station at 0500 station time, which was a fiction she maintained because someone had to keep the clocks honest even if no one else lived here.

She lay in her bunk for exactly ninety seconds — she'd timed it once, out of boredom, and now the ritual held — and then she rose, pulled on the same gray thermal undersuit she'd worn for three days running, and went to check the seeds.

That was the joke, if she'd ever had anyone to tell it to. Twelve years as caretaker of the Halcyon seed vault, and in twelve years not one seed had been planted. The vault's manifest listed eleven thousand four hundred and six accessions: wheat bred for high-radiation soils, barley that could drink brine, soy lines engineered back when Earth still believed the colonies would need feeding. All of it sealed behind three meters of regolith-shielded composite, climate-locked at minus eighteen Celsius, waiting for a spring that the war had canceled.

Her job, according to the contract she'd signed with a Directorate logistics clerk who never looked up from his terminal, was *maintenance of viability*. She ran the diagnostics. She logged the humidity. She replaced coolant pumps when they died, which was roughly twice a year, and she filed reports nobody read into a network nobody audited anymore. The Directorate had bigger concerns than a seed vault orbiting a rock nobody wanted. That was the whole reason the posting existed, she suspected — not because the seeds mattered, but because the vault was too expensive to demolish and too embarrassing to acknowledge.

Fine by her. Embarrassment made excellent camouflage.

She walked the main corridor with her coffee bulb steaming in one hand, reading the status strip embedded in the wall like other people read scripture. Sector A, nominal. Sector B, nominal. Sector C showed a thermal drift of four-tenths of a degree over the last cycle, which meant the door seal on rack 12 was weeping again, which meant today would involve gaskets and profanity. She noted it, finished her coffee, and let herself feel the small, clean satisfaction of a day with exactly one problem in it.

This was the peace she'd built, plank by plank, bolt by bolt. Not happiness — she'd stopped using that word around year six — but quiet. Predictability. A life so small it fit inside her own skull without crowding anything.

People from the colony downwell sometimes asked her, over cargo hatches and transaction chits, how she stood it. Alone up there, no company but the machines. She always gave them the same answer, delivered with a shrug she'd practiced until it looked genuine: *I like my own company.*

It wasn't quite a lie. It also wasn't the truth, and she knew the difference the way you know a hairline crack in a load-bearing wall — invisible most days, fatal only if you thought about it too hard.

* * *

By 0900 she had the gasket swapped and her hands smelling of solvent, and the *Magpie's Wake* was telling her, in the patient amber text of an aging freighter's interface, that she had a run to make.

The *Wake* was forty years old and had been resold more times than Odile cared to research. Nine meters of working cargo bay, a drive that idled like a sleeping animal and complained like a waking one, and a transponder registered to a shipping cooperative that had folded six years ago and whose registration renewals Odile paid through a shell account she'd set up in her first month. On paper, the ship barely existed. In practice, it was the only thing besides the vault that answered when she spoke to it.

Today's run was routine, which was the best kind. Downwell to Meridian Colony — the name still made her mouth twist, though she'd never asked anyone why the colony had taken it — with a mixed pallet: medical resin, water reclamation filters, protein stock, and a crate of actual paper books some colonist had ordered from a salvage broker at obscene expense. Fourteen hours' burn each way if she pushed it, and she wouldn't push it, because pushing engines cost fuel and fuel cost money and money was the one language the whole system still spoke fluently.

Her debts were modest and meticulously tracked, the way she tracked everything. Docking fees at Meridian, outstanding. Parts invoice from Ferrow's yard at Ceres Gate, outstanding, accruing interest at a rate Ilsa Ferrow herself had personally described as "friendly, Odile, I promise." Air, water, and calories, self-supplied, free. What she owed the universe beyond that she kept in a different ledger, one she didn't open.

She preflighted the *Wake* by touch, mostly. Throttle response, O2 scrubbers, hull integrity scan that came back green except for the dent on the port quarter she'd stopped seeing years ago. She cast off from the vault's single ancient docking arm at 0940, and as the station fell away behind her — a gray cylinder studded with sensor masts, half its lights dead, looking for all the system like the derelict everyone assumed it was — she felt the familiar loosening in her chest.

Space didn't ask her questions. Space just was, vast and cold and indifferent, and there was a comfort in indifference that company could never match. Out here, contested orbits and all, nothing knew her name.

* * *

The burn downwell was seven hours of nothing, and Odile had learned to love nothing the way sailors love flat calm. She ran diagnostics she didn't need to run. She ate rehydrated noodles out of the pouch. She listened to the drive's rhythm the way you'd listen to a heartbeat — steady, steady, steady — and she let her mind go pleasantly blank.

Somewhere past the halfway mark, drifting on the edge of doze in the pilot's chair, she caught herself humming.

She stopped. Sat up. Tried to remember what the tune was.

She couldn't. It wasn't any song she knew — not a Directorate anthem, not a Drift work chant, none of the tinny pop that bled out of Meridian Colony's public speakers on market days. It was five notes, maybe six, descending, with a pause in the middle like something missing. Like a sentence with a word left out.

She sat with it for a while, the way you'd sit with a strange sound your house makes at night, deciding whether to be afraid. Then she filed it under *fatigue* and forgot it, mostly, for the rest of the burn.

Meridian Colony resolved out of the dark as a cluster of habitat toruses wrapped around a captured asteroid, lit warm gold along its spin axis, traffic beads crawling its approaches. It was the largest settlement in this sector of the Drift — nine thousand souls, give or take, farmers and machinists and teachers and the usual sediment of people who'd come out here to escape something and stayed because their kids had friends. The war touched it lightly these days. Directorate patrols passed at a respectful distance; Drift militia boats loitered with studied casualness; both sides pretended the other wasn't there, and the colony pretended right along with them, because pretending was cheaper than fighting.

Odile hailed traffic control with her call sign and her manifest and got, as always, the bored voice of a controller named Petru who had been bored for approximately the entire decade she'd known him.

"*Magpie's Wake*, you're cleared berth nine, standard approach. Cargo, crew?"

"Cargo fourteen tons mixed. Crew one."

"One." He said it the way he always said it, with that faint note of pity people couldn't quite hide. "See you in twenty, Ashgrove."

Berth nine was at the end of the commercial arm, past the hydroponics windows where the colony grew its greens under full-spectrum light, rows of lettuce and kale glowing like a held breath. Odile docked, cycled, and began the unload with the dock's cargo frame doing the heavy lifting while she rode the pallets and checked seals.

That was when the children arrived.

They came in a loose pack of five or six, the way colony kids did after shift-change, drawn to the docks the way kids everywhere are drawn to ships and strangers and anything that isn't a classroom. Usually they hung back at the safety line and heckled her about her old ship or asked if she'd ever seen a Directorate cruiser up close. Usually she gave them the same dry answers and they lost interest in four minutes flat.

This time they didn't heckle.

They just stood there. Six of them, ages somewhere between seven and eleven, lined along the safety rail, and every one of them staring at her. Not at the ship. At her. With an attention that had weight to it, that pressed against her skin like a change in pressure.

"Take a picture," she said, going for gruff, "it lasts longer."

None of them laughed. A girl at the center — small, dark-haired, maybe eight, with the serious face some children wear like borrowed clothing — tilted her head slowly, the way you'd tilt your head to listen to something very far away.

"You count backwards," the girl said.

Odile's hands went still on the pallet controls. "What?"

"When you're asleep," the girl said. "You count backwards."

"I don't—" Odile looked past them for their parents, their minders, anyone. The dock was busy enough, adults moving freight two berths down, but nobody was watching this. Nobody had noticed six children gone silent and strange all at once. "I think you've got me confused with somebody else, kid. Go home."

The girl didn't move. Neither did the others. And here was the thing Odile would turn over later, again and again, in the small hours in her bunk aboard the *Wake*: it wasn't creepy, exactly, what was in their faces. It was worse than creepy. It was *recognition*. They looked at her the way you look at someone you've been expecting — not surprised to see her, not curious about her, just confirming. Checking her off against something.

"We dreamed you," said a boy at the end of the line, younger, gap-toothed. He said it without drama, the way you'd report the weather. "You were in the green corridor. You had the keys."

"I don't have keys," Odile heard herself say, absurdly, and then hated herself for answering at all, for engaging, for treating it as anything but a colony kid's game.

"The door was locked," the girl said. Her eyes hadn't left Odile's face. "But you knew where it was."

For a moment — a moment she would measure later in heartbeats and find longer than it should have been — the dock noise fell away from Odile entirely. The cargo frame's servos, the distant PA, the hiss of atmosphere handlers, all of it dropped into a hole, and there was just the girl's voice and the strange descending tune rising unbidden in the back of Odile's skull, five notes, six, with the pause in the middle like a held breath.

Then someone's mother appeared at the far end of the arm calling a name — not any of these names — and the spell broke. The children scattered the way children do, sudden and total, as if the last ninety seconds had been erased from their operating memory. The girl went last. She turned at the safety line and looked back at Odile once, and what was in her face then wasn't recognition anymore.

It was apology.

* * *

Odile finished the unload on autopilot. Signed Petru's manifests. Took on return cargo — scrap titanium, a pallet of frozen embryos for the ag-coop, mail — and cast off berth nine at 1930 local, and it wasn't until she was through the colony's traffic shell and alone in the black that she let herself stop moving.

She floated in the pilot's chair with the drive cold and the cabin quiet, and she made herself review it like a diagnostic, because diagnostics were what she had instead of people.

Six children. Identical behavior. Identical statements. *Counting backwards. Green corridor. Locked door.* Not a game — games have variation, escalation, laughter. This had been recitation. Reportage. Six separate kids delivering the same inventory of details about a stranger they'd never met, with the flat certainty of witnesses describing the same accident.

And underneath it, humming in her own head like a fault tone: that tune. Which she had been humming before the children ever opened their mouths. Seven hours earlier, alone in the *Wake*, halfway down a burn through empty space.

Coincidence. She wrote the word in her mental log and underlined it. Fatigue, suggestibility, colony kids being weird because colony kids are always weird, and a middle-aged woman whose brain filled silence with nonsense melodies. There was no pattern here. Patterns were what lonely people found in static, and she was not lonely. She had chosen this. She had built this quiet plank by plank and it fit her exactly and she liked her own company and—

The comms panel chimed. An automated notice, station-relay formatted, the kind of thing she got a dozen of a week: traffic advisories, tariff updates, the cooperative's ghost-registration reminders.

This one was addressed to her by name. Not to the *Wake*'s call sign. To *Ashgrove, O., Caretaker, Halcyon Vault.*

No sender. No origin stamp. The routing header showed it had bounced through six relays across four jurisdictions, laundered so thoroughly that tracing it would take a forensic team and a warrant, and the message body was one line long:

*The corridor is green. The door is locked. Begin counting.*

Odile stared at it for a long time. Long enough for the cabin temp to drop half a degree. Long enough for the *Wake*, sensing her stillness, to dim the console lights to night mode, patient as an old dog.

Then, very quietly, in the empty ship, in the empty dark, in the twelfth year of her perfect and deliberate solitude, Odile Ashgrove began — without deciding to, without wanting to, her lips moving around numbers she had no memory of learning —

to count backwards.
