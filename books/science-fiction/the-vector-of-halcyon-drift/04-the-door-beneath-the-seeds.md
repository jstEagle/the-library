## 4. The Door Beneath the Seeds

The signal had been coming from beneath her feet for three days before Odile worked up the nerve to do anything about it.

She told herself she was being methodical. That was the lie she'd chosen, and like most of the lies she told herself, it was comfortable enough to sleep in. A caretaker didn't rush. A caretaker checked atmospheric seals twice, logged humidity readings in triplicate, walked every corridor of the Halcyon seed vault on a schedule so fixed that her own body had become a clock for it. Methodical. Deliberate. Twelve years of small careful motions layered over whatever it was she'd been before.

But at 0300 station time, standing in the dark of her bunk with the signal's waveform projected against the ceiling — that soft repeating pulse, rising and falling like something breathing under a blanket — she wasn't being methodical. She was being afraid.

* * *

The vault's public schematics showed four levels. Germination halls, cryoseed stacks, the climate core, and the maintenance ring where Odile kept her quarters and her ship's docking cradle. Four levels, clean as a hymn. She'd walked them all ten thousand times. She knew which floor panels creaked, which airlocks whistled in the cold cycle, which of the ancient grow-lamps still held a ghost of charge after a century of nobody needing them.

The schematic in the maintenance terminal's deep archive showed something else.

It took her two hours of digging to find it, because whoever had built the file had buried it under a shell directory labeled with a decommissioned coolant system's designation — boring enough that even a diligent caretaker would skim past it. But Odile had spent twelve years learning this place the way you learn a face, and the mass figures had never added up. She'd noticed it years ago, in her first month, and had filed the discrepancy away in the same drawer where she kept everything else about herself she didn't want to look at: the sublevel footprint was forty percent larger than the structure above it.

A building can't be bigger inside than out. Unless part of it isn't in the building at all.

The access shaft was behind the old nutrient tank farm, behind a panel she'd repainted twice without ever wondering why the seam lines didn't match the others. Her hands knew the panel was wrong before her mind admitted it — some animal part of her had been cataloguing that mismatch for a decade, the way you know a room is missing a picture before you know which picture.

She stood in front of it for a long time, headlamp off, letting her eyes adjust to the emergency strips' amber glow. The vault hummed around her, its thousand systems ticking through their patient cycles, and underneath the hum — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable now that she was this close — the pulse.

*Like a heartbeat,* she thought, and hated herself for thinking it, because hearts belonged to things that were alive, and this was a building, and buildings did not have heartbeats, and if she started down that road she was going to end up somewhere she couldn't come back from.

She opened the panel anyway.

* * *

The shaft dropped eight meters, rungs worn smooth by hands that hadn't existed in her lifetime. At the bottom, a corridor. Not vault architecture — she knew vault architecture down to the bolt pattern. This was older, denser, built by people who'd expected their work to matter for centuries. The walls were lined in a composite she recognized from Directorate hull plating, which made no sense at all, because the Halcyon vault predated the Directorate's presence in the Drift by thirty years, and because nothing Directorate should be buried under a colonial seed repository like a body under floorboards.

Her light found the door at the corridor's end.

It was round, two meters across, matte grey, seamless except for a single recessed panel at chest height. No handle. No lock mechanism she could identify. Just the panel, and above it, stenciled in faded white lettering that had been applied by hand — someone had stood here, someone with a stencil and a spray can and a sense of obligation — the words:

**CARETAKER ACCESS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**

Odile laughed. It came out of her like something breaking.

Twelve years. Twelve years of tending seeds no one would ever plant, of logging temperatures in rooms no one would ever enter, of keeping a lighthouse lit for ships that had stopped sailing past. She'd thought the loneliness was the price of the work. She'd thought the emptiness was the point — that the Directorate paid her a caretaker's stipend because someone, somewhere, believed in the sanctity of a seed bank, in the idea of a future worth refrigerating.

And the whole time, there had been a door. And the door had been waiting for her specifically.

Her palm hovered over the panel. The pulse was louder here, close enough now that she could feel it faintly through her boot soles, up through the bones of her legs, a rhythm just slightly slower than a human heart. Resting rate. Sleeping rate.

"No," she said aloud, to the corridor, to herself. "Whatever you are, you're not alive."

The panel warmed under her hovering hand before she touched it. Read her. And opened.

* * *

The room beyond was cold in a way the vault never got — not the dry chill of preservation but the deep, mineral cold of rock that has never known sunlight. Her breath fogged. Her headlamp beam swept across a space larger than the entire germination hall above, and what it found made her stop walking entirely.

Racks. Hundreds of them, receding into the dark, holding row upon row of sealed canisters — and she knew canisters, she'd inventoried ten thousand of them upstairs, but these were wrong. Too large. Too dense. Each one plumbed into the racks with umbilicals of frosted cable, each one fitted with a viewport the size of her spread hand.

She looked through the nearest viewport and saw, suspended in amber fluid, a human thumb.

Just the thumb. Regrown tissue, pristine, tagged with a code she didn't recognize. She stepped back. Checked the next. A section of cornea. The next: a loop of intestine, coiled neat as rope. The next rack over, deeper rows: whole organs, whole limbs, and then — her light shaking now, her breath coming too fast in the freezing air — whole bodies. Small ones.

Children. Dozens of children, folded into the canisters like letters into envelopes, faces slack and blue-pale, chests motionless behind the glass.

Odile put her hand against a rack strut to steady herself and the metal was warm. Everything in this room was warm. The cold was a costume; underneath it, the whole sublevel ran hot as a living thing, and the pulse she'd been hearing for three days came from everywhere at once — from the floors, the walls, the racks, the small sleeping shapes — one shared rhythm, one shared breath, a hundred hearts keeping time together because they had been tuned to keep time together.

She was running for the door when her light caught the console.

It sat alone in an alcove near the chamber's center, a single workstation, active, its screen glowing with a prompt that had apparently been waiting a very long time:

**FOR THE CARETAKER. PLAY? Y/N**

Her finger hovered. Some last instinct told her to go back up, seal the panel, repaint it, log a routine inspection, and spend the rest of her life not knowing. That instinct had kept her alive through twelve quiet years and it was screaming.

She pressed Y.

* * *

The screen filled with a face.

A woman, middle-aged, silver at the temples, wearing the plain grey of someone who had stopped caring what her clothes said about her. Behind her, shelves of equipment Odile half-recognized from the racks surrounding her. The recording's timestamp, when Odile forced herself to look at it, was sixty-one years old.

"If you're watching this," the woman said, "then the selection protocol worked, which means I owe you an apology and an explanation, in whichever order you can stomach."

Odile stood very still. On the screen, the woman leaned toward the pickup, and her eyes were tired in a way Odile recognized from her own mirror — the tiredness of someone carrying a weight they've decided not to set down.

"My name is Dr. Ines Okonkwo-Varga. I was chief archivist of the Halcyon Deep Storage Facility. You know it as a seed vault. It is not a seed vault. It was never a seed vault. The seeds upstairs are real — we needed something true to hide behind, and nothing hides better than something boring — but the seeds are the envelope. You are looking at the letter."

The camera panned briefly, showing the racks, the canisters, the small pale shapes. Then back to her face.

"What you're seeing is the Continuity Archive. When the war began — the real beginning, not the one in your history files — a group of us understood that both sides were going to burn the record of how it started. Earth would burn it to protect itself. The Drift would burn it to protect itself. And the thing that actually started it would burn it simply by continuing to operate. So we built a place where the truth could be kept the way you keep seeds: cold, patient, and alive."

Alive. The word landed in Odile's chest like a dropped tool.

"The archive is not data," the woman continued. "Data can be forged, intercepted, corrupted. Data can be denied. The archive is witnesses. Genomes, yes — but also cultured neural tissue, memory scaffolds, full biological continuity for two hundred and six individuals who saw what we saw. They are not dead. They are not asleep, exactly. They are *held*. And one of them—" the woman paused, and her voice changed, softened, the way voices change around names that hurt, "—one of them is not an adult. One of them is the key."

The screen cut to a second image: a single canister, isolated in its own alcove, apart from the ranks of the others. Viewport lit from within. And inside, floating in amber light, a girl. Six, maybe seven years old. Dark hair drifting around a face that was, impossibly, not peaceful — the brow faintly furrowed, the lips slightly parted, as though even in suspension she was mid-sentence, mid-question, mid-dream.

"Her designation is WRN-1. We called her Wren. She was grown, not born — assembled from the archive's own records, a living index, a witness designed to carry everything the others hold. If the archive is ever threatened, she wakes. If the archive is ever needed, she wakes. And when she dreams—"

The woman stopped. Looked directly into the lens, directly at Odile, across sixty-one years, and Odile felt her skin prickle.

"When she dreams, she broadcasts. That was the failsafe we could never fully test. If you are seeing this recording because you followed a signal — a repeating pattern, a frequency, something that felt like a voice counting — then the failsafe has fired. Which means the archive believes it is dying. Or that the time has come. We honestly could not design it to tell the difference, and I am sorry for that. I am sorry for all of it."

The woman reached toward the camera. The image blurred, steadied.

"One more thing, caretaker. You were selected, not hired. The position was posted twelve years ago through channels you will not be able to trace, and the person we chose was chosen for reasons that will become clear, or won't, depending on how much of this you can bear. Check the roster. Check your own file. And then decide — quickly, because if the failsafe has fired, you will not be the only one who heard the signal — decide whether you are the kind of person who guards what hides, or the kind who lets it out."

The screen went dark.

In the silence that followed, the only sound in the vast cold-warm chamber was the pulse — slower now, Odile realized. Slower than it had been three days ago. Slower than it had been an hour ago. Counting down, or winding down, or simply waiting, the way a held breath waits.

She turned toward the isolated alcove. Toward the small furrowed brow behind the glass. Toward the girl who was dreaming loud enough to reach through a planet's crust and into the heads of children she'd never met.

"You're the message," Odile whispered. Her breath fogged the viewport, and for one absurd moment the condensation made it look like the girl was behind a window on a winter morning, close enough to touch. "Somebody built you to be a message."

The girl's eyelids moved. A flicker, fast as a fish turning under ice. Rapid eye movement. Dreaming. Actively, urgently dreaming, sixty-one years into a sleep that was supposed to be patient.

And beneath Odile's boots, faint but unmistakable, the pulse skipped — stumbled, caught itself, resumed — one beat short.

Somewhere above her, through forty meters of rock and steel, seven children on the colony were dreaming of a green corridor and a locked door and a voice counting backward.

The number, Odile understood suddenly, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with the floor, was getting smaller.

She put her palm flat against the canister's warm glass, alone in the dark with two hundred and six sleeping witnesses and one waking dream, and heard herself say, in a voice she didn't recognize as her own:

"Okay. Okay. I'm the caretaker. What do you need me to do?"

The console screen flickered back to life.

**WRN-1 STASIS INTEGRITY: 61%. DECLINING.**

**WAKE PROTOCOL AVAILABLE. RECOMMENDED.**

**RECOMMENDED BY WHOM?** she almost typed. But her hand was already moving, and the answer, when it came, arrived not on any screen but through the soles of her feet, through her knees, her spine, her ribs — the pulse shifting, quickening, gathering itself like a runner in the blocks — and in the alcove before her, behind the fogged glass, the little girl's eyes moved again, faster now, chasing something through the green.

Odile's finger found the wake protocol's confirmation.

She hesitated exactly one breath — long enough to understand that whatever happened next, the twelve quiet years were over, and the woman she'd been pretending to be was about to be opened like the panel behind the nutrient tanks, like the door, like everything else in her life that had been waiting for her to stop being afraid —

— and pressed it.
