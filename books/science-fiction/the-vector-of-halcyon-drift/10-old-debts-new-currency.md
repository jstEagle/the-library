## 10. Old Debts, New Currency

The station was called Tallow's Rest, which was a lie twice over — there was no tallow and nobody rested there — and it hung in the shadow of a dead gas giant like a tick on the underside of something vast and indifferent. Odile brought the *Marrow* in dark, transponder cold, riding the giant's magnetic noise the way she'd once ridden her mother's hip: close, familiar, pretending not to be afraid.

Wren slept in the aft compartment, strapped into the cot Odile had rigged from cargo netting and an old acceleration couch. She slept the way she did everything — as if from somewhere else, as if sleep were a country she visited rather than lived in. Twice during the burn in, Odile had heard her counting. Backward. Softly. The numbers falling out of her like coins from a torn pocket.

*Forty-one. Forty. Thirty-nine.*

Odile had stopped asking what came after one. She wasn't sure she wanted to know.

She docked at Berth Nine, the berth that didn't officially exist, paid the berthing drone in unmarked scrip, and stood for a long moment at the airlock with her hand flat against the hull of her own ship. Twelve years she'd kept the *Marrow* clean. Clean manifests, clean transits, clean silence. A woman with no debts drew no attention, and a woman with no attention stayed alive. It had been the closest thing to a philosophy she'd ever held.

Now she was about to walk into Ilsa Ferrow's den and spend the only currency she'd sworn never to touch.

Her Directorate clearance codes sat in a shard drive against her sternum, warm from her own body heat, feeling less like a key and more like a splinter working its way toward her heart.

* * *

Tallow's Rest had been a refinery once, back when anyone bothered refining anything this far out. Its bones were industrial and its flesh was improvised — hab modules bolted into the old processing spines, hydroponic racks growing things that were technically food, corridors that smelled of coolant, fry oil, and the particular ozone tang of too many illegal power taps. The gravity was a compromise. The lighting was a rumor. Every third person watched her with the flat professional interest of someone deciding whether she was a customer or a problem.

Ilsa Ferrow kept court in the old foreman's office, three decks up, behind a door that had been painted to look like a maintenance hatch and guarded by a woman built like a docking clamp. The guard looked Odile over, then at the plain courier bag she carried, then at her face again.

"She's expecting me," Odile said.

"Everyone says that."

"Tell her the seed vault's caretaker is here."

Something flickered in the guard's expression — not recognition exactly, but the memory of a name filed under *weird*. She touched her collar comms, murmured four words, and listened. Then she stepped aside with the exaggerated courtesy of a person who has just learned something interesting and intends to be paid for it later.

The office beyond was smaller than its reputation. Ilsa Ferrow worked at a desk made from a decommissioned engine cowling, and she was exactly what twelve years of freight gossip had promised and nothing like it at all: small, gray-haired, precise, with reading glasses pushed up into her curls and hands that moved like she was always mid-transaction even when they were still. Her eyes were the color of old money and just as patient.

"The caretaker," Ilsa said. "Sit down. You look like a woman carrying something heavy."

"It's light," Odile said, sitting. "That's the problem."

Ilsa smiled without warmth and gestured at the teapot on her desk — actual tea, actual ceramic, both extravagances that said more about her reach than any fleet of ships could have. "Drink?"

"No."

"More for me." She poured, unhurried, and Odile understood the performance for what it was: Ilsa Ferrow never rushed, because rushing told people you needed them. "So. The whole Drift is whispering about you, caretaker. A ghost ship running dark out of Halcyon. A child aboard who sleeps like the dead and talks like a countdown. Two fleets pulling their patrol patterns apart looking for you, and every smuggler, broker, and bounty skimmer between here and the Belt sharpening their knives." She sipped. "And you come to me. Which means either you're very smart or very desperate."

"I'm paying," Odile said. "Does it matter which?"

"It matters enormously." Ilsa set down the cup. "Smart clients negotiate. Desperate clients accept terms. And clients who are both—" she tilted her head, "—those are the ones who end up owning half my routes or owing me their lives. Sometimes both. So before we talk price, tell me what you're actually selling. Because I know what you're buying. Safe passage for two through contested space, off the grids, past both fleets' picket lines, to a destination you haven't said aloud yet. That's the most expensive ticket in the system right now. What's in the bag?"

Odile took out the shard drive. Set it on the engine-cowling desk between them, where it lay small and black and ordinary, like a beetle.

"One full set of active Directorate clearance codes," she said. "Deep-network tier. They'll open supply depots, dry-dock queues, customs seals, and at least three orbital transfer windows that officially don't exist. They're live. They'll stay live until the Directorate notices they've been used, which — if whoever uses them is careful — could be months."

Ilsa didn't touch it. That was the discipline, Odile thought. Everyone else would have snatched it already.

"And where does a seed vault caretaker get deep-network clearance codes?" Ilsa asked softly.

"That's not part of the sale."

"Everything is part of the sale. That's the first law of commerce and the second law of survival." But she still didn't touch the drive. Instead she leaned back, and her eyes went distant in a way that Odile recognized — the look of a woman doing arithmetic with other people's futures. "You understand what you're handing me. These aren't just keys. They're provenance. A live deep-tier code set means someone inside the Directorate either died, defected, or is being robbed blind. Any of those three makes you the most dangerous kind of merchandise. Not because of what you have. Because of what you *know*."

"I don't know anything," Odile said. "That's the truth. I woke up twelve years ago with a contract, a ship, and a vault, and I've been doing my job ever since. Whatever else I was, it got left behind somewhere I can't find."

"Liar," Ilsa said, without malice, almost fondly. "But a good liar only lies about the important things, so I believe you believe it." She picked up the drive at last, turned it over once in her fingers like a jeweler checking for flaws, and set it down again. "Here's my problem, caretaker. This is worth more than safe passage. This is worth a fleet refit, or a decade of route monopolies, or enough leverage to make three sector governors dance on strings. And you're offering it to me for a boat ride. Either you don't know its value — unlikely, given you guarded it for twelve years — or you need passage so badly that value has stopped meaning anything to you."

Odile looked at her hands. They were steady. She'd made sure of that before walking in; a trembling negotiator was a dead negotiator. But inside, where steadiness couldn't reach, she felt the thing she'd been refusing to feel since the vault sublevel: grief. Clean, simple grief, the kind you feel when you sell a house you grew up in, or burn letters you were supposed to keep forever.

Twelve years of neutrality. Twelve years of being no one, of hauling honest cargo on dishonest routes, of tending seeds that were never going to be planted, of keeping her head down and her record clean so that whatever she'd been before could stay buried. And now she was digging it up and trading it by weight.

"I need to get her somewhere neither fleet can reach," Odile said. "Somewhere with a broadcast array big enough to matter, and people stubborn enough to use it. And I need it fast, because there's a number dropping, and when it hits zero, everything changes whether we're ready or not."

"The countdown." Ilsa's voice had gone very quiet. "My people say children all across the Drift are dreaming the same dream. My niece is one of them. She told her mother the green corridor was 'almost done.' Almost done with *what*, caretaker?"

"I don't know. But I'm carrying the reason, and both fleets want her, and one of them wants her badly enough that they've burned protocol chasing us. If they take her, the message dies with her, and whatever's coming comes unwitnessed. Unwitnessed, it becomes their story. Their story started a war once already." Odile met Ilsa's eyes. "You've run cargo long enough to know that wars aren't started by accidents. They're started by people who benefit. Somebody benefits from this one staying dark."

For a long moment the only sound was the station itself — the groan of old metal flexing against thermal drift, the hum of stolen power, somewhere below a burst of laughter cut short by a slammed door.

"You said 'started a war once already,'" Ilsa said. "Not 'could start.'"

Odile froze, one heartbeat too long.

Ilsa smiled, slow and terrible, and Odile understood she'd just handed over more than she'd meant to — that the whole conversation had been a scale, and Ilsa had been weighing her words instead of her goods. "There it is," Ilsa murmured. "You know something about the war's beginning. Something specific. Something that scared you enough to dig up codes you'd rather have died holding." She tapped the shard drive once, lightly, like tapping a keg. "This isn't payment anymore, caretaker. This is a down payment. On information."

"The codes buy passage. Full stop."

"The codes buy passage," Ilsa agreed pleasantly, "and your terror buys my curiosity, and my curiosity is the most expensive thing I own. Here are my terms, and they're generous, so listen. One: I move you and the girl myself — my ship, my crew, my routes, dark the whole way. Two: you tell me your destination when we're underway, not before, and I'll pretend that doesn't insult me. Three: the codes are mine, free and clear, and you'll answer one question whenever I ask it — not today, maybe not this month, but someday, and you won't lie when I do."

"And if I refuse the third term?"

"Then the codes are worth half, and half of priceless is merely enormous, and you'll take enormous because you have no better offer, and we'll both hate the transaction." Ilsa spread her small, precise hands. "Those are the terms. Take them or go find a braver fool."

Odile sat with it. Around her, the office felt suddenly like the inside of a lock — tumblers waiting, springs loaded, everything depending on whether she turned the right way. She thought about Wren asleep in the aft compartment, counting backward to a zero that meant *something*, and how tenderness had arrived in her life disguised as a cargo manifest, and how every road forward now ran through somebody's greed.

Every ally has a price tag. Fine. At least Ilsa's tag was printed clearly.

"The question," Odile said. "When you ask it. I answer honestly. But I choose *when* — within reason. Not while I'm bleeding, not while she's in danger."

"Accepted." Ilsa extended her hand across the engine cowling, and her grip was dry and strong and final. "Welcome to Ferrow Lines, caretaker. We're a small family concern. We keep our promises and our ledgers, in that order."

They shook once. Then Ilsa slid the shard drive into a pocket of her coat, and Odile watched twelve years of careful anonymity disappear into gray wool, and felt — unexpectedly — lighter. Lighter and emptier, the way a room feels after the furniture's been sold.

"One more thing," Ilsa said, as Odile rose. "Professional courtesy. Before I commit ships to this, I want to know what I'm smuggling. Not the girl herself — I've smuggled stranger cargo, and prettier. The *message*. You said both fleets want her. Fleets want lots of things. But my sources say the Directorate's chase orders came down with a classification header nobody's seen since the war's first month. An old header. A header that was supposed to be retired with everyone who remembered why it existed." Her eyes had gone sharp behind the mildness. "Which tells me this goes back further than you think, caretaker. Maybe further than you *can* think. So when my question comes — and it will come — you should start preparing your answer now. Because I have a feeling it's going to be about where you got those codes. And I have a stronger feeling that you don't actually know."

Odile stood very still.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. The splinter under all the others. She'd carried those codes for twelve years like a birthmark — assumed, unquestioned, hers. But she couldn't remember receiving them. Couldn't remember the issuance, the authorization, the face of the officer who'd handed them over. Her memory of her own past was a warehouse she'd never audited, and she'd just sold the one item in it whose provenance she couldn't prove.

"I'll be ready," she said, and hated how much it sounded like a prayer.

"See that you are." Ilsa returned to her tea, already dismissing her, already elsewhere — a woman with new assets to move. "Your ship's cleared to lift on my mark. Berth Nine stays dark. And caretaker—" Odile paused at the door. "—the girl. When she counts in her sleep. Does she ever say what happens at zero?"

"Not yet."

"Hm." Ilsa sipped. "My niece says it's a door."

* * *

The corridor back to Berth Nine was longer on the way out; it always was. Odile walked it past the fry stalls and the power-tap sparks and the watchers, and felt the station's gravity pressing her down like a hand reminding her of her place. In her chest, where the shard drive had rested against her sternum for twelve years, there was a strange hollow coolness, like a tooth pulled.

She'd spent her past. That was the shape of it. Not lost it, not escaped it — spent it, converted it, fed it into the machinery of someone else's ambition because it was the only coin she carried. Somewhere ahead, Ilsa Ferrow was already thinking about what a deep-tier code set could open, and somewhere behind, the Directorate was already noticing that its keys had begun to walk, and somewhere between those two facts, Odile Ashgrove was going to learn exactly what her buried life had been worth — and to whom.

At the airlock, Wren was awake. She stood at the little viewport in the *Marrow*'s inner hatch, small and pale in the emergency lighting, watching the dead gas giant turn.

"Auntie Odile," she said — she'd started calling her that three days ago, unprompted, and Odile had not corrected it and did not intend to — "the number went down again."

Odile's mouth went dry. "How far?"

Wren frowned, the way she did when translating between worlds. "It skips now. It used to go one at a time. Now it goes... in pieces." She held up her hands, measuring something invisible. "Like it's hurrying."

"When?"

"Soon." Wren turned back to the viewport, and her reflection in the glass looked older than her years and younger at once, a child wearing the expression of a witness. "The voice sounds scared now. It didn't used to."

Outside, on the dark side of the station, a ship began to undock — Ilsa's vessel, running lights dead, sliding away from Tallow's Rest like a fish leaving a reef. And tucked in its wake, silent and patient, went the *Marrow*, carrying a caretaker with empty pockets, a sleeping archive with a deadline, and a debt to a smuggler queen that wouldn't come due as money.

It would come due as a question. And Odile was already afraid of the answer.
