## 15. The Price of the Codes

Ilsa Ferrow had run contraband through eleven jurisdictions and outlived three bounty contracts, and she had learned one law above all others: nothing is free, and everything has a price tag you don't see until the invoice arrives.

The invoice arrived on a Tuesday.

She was in her office aboard *Cormorant's Rest*, the converted ore hauler that served as her flagship, her counting house, and occasionally her courtroom, when the first ping came through — a priority channel she kept for exactly three people, all of whom were supposed to be dead or imprisoned. It wasn't any of them. It was a market feed: Directorate Depot Kestrel-9, outer belt, had gone dark for six hours and come back up missing forty tons of military-grade fusion catalysts, two armored shuttles, and the personal effects of a garrison that had been, by every report, asleep at its post.

Forty tons of catalyst. That was not a smuggling quantity. That was a war-starting quantity.

Ilsa read the feed twice, then a third time, and felt the particular cold that comes when you recognize your own fingerprints on someone else's crime.

Because she knew who'd had access to a hole in Kestrel-9's perimeter. She knew because she'd sold it — or rather, she'd sold what it opened. Three weeks ago, a woman with tired eyes and a clean record twelve years deep had sat across Ilsa's desk and pushed a data chip across the scarred metal surface like a penitent laying down coins. Directorate clearance codes. Live ones. Tier-four logistics authentication, the kind of keys that opened supply-chain doors all across the inner system without tripping a single alarm, because they didn't look like break-ins. They looked like paperwork.

*What do you want for them?* Odile Ashgrove had asked, and Ilsa had named a price — safe passage for herself and a child through contested orbits, no questions, no logs — and Ashgrove had paid it without haggling, which should have been Ilsa's first warning. Smugglers haggle. People spending something they're ashamed of don't.

She'd told herself the codes would sit in a drawer. Insurance, maybe. A bargaining chip for a woman running from something she wouldn't name. Ilsa had taken them, verified them, and — because she was Ilsa Ferrow, and because a key that opens one door can open every door shaped like it — she'd made copies.

That was the second warning she'd ignored.

* * *

By Wednesday, the shape of it was clear.

Kestrel-9 hadn't been robbed by ghosts. The raid had come in on authenticated logistics channels — ships broadcasting valid Directorate resupply signatures, waving through checkpoints with tier-four credentials, walking into the depot's belly like family. The garrison hadn't been asleep. They'd been *expecting a delivery*. By the time anyone realized the delivery was armed, the catalysts were gone and two depot security officers were dead, and the whole thing had the neat, ugly signature of professionals using someone else's keys.

And Ilsa knew whose keys, because only four copies existed. One in her vault. Two in dead men's pockets — she'd checked, quietly, over two days of increasingly unpleasant calls. And one set that had left her possession nine days ago, sold to the Vaskar Combine, her nearest rivals in the belt lanes, in exchange for a truce she'd needed more than she'd admitted.

She hadn't given them the codes herself. She'd traded *access to* them — a brokered introduction, a cut of whatever came through. Standard practice. Clean hands, dirty ledger.

Except now there were dead men at Kestrel-9, and the Directorate did not do nuance.

The retaliation orders came down Thursday morning, broadcast wide enough that even neutral stations caught the edge of them: unauthorized use of Directorate authentication protocols constituted an act of hostile action under wartime statute, and any station, vessel, or habitat found harboring parties connected to the Kestrel incident would be subject to interdiction, seizure, and — this was the word that made Ilsa's coffee go cold in her hand — *escalatory response*.

Escalatory response. In Directorate language, that meant they'd stop distinguishing between the thieves and everyone floating near the thieves. It meant quarantine blockades. It meant cargo holds sealed and crews detained. It meant, if history was any guide — and Ilsa had a long memory for Directorate history — that a few neutral stations were going to be made examples of, because fear was cheaper than investigation and examples traveled faster than justice.

She spent Thursday afternoon doing arithmetic she hated.

Three neutral stations sat within easy reach of the Vaskar Combine's operating range. Ferro Landing, which took Ilsa's fuel and asked no questions. The Tannhäuser Ring, half-market, half-refugee camp, home to eleven thousand people who'd fled both sides of the war and wanted nothing but to be left alone. And Little Ceres, a mining habitat so old it predated the war entirely, its tunnels carved by men and women who'd never seen Earth and never wanted to.

Any of the three could be chosen. All of the three could be chosen. The Directorate didn't need proof to make an example; it needed proximity and a population too weak to object.

Ilsa stood at her office window, looking out at the traffic lanes of Cormorant's Rest — tugs and haulers and a passenger skiff painted like a carnival — and understood, with the clarity that only arrives after the damage is done, exactly what she had done.

She'd treated a woman's shame as inventory. She'd priced it, copied it, and moved it down the chain like any other commodity, and now the commodity had turned into corpses and a retaliation order, and the interest was compounding by the hour. Every hour the codes stayed live, another door opened somewhere. Every door opened was another entry in the Directorate's case file against *everyone*. She had thought she was selling a key. She'd actually been selling a fuse, and she'd handed the lit end to the Vaskars, who were many things but careful was not among them.

"Gerta," she said to her comm. "Get me everything on the Vaskar movement patterns since the ninth. Ship registrations, transponder gaps, fuel purchases. And find out where Odile Ashgrove is right now."

"You think she's still alive?" Gerta asked. Gerta had earned the right to ask blunt questions twenty years ago, when she'd pulled Ilsa out of a burning airlock on Titan Station.

"I think," Ilsa said slowly, "that she sold me those codes knowing what they were worth, which means she knew what they could do, which means either she's the most reckless woman in the Drift or she's desperate enough not to care. And desperate women with secrets tend to be in motion." She paused. "Also, pull our own copy of the codes. I want them ready to burn."

"Burn? Those are worth—"

"They're worth a tribunal seat and a firing squad, depending on which side writes the verdict. Get them ready."

* * *

The Vaskar picture assembled itself over Friday like a slow-motion accident.

Four raids, not one. Kestrel-9 had just been the loudest. A communications relay at Meridian Point — stripped of its black-box cores. An unmanned munitions cache in the Trove cluster, emptied so cleanly the Directorate didn't notice for thirty hours. And a hospital ship, *Mercy of Tycho*, boarded at a scheduled resupply stop by men carrying valid Directorate medical-logistics credentials, who took its entire stock of burn grafts and antiradiation therapeutics and left the crew zip-tied in the chapel.

The hospital ship was where Ilsa stopped being able to breathe evenly.

She'd known the Vaskars were ruthless. Everyone in the trade knew. But taking medicine off a hospital ship wasn't ruthlessness; it was a different category entirely, and it meant the codes weren't just a tool anymore, they were a license, and licenses get used until they're revoked. Every hour those tier-four authentications stayed valid, some new atrocity could walk through a checkpoint wearing the Directorate's own face.

And the Directorate would not blame the Vaskars. The Directorate would blame the *source*. Leaked credentials meant an inside leak, and inside leaks meant purges, and purges meant sweeping nets thrown across every station, every freight lane, every independent operator who'd ever done business in the gray. Her name would surface. Ashgrove's name would surface. And between them, like a coin dropped into a machine, the whole apparatus of the war would grind one notch tighter — more blockades, more seizures, more reasons for both fleets to treat the space between them as a shooting gallery.

Ilsa had spent twenty years building a reputation as the woman who kept the lanes open. Neutral ground, fair prices, no questions. It wasn't idealism; it was business model. War was bad for shipping. Fear was bad for shipping. And she had just personally manufactured a shipment of fear with a forty-ton manifest.

"Found her," Gerta said, appearing in the doorway with a tablet. "Or found her wake trail, anyway. Ashgrove's ship — the *Marrow's Grace*, old hull, patched transponder — docked at Ferro Landing two days ago. Took on water and oxygen, paid cash, left inside six hours. Heading puts her on a drift toward the Halcyon approaches."

"The seed vault," Ilsa said. "Of course. She's going back to her hole." She stared at the trajectory line crawling across the display. "Gerta, how fast can we get a message to her that the Directorate can't read?"

"Depends how paranoid you want to be."

"Assume she's already paranoid. Assume everyone's reading everything. I want it buried in something boring. A freight quote. A customs notice. Something she'll open because it looks like money."

Gerta tapped at the tablet. "There's a standard hazard advisory we push to all contract haulers. Weather in the lanes, piracy reports, that sort. Nobody reads them, but everybody opens them, because skipping one has bitten people before." She looked up. "I can embed a private layer in the header. Old Titan Station cipher. She'd need to know it exists, though."

"She'll know it exists," Ilsa said. "Anyone who's been running dark as long as she has checks headers. It's the first place I'd look." She took the tablet and began to compose, deleting her first draft twice before settling on the plainest possible language, because there was no clever way to say what needed saying:

*The keys you gave me are loose. They've hit a depot, a relay, and a hospital ship. Directorate is calling it hostile action. Retaliation will fall on neutrals — Ferro, Tannhäuser, Little Ceres are all in the blast radius. If any copy of those codes traces back to you, it won't be a warrant, it'll be a fleet. Burn anything you have. Tell me where you got them and who else knows. This isn't a favor I'm offering. It's a fire alarm.*

She read it four times. Then she added a fifth sentence, because honesty was a currency too, and she'd learned late in life that spending it early was cheaper than spending it late:

*I copied them. That's on me. But I didn't pull the triggers, and neither did you, and whoever did wants us both holding the bag.*

"Send it," she said.

* * *

The reply came back in nineteen hours, embedded in a routine cargo manifest acknowledgment, and it was four words long:

*Meet me. Old debts.*

No coordinates. No time. Just a reference to a phrase they'd used once, across Ilsa's desk, when Ashgrove had explained why she trusted a smuggler queen with the most dangerous secret in the system: *You settle old debts straight. That's your reputation.* Ilsa had laughed at the time. Nobody in her line of work settled anything straight. But Ashgrove had believed it, or needed to believe it, and now she was invoking it, which meant she was coming in scared and honest, and scared-and-honest was the most dangerous combination Ilsa knew how to handle.

She set the meet for the Tannhäuser Ring — deliberately, coldly, choosing the station most likely to catch fire if things went wrong, because if the Directorate was going to make an example of somebody anyway, better it happen while she was watching than while she was elsewhere. There was a tea house on the Ring's third arm, run by a widow named Oksana who owed Ilsa more than money and asked fewer questions than anyone alive. Back booth. Sight lines to two exits. Standard.

Ashgrove arrived looking worse than the last time Ilsa had seen her, which Ilsa hadn't thought possible. The woman had always carried exhaustion like a coat — put it on, take it off, never quite shed it. Now it was fused to her. She slid into the booth opposite Ilsa, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because the tea house was full of ordinary people drinking ordinary tea, and the conversation they needed to have could end worlds.

"You got my message," Ilsa said.

"I got your message." Ashgrove wrapped her hands around a cup she didn't drink from. "You copied my codes."

"I did. And I moved them, indirectly, to people I shouldn't have, and I'm telling you that to your face because you deserve to hear it from me before you hear it from a tribunal transcript." Ilsa held her gaze. "The Vaskars hit Kestrel-9 with your authentication. Also a relay, a munitions cache, and the *Mercy of Tycho*. Forty tons of catalyst. Medicine off a hospital ship. The Directorate has classified it as hostile action by unknown actors using compromised internal credentials. Which means they think they have a mole problem, and when the Directorate thinks it has a mole problem, it stops investigating and starts burning."

"I know." Ashgrove's voice was flat. "I've seen the interdiction notices. My last three contract offers came back flagged."

"Then you know what's coming. Tannhäuser. Little Ceres. Maybe Ferro. They'll pick whichever one gives them the best footage." Ilsa leaned forward. "So here's my question, and I want the truth, because the truth is the only thing left in this room that's worth anything. Where did those codes come from? Not who you stole them from — I don't care about that. Where did they *come from*? Because tier-four logistics authentication doesn't exist in a vacuum. It's issued, tracked, rotated. Someone inside the Directorate either lost them or let them go, and if someone let them go, then this raid wasn't a raid. It was an invitation. Somebody wanted the Vaskars to walk through that door wearing Directorate keys."

Ashgrove was quiet for a long moment. Around them, the tea house murmured — a child laughing two tables away, the hiss of the kettle, the widow Oksana humming to herself behind the counter. Ordinary sounds. Ilsa held onto them like handrails.

"You're smarter than your reputation," Ashgrove said finally.

"My reputation is the smart part. The rest of me is just tired." Ilsa waited. "Answer the question."

"I didn't steal them," Ashgrove said. "They were mine. Twelve years ago they were issued to me, along with an identity, a cover story, and a posting I didn't choose. I'm not a caretaker who stumbled into something, Ilsa. I'm a Directorate asset who was wiped and planted at Halcyon, and I only found that out three weeks ago, and every day since then the floor has gotten less solid." She turned the cup in her hands. "Those codes were probably left in my head on purpose. A failsafe. Or bait. I genuinely don't know which, and that's the part that keeps me up at night — because if they were bait, then whoever set them wanted exactly what happened. They wanted the codes loose. They wanted the raids. They wanted the retaliation."

"Why?"

"Because fear is cheaper than truth," Ashgrove said, "and someone out there has been selling fear to both sides of this war since before it started. I have evidence. Real evidence. And getting it to where it needs to go is the only way any of this — the depot, the hospital ship, your stations, my twelve years — means anything other than waste."

Ilsa sat back. Through the tea house window she could see the Ring's promenade, the market stalls, the families, the eleven thousand people who had fled one war and were about to be conscripted into another as statistics. Guilt sat in her chest like ballast — not the sharp guilt of a fresh mistake, but the heavy kind, the kind that compounds. She had taken a woman's shame and treated it as stock. She had let it move downstream. And now the debt was coming due everywhere at once, on ledgers she'd never signed.

"If I help you," Ilsa said slowly, "if I put my ships, my contacts, my whole rotten network behind whatever you're carrying — I need to know it ends the burning instead of feeding it. I need to know there's an actual destination and not just another corridor."

"There's a destination," Ashgrove said. "Halcyon. Everything converges there. Both fleets, the thing that started the war, all of it. Soon."

"How soon?"

Ashgrove glanced toward the window, toward the stars beyond the Ring's curved glass, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"Soon enough that the countdown in a child's dream just skipped a number. And Ilsa — the number it skipped was the one that meant *they know where we are*."

Outside, on the promenade, a public screen flickered to life with an emergency bulletin: Directorate forces establishing a quarantine cordon around the Tannhäuser Ring, effective immediately, pending investigation of credential misuse. Eleven thousand people, locked in with them.

Ilsa looked at the screen, then at Ashgrove, and understood that the invoice had only just begun arriving.

"We need to move," she said. "Now. Before they seal the docks."
