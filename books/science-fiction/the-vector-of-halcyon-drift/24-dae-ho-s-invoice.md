## 24. Dae-ho's Invoice

The message came in on a frequency that didn't exist.

Not encrypted — encrypted was fine, encrypted was professional. This came in on a band that had been decommissioned before the war, a sliver of spectrum the Directorate had sold off to a fertilizer concern and then quietly buried in regulatory paperwork. Nobody listened there. Which meant Dae-ho had chosen it deliberately, the way you'd knock on a wall of a house instead of using the door: *I know where you live. I've always known where you live.*

Odile read it three times while the *Magpie's Nest* drifted dark off the shoulder of a dead refinery, running cold so her heat signature looked like slag. Wren slept in the aft compartment, curled around a blanket that smelled of greenhouse soil and old hydroponic nutrient, and every few minutes the ship's passive array picked up the faint rhythm of her breathing and Odile found herself timing it against nothing at all, just to have something to count.

The message was four lines long.

*Invoice attached. Favor rendered at Ceres Gate: one transit log erased, one checkpoint waved through, one career quietly risked. Payment terms negotiable. Meet me at Tannhäuser Anchorage, Bay 14, 0600 station time, or I begin shopping your cargo manifest to parties who will pay for it in ways you won't like.*

There was no attachment. There didn't need to be. The invoice was Odile herself, itemized.

She sat with it for a long time, the cockpit lit only by the amber standby glow of instruments she'd killed an hour ago. Somewhere below, the refinery turned its slow dead orbit, its tanks full of forty-year-old propellant nobody would ever burn. She'd hidden in worse places. She'd hidden in this place, once, twelve years ago, though she couldn't remember why — and that was the trouble with her memory these days, wasn't it. It had holes in it shaped like other people's decisions.

*Dae-ho,* she thought. *You soft-eyed, careful-handed bastard.*

He'd waved them through Ceres Gate with a smile that hadn't reached his eyes and a hand that hadn't trembled, and he'd wiped their transit log with two keystrokes, and he'd said — she could still hear it, that mild, reasonable voice — *"Everyone's invoice comes due eventually, Captain. Mine's just better organized than most."*

She'd thought it was a joke. That was the thing about mercy; you wanted so badly to believe it was free that you'd ignore the receipt tucked into your pocket until it started to smolder.

Wren's evidence sat in the vault of the *Magpie's Nest*'s core, triple-wrapped, quantum-sealed, and worth more than both fleets combined. The full record of Meridian. The manufactured war. Every staged casualty, every phantom cargo manifest, every forged order that had set Earth against the Drift and kept them bleeding for eleven years. And now a man who owed his career to a single act of kindness wanted to broker it to the highest bidder.

Odile closed the message. Opened it again. Read the last line once more.

*Parties who will pay for it in ways you won't like.*

She knew what that meant. It meant Voss. It meant Directorate black teams. It meant Meridian's agents, whoever and whatever they were, the ones who'd seized Marek's broadcast array with his own access codes — codes Marek had confessed to her in a transmission so drenched in shame she'd had to play it twice to hear the words under the guilt. It meant Ilsa Ferrow's rivals, the ones who'd used Odile's clearance codes to crack a depot and gotten neutral stations burned in retaliation. Everyone she'd touched in the last month had come away bleeding, and most of the blood traced back to her own hands, even when someone else had held the knife.

*Every ally has a price tag.* Her mother — no. Not her mother. The woman whose memories had been laminated over hers, the woman from the planted identity, the fiction of Odile Ashgrove's childhood — used to say that about the traders who came through the drift towns. Odile had thought it cynicism. Now she understood it was just arithmetic.

She keyed the intercom low. "Wren. You awake?"

A pause. Then, small and sleep-frayed: "Is it morning?"

"There's no morning out here. But it's that part."

Footsteps, soft. Wren appeared in the cockpit doorway wearing the oversized jacket Odile had traded for at Ferrow's station, sleeves rolled four times, hair flattened on one side. Twelve years old and carrying the entire history of a manufactured war in her head, and what she cared about was whether it was breakfast time.

"Who's the message from?"

"A friend."

"You made the face you make when it's not a friend." Wren climbed into the second seat, folded herself up, and studied the dead console as if it might confess something. "Ilsa says you make two faces. The cargo face and the real face. She says the real face is bad for business."

"Ilsa talks too much."

"She also says you'll die before you let anyone sell me." Wren said it plainly, without drama, the way children state weather. "Is that true?"

Odile looked at her. In the amber light the girl's face was all sharp angles and sleep, and behind her eyes — behind the ordinary child's eyes — lived the archive, the evidence, the message that needed a living witness to authenticate. Meridian's defectors had built her to be opened, and everyone in the settled system was now maneuvering to be the one holding the key.

"It's true," Odile said.

"Okay." Wren nodded, satisfied, and then ruined it completely by adding: "But Ilsa says dying is easy and getting outmaneuvered is hard, so maybe do the second one first."

* * *

Tannhäuser Anchorage hung in the shadow of a gas giant like a splinter of bone caught in a throat — a former mining platform, half-derelict, leased in sections to smugglers, refitters, and people who needed to not be anywhere in particular. Bay 14 was at the far end of the commercial ring, past a row of shuttered chandleries, under lighting that flickered at a frequency designed to make facial recognition unreliable.

Odile went in alone. That was the terms she'd counter-offered, and to her mild surprise Dae-ho had accepted, which told her either he genuinely wanted a conversation or he was confident enough not to need insurance. Both possibilities were bad.

He was already there, sitting on a cargo crate outside a sealed warehouse, hands wrapped around a cup of something steaming, dressed in civilian clothes that were almost convincing. Off-duty cut, off-duty posture. Only his eyes moved like a man working a checkpoint — sweeping the bay, cataloging exits, pricing everyone who passed.

"Captain Ashgrove." He stood, offered the cup. "Tea. Real leaves, not pressed algae. Consider it a goodwill gesture against the invoice."

"I don't drink tea from men who threaten me."

"It wasn't a threat. It was a disclosure." He set the cup down between them anyway, on the crate, equidistant, absurdly formal. "There's a difference. A threat is *do this or else*. A disclosure is *here is what happens if you do nothing*. You strike me as someone who respects the distinction."

"I respect that you're stalling."

Something flickered across his face — not quite hurt, more like appreciation. "Yes. All right." He sat back down, and Odile remained standing, which put him looking up at her, and she watched him decide whether to mind. He decided not to. "Do you know how many times I've waved ships through Ceres Gate that should have been boarded? Eleven years, Captain. Hundreds. Grain ships carrying munitions. Munitions ships carrying grain. Orphans in cargo containers. One time, an entire orchestra fleeing the Jovian purges, forty musicians wedged into a freighter rated for twelve. I waved them through because the alternative was feeding them into a war that—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Because the war made monsters of the paperwork. And I told myself the people I saved balanced the people I processed. Do you know what I learned at that gate?"

"That everyone has a price tag," Odile said. "It's arithmetic."

Dae-ho smiled, thin and genuine and terrible. "That mercy is a currency. And I have been spending mine for eleven years without ever once being paid back. Until you. Until that transit log." He leaned forward. "So yes. I want payment. And here is my proposal, and I want you to hear all of it before you reach for whatever you're carrying under that jacket, which is a very old-model holdout pistol and would embarrass us both."

Odile didn't move. "Talk."

"The evidence in your hold — the Meridian record, the authenticated broadcast, all of it. Your plan, as far as I can reconstruct it, is to deliver it to both fleets simultaneously. Let the truth land like a bomb on everyone at once. Yes?"

"Yes."

"And what happens?" He spread his hands. "Genuinely. Walk it forward. Two fleets receive proof that their war was manufactured. Fleets are not courts, Captain. Fleets are weapons with command structures bolted on. Half the officers will believe it. Half will call it enemy propaganda — because that is *exactly* what it will look like, arriving mid-crisis, unverified, from a smuggler's hull. The ones who believe it will fracture. The ones who don't will double down. And Meridian — whatever Meridian actually is — gets a battlefield full of confused enemies and a window to disappear, scrub itself, rebuild. You don't end a war with a bomb of truth. You end it with a *trial*. And trials need evidence chains. Custody records. Authentication that survives cross-examination."

"And you're offering to be the chain."

"I'm offering to be the *notary*." His voice went quiet, and for the first time the checkpoint-keeper fell away and she saw the man underneath, tired and precise and ashamed of something. "Route it through me. I have standing. I have clean custody procedures. I have contacts in both fleets' judge advocate corps — real ones, people who owe me, people who will certify provenance. Give me the record and I will build them a case no one can dismiss. Not a broadcast. A *prosecution*."

"And your fee?"

"Ten percent." He said it without flinching. "Of whatever the record sells for, after delivery. Because it *will* sell, Captain — historical archives, compensation claims, war-crimes tribunals, three governments' worth of insurers untangling eleven years of losses. The record is worth billions. Ten percent of billions buys me a life where I never have to wave another ship full of orphans through a checkpoint and pretend it balances anything."

Odile let the silence stretch. Around them the bay lights flickered their paranoid rhythm. Somewhere deeper in the anchorage a welder spat and hissed.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I shop it." No apology in it. "To Ilsa, who'll take twenty. To the Drift council, who'll bury half of it to protect their own wartime decisions. To Voss, who will pay anything and destroy everything. I'd rather it be me, Captain. I'm the cheapest monster available."

"You're not a monster," Odile said. "You're an invoice. There's a difference."

His smile came back, smaller. "My grandmother would have liked you."

And that was when Odile understood — really understood, down in the place where decisions get made before the conscious mind files the paperwork — that Dae-ho was telling the truth. All of it. The custody chain, the ten percent, the threat. He was exactly what he appeared to be: a decent man monetizing his one act of grace because decency had never once paid the rent. And that made him more dangerous than any fanatic, because you couldn't shock him, couldn't shame him, couldn't appeal to the cause. He'd already priced his soul and the number was reasonable.

Which meant she couldn't betray him. Betrayal required leverage, and his leverage was simply *truth-telling*, and you couldn't kill truth-telling without becoming the thing the truth was about.

But she could outmaneuver him.

"Here's my counter," she said. "You get your ten percent. Full custody chain, your notary framework, your judge advocates. Everything you asked for."

Dae-ho's eyebrows rose a fraction. Suspicion, immediately, correctly. "And?"

"And the routing goes through me first. You don't touch the raw record. I give you certified copies — authenticated, sealed, timestamped by the vault archive itself, which carries more evidentiary weight than anything you could generate. You build your case from the copies. The original stays with the living witness." She paused. "With Wren."

"The child is the authentication key. I know. That's precisely why I—"

"That's precisely why you'll never hold her," Odile said, and let something surface in her voice that she usually kept submerged, something from before the laminate, from whatever self had existed under the planted identity — something that made Dae-ho go still. "Because the moment the original leaves my custody, every party you've described — Voss, Meridian, Ilsa's rivals, the fleets — stops negotiating and starts extracting. Copies are safe. Copies are *arguable*. The original is a person, and persons get disappeared. You want a prosecution? Prosecutions need a witness on the stand. A dead or vanished witness ends your case and your ten percent in the same breath. Keeping her alive isn't sentiment, Dae-ho. It's asset protection. Your asset."

He stared at her for a long moment. The flickering light strobed across his face, honest and dishonest by turns.

"You've done this before," he said slowly. "Negotiated. At this level. Before you were a cargo hauler."

The question landed exactly where he'd aimed it, in the hole in her memory, in the shape of someone else's decisions. Odile felt the vertigo rise — the old scaffold of her identity creaking — and did what she'd learned to do: stepped onto it anyway.

"I keep books," she said. "Twelve years of tending a vault teaches you what's worth protecting and what's worth copying. Take the deal or start shopping."

Dae-ho exhaled. Picked up the tea, considered it, drank. Set it down.

"Copies. Certified. Delivered within seventy-two hours of my first tribunal contact." He extended his hand. "And Captain — the invoice includes one non-negotiable line item."

"Name it."

"When this goes to trial, I testify to what I saw at Ceres Gate. What I erased. My part, on the record." His jaw tightened. "If we're building the truth, I'm not going to stand outside it holding a receipt. I've spent eleven years pretending mercy balances the ledger. I want it written down that it doesn't."

Odile took his hand. His grip was dry and steady and cost him something visible to maintain.

"Pleasure doing business," she said.

They were still shaking on it when the bay lights stopped flickering — all at once, everywhere, snapping to full steady white — and every screen in Bay 14, every public terminal, every cheap wrist display on every passerby in the anchorage, lit up with the same image: a green corridor, endless, receding, and a single line of text beneath it.

**COUNTDOWN RESUMED. 47 HOURS.**

Beneath it, a signature block that made Odile's blood go to ice water:

*Meridian Logistics. Thank you for your patience.*

Dae-ho's hand went slack in hers. "That's not possible. We have the only—"

"No," Odile said, already moving, already running the math, already hearing Wren's voice in her head saying *the countdown was never a threat, it was a deadline* — "we have a copy. They have the array. And they just told the whole system they're coming to Halcyon."

She was through the bay doors before the screens went dark.
