## 9. Archive Fragment One

The core was still warm.

That was the detail that kept her awake, more than the spiral etched beneath her own maintenance panel, more than the hair-fine filaments that had no business surviving forty years of dust and cold and her own oblivious hands. Warmth meant power. Power meant purpose. Purpose meant someone had wanted this thing found — or at least, wanted it *kept* — and had trusted the keeping to a stranger who would never know she'd been chosen.

Paloma Thackeray sat cross-legged on the floor of her office at two in the morning, the archive core resting on a folded blanket like a sleeping animal, and told herself for the fourth time that she would only look a little longer.

She had carried it down from the ridge in her tool satchel three nights ago, wrapped in the thermal liner from her field kit, heart hammering the whole descent as though the dark itself were watching. She'd told herself it was temporary. That she'd examine it, log what she found, put it back. That was the investigator's discipline: touch everything, change nothing, leave no fingerprints but your own honest ones.

But the core had other ideas about honesty.

It had woken for her. She was almost sure of that now. The first night, when she'd set it on her desk beside a cooling cup of tea, a single amber light had pulsed once — slow, patient, like something turning over in its sleep to confirm the room was familiar. The second night, the pulse had come twice. Last night, three times, evenly spaced, and then the casing had grown warm against her palm in a way that felt less like electronics and more like recognition.

*You're early,* the warmth seemed to say. *But you're the one.*

She had spent her career among documents that lied by omission. Insurance claims built from careful silences. Incident reports where every fact was technically true and the truth had been surgically removed anyway. She knew how institutions forgot things on purpose. What she had never encountered was an institution's opposite: a machine built to remember against everyone's will.

Her handheld lay on the floor beside her, connected to the core by an adapter Sefa had machined for her without asking questions — though his eyes had asked plenty when he'd handed it over, and she'd answered none of them, and they had both agreed to let the silence stand. The interface was crude. The core spoke in protocols older than the colony itself, dialects of data that predated Sector Nine's founding charter, and most of what she'd coaxed out so far was structure without content: indexes, checksums, sealed partitions stacked like bulkheads in a flooded ship.

One partition, though, was different. It wasn't sealed. It was *waiting* — flagged with a status code that, when she finally decoded it, translated to a phrase so plain it made her laugh aloud alone in her office, a short startled bark:

**READY FOR RETRIEVAL BY AUTHORIZED MAINTAINER.**

Authorized. Her. The woman whose official job was calculating depreciation on damaged cargo lifts.

She pressed play at 2:14 in the morning, because there was no hour of daylight brave enough for it.

* * *

Static first. A long breath of it, the sound of empty air being recorded by someone who hadn't yet decided to speak. Then a click — a physical click, the sound of an actual switch under an actual thumb — and a woman's voice filled the small office.

"This is Oyelaran Adéyemi, recording for the Meridian Archive cell, day six of the incursion."

Paloma's pen stopped moving.

She had prepared herself for many things. Propaganda, maybe. A dissident's rant. Technical specifications, coordinates, the dry bones of some smuggling operation dressed up in heroic language. She had not prepared herself for a voice like this — young, unhurried, with the faint roughness of someone who had been crying recently and had decided, with visible effort, to stop.

"Day six," the voice said again, as if tasting the words. "I want to be precise about days, because precision is going to matter. In a year, maybe two, people are going to argue about when the war started, and whoever controls that argument controls everything after it. So. Day six since the attack on Meridian Gate. Day six since we learned what the attack actually was."

A pause. In the background, faintly, Paloma could hear other sounds — a door, footsteps, the hum of ventilation in a place larger than any room on Sector Nine. The recording had been made somewhere with infrastructure. Somewhere important.

"I'm going to say it plainly, because plainly is all I have left. There was no enemy fleet at Meridian Gate. There were no enemy ships at all. The incursion was manufactured — staged, scripted, performed — by the Consortium's own Office of Colonial Security, with authorization signed at the highest level I have ever seen a document reach."

The ground tilted.

It was not a metaphor. Paloma felt it physically — a lurch in her inner ear, the sensation of a floor that had been solid for thirty-eight years revealing itself as a deck in heavy weather. She gripped the edge of her desk. The tea she'd made at midnight sat untouched and cold, and she stared at it because staring at anything else felt dangerous.

Meridian Gate. Every child on Sector Nine learned the name before they learned to write it. The battle that opened the war. The enemy strike that killed — the number was recited like liturgy — one thousand one hundred and fourteen colonists. The reason the Consortium existed in its current form. The reason the sector had traded its autonomy for protection. The reason her grandfather had spent eleven years in the orbital militia and come home with a tremor in his left hand and a medal he never once let anyone see him touch.

The reason for everything. And a woman's voice, six days old into the war, was saying *there was nothing there.*

"The casualties were real," the voice continued, and Paloma flinched at how directly it answered the thought. "I want that on the record too, because I've already heard people suggest otherwise, and that suggestion is its own kind of obscenity. Eleven hundred people died at Meridian Gate. They died because the defense grid was ordered to stand down for ninety minutes while a convoy of empty hulls — drones, debris, hollow shells painted to read as hostile signatures on every sensor we had — was walked through our perimeter and destroyed in front of the whole colony's eyes. The people who died died in the panic, in the crush, in the shelters that failed because failure had been budgeted for. Someone ran the numbers on how much death was needed to make the story believable, and then someone else signed off on those numbers, and then eleven hundred human beings stopped existing."

The voice caught. Stopped. When it resumed, the roughness was back, but the tears had been put away again, firmly, like tools returned to a kit.

"My father was dock crew at Gate Two. He was one of the crush deaths. I am not doing this for ideology. I am doing this because I have read the requisition orders, and I can quote them, and I will keep quoting them until someone makes me stop."

Paloma realized she had written *Oyelaran Adéyemi* on her legal pad and underlined it three times, the pen pressing hard enough to emboss the page beneath. She didn't remember deciding to write it.

"How do I know?" the voice asked, anticipating her — anticipating every listener's — objection. "Because I was junior archivist attached to the Office of Colonial Security's records division, and my job was filing, and filing means reading, and reading means knowing. On day one I processed the sensor logs from the Gate picket array. Raw logs. Pre-analysis. And raw logs don't lie the way summaries lie — they just sit there, being true, waiting for someone to compress them into something useful. The hostile signatures came from hulls with Consortium registry prefixes. The approach vector originated from a Consortium staging platform. The stand-down order carries a countersignature I have memorized and will not repeat on this recording, because names can be denied, but I carry it, and others in this cell carry it, and when the time comes we will produce the documents themselves."

Footsteps again, closer. The voice dropped, went faster.

"They're reorganizing the archives. Standard wartime consolidation, the notice says. Which means purge. Everything inconvenient goes into deep storage or goes into the incinerator, and I have watched enough of this administration to know which. So we're building a copy. Not in the central systems — they'll sweep those within the month. Out here. Distributed. Buried in places no one thinks to audit, keyed to open for people who were never meant to be part of this, because the cell has one rule above all the others—"

A door opened somewhere in the recording. The voice cut off mid-word. Ten seconds of silence stretched like wire, and Paloma found she was holding her breath, leaning toward the handheld as if proximity could protect a woman dead for four decades.

Then, quieter, hurried, the voice returned:

"—the rule is: don't give it to believers. Believers can be predicted, and predicted people can be managed. Give it to someone ordinary. Someone whose job is doubt. An auditor. An adjuster. An investigator. Someone paid to be suspicious, who checks things because checking is the work, not the cause." A breath that might have been a laugh. "Someone who reads insurance claims for a living will read ours too. They won't be able to help it. We're counting on that. We're counting on the fact that a mind trained to find the flaw in a story cannot walk past the largest flaw ever filed."

Paloma set the pen down very carefully, because her hand was not steady, and she did not want to snap it.

They had built the beacon for her. Not her — not *her*, the word was absurd — but for the shape of her. For a person who would climb a ridge on a Tuesday night because a maintenance schedule said so, who would notice that accidents arranged themselves in a perimeter, who would open a hatch because a hatch that opens is a question that demands an answer. Forty years ago, strangers had looked across time at a type of person and said: *she'll find it. People like her always find it.*

And she had. God help her, she had.

"One last thing," the voice said, and here it changed — softened, slowed, the archivist stepping aside for the daughter, the orphan, the twenty-something-year-old carrying a war's worth of paper truth in hands that shook. "If you're hearing this, then the cell succeeded, and I'm probably gone, and the war I'm living through is history to you, which means we lost the only thing that mattered: the chance to stop it before it started. Eleven hundred at Meridian Gate. Tens of thousands since. All of it purchased with a signature. If you're hearing this, please — please — finish what we couldn't. Don't avenge us. Correct us. Take the record and make it public and make them answer for it, not with another war, but with the one thing they cannot survive."

A pause long enough that Paloma thought the recording had ended.

"The truth," Oyelaran Adéyemi said. "They cannot survive the truth. Everything else they've built to withstand. Not that."

A click. Static. Silence.

* * *

For a long time Paloma did not move.

The office around her reassembled itself slowly, piece by piece, as if she were seeing it for the first time: the filing cabinets with their decades of claims, the wall calendar from the Consortium suppliers' association — a free gift, cheerful landscapes of domes she now understood were built on a foundation of corpses — the window with its view of the ridge, black against the slightly-less-black sky, where a beacon she had tended for nine years stood holding a dead woman's testimony in its belly.

She got up. Walked to the window. Pressed her forehead against the cold glass.

Her whole life had been furnished with the war. Not the fighting — Sector Nine was too far out for the fighting — but the war's *meaning*, which had soaked into everything the way water soaks into plaster. The rationing schedules that ended when she was ten. The Victory Day assemblies at school, the holograms of burning enemy carriers over Meridian Gate, the solemn teachers explaining why the Consortium's authority was not tyranny but scar tissue. Her grandfather's silence. Her mother's careful, grateful voting. The entire architecture of the sector's obedience, load-bearing walls all, and behind every one of them the same keystone: *they attacked us first, and we survived, and survival earned us our rulers.*

Begun with a lie. All of it. The war that had defined every life on the colony for forty years had been a production — staged, scripted, performed — with a body count calibrated for believability.

And the worst of it, the part that made her stomach turn over slowly, was how *well* it explained things. The impossible accidents forming their neat perimeter. The beacon scrubbed from every record. The children dreaming of a countdown. Of course the Consortium erased it. Of course they patrolled the forgetting decade after decade, patching leaks, relocating families, tuning the power grid to soften adult minds. You did not commit a crime that large and then relax. You committed it and then spent forty years standing guard over the corpse of the truth, and you called the guarding *security*, and you taught the children to thank you for it.

She thought of Voss's polite request for her files. *Politely, which is worse than a subpoena.* She thought of his face when she'd said the word *beacon* — the half-second hitch, the smile recalibrating. He knew. Whatever Voss knew, he knew it downstream of this. He was standing guard too, probably sincerely, probably believing with his whole well-ordered heart that the forgetting was mercy.

The recording's last line kept replaying in her head. *Don't avenge us. Correct us.*

Paloma turned back to the desk. The core pulsed its slow amber light — once, twice, patient as a heartbeat — and she understood suddenly that it was not idle. It was asking. *More?* it was saying. *There is more. Do you want more?*

She thought about the smart move. The smart move was to seal the core back up, return it to the ridge, file a report about anomalous interference, request a transfer, get as far from Sector Nine as her debts allowed. The smart move was the move of a woman who understood exactly what happened to people who held contraband truths inside police states. She had read enough claims files involving "industrial incidents" involving inconvenient witnesses to have no illusions.

But the voice had said something else, earlier, and it was that line that had hooked her below the waterline of caution: *a mind trained to find the flaw in a story cannot walk past the largest flaw ever filed.*

They had counted on her curiosity the way engineers count on load tolerances. And she resented it — she genuinely, fiercely resented being a variable in a forty-year-old equation — but resentment changed nothing. The flaw was filed. She had read it. There was no version of Paloma Thackeray that could unread it.

"Yes," she said aloud, to a machine, at three in the morning, feeling foolish and resolute in equal measure. "I want more."

The core's light steadied. Across her handheld's screen, partitions began to shuffle like a deck being searched, and then the display settled on a single new entry, unsealed, waiting:

**FRAGMENT TWO — STATUS: DAMAGED. RECOVERY ESTIMATE: PARTIAL.**

And beneath it, a date stamp that made her breath catch — not because of when it was recorded, but because of the location tag appended after it, a tag she recognized from a decade of accident reports, from a claim she'd closed herself three years ago, from the eastern edge of the perimeter her impossible accidents had drawn around the ridge:

**RECORDING ORIGIN: SECTOR NINE.**

Oyelaran Adéyemi had been here. The cell had buried pieces of itself *here*, on this colony, in the soil Voss's enforcers patrolled — and the second fragment, the damaged one, had been recorded from the ground Paloma was standing on.

She reached for her coat.
