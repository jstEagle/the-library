## 12. Countdown Made Visible

The dream changed on a Tuesday, and Paloma knew it before anyone told her.

She knew it the way she knew weather coming off the salt flats — not by seeing it but by feeling the pressure drop behind her eyes, in the small animal part of the brain that predates language. She was at her desk in the insurance office, cross-referencing a water reclamation claim that didn't interest her, when the colony's morning chatter shifted key. The canteen was louder than usual. The transit platform held clusters of parents who stood too close together and spoke too quietly. And through the open window of the office next door, she heard a woman say, in the flat voice of someone reciting something they've said eleven times already:

"—and this morning they all had the same number. Not close. The same."

Paloma's pen stopped moving.

She had spent ten years building a career out of noticing what other people explained away. A lift that failed without killing anyone. A shipment that moved itself off every manifest. Accidents arranged like fence posts around coordinates no file admitted existed. She had learned to trust the shape of coincidences the way other people trusted load-bearing walls — you couldn't see them holding anything up until the day they didn't.

Nine children now. It had been seven, then eight, and yesterday — according to Sefa, who heard everything from everyone because his workshop was the colony's true parliament — a girl from the hydroponics families named Tola had woken her mother at 3 a.m. to ask why the numbers were getting smaller.

Numbers getting smaller.

Paloma closed the claim file. She sat very still for a moment, listening to the ventilation hum, and then she did what she always did with dread: she made a list.

*1. Interview the children — separately if possible, together if necessary.*
*2. Get the exact figures. All of them. In order.*
*3. Open the core again tonight.*

She underlined the third item twice, then crossed out the underline, then underlined it again, because there was no one to see her do it and no reason not to be honest with the paper.

* * *

Getting the numbers took most of the day, and it was Idris Okonkwo who gave them to her cleanest.

She found him on the steps of the school annex, sketching — of course he was sketching; the boy drew the spiral the way other children breathed, automatically, without knowing he was doing it — and when she sat down beside him he didn't startle or shy away. He simply moved his elbow to make room, which was its own kind of intimacy, and kept drawing.

"They're all the same now," he said, before she'd asked anything. "Ama says I shouldn't talk about it. But you're the accident lady, so." He shrugged, a twelve-year-old's shrug, engineered to convey that nothing mattered very much while everything mattered enormously. "You investigate things."

"I investigate things," Paloma agreed.

"Then investigate this." He turned his schoolbook around.

On the page, beneath three days of spirals, was a column of numerals in his careful, cramped hand: **91**. And below it, smaller, as though he'd written it and immediately regretted the certainty of it: **90 tomorrow.**

"You're sure? Every child, the same figure?"

"Tola saw ninety-two two nights ago. Kessie saw ninety-one first. Now we all see the same one, and it goes down every night, and—" He stopped. His pencil hovered over the page. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into a register no twelve-year-old should have needed: "It doesn't feel like counting down *to* something, Ms. Thackeray. It feels like counting down *until* something stops."

Paloma looked at the number for a long time.

Ninety-one.

She thought about the maintenance hatch, and the second mechanism beneath her own modest additions — the archive core, still warm after decades of official nonexistence, built by someone who had wanted to be found by exactly one kind of person. She thought about the woman's voice in the first fragment, dated to the war's opening week, saying *the false incursion at Meridian Gate* as calmly as a station announcer reading delays.

And she thought, with a coldness that started in her sternum and spread outward, about timers.

Everything decays. That was the first law of the fringe — before politics, before war, before Consortium and colony. Storage media decayed. Power cells decayed. Grief decayed, and memory, and eventually the resolve of the people who buried things and swore to remember. Whoever built the archive had known that better than anyone. They hadn't just hidden the truth. They had put an expiration date on it — a dead-man's switch running in reverse. If no one came, if no one unlocked it, if the maintainer they'd hoped for never arrived, then the data would quietly, cleanly, permanently erase itself, and Sector Nine could go on forgetting on schedule forever.

Ninety-one days.

"Idris," she said carefully, "when did the dreams start matching exactly?"

"Six nights ago." He frowned, doing arithmetic with his lips. "No — seven. The night after the workshop blew out."

The night after Sefa's pressure blowout. The night Idris lost his grandmother's maiden name and couldn't mourn properly for it. Paloma filed the correlation where she filed everything, in the drawer that was getting dangerously full, and stood up.

"Don't write the number down anymore," she said. "Not anywhere. If it changes, tell me in person."

"Why?"

Because paper can be seized, she almost said. Because children who keep records become evidence. Because I am asking you to help me hide something inside your own head, which is the only vault left in this colony that Voss hasn't audited.

"Because some things are safer remembered than written," she said instead, and Idris looked at her with those serious dark eyes, and nodded once, and went back to drawing spirals he didn't know the meaning of.

* * *

The ridge at night was the only place in Sector Nine where Paloma felt proportionate to her own life.

Below her, the colony glowed its ordinary amber — dome lights, grid lines, the slow traffic of a place that believed it was at peace because it had been told so for forty years. Above her, the sky did what fringe skies did: it went on forever, indifferent and enormous, salted with stars whose light was older than the lie that had bought this colony its quiet.

The beacon stood against it all, thin and patient, wearing its disguise of decommissioned relay hardware the way she wore her insurance badge. She climbed the maintenance ladder with the ease of a decade's habit, popped the outer panel, and let herself into the space behind her own additions — past the signal couplers she'd installed, past the power regulators she'd rebuilt with parts bought through falsified claims, down to the thing that had been here before her and would, if she failed, outlast her purpose here.

The archive core sat in its cradle like a sleeping animal.

Still warm. After forty years, still warm, drawing trickle current from lines she'd never traced, patient as a seed. She had opened it once before and come away shaken, full of awe and vertigo, certain that whoever built it had designed it to be found by an investigator — by someone who read manifests against reality and refused to accept the difference. Now she knelt in front of it with a diagnostic slate and the specific dread of a person about to confirm a suspicion she'd been carrying around like a stone.

"Let's see what you're counting," she murmured.

The interface woke reluctantly, the way old machines do — flickering, sulking, then steadying into columns of pale green text that floated in the dark of the housing. Most of it was still locked. Fragment One played when she asked, the woman's voice filling the small metal space, calm and terrible: *the false incursion at Meridian Gate... authorization logged under Consortium seal... we are archiving this so that someone, someday, can prove it happened...*

But beneath the playback menu, in a subsystem she hadn't accessed before, there was a new line. Or rather — an old line she'd finally earned the clearance to see.

**TEMPORAL INTEGRITY: DECAY PROTOCOL ACTIVE**

**ELAPSED SINCE SEALING: 14,610 DAYS**

**REMAINING BEFORE FULL SELF-ERASURE: 91 DAYS**

**NOTE TO FINDER:** *If you are reading this, the archive has waited longer than we dared hope. We could not make it last forever. Forever is how they win — they simply wait longer than memory does. So we set a clock instead. Ninety-one days is enough to verify, to gather, to choose. It is not enough to hesitate. Forgive us the urgency. We were out of time when we built it, and we gave you what we had left.*

Paloma read it three times.

The green text hung in the dark, patient, merciless. Ninety-one days. The same number nine children had carried out of the same dream, delivered nightly into their sleep by a mechanism none of them had ever seen, in a colony none of their parents had ever been allowed to question.

The beacon wasn't just an archive. It was an alarm clock. It had been whispering into the colony's dreaming minds — children's minds, untouched, unjammed, unmanaged — for who knew how long, faint as background radiation, and now that a finder had finally come, now that the lock was turning, it had stopped whispering and started counting. Out loud. In unison. Where the right person couldn't fail to hear it.

"It's you," she said softly, to the warm core, to the ghost in the machine, to Oyelaran or whoever else was in there. "The dream is you. You've been calling for help the only way left."

The core hummed. If it was an answer, it was an answer the way tectonic plates answer — real, and far too slow.

She sat back on her heels in the cold and did the arithmetic that investigators do, the arithmetic that turns dread into schedule. Ninety-one days to fully unlock a broadcast system that required — she was almost sure, from the fragments of architecture she could read — something the designers had called *keys*, plural, nature unknown. Ninety-one days to do it while avoiding the attention of Deputy Administrator Voss, whose polite requests for files had been worse than subpoenas, and whose courtesy had frayed exactly once, at the word *beacon*. Ninety-one days during which nine children would go on dreaming a countdown they couldn't stop, losing pieces of themselves to a mechanism that was already taking payment — Idris's grandmother's maiden name, gone, spent like a coin dropped into a slot.

And ninety-one days, she thought, looking at the note again — *it is not enough to hesitate* — during which she would have to decide, finally and irrevocably, whether she was the archivist this machine had been waiting for or just the latest person to find it and flinch.

Her handheld chimed. Colony hour, the automated reminder she'd set years ago to make herself go home, eat, sleep, maintain the fiction of a normal life. She silenced it without looking.

Instead she photographed nothing and memorized everything — the protocol line, the day count, the phrasing of the note — and then, before closing the housing, she did the one thing she'd been avoiding since the night she'd found the core. She spoke to it directly, the way you speak to a sleeping person you need to wake.

"Ninety-one days," she said. "I hear you. I'm here. Tell me what you need."

The green text flickered. Just once — a stutter in the decay counter, a single character blooming and dying in the corner of the display, too fast to photograph, too strange to dismiss.

It looked, she would swear afterward, sitting alone on the ridge with the wind coming off the flats, like the beginning of a word.

* * *

She got home past midnight and found a message waiting on her desk terminal. Official formatting. Consortium seal. Deputy Administrator Voss's name, and beneath it, in his immaculate, courteous prose, a single sentence:

*Ms. Thackeray — given recent public health concerns regarding juvenile sleep patterns, I would appreciate your professional assessment of the attached incident reports at your earliest convenience. No urgency. — V.*

Attached were nine files. Nine children. Nine names, including Okonkwo, Idris.

*No urgency,* from a man who had requested her entire accident archive by name. From a man who had gone pale, microscopically, at the word *beacon*.

Paloma sat in the dark of her apartment with the message glowing on the screen and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the end of very long days, what had actually happened on the ridge. The countdown wasn't just the archive's anymore. Two clocks were running now, wound against each other: hers, and his. The archive giving her ninety-one days to pull the truth into the light; Voss giving her — what? Weeks? Days? However long it took him to finish deciding whether she was a curiosity or a problem?

She thought of Idris's column of numbers, going down. She thought of Ama's kitchen, where she was unofficially family and officially nobody, where dinner happened at the same hour every night and no one had ever asked her what she did on the ridge. She thought of the note in the core: *we gave you what we had left.*

"No urgency," she repeated aloud, to the empty room, and almost laughed.

She typed a reply — *Happy to review, Deputy Administrator. I'll have my assessment within the week* — and sent it, and then she took out a pen and a sheet of actual paper, the kind that doesn't transmit, and wrote at the top of it, in her own handwriting, the only sentence that mattered:

**91 days.**

Below it, she began a list. Not of evidence this time. Of names.

Nine children. One mechanic. One grandmother. One retired teacher who had kept a letter hidden in a hymnal for forty years.

And at the bottom, after a long pause, her own — written small, the way you sign something you suspect will cost you.

Outside, the colony slept its managed sleep, and somewhere beneath the amber glow, nine minds dreamed the same descending number, and the beacon on the ridge counted faithfully toward the moment when either the truth would finally speak, or the last chance for it would quietly, cleanly, permanently stop.
