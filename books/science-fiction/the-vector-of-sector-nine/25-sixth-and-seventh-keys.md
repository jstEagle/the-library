## 25. Sixth and Seventh Keys

The candles were Sefa's idea again — a birthday party for no one, frosting on a sheet cake nobody would eat, paper hats because Brin insisted that ritual needed absurdity to survive scrutiny. The workshop smelled of solder and sugar and the ghost of machine oil, and nine children sat in a half-circle on crates and overturned buckets while the archive core hummed on Sefa's workbench like a kettle deciding whether to sing.

Two keys remained after tonight. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it.

The sixth key belonged to Naledi Ferro, who was fourteen and the oldest of the dreamers, and who had spent the entire day being aggressively normal — arguing about homework, complaining about the cake's icing ratio, laughing too loudly at Sefa's bad jokes. Paloma watched her perform ordinariness the way she herself had once performed indifference at accident scenes, and recognized it exactly for what it was: armor, donned carefully, one plate at a time.

Naledi stood up when Ama nodded. She didn't look at the core. She looked at Paloma.

"You're going to hate this," Naledi said.

"I doubt it," Paloma said, though her chest had already begun to tighten, some animal part of her understanding before her mind did what was about to happen.

"It's the picnic." Naledi's voice wobbled and steadied. "The one up on the north ridge, two years ago, when the transport broke down and we all got stranded waiting for Sefa. You taught us constellations. Except you made them up. Half of them weren't real. The Kettle. The Long Spoon. The Very Disappointed Fish."

The children laughed — wet, uneven laughter, the kind that comes out of people holding something back.

"You said," Naledi went on, and now she wasn't performing anything at all, she was just a girl standing in candlelight trying not to cry, "you said the sky didn't care what things were actually called, only that somebody bothered to name them. And Idris said that was the most romantic nonsense he'd ever heard, and you said *good, remember it anyway.*"

Paloma remembered. She remembered the cold ridge wind, the blanket spread over gravel, Idris pointing at a random star and demanding to know its name, her own voice inventing *the Very Disappointed Fish* with total confidence while four children howled. It was one of those evenings she kept folded away against the loneliness, proof that she had once been part of something.

"Are you sure?" she asked quietly. "There are other memories. Ones that aren't—"

"That's the point," Naledi said. "It has to be one I'd fight to keep. Otherwise it isn't worth anything, right? That's what the Vector said. Freely given means *costly* given." She wiped her nose with her sleeve, defiant. "So. Here."

She pressed her palm to the core's contact plate.

The hum changed pitch. The candle flames leaned, all of them, toward the workbench as if drawn by breath. And Naledi's face went through a sequence of expressions so fast and so private that Paloma felt like an intruder watching them: wonder, then grief arriving early, then a strange blankness where the grief had been, like snow filling footprints.

Then Naledi blinked, looked around at the party as if confirming she knew why she was here, and frowned faintly.

"Why are we having cake?" she asked. "Whose birthday is it?"

Nobody answered. Ama made a small sound into her hands.

"We're celebrating," Brin said smoothly, "the fact that we're all together."

"Oh." Naledi considered this. "Okay. Can I have the corner piece? Corner pieces have more frosting."

She could still eat the cake. She could still sit among them. What she could not do — what had been lifted out of her like a splinter drawn clean — was remember a cold evening on the north ridge, a blanket over gravel, a woman making up names for stars. Somewhere in Naledi's mind there was now a two-year-old absence shaped exactly like Paloma Thackeray's laughter, and Naledi would walk past that absence every day without ever feeling the edges of it.

Paloma excused herself to check the door locks, which did not need checking, and stood in the dark of the workshop entryway until her eyes stopped burning.

* * *

The seventh key belonged to Tomas Okonkwo-Reyes, thirteen, Idris's cousin, a boy who read engineering manuals for fun and spoke roughly half as many words per day as anyone else at the table. He had agreed to give his memory three days ago with a single nod, and since then had said nothing about it, which everyone understood to be his way of screaming.

He waited until the candles had burned down to stubs and Naledi was dozing against Sefa's shoulder, full of cake and emptied of one evening. Then he stood, walked to the core, and put his hand on the plate without ceremony — and stopped.

"It won't take it," he said.

Sefa leaned in. "What do you mean?"

"It's asking me to confirm." Tomas's brow furrowed. "It's showing me the memory first. So I know what I'm paying." He looked up, and for the first time since Paloma had known him, the boy looked frightened. "I didn't know it would do that."

"What does it show?" Ama asked gently.

Tomas didn't answer her. He looked at Paloma instead, and something in his expression made her cross the room before she decided to.

"It's the lessons," he said. "Wednesdays. After school. You taught me math that wasn't on any school curriculum — orbital decay, signal loss, why insurance investigators think in probabilities. You said—" His throat worked. "You said most people see accidents as random. You said randomness is just a story people tell when they haven't looked long enough. You said *everything leaves a pattern if you love it enough to keep records.*"

Paloma's breath caught. She had forgotten those Wednesdays — no. Not forgotten. Filed. Tucked into the same drawer as the ridge and the picnics and the dinners, the accumulated evidence of a life she had built without ever admitting she was building one. Every Wednesday for two years, this silent boy across her desk, feeding him the tools she'd used to survive, never once thinking of it as kindness. It had just been what she did on Wednesdays.

And now he was going to pay for the war's oldest lie with exactly that.

"Tomas," she said, kneeling so they were eye level. "You can choose a different one. A smaller one. The Vector takes what's offered. Offer something cheaper."

"It doesn't work like that." His voice was flat, certain. "I asked it last night. In the dream. It said the key has to be true, and true things are never cheap." He swallowed. "Besides. If I forget the Wednesdays, I'll still know the math. Right? The math stays even if the person goes?"

"The math stays," Paloma said, and hated how much it sounded like a lie.

"Then it's a good trade." He turned back to the plate. "I always thought you were lonely," he added, conversationally, terribly, the way children deliver their most devastating observations. "You pretended you liked being alone. But you kept every drawing I ever gave you. They were in your desk drawer, under the incident files. I saw them once. You organized your sadness really neatly."

"Tomas—"

"Here." He pressed his palm down. "Confirming."

The candles leaned. The hum deepened into something almost like speech, almost like a name being taken into custody. Tomas's face went through the same terrible weather Naledi's had — wonder, grief, blankness — and then he stepped back from the core, rubbed his hand on his trousers, and said:

"So whose birthday is it?"

* * *

They let the party die slowly after that, because abrupt endings made the absences louder. Sefa boxed the untouched half of the cake. Brin gathered paper hats with the tenderness of a woman collecting shrapnel. Ama held each child on her way out the door a moment longer than usual, and none of the children who had given — five of them now, five gaps walking around in five heads — understood why the old woman's hugs had gotten heavier. They accepted them anyway. Children accept love the way ground accepts rain: without needing to know the forecast.

Idris stayed behind to help stack chairs, which meant he stayed behind to talk, because stacking chairs was the Okonkwo family's universal cover for saying real things.

"You watched both of them," he said, not looking up from the chair in his hands. "When they forgot. You watched yourself disappear."

"Yes."

"And you're next." He set the chair down. "The ninth key. Yours. We all know it. Even the ones who've forgotten probably know it somewhere, in the shape of the hole." He finally met her eyes, twelve years old and ancient. "Does it help or hurt? Watching it happen first?"

Paloma considered lying to him. She had earned the right, maybe; she'd lied to this family for months by omission, wrapped the beacon in silence the way you wrap a wound. But the children had paid five memories for the truth, and the truth owed them better than her comfort.

"Hurt," she said. "It hurts like watching a house burn from inside another house, knowing yours is scheduled."

Idris nodded slowly, absorbing this with the seriousness he brought to orbital mechanics.

"Okay," he said. "Then here's what I figured out. From my key." He tapped his own temple, where his father's face used to live. "I can't see Dad anymore. But I know he was good, because everyone's behavior around the gap proves it. Ama sets out his cup sometimes and cries without knowing why. You flinch when his name comes up, gently, like it's hot. The hole has a shape, and the shape tells the truth." He shrugged, the shrug of a boy pretending courage was easy because he'd seen it done well. "So when you forget us — we'll still be here. Being the shape. You'll walk past us every day and feel something you can't name, and eventually you'll investigate it, because that's what you do. You find patterns in things other people call random."

Paloma laughed — a broken, grateful sound — and pulled him into a hug that lasted longer than either of them pretended it did.

* * *

She climbed the ridge alone after midnight, as she always did, though tonight the maintenance ritual felt like a vigil. The beacon stood against the stars, patient as a headstone, and beneath her decades of careful additions the archive core's twin pulsed in her handheld, counting down days she no longer bothered to convert.

Eighty-one. Then eighty. The numbers were becoming a heartbeat.

She opened the maintenance log — her log, forty years of entries in her own handwriting, the one continuous act of faith in her life — and found herself reading the early pages like letters from a stranger. *Replaced corroded junction. Repainted casing. No one knows I'm here. That's fine.* Had she believed that, once? That being unknown was fine? Or had she simply not yet met the Okonkwos, the Wednesdays, the picnic on the north ridge — the evidence accumulating year by year that she was loved and had failed to file it correctly?

The Vector stirred in her handheld, that presence like a voice heard through water.

*THE MAINTAINER IS DISTRESSED.*

"I'm fine," Paloma said automatically, then corrected herself, because the thing deserved honesty; honesty was the whole architecture of what they were doing. "I'm grieving in advance. It's inefficient. Grieving twice for the same loss."

*IT IS NOT INEFFICIENT,* the Vector said. *IT IS THE ONLY WAY YOUR SPECIES SURVIVES WHAT IT CHOOSES. YOU PRACTICE THE LOSS SO THAT WHEN IT COMES, LOVE IS STILL STANDING IN THE SAME PLACE.*

Paloma stared at the words glowing on the small screen. Forty years dormant, and the archive had learned comfort somewhere — or had been built by someone who'd needed it and never received it. Oyelaran, maybe. A girl relocated mid-sentence, teaching a machine to say the things no one said to her.

"Did she lose people?" Paloma asked aloud. "The woman who built you. Did she give something?"

A pause, long enough to be an answer.

*SHE GAVE EVERYTHING TWICE,* the Vector said. *ONCE WILLINGLY. ONCE NOT.*

Below the ridge, the colony lights burned in their neat grid, and somewhere among them five children slept dreamless in the ways that mattered, carrying holes shaped like picnics and lullabies and a father's face and a Wednesday afternoon. Two keys remained. One belonged to a grandmother who refused to be excluded. And one —

One belonged to her.

Paloma closed the log, laid her palm flat against the beacon's warm casing, and felt, unmistakably, the machine lean into her touch like something alive saying *not yet, hold on, stay.*

She stayed. But far below, in the mortuary district, a light burned late in the attendant's office — and a man with freshly scoured eyes was writing down a route, a coffin, and a name.
