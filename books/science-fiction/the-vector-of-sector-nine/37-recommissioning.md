## 37. Recommissioning

The assembly hall had not been full in forty years.

Paloma knew this the way she knew most things now — by being told. Brin had mentioned it twice on the walk over, and Sefa had said it once at dinner last week, and Idris had drawn her a picture of it: a long room with a domed ceiling, rows of seats, and a crowd so dense that people stood along the walls. He'd colored the crowd in careful shades of brown and gray and one defiant patch of green for Sefa's work jacket. *This is what it looked like when they voted to go to war,* he'd written underneath, in his blunt schoolbook hand. *Ama says tonight will look like that too.*

She studied the drawing again now, folded open on her knee while the hall filled around her. It was a good likeness. She recognized the room from having sat in it — she supposed she must have sat in it; everyone did, on census days and water-ration hearings — but recognition was all it was. The room held no memories for her. It was like looking at a photograph of a stranger's kitchen.

What she felt instead was something stranger: an almost physical pull toward the front of the hall, where a table had been set up beneath the old Consortium seal. Someone had covered the seal with a sheet of plain duraplast, and someone else — she suspected Idris, from the wobble of the letters — had stenciled over it in white paint: **SECTOR NINE PUBLIC LEDGER**.

The pull was not toward the seal or the stencil. It was toward the thing behind the table. A case of some kind, long and narrow, sitting on trestles, draped in gray cloth.

Paloma's hands ached. That was the only way she could describe it. Her hands wanted to be on whatever was under that cloth, the way a sailor's hands want rope.

"You're doing the thing," Idris said beside her. He'd claimed the seat at her left without asking, which apparently was customary. "The staring thing."

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring at the beacon."

Paloma followed his gaze and found, to her mild surprise, that he was right. The cloth had slipped at one corner, and beneath it she could see a sliver of casing — dark metal, older than any alloy the colony currently manufactured, scored with fine lines that might have been damage or might have been script. Her fingers had gone still on her knee. Her breathing had gone slow and even, the way it did when she was concentrating.

She made herself look away. "It's just equipment," she said.

"Sure," Idris said, in the tone of a twelve-year-old humoring an adult, which was a tone she was beginning to suspect he'd practiced on her before, though she couldn't have said why the suspicion felt warm rather than annoying.

The hall filled. Ama arrived in her good shawl and took the seat at Paloma's right, patting her hand once, twice, three times — a rhythm Paloma had learned meant *I am here, you are here, we are here together*, though no one had told her that either. Brin came in leaning on Sefa's arm and refused all offers of a chair nearer the front, saying she could see fine from anywhere God put her, which was a joke about a deity none of them believed in, and everyone laughed anyway because it was Brin's joke and had probably always been Brin's joke.

And then Deputy Administrator Voss walked in through the main doors, alone, and the laughter died the way wind dies.

* * *

He looked smaller than she remembered. That was odd, because she barely remembered him at all — a face in files, a name on forms she'd signed as an insurance investigator in another life. The others' reactions told her more than her own memory could. Sefa's jaw set. Brin's hand found Ama's. Idris went very still in the way of children who have learned that stillness is armor.

But Voss did not take the administrator's platform. He walked down the center aisle like any other colonist, found a seat in the third row, and sat with his hands folded, waiting. When people stared — and they stared — he met their eyes and did not look away. There was something in his face Paloma couldn't name. Not guilt exactly. Guilt would have been easier to read. This was closer to a man who had carried something heavy for a very long distance and was not yet sure he was allowed to set it down.

"He testified," Idris whispered. "Three weeks ago. In the public tribunal. He named his father. He named eleven others." A pause. "They let him stay. Ama argued for it."

"Ama argued for it?"

"Ama said a colony that can't survive its own worst man coming home isn't much of a colony." Idris shrugged, but his shoulders stayed high around his ears. "Sefa didn't speak to him for two weeks. Then Sefa's press broke and Voss fixed it at four in the morning and left before anyone woke up. So now nobody knows what to do with him."

Paloma watched the man sit among people who had every reason to hate him, and felt nothing but a faint, unplaceable ache behind her sternum, like a word on the tip of the tongue. She decided it was indigestion.

The moderator called the session to order.

* * *

They had prepared, of course. There were speeches. Brin gave one, about history and whose job it was to keep it, and got a standing ovation for a line Paloma suspected she'd been polishing for decades: *"We spent forty years paying rent on a lie. Tonight we decide whether to buy the truth outright."*

A woman from the hydroponics cooperative spoke about transparency in infrastructure. A young man Paloma didn't know spoke about his grandfather, relocated during the war, whose name appeared nowhere in any record until six weeks ago, when the broadcast landed and the archives unlocked themselves like fists opening.

And then the moderator said the words Paloma had been half-dreading since the notices went up on every public board:

"The question before the assembly is simple. The beacon on the north ridge has been decommissioned, in secret, for forty years. Its purpose was concealed. Its maintenance was conducted illegally, by persons unknown, at personal risk. The proposal before us is to recommission it — openly, publicly, under charter — with a standing roster of maintainers, a published maintenance log, and a public ledger recording every entry, every repair, every hour of service, signed and witnessed. Are there arguments against?"

Silence. Not hostile silence. The silence of a room where every argument against had already been tried, in kitchens and workshops and the quiet hours after nightmares, and had lost.

"Then are there volunteers for the maintainer roster?"

And here was the thing Paloma had been turning over for days, the thing she'd rehearsed and abandoned and rehearsed again: she had planned to say nothing. To sit with her family — whoever these people were to her, and they were everything to her, she knew that if she knew nothing else — and let others step forward. Let the colony choose its own keepers. She had no right to volunteer for a duty she couldn't remember performing. She had no memory of the ridge, of the climb, of the hatch, of the years of nights. Whatever she had given — and she understood, in the abstract, gentle way the children had explained it to her, that she had given something enormous — it meant the woman who had maintained that beacon was, in every way that mattered, gone.

Except.

Except that her hands ached toward it. Except that when she closed her eyes she could feel the weight of a tool kit she didn't own, the specific cold of a specific altitude, the particular resistance of a particular latch. Except that three times this month she had dreamed of climbing, and woken with sore calves, and stood at her window facing north without deciding to.

Vocation, Brin had called it once, watching Paloma reorganize a toolbox that wasn't hers by instinct rather than instruction. *It's not a skill, child. Skills live in the head. That lives lower down. That's vocation.*

Paloma raised her hand.

The motion traveled through the hall like current through wire. Heads turned. Ama's grip tightened on her wrist and then, deliberately, loosened — a letting-go that was also a blessing. Somewhere behind her, Sefa made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled into a cough.

"State your name for the ledger," the moderator said.

"Paloma Thackeray."

"And your qualifications?"

She opened her mouth to say *none that I can document* — and stopped. Because the answer rose up out of her like water finding a crack, fluent and certain and entirely unavailable to her conscious mind:

"Forty-one months of continuous operation without a single missed maintenance window. Preventive replacement schedule calibrated to component fatigue curves rather than manufacturer intervals. Full diagnostic familiarity with pre-war archive architecture." She blinked. Her own voice sounded foreign in her ears, competent and dry as an incident report. "Also, I know where the spare fuses are hidden."

The hall laughed — startled, then warm. But the moderator didn't laugh. The moderator, an elderly man named Okoye whom Paloma vaguely knew from the water board, looked at her for a long moment with an expression she couldn't parse, and then said quietly:

"Noted for the ledger. Any second?"

Every hand in the hall went up. Every one. Even Voss's, in the third row, raised steady and unhidden, and Paloma saw Ama see it, and saw Ama nod once, small and hard, like a debt being acknowledged across a battlefield.

* * *

The vote itself was almost an afterthought. Two hundred and six in favor. Four abstentions. Zero against. When the moderator announced it, the sound the hall made was not cheering, exactly. It was deeper than that — a collective exhalation forty years held, the sound of a body releasing a breath it had forgotten it was taking. People embraced strangers. An old man in the back row wept openly into his hat. Ama pressed her shawl to her mouth and shook, and Idris leaned into Paloma's side, and she put her arm around him because her body knew how even though her mind had never been taught.

Afterward, the cloth came off the case.

It was the beacon's original nameplate — recovered from the ridge, Sefa explained later, cleaned and mounted for the ceremony. Beneath forty years of grime and one careful scrubbing, the metal bore an inscription in the old colonial standard, the kind used on foundation stones and ship hulls:

**THIS LIGHT KEEPS WHAT WAS ENTRUSTED TO IT.
MAINTAIN IT AS YOU WOULD A PROMISE.**

Beneath that, smaller, a line of names. The archivists. The builders. And at the end of the list, added later in different engraving, shallower, done by hand with improvised tools:

**P. THACKERAY — MAINTAINER.**

Paloma stood in front of her own name and felt the strangest sensation of her strange new life: grief and pride occupying the same space, indistinguishable, like two voices singing the same note an octave apart.

"That's you," Idris said softly.

"It's someone with my name."

"It's you." He didn't argue further. He'd learned, she could tell, that arguing with her about this got him nowhere. Instead he just stood next to her, shoulder almost touching her elbow, and let the fact sit between them without requiring her to hold its weight.

Ama joined them. She looked at the nameplate for a long time, her lips moving slightly, and Paloma realized she was reading the archivists' names aloud under her breath — committing them, perhaps, or greeting them, the way you'd greet ancestors at a shrine.

"She kept it running," Ama said at last, not looking at Paloma, speaking to the plate, or to the woman the plate named. "Through everything. Through the sweeps and the harmonics and the fear. Alone, most nights." Her voice caught and steadied. "Alone, all those nights."

"I wish I remembered her," Paloma said. It was the truest sentence she owned.

Ama turned then and took both her hands, and her grip was fierce and papery and absolutely certain. "You don't need to remember her," she said. "You need to *be* her. Memory is just the story we tell. The hands remember the truth." She pressed Paloma's palms together, folding her fingers closed around nothing, around air, around the shape of a tool. "Do you feel that?"

"I feel— " Paloma frowned. "Cold metal. A latch. North-facing wind."

"Yes," Ama said simply, and released her. "That's her. That's enough of her. The rest we'll tell you at dinner, slowly, for the rest of your life."

* * *

The first official entry in the public ledger was written that same night, by lamplight, at a table dragged out onto the assembly hall steps because no one wanted to go indoors yet. The moderator wrote the header in careful block capitals: *Sector Nine Public Ledger, Volume One. Beacon Station, North Ridge.* Then he passed the pen to Paloma, because protocol said the senior maintainer entered first, and the assembly had confirmed her by acclamation before anyone thought to ask whether she wanted the title.

She hesitated over the blank line.

Around her, the colony was doing what colonies do after historic votes: arguing happily, eating food that had appeared from somewhere, children chasing each other between the legs of adults who pretended not to notice. Idris was teaching two younger kids a card game with elaborate cheating rules. Sefa and Brin were deep in a dispute about whether the beacon's transmission array should get a weather housing, a dispute Paloma could tell had been running for weeks and was mostly an excuse to enjoy each other's company. Voss had left quietly an hour ago, pausing at the door to look back at the hall — at the nameplate, at the people — with that same unreadable expression, before walking out into the dark alone.

Paloma looked down at the pen in her hand and found her fingers had already positioned it correctly. Found that she knew, without knowing, how maintenance logs were formatted: date, time, component status, actions taken, signature. Found that her hand wanted to write a header she hadn't consciously chosen.

She let it write.

*Entry 1. Resumed active watch. All systems nominal. No anomalies.*

Then, below it, in smaller script, her hand added a line she did not remember deciding to add:

*To whoever reads this ledger: the light was never dark. Someone kept it. Ask the children what she gave. Tell them thank you.*

Paloma stared at the words. Her handwriting. Her phrasing. A message from a woman she had never met, delivered through her own fingers.

"Paloma." Idris had appeared at her elbow, card deck forgotten, looking up at her with an expression she'd come to know well lately — the careful gentleness the whole family wore around her, love handled like something breakable. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing." She capped the pen. Closed the ledger. "Everything's nominal."

He smiled at that — a real smile, quick and crooked, gone as fast as it came, like it had surprised him. "That's what she used to say," he said. "Word for word. Ama will want to know that."

He ran off toward the card game, and Paloma sat with the ledger under the lamplight, on the steps of a hall full of people who loved a woman she couldn't remember being, and felt — beneath the loss, beneath the vertigo, beneath everything — a small, stubborn, unmistakable warmth.

Vocation. Lower down than memory. Still burning.

Far to the north, on the ridge, the beacon turned its light through the dark, keeping what was entrusted to it. And that night, for the first time since the broadcast, nine children fell asleep — and the dream came back.
