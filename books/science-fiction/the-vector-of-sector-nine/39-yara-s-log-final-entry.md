## 39. Yara's Log, Final Entry

*Recovered from the personal effects of the author known as "Yara Galloway," aboard the survey vessel Meridian's Daughter, in orbit above Sector Nine Colony. The journal is handwritten on salvaged paper stock, bound with wire from a decommissioned relay array. Carbon dating places its composition approximately one hundred and twelve years after the Broadcast. Transcribed without emendation by the Colonial Historical Trust.*

---

**Entry 1 — Arrival Day**

I came to Sector Nine to write about a beacon.

That sentence sounds smaller than it is. Let me try again. I came to Sector Nine because every archive I consulted — and I consulted all of them, the Consortium remnant libraries on Ceres Station, the recovered Meridian Gate files, three generations of doctoral theses that have worn grooves in each other's citations — every single source agreed on one fact: there is a structure on the northern ridge of Sector Nine Colony that has been maintained continuously for over a century, and no record explains why it was built.

A hundred years of maintenance. That is not neglect. Neglect does not leave oil in the hinges. A hundred years of someone climbing a ridge in the wind to keep a machine alive, and not one document saying what the machine was *for*. The war ended because of that ridge. Every child in the sector learns it before they learn their own blood type. And the histories — even the honest ones, even the ones written after the arrests and the trials and Voss's testimony, which I have read so many times I could recite his father's voice along with him — the histories all go quiet at exactly the moment you'd want them loudest. They say: *a signal was broadcast.* They say: *the truth was carried on willingly given memory, and the jammers failed against it.* They do not say who kept the thing warm in the dark decades before anyone was listening.

So I came to find out. Writers are scavengers of other people's silences. We tell ourselves it's a vocation.

The colony received me the way colonies receive everyone: with suspicion shaped like hospitality. My berth in the guest quarters smells of recycled air and someone else's cooking. Outside my window the ridge stands black against the stars, and at its crown there is a light that pulses once every eleven seconds. I timed it. Eleven seconds, steady as a pulse, steady as something alive deciding to keep being alive.

Tomorrow I begin interviews. Tonight I am going to sit by this window and watch the light blink and feel like a man reading the last page of a book first, knowing he'll spend the rest of his life learning why it ends that way.

---

**Entry 4 — The Roster**

I saw the roster today.

It hangs in the colony administrative hall now — the same building where, a century ago, Deputy Administrator Voss kept his polite, terrible office. They repurposed it. There's a mural on the east wall: nine children holding hands around a spire of light, rendered in the naive, earnest style of post-war public art, and beneath it, framed under glass, the Maintainer's Ledger.

The ledger began as an insurance archive. I know this because the first entries are written in the margins of genuine claims documents — cargo lift failures, pressure blowouts, water reclamation faults — a decade of impossible accidents recorded in a neat, methodical hand, each one annotated with coordinates that, when I plotted them tonight on my cabin floor with string and pins, formed a perfect perimeter around the beacon's position. She knew. Whoever she was, she saw the shape of the lie years before anyone else did, and she wrote it down where no one would think to look, because she understood that the safest place for a dangerous truth is inside something boring.

Her name heads the roster. It has headed the roster since the recommissioning vote, when the colony chose — openly, publicly, with a public ledger and a published schedule — to keep the light burning. Her name is first. Every maintainer since has signed beneath it, and beneath theirs, and theirs, down through four generations of handwriting, some careful, some scrawled, one entry apparently written by a child with a crayon, which the archivists have preserved rather than corrected.

Paloma Thackeray.

No one recalls her face.

I want you to understand how strange that sentence is, how it sat in my chest all day like a swallowed stone. This colony remembers everything else. They remember Idris Okonkwo's sketchbooks — the originals are in the school display case, spirals drawn by a twelve-year-old who didn't know why. They remember Ama Okonkwo's bargain with Voss; there's a plaque about grandmothers that makes tourists cry. They remember Sefa's harmonics discovery, Brin's hymnal, the coffin on the crematorium road, all nine keys given, all nine names. The children are remembered individually, lovingly, with the specificity that only survivors can give. But the woman who gave the ninth key — the dearest memory, the found family entire, every dinner, every name, every night at the table — she survives as a signature and a vacancy.

They know what she did. They cannot show you her face. The memory she gave was the memory of them, and the price of carrying them out of the dark was never seeing them again.

I asked the schoolteacher — a patient woman named Ferro with dust on her sleeves — whether any image survived. Portraits, photographs, a description.

She thought about it honestly, which I appreciated. "There are echoes," she said. "In the dream."

"The dream?"

"The children still share it. Not all children, not always — but enough, often enough, that we don't call it coincidence anymore. No countdown now. Just the red star, and the voice saying thank you." She paused at the window, looking up at the ridge. "And sometimes, underneath the voice, there's laughter. A woman's laugh. The children say it sounds like someone who has just heard a good joke told badly." She smiled at me. "We think that's her."

**Entry 9 — What the Schools Teach**

Today I sat in the back of a history classroom and watched eleven-year-olds learn about the false incursion at Meridian Gate.

I confess I expected propaganda. Every generation curates its founding myths, and Sector Nine's founding myth involves children paying for truth with pieces of themselves — the kind of story that curdles easily into either hagiography or horror. Instead the teacher, a young man named Osei with his grandmother's ledger-signature three rows down from Thackeray's, taught it like this:

He asked the class what the war was about. Hands went up. Territory, said one child. Resources, said another. Fear, said a third, and Osei nodded at that one longer than the others.

Then he played them the recording. The woman's voice — Oyelaran, dated to the war's opening week, describing the incursion that never happened, the attack the Consortium manufactured to justify forty years of power. The room went absolutely still. These children have heard it before; it's in the curriculum. But hearing it again made them quiet again, and I understood that this was the point. Not memorization. Re-quieting. The lesson wasn't the facts. The lesson was the silence afterward, and what you decide to do with it.

Then Osei asked the question I'd been waiting for, the one I'd have asked at their age: "Was it worth it? Nine children gave away memories. One woman gave away everyone she loved. Was the truth worth that price?"

A girl in the front row — sharp-faced, unimpressed, clearly the class skeptic — raised her hand. "We wouldn't be here if they hadn't," she said. "But I don't think it was a price. I think it was a choice. Those are different things."

Osei looked at her for a long moment. "Explain."

"A price is taken from you. A choice is what you hand over when you'd rather lose it than keep it safe while everything else burns." She shrugged, twelve years old and terrifying. "My grandmother says the dream already took pieces of them anyway. So they chose what it took. That's not a price. That's dignity."

I wrote her words down verbatim. I will steal them shamelessly. Writers are scavengers, and that girl is a quarry.

Afterward I walked up to the ridge alone. The beacon hummed under my palm, warm the way the archive core was described as warm — warm after decades, warm against all physics, warm like a held breath. The maintenance logbook sits in a weatherproof case bolted beside it, and I read the most recent entries. Oil in the hinges. Alignment verified. Signal strength nominal. Signed, dated, and beneath every signature, in every era, the same small ritual notation that appears in the very first entry, in Paloma Thackeray's methodical hand:

*Light held. Purpose remembered.*

Even though she couldn't remember it herself. Even though the purpose had been cut out of her along with the faces. She wrote it anyway, or the ones after her wrote it for her, and somewhere in that distinction — between what a person knows and what a person keeps — is the whole story I came here to find.

---

**Entry 15 — The Echoes**

I have been granted access to the Vector Archive itself. I should record how extraordinary that is, plainly, before my professional detachment smooths it over: I put my hand on a machine that ended a war, and it let me listen.

The archivists were generous but firm about protocol. The core still runs on willing memory — that hasn't changed, can't change, they explained; freely given memory cannot be retracted or forged, which is precisely why the broadcast couldn't be jammed, and precisely why the archive remains incorruptible a century on. What they showed me was the echo layer. When the keys were given, the Vector — whatever it truly is, an intelligence, an instrument, a voice that learned to say *deliver us* and then, later, *thank you* — retained resonances. Not the memories themselves. Shadows of them. Enough to teach with. Enough, the archivists believe, for the children's shared dream to carry forward.

I listened to the echo of Idris Okonkwo's key. His father's face. He gave it at thirteen — no, twelve, forgive me, twelve, the age he drew the spirals — and the echo is not the memory but the shape of its absence, a contour of loss so precise it functions almost like presence. You can hear, if you listen the way the archivists taught me, the exact dimensions of a man's smile as defined by the hole it left in his son.

And then they played me the ninth echo. Thackeray's.

I am going to set down what happened as honestly as I can, because I suspect it matters more than anything else in this journal.

The echo is not one memory. It is a constellation. Dinners at Ama's table — I could hear dishes, hear a grandmother's laugh, hear a boy explaining card games with the ruthless pedantry of the young. Nights on the ridge. A mechanic named Sefa swearing cheerfully at engines. A retired teacher reciting poetry she'd hidden in a hymnal for forty years. Nine names. Dozens of ordinary evenings. An entire family, catalogued with the methodical tenderness of a woman who had been lonely for a very long time and knew exactly what she'd found.

And threaded through all of it — this is the part I keep returning to — the echo carries her attention. Not just what she remembered, but *how* she remembered: the insurance investigator's habit of noticing, the way she counted the chairs at the table, checked the locks, noted who laughed at which joke. She archived her love the way she archived impossible accidents. Cross-referenced. Perimetered. Kept.

The head archivist, an old man named Dey who signs his maintenance shifts directly beneath her name, watched my face while it played. "Everyone cries at the ninth echo," he said, not unkindly. "You're the first one who laughed."

I hadn't realized I had. But somewhere in the middle of it — at a moment where a boy's voice in the echo insists, with tremendous dignity, that he has definitely won this hand — something in me recognized the joke, and I laughed, and then I cried, and Dey nodded like both were expected.

"Sometimes people ask why we don't reconstruct her," he said afterward, walking me down off the ridge in the dark. "We have the echoes. We could build a likeness, a simulation, something. The technology exists." He stopped at the trailhead and looked back up at the light pulsing its eleven-second pulse. "But she didn't give her memory so we could rebuild her. She gave it so the truth could survive without her. Reassembling her would undo the gift. So instead we do what the children started doing, a few years after the Broadcast." He smiled. "We tell stories about a woman who maintained a beacon. And when a stranger comes asking — writers, historians, lost people — we introduce her slowly. The way you'd introduce anyone worth knowing."

---

**Entry 22 — Final Entry**

I have finished the book. It took me two years, and I have decided to publish it under a pen name, which will strike future readers as either modesty or cowardice, and is in fact neither. It is inheritance. The people in this story paid in memory for the right to be forgotten safely; the least I can do is stand behind their story without standing in front of it.

Tonight is my last night aboard station before the long transit home. The colony threw me a dinner — at the Okonkwo house, of course; it is still the Okonkwo house, five generations on, still the table where truths get told. Idris's great-great-granddaughter presides, a matriarch with Ama's eyes and Sefa's hands, and when I rose to leave she pressed something into my palm and closed my fingers over it.

A playing card. The ace. Worn soft at the edges.

"She'd have liked you," the matriarch said. "The woman who built this. She liked people who asked the second question."

I walked up the ridge one final time. The night was clear, the red star visible low on the horizon — the star from the dream, the star from the map, still burning its slow burn a hundred years on. The beacon pulsed. Eleven seconds. Light held. Purpose remembered.

And I stood there in the wind, holding a card I had no reason to treasure, and I understood at last what I'd actually come to Sector Nine to learn. It was never about the mechanism. It was never even about the lie, or the exposure, or the war's ugly ending. It was this: that memory is not what we keep. Memory is what we give. Everything else — the archives, the ledgers, the names on rosters, the journals of visiting writers — is only the packaging. The gift itself is a woman choosing, at the top of a cold ridge, with her whole found family watching and none of them knowing her, to hand over the best thing she owned so that strangers a century unborn could stand in the truth.

Her name heads the roster. No one recalls her face. But her laughter lives in the signal, and her handwriting opens the ledger, and every maintainer who climbs this ridge writes her notation beneath their name like a heartbeat continuing:

*Light held. Purpose remembered.*

I signed below her tonight. Dey witnessed it. Four generations of signatures, and now mine, and someday someone else's, and the light goes on.

One last thing, and then I close this book for good. As I came down off the ridge, a child passed me on the path — seven or eight, running uphill against the cold with the absolute conviction of youth, late for something important. She stopped when she saw me, caught her breath, and studied my face with the frank appraisal children reserve for strangers and mysteries.

"You're the writer," she said. "Are you here about her?"

"About who?" I asked, though I knew.

"The woman who maintained the beacon." The child grinned, and for half a second — I swear this, I swear it on every page I've written — her grin was shaped exactly like a laugh I'd heard in an echo, a laugh threaded through a shared dream, a laugh belonging to someone no one recalls. "She's still up there, you know. In the light. Grandmother says if you stand very still and listen to the pulse, sometimes it sounds like she's counting us."

Then she ran on, up toward the ridge, toward the light, small and swift and certain, and I stood in the dark and listened.

Eleven seconds. Ten. Nine.

Counting.

*— Yara Galloway, aboard Meridian's Daughter, last night in orbit above Sector Nine*
