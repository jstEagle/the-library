## 1. Nine Seconds

The morning began, as every morning had for eleven years, with Jonah Yarrow arguing with a tomato plant.

"You're being dramatic," she told it. "You have water. You have light. You have the exact nutrient ratio that your great-great-grandmother thrived on back when anyone on Earth still cared whether you lived or died. And yet here you are, sulking."

The tomato plant, a lanky heirloom variety designated VES-114 in the vault's catalog and *Gerald* in Jonah's private one, drooped its leaves in what she chose to interpret as contrition.

Station Meridian hung at the trailing edge of the asteroid belt like a paperclip someone had set down and forgotten. It was shaped — from the outside — like a spindle wound with thread, the old solar collectors wrapped around its spine in tarnished silver coils. From the inside, it was shaped like everything Jonah had ever known: four kilometers of corridors, cold-storage vaults, hydroponics bays, and one greenhouse where she grew things that weren't strictly necessary, just to prove she could.

She checked Gerald's soil moisture, adjusted the drip line by a fraction of a milliliter, and moved on to the wheat trays, humming. The hum was an old habit. In her first year aboard she'd caught herself going days without speaking aloud and decided, with the grim practicality of a woman who'd read too many articles about long-duration isolation, that she would talk to the plants instead. It was either that or start narrating her own life like a documentary, and she'd seen how that ended for people in fiction.

"Good morning, everyone," she said now, to three hundred seedlings and forty thousand seeds sleeping in the dark below her feet. "Today is Day Four Thousand and Sixty-Two of the Meridian Custodial Log. Weather outside is vacuum, as usual. Highs near absolute zero. Chance of meteor showers: nonzero, but I've stopped taking it personally."

The vault AI, Meridian-Core, did not answer. It hadn't answered in years — not properly. It still ran the environmental systems, still kept the cryo-drawers at their precise temperatures, still logged everything with the tireless patience of a machine that didn't know it had been abandoned along with everyone else. But its higher functions had degraded into something Jonah thought of as *Core-speak*: fragments of protocol language, half-finished sentences, the occasional unprompted recitation of shipping manifests from a decade ago. Talking to Meridian-Core these days was like talking to a brilliant uncle who'd had a stroke and mostly remembered the weather.

"Core, status check," she said anyway, because hope is also a habit.

There was a pause — there was always a pause, long enough that she'd learned to count to five before giving up — and then the speakers crackled:

*"Vault integrity nominal. Drawer temperatures nominal. Reminder: shipment 88-C is overdue by four thousand and sixty-two days."*

"Thank you, Core. I'll chase that up with the fleet."

*"Noted. Have a productive cycle, Custodian Yarrow."*

It was the same sentence every morning, delivered in the same mild voice, and Jonah had come to love it the way you love a scar: because it marked something that had happened to you and hadn't killed you. She pressed her palm briefly against the warm bulkhead, which was as close to affection as she allowed herself before breakfast, and went to make coffee.

* * *

Coffee on Meridian was a ritual involving recycled water, beans grown in Bay Three, and a grinder that sounded like a small animal being murdered. Jonah had named the grinder nothing, because some relationships shouldn't be encouraged.

While it screamed, she recorded the morning log.

This was the part of the day that mattered most, though she'd never have admitted it. Every day at 0700 station time, Station Meridian broadcast a maintenance log on the old colonial charter band, aimed at the colony fleet's last known heading. The fleet — eleven ships carrying the last organized remnant of humanity's exodus, the people who'd built this vault and staffed it and promised, *promised*, they'd send relief crews on rotation — had gone silent six years ago.

Jonah had kept broadcasting anyway.

The first year after the silence, the logs were professional. *Vault humidity nominal. Backup generator two requires bearing replacement. Requesting instructions regarding pollen filtration schedule.* She'd read them aloud in her best radio voice, crisp and calm, a woman reporting for duty to superiors who were merely delayed.

By year three, the logs had developed opinions. *Generator two's bearing has been replaced twice and I want it noted that I did it alone, upside down, in the dark, and I would like a raise. Requesting instructions. Or a reply. A single word would do. 'No' would do.*

By year five, they were letters. *Day 2,841. I fixed the coolant loop in Vault C today. You'd have liked it — it was elegant work, if I say so myself. The rye came up in Bay One. First germination in eight months. I stood there like an idiot and applauded. I hope wherever you are, someone applauded for you.*

Now, on Day 4,062, the logs had settled into something between report and diary, addressed to no one and everyone, transmitted into the dark at lightspeed toward coordinates where, as far as Jonah knew, there was nothing left to listen.

"This is Custodian Jonah Yarrow, recording maintenance log for Station Meridian, colonial charter band, standard encryption." She wrapped both hands around her mug and watched steam curl in the low gravity. "All vaults nominal. Cryo-drawers holding at minus one-ninety-six, give or take a rounding error I refuse to feel bad about. Greenhouse yield this week: two hundred grams of wheat, forty grams of soy, and one tomato, which I ate, and which was transcendent, and which I will not be apologizing for.

"Backup generator two continues to run smoothly, which surprises us both. Solar collector efficiency down another point two percent; I'll do a cleaning pass Thursday. Water reclamation at ninety-four percent. The mice — yes, we still have mice, don't ask me how, the universe finds a way — remain confined to cargo ring two, where I have declared them a sovereign nation and extended diplomatic recognition in exchange for their continued avoidance of the seed vaults.

"In personal news: I dreamed about rain again. Real rain, the kind that falls sideways and ruins picnics. I woke up homesick for weather I've never liked. Make of that what you will.

"Meridian out."

She let the recorder run a moment longer than necessary, the way you linger in a doorway after saying goodbye. Then she cut the transmission and got to work.

* * *

The work filled the day the way water fills a hull: completely, and without asking permission.

Mornings were the vaults themselves — four kilometers of climate-controlled darkness stacked with drawer after drawer of seeds. Wheat and rice and maize. Potatoes and yams and cassava. Apples, citrus, coffee, cacao. Ten thousand years of agriculture folded into cold sleep, waiting for a world worth planting in. Jonah walked the aisles with her tablet, checking seals, logging temperatures, replacing the occasional gasket. She knew the vaults the way other people knew their own heartbeat. She could navigate them blind. Once, on a dare she'd made to herself, she had.

Afternoons were systems: scrubbers and pumps and the endless negotiation with the solar collectors, which collected dust and micrometeorite damage with the enthusiasm of teenagers collecting bad decisions. Evenings were the greenhouse, where she talked to Gerald and his descendants and pretended the tomatoes couldn't hear her gossiping about the mice.

And nights — nights were hers. She read. She exercised badly. She watched recordings of old broadcasts from Earth, decades stale, and laughed at jokes nobody living had written. She kept a paper journal, actual ink on actual cellulose, because digital files could be edited and she wanted, somewhere, a record she couldn't accidentally revise.

Some nights she stood at the observation blister and looked at the stars and felt very small and very fine, like a single correct note held in an empty concert hall.

Tonight, though, she was elbow-deep in the water reclamation unit, fishing a filter membrane out of its housing, when the universe noticed her back.

* * *

It started as a wrongness in her inner ear.

Jonah had spent eleven years calibrated to Meridian's spin gravity — a modest third of a g, enough to keep water in cups and moods level. Her body knew it the way it knew the sound of her own name. So when the floor *let go*, she knew before her mind caught up. The wrench drifted out of her hand. Her stomach floated up behind her ribs. The coffee mug she'd left on the workbench rose, tumbled, and shed a slow brown galaxy across the compartment.

"Oh," said Jonah, with tremendous dignity, "no."

Then the alarms started.

They came all at once — not one alarm but all of them, the whole station waking up screaming. Pressure warnings from the greenhouse. Coolant flow alerts from the vaults. The spin stabilizers reporting, in increasingly frantic machine language, that the flywheels were losing authority over the rotation. Somewhere below her feet, forty thousand years of agricultural heritage sloshed gently against its containment.

Jonah pushed off the workbench and swam for the corridor, catching the handrail, hauling herself along in a graceless breaststroke she hadn't practiced since certification training a decade ago. Emergency lights strobed red. Through the viewport of Junction Four, she saw the stars wheeling past — faster than they should, then slower, then *not at all*, the whole sky stuttering like a corrupted video file.

Her tablet, strapped to her forearm, was shrieking a summary she couldn't bear to look at directly. She looked anyway.

**GRAVITY ANOMALY — SYSTEM-WIDE. ALL STATION MASS SENSORS REPORTING FLUCTUATION. SOURCE: UNKNOWN.**

System-wide. Not a flywheel fault. Not a structural failure. Not anything that lived inside Meridian's aging bones. Something was reaching through the entire system — through the belt, through the dark, through every rock and ship and station — and squeezing.

For nine seconds, Jonah Yarrow floated in the middle of her home and felt the universe put a hand on it.

Terror came first. Terror was honest: she was alone, four light-hours from the nearest human being (whoever they were now), and physics itself was misbehaving. Her hands shook. Her breath came short and fast in the thin air.

But underneath the terror, unwelcome and unmistakable, something else bloomed.

*Contact.*

That was the shameful truth of it, the thing she'd never have put in a log. For nine seconds, the universe had reached out and touched her station — clumsily, violently, the way a giant might poke a snow globe — and every instrument she owned was singing, and somewhere out there was a *cause*. An event. A thing that happened, with a time stamp, that she could point to and say: *I was there. I felt it. It was real, and so am I.*

Six years of silence, cracked open by nine seconds of catastrophe.

She hated herself for the thrill. She felt it anyway, bright and electric, right up until the greenhouse sprinklers blew.

* * *

The problem with a gravity flicker, Jonah discovered while swimming through her own flooding greenhouse, is that water doesn't care about your schedule.

Bay Three's irrigation system ran on timers calibrated for steady spin gravity. When gravity vanished, the reservoirs emptied upward and outward in slow, beautiful, catastrophic blooms. When gravity returned — snapping back mid-cycle, hard and sudden — all that suspended water remembered which way was down and fell at once.

Jonah came around the corner into a rainstorm.

It was, absurdly, lovely for about two seconds: a full bay of falling water, catch-lights strobing red and white, droplets hanging in the emergency lights like beads on black string. Then it landed, all of it, and became knee-deep floodwater full of topsoil and shattered planter fragments and one extremely indignant tomato plant bobbing past like a tiny green castaway.

"Gerald!" Jonah lunged, caught the plant by its stake, and cradled it above the waterline. "I've got you. Don't be dramatic. This is not about you."

It was, in fairness, a little about him. The flood had drowned two of the wheat trays and knocked the soy racks flat. Water was seeping toward the electrical conduits along the south wall. Somewhere in the chaos, the irrigation main was still open, cheerfully adding to the problem.

Jonah wedged Gerald into an empty planter on the highest shelf, kissed her fingers, and touched them to a leaf. "Stay there. Be brave. Think of your ancestors."

Then she waded to the main shutoff, turned it with both hands and one muttered curse, and began the long business of putting her world back together.

It took hours. It took towels — she owned forty-one towels and used every one. It took a wet-vac improvised from a cargo loader fan and more profanity than she'd produced in the previous year combined. The mice, sensing opportunity, staged a brief incursion into the flooded corridor and were repelled with a broom. The wheat trays were beyond saving; she drained them, mourned them, and quietly scheduled new sowings. The soy racks she righted and staked. The conduits stayed dry by a margin of centimeters, and she wrote a note to move them higher, and knew she wouldn't, and wrote it anyway.

By the time she finally sat down — soaked to the thigh, hair plastered to her skull, drinking reclaimed-water tea because the coffee had joined the flood — the station was quiet again. The alarms had gone back to sleep. Meridian-Core, unflappable even in its decay, had already restored irrigation schedules to nominal.

Only the sensor data remained, scrolling patiently on her tablet, waiting for her to understand what had happened.

She pulled up the mass-fluctuation record and stared at it for a long time.

The curve was clean. That was the eerie part. Whatever had gripped the system hadn't been an accident — accidents were messy, noisy, full of harmonics and error bars. This was smooth. Deliberate. A modulation, rising and falling across exactly nine seconds, and then gone. And the timing sync across her instruments — the ones pointed inward at the belt, outward at the deep dark — placed the epicenter nowhere near any human outpost she knew of.

Somewhere out there, something had flexed, and the whole system had felt it.

Jonah sat in her ruined greenhouse, among the mud and the towels and the survivors, and felt the loneliness crack open like ice on a spring river. Because whatever had done this — weapon, experiment, accident, miracle — it meant the dark wasn't empty. It meant events were happening. It meant that somewhere, someone had built a machine capable of reaching across a star system and squeezing it, and machines like that required builders, and builders required motives, and motives required *people*.

Six years of silence. Nine seconds of contact.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the plants, to the mice, to the empty station, to whoever or whatever was listening. "Okay. I see you."

Far overhead, through the greenhouse glass, the stars hung motionless and innocent, and did not answer.

But her tablet chimed — a soft, routine notification, the kind she'd dismissed ten thousand times. Automatic telemetry flag. Deep-space array, sector seven, anomalous energy discharge detected at timestamp matching the flicker. Origin coordinates attached.

Origin coordinates attached to a derelict relay station that had been dead for thirty years.

Kestrel-7.

Jonah looked at the numbers for a long moment. Then she opened a fresh log file, titled it *Unofficial — Do Not Transmit*, and began, for the first time in six years, to write down what she actually thought.
