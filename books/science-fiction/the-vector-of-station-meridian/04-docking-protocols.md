## 4. Docking Protocols

The Vespera announced itself the way a well-bred guest announces itself: politely, precisely, and with an undertone of *I could have simply let myself in*.

"Station Meridian, this is Remnant corvette *Vespera*, requesting docking clearance on behalf of Fleet Command." The voice was clipped, professional, and so relentlessly calm that Jonah found herself wondering what it sounded like when it wasn't. "We are honored to accept your hospitality."

*Hospitality.* Eleven years she'd broadcast into the dark, and the first humans to answer had done so by assuming there would be tea.

She sat in the docking control room for a full minute before answering, because her voice — her actual voice, the one that wasn't a maintenance log read aloud to nobody — hadn't been used for conversation since the last supply drone, which had not been conversational. She cleared her throat. It felt like starting an engine left out in the cold.

"Meridian copies, Vespera. You're cleared for bay two. Approach slow; my guidance lasers are older than both of us."

A pause. Then, faintly amused: "Understood. Slower than both of us."

Jonah watched the corvette grow from a bright point to a shape to a *ship*, all hard angles and hull plating scarred by reentry burns and whatever else the Remnant had been doing out there in the years since they'd stopped writing back. She caught herself standing up straighter. Then caught herself doing it, and made a conscious effort to slouch, and then felt ridiculous either way, and settled on standing normally, which after eleven years alone required more concentration than docking a ship.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, she thought, as the clamps engaged with a shudder she felt through the deck plates and up through her boots and into her teeth. Not the danger. The *audience*. For eleven years she'd been the only witness to her own life. She talked to plants. She narrated her breakfast to a camera that archived it and never once judged her for eating cold noodles over the sink at 0300. Now there were going to be people — plural, uniformed, armed people — walking through her home, and every one of them would be a mirror, and she had no idea what they'd see.

The airlock cycled. Jonah stood at parade rest at the bottom of the ramp, which was a posture she'd invented forty seconds ago based on vague memories of old fleet dramas.

Captain Ilsa Rehn came down first.

Jonah had imagined the meeting a hundred ways during the approach window, and none of them matched. Rehn was shorter than expected, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that had stopped spending effort on expression somewhere around a decade ago and now spent it only where strictly necessary. Her uniform was immaculate in the way of someone who had subordinates whose entire job was immaculateness. Behind her came four crew — two Marines who moved like furniture being careful, and a slim, quick-eyed man carrying a data case, whom Rehn introduced as "Aide Pell," in the tone of someone introducing a tool.

"Captain Yarrow," Rehn said, extending a hand.

"Just Jonah, actually. I'm not — there's no fleet anymore, technically. I just kept the title because it came with the station."

Something flickered across Rehn's face. It might have been sympathy. It might have been assessment. After eleven years of reading plant health off leaf color, Jonah was out of practice with faces, and this one gave away nothing it didn't intend to.

"Then we'll say Keeper," Rehn said. "It's more accurate, and it costs you less."

"That's very kind."

"It isn't kindness. It's bookkeeping." And then, before Jonah could decide whether that was a joke or a threat, Rehn smiled — briefly, precisely, like a stamp applied to a document. "Thank you for having us, Keeper. We won't take more than we need."

Which, Jonah thought as she led them down the main spine toward the vault, was exactly the sort of sentence that meant the opposite of what it said.

* * *

The tour took ninety minutes, and Jonah spent all of them performing a version of herself she didn't entirely recognize.

It wasn't lying, exactly. Everything she showed them was real: the hydroponics rings turning their slow green carousel, the water reclamation stacks humming their chord of small complaints, the seed vault itself — row upon row of cryo-drawers holding eleven thousand years of agricultural memory, wheat and rice and sorghum and things with names like *quinoa*'s obscure cousin. She explained the pollination drones. She explained the backup power. She watched Pell's eyes track everything and catalogued him right back: he touched nothing without asking, which was either excellent manners or excellent training, and he asked about power draw twice, which was either engineering curiosity or something else.

"And this," Jonah said, gesturing broadly at a bulkhead near the vault's lower level, "is environmental control. Filters, scrubbers, the works. Very boring. You don't want the tour."

Behind that bulkhead lay the deep archive: Meridian-Core's original command core, the pre-collapse records, the master telemetry buffers, and a number of other things that Jonah had decided, roughly six hours ago when the fleets appeared on her scopes, were no one's business but hers and the station's.

Rehn looked at the bulkhead. Looked at Jonah. Looked back at the bulkhead.

"Boring," Rehn repeated, in a tone that suggested she found boredom personally offensive.

"The most boring part of the station," Jonah confirmed. "I've considered having it removed."

"I'm sure." Rehn let it go with the particular grace of someone filing a debt rather than forgiving one. "Show me the greenhouse instead. My botanist will want a report, and I'd rather he hear it from you than from his imagination."

The greenhouse was Jonah's favorite room and also the most dangerous one to show strangers, because it was where she lived, in every way that mattered. Tomatoes gone slightly feral along the south trellis. A lemon tree grown from a seed that had no business being in an agricultural vault and that Jonah had never had the heart to evict. Her hammock, strung between two support beams, with a blanket still in it from last night.

She watched Rehn take it in — the hammock, the mug on the rail, the little hand-painted signs on the planter rows reading things like *SPEAK TO THEM, THEY'RE LISTENING* — and braced for pity or amusement.

What she got instead was quiet. Rehn stood among the green for a long moment, and some of the officer drained out of her posture, and what remained looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"We had gardens," she said, mostly to the tomatoes. "On the transit ships. First generation, everyone did. Second generation, half. Third generation—" She stopped. "Space is very good at teaching you what's optional."

"Lemons aren't optional," Jonah said. "I checked. I ran the numbers for three years trying to justify cutting this tree, and the numbers said cut it, and I didn't, and I've never regretted a day of it."

Rehn looked at her then — really looked, the assessment finally surfacing all the way — and whatever she saw seemed to adjust something in her ledger. "Keeper," she said again, softer this time. "How long has it been? Since another person stood here?"

"Eleven years, four months, nine days." Jonah winced. "I didn't mean to have that ready."

"No," Rehn said. "You meant to have everything else ready. That's why I noticed."

They walked back toward the docking ring in something almost like companionship, and Jonah hated how much she enjoyed it, and hated more that she noticed herself enjoying it, because enjoying it meant it could be taken away, and everything about the two fleets sitting in her sky said *taken away* was the house specialty.

At the junction by bay two, Rehn paused. "One question, Keeper, and forgive its bluntness. Nine seconds ago — the gravity event. Did your instruments record anything we should know about?"

"They recorded everything," Jonah said, honestly. "I'll give you the raw feed whenever you want it."

"Raw?" Pell spoke for the first time. His voice was mild and quick, a hummingbird of a voice. "Unprocessed?"

"It's my station," Jonah said. "I don't process things for guests. Guests get what actually happened."

Pell smiled. It was a nice smile. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which remained fixed on her with the pleasant, total attention of a man reading fine print. "I look forward to it."

* * *

They left — ostensibly to their ship, actually to wherever officers go to make decisions about other people's homes — and Jonah stood alone in bay two, exhaling for what felt like the first time in hours.

Being perceived, she decided, was exhausting. Not because anyone had been unkind. Because everyone had been *careful*. Every word exchanged had been weighed before delivery, including hers, especially hers. She'd spent eleven years saying exactly what she thought to an audience of tomato plants, and it had turned out that honesty was a muscle, and hers had atrophied into something strange and overdeveloped, flexing at all the wrong moments. *I don't process things for guests.* Who says that? Nobody says that. Plants say that. Plants don't even say that.

She was halfway through composing the log entry — *Day 4,041: Company. Verdict pending.* — when the proximity alarm chimed, soft and apologetic, the way she'd programmed it, because she couldn't stand alarms that shouted.

Something was shadowing the docking bay.

She pulled the scope feed. There, holding position just outside weapons-safe range — close enough to watch, far enough to claim innocence — hung a Concord shuttle. Sleek, dark-hulled, running dark except for a single navigation light blinking in a pattern Jonah recognized from old fleet manuals: not a distress code, not an identification code. An *observation* code. *I am watching. Do not mind me.*

They hadn't requested clearance. They hadn't hailed. They were simply there, parked in her sky like a crow on a fence, taking notes.

Jonah stared at the shuttle for a long time. Part of her wanted to hail it and demand answers. Part of her wanted to wave. Part of her — the part that had eaten cold noodles alone at 0300 for eleven years — wanted to put the kettle on, because whoever was inside that shuttle was also a person, probably also lonely, definitely also performing.

She keyed the general channel instead, kept her voice level, and addressed the empty bay as much as the sky.

"Meridian to unidentified shuttle. This is a neutral station. If you're going to loiter, loiter honestly — announce yourself, or move along. I count everything that orbits my home, and I keep records."

Silence. Then, after ten seconds, the shuttle's light changed pattern — acknowledgment, nothing more — and it slid smoothly behind the Vespera's bulk, where it could watch without being watched.

Jonah logged it. *Concord observation asset, uninvited, non-responsive. Filed under: things that are going to get worse.*

Then she went down to the bulkhead marked ENVIRONMENTAL CONTROL, laid her palm against the seal, and stood there listening to the deep archive breathe on the other side — the fans, the cryo-pumps, the old AI's dreaming hum.

"Nobody's getting in there," she told it quietly. "Not the polite ones, not the watchers. Whatever happens next, that stays ours."

The archive hummed back, which was either agreement or machinery, and after eleven years Jonah had learned those could sound identical.

Above her, through the bay's viewport, the two fleets hung in the dark like held breath — and the Concord shuttle blinked its patient, watching light, and waited for her to slip.
