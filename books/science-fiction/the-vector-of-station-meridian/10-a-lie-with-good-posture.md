## 10. A Lie With Good Posture

The hydroponics chapel still smelled of other people.

Jonah noticed it the moment the delegations left — a residue of cologne and recycled air and someone's over-sweet synthetic coffee, layered over the green smell of the barley racks like fingerprints on glass. For eleven years the station had smelled only of her: her sweat, her soap ration, the particular yeastiness of her sourdough starter, which she had named Gerald and talked to more than was strictly sane. Now the air itself felt crowded.

She stood in the doorway of the chapel long after the last shuttle undocked, holding a tray of cups nobody had finished, and let the silence settle back over her like a coat that no longer fit quite right.

"They drank my tea," she told the barley. "Both fleets. The same tea. That's practically diplomacy."

The barley did not answer. The barley never did. But it grew toward the lamps with the dumb, patient optimism she'd come to think of as the only honest posture in the system, and she found herself envying it.

* * *

She didn't decide to lie so much as she failed to fall asleep and then stopped pretending she'd been trying.

Her bunk was too quiet in the way it always was, but now the quiet had an audience. Somewhere out there, two fleets hung in the dark like wolves deciding whether the thing between them was prey or rival, and both of them had spent the afternoon looking at her — at Meridian, at the vault, at eleven years of her life's work — the way people look at a house they intend to repossess.

She got up. Made tea she wouldn't drink. Sat in the vault's monitoring room with its amber emergency lighting, surrounded by the soft hum of climate systems keeping two hundred thousand seed varieties alive, and opened the fleet channel she'd used for maintenance logs for a decade.

The interface looked the same as it always had. That was the strange part. The same fields, the same blinking cursor, the same little prompt: *RECIPIENT?* She had typed into this box thousands of times. Nitrogen levels. Germination rates. A joke about Gerald once, in year four, when she'd still believed someone might laugh.

"Okay," she said to the empty room. "Okay. Think."

Because here was what she knew, laid out on the table of her mind like instruments before surgery:

One. Both fleets wanted Meridian. Not for the seeds — she'd watched their faces during the tour; seeds were abstractions to them, a moral argument, not food — but for the position, the neutrality, the symbolism. A flag planted here was a flag planted in the story each side was telling about itself.

Two. Neither fleet could afford to fire first. The truce table had held today, barely, held by etiquette and mutual suspicion and the fact that whoever shot first would hand the other side the galaxy's sympathy.

Three. But occupation wasn't firing. Occupation was patience with guns. Six days, the negotiators had said, and then joint administration — which everyone present understood to mean a slow war conducted through requisition forms and checkpoint incidents until one side got tired of losing gracefully.

Four. And four was the one she kept circling back to, the one that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: they were all waiting for her to be harmless. The neutral hostess. The keeper of beans. The woman who had spent eleven years talking to plants and dead air. Harmless things get taken.

Jonah Yarrow looked at the cursor blinking in the dark and understood, with the calm clarity of three in the morning, that she had exactly one asset left, and it was not the vault, and it was not the telemetry, and it was certainly not the black box fragment locked in the deep archive like a heart she couldn't afford to show anyone.

Her asset was that both fleets believed she had nothing left to lose.

Which meant — and here her stomach turned over, slow and seasick — she could tell them anything.

* * *

She drafted it seven times.

The first draft was too apologetic. The second was too dramatic; she'd written *I regret to inform you* and immediately heard how much it sounded like a hostage video. The third she abandoned entirely when she caught herself addressing "the honorable delegations" in a voice that belonged to a woman who ironed things.

By the fifth draft she'd learned something important, which was that a good lie is mostly true. So she built it out of true materials, the way you build a fire from whatever's dry:

It was true that Meridian's archives were vast, older than either fleet, and incompletely mapped even by her. It was true that the station's original charter included contingency protocols neither government had ever declassified. It was true that she was the sole registered custodian, biometrically bound to the deepest tiers since the day the colony fleet's advance party had installed her — she remembered the needle, the cold pad against her palm, the technician saying *congratulations, you're the key now* like he was handing her a mop.

So she took those truths and set them in a row, and between them she placed the invention, carefully, the way you slip a forged page into a ledger:

*Meridian's deep archive is protected by a dead-man's-switch protocol,* she wrote. *Keyed to my biometrics. If I am removed from custody, incapacitated, or if control of the station transfers without my active authorization, the archive initiates a full cryptographic purge. Every record. Every seed lineage. Every piece of data both your commands have spent this week trying to read.*

She stared at that paragraph for a long time. It was elegant, she had to admit. It used their own greed as the lock. You couldn't seize the prize without destroying it, and the only person who could disarm the trap was the harmless hostess — which meant the harmless hostess suddenly had to be kept safe, comfortable, and exactly where she was.

It also meant, said a small voice in the back of her skull that sounded suspiciously like her mother, that you've just made yourself the most important person in the system by lying through your teeth.

"I prefer *strategic narration*," Jonah told the voice, and hit transmit.

* * *

The reply came faster than she expected, which was how she knew it had landed.

First the Remnant: a formal acknowledgment from Captain Rehn's office, requesting — *requesting*, note the word choice, someone aboard the Vespera had done quick math and decided courtesy was cheaper than boarding parties — a briefing on the protocol's technical specifications. Then the Concord, forty minutes later, with a message so nearly identical in structure that Jonah suspected both sides had the same manual, possibly written by the same contractor, possibly Halcyon, and she filed that thought away where it stung.

Then, most tellingly: both fleets' picket lines shifted. She watched it on the scope display with her tea going cold in her hand. Boarding shuttles powered down. Marine contingents stood down from alert to standby. Two warships that had been drifting toward docking range all evening eased back like dogs called off by a whistle only they could hear.

"It worked," she said aloud, and then, because the room was empty and honesty had to start somewhere: "Oh, God, it worked."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, nothing like her usual laugh, which was the laugh of a woman amused by her own jokes in an empty corridor. This one had teeth in it. She put her hand over her mouth as if the station might report her.

That was the intoxicating part, and she would be honest about it later, in the log that mattered, because she promised herself she would be honest about everything eventually, just not yet, not while the lie was still load-bearing: it felt *good*. Eleven years of broadcasting into silence, of being a rounding error in everyone's tactical planning, and now two fleets with a combined tonnage she didn't want to calculate had rearranged themselves around a sentence she'd made up in her pajamas. Words. She had moved warships with words. No wonder people did it professionally. No wonder the galaxy was such a mess.

And underneath the intoxication, coiled and patient, the shame. Because she knew exactly what she'd built. She'd built a wall out of a falsehood, and walls don't care how good your reasons are; they keep out whatever's on the other side, including rescue, including truth, including the version of herself she'd been trying to grow back to ever since the nine seconds cracked her open.

Gerald the sourdough needed feeding. She went and fed him, because some relationships in her life remained uncomplicated.

"You'd have liked it, though," she confided, folding flour into water. "The sheer audacity. Very rising-dough energy."

* * *

The technical requests arrived overnight, and this became the second act of the lie, the part she hadn't fully planned and had to improvise with her heart going like a trapped bird.

They wanted specifications. Frequencies, failover logic, verification methods. And Jonah — who had spent eleven years becoming genuinely, boringly expert in one narrow field, and who understood that expertise is mostly confidence plus paperwork — sat down and wrote them a protocol.

She based it on real architecture. Meridian-Core's actual security layers gave her the vocabulary; the old colonial charter's declassified fragments gave her the bureaucratic plausibility; and her own genuine biometric bindings gave her the foundation of truth that made the whole edifice stand up under weight. She invented a purge sequence with a plausible name — ARCHIVE INTEGRITY PROTOCOL MERIDIAN-PRIME, because bureaucracies trust anything with a prime in it — and a verification handshake, and a response latency, and a list of trigger conditions long enough to look thorough rather than evasive.

Where she didn't know, she obfuscated. Where she couldn't obfuscate, she classified. *Tier-One custodial data, clearance restricted to sitting custodian per Charter Section 9.* Both fleets' lawyers would chew on that for days, and lawyers, she had learned this week, move at the speed of continental drift.

Meridian-Core watched her work. The AI had been awake more often lately, surfacing in fragments, its degraded voice arriving like weather from a distant country.

*Query,* it said at 0300, in its rusted liturgy of a voice. *Protocol MERIDIAN-PRIME does not exist in my index.*

"No," Jonah agreed. "It doesn't."

*You are authorizing me to reference a protocol that does not exist.*

"I'm asking you to acknowledge that a protocol exists whose existence is classified above your index level." She held her breath. Lying to fleets was one thing. Lying to the only other resident of her home felt different, heavier, like lying to a dog.

A pause of four full seconds, which for Meridian-Core was practically a philosophical crisis.

*Acknowledged,* it said at last. *Note appended: index discrepancy logged. Custodian Yarrow, your logs have historically been accurate within negligible margins.*

"I know," Jonah said quietly. "I'm keeping score too."

*Noted,* said Meridian-Core, and if an AI could disapprove in one word, it had just done it, and Jonah found she deserved it, and that knowing she deserved it was somehow the worst part of the whole night.

* * *

By dawn, the board had changed shape.

The Remnant sent a formal notice suspending all boarding operations pending "custodial clarification." The Concord followed ninety minutes later with language so similar it was practically a duet. And then — the detail that made Jonah sit down slowly on the monitoring room floor, because sometimes your body knows before your mind does — both fleets independently requested that she be provided with additional security, provisions, and medical support, *to ensure custodian continuity.*

They were protecting her now. Both of them. Because she was the tripwire, and tripwires get guarded, and somewhere in the machinery of two armed camps, Jonah Yarrow had transformed from rounding error to indispensable fiction.

She should have felt safe. Instead she felt like a coin that two hands were hovering over, and she understood with perfect clarity that the moment either side decided the coin was counterfeit, the hands would close differently.

There was a knock at the comms panel — a hail, actually, routed through with old-fashioned politeness. Concord shuttle, single passenger, flag of parley. Envoy Okoro, requesting a word.

Jonah looked at herself in the dark reflection of the scope display: unwashed hair, tea-stained shirt, eyes ringed with the specific bruising of a woman who had lied to two governments before breakfast. She thought about the contraband tea, and his grief that had seemed genuine, and the way sympathy had been aimed at her like a weapon with excellent manners.

She thought about the fact that of everyone in the system, Dae-Sun Okoro was the one who had looked at her longest, and the ones who look longest are the ones most likely to see the seam.

"Meridian to Concord shuttle," she said, and made her voice warm, and hated how easily it came. "Envoy Okoro. Come aboard. I'll put the kettle on."

Somewhere below her feet, in the deep archive, the black box fragment sat in the dark holding the real truth, patient as a seed, waiting for a way out that she hadn't figured out yet — and above her, in the cold, two fleets circled a lie with very good posture, and waited to see how long it could hold its breath.
