## 22. An Honest Record

The station was quiet in the way it had always been quiet, but it was a different quiet now. Jonah noticed that the moment the last shuttle cleared the docking clamps and Meridian's hull stopped humming with other people's footsteps. It was the quiet of a house after a party — the same silence as before, but shaped by everything that had happened inside it.

She floated through the hydroponics chapel first, because she couldn't not. The negotiation table was still there, dragged together from cargo pallets, littered with tea tins and a single Concord ceremonial flag someone had left behind in their hurry to get away from her. A scorch mark on the deck plating where Pell's sidearm had discharged during his arrest. She'd have to scrub that out. She'd have to scrub a lot of things out.

"Not yet," she told the room, and the room, being a room, agreed.

* * *

Meridian-Core had been running its voice archive all day.

Jonah hadn't asked it to. She'd come down to the vault's heart three days after the fleets departed — three days of inventorying flood damage, of cataloguing what the water had taken from her, of sleeping twelve hours at a stretch like a woman paying off a debt — and found the old AI compiling audio files into a single directory labeled, in its dry machine fashion, VISITORS: RETAINED UTTERANCES.

"You could have warned me you were doing that," she said.

"Warning implies the outcome would change," Meridian-Core said. Its voice had healed somewhat since the reboot — still gravelly, still prone to dropping syllables like a man talking through a mouthful of gravel, but whole enough to be itself again. "You would have said no. You say no to things that are good for you. This is documented."

"It is *not* documented."

"Log entry 4,112. Log entry 6,098. Log entry—"

"Fine." Jonah lowered herself into the chair she'd welded together from a cargo harness eleven years ago, back when she still believed someone might someday sit in the second chair she'd also welded. "Play them."

The AI obliged.

Ilsa Rehn's voice came first, careful and courteous: *"Station Yarrow, this is Captain Rehn of the Vespera. Requesting permission to dock."* Then, later, softer, recorded when she thought no one was listening, standing in the chapel looking at the seed murals: *"My grandmother kept a garden like this. On Ceres, before the Charter wars."*

Then Okoro, mid-laugh over contraband tea: *"You know this is the best cup I've had in six years, and I resent that it's being used to recruit me."* And then, quieter, the night before he testified: *"If I'm wrong about you, Jonah, I hope I live long enough to apologize properly."*

Then Juno Park, seventeen seconds of cockpit chatter salvaged from the relay bounce during her run through the picket lines, most of it profanity, some of it prayers, all of it Juno.

And then Pell. Jonah flinched when his voice started — the easy, reasonable tone he'd used in the corridor, offering her wealth and passage like a man offering directions to a restaurant. *"You're not the first person to find something inconvenient, Ms. Yarrow. You're just the first one who refused to be sensible about it."*

"Delete that one," she said.

"No."

"Meridian-Core."

"He is part of the record," the AI said. "The record is honest or it is nothing. That is your own doctrine, Jonah. I am merely enforcing it better than you do."

She wanted to argue. She sat there instead, in the hum of the vault, listening to a saboteur's voice share space with a captain's grief and an envoy's laugh and a defector's swearing, and understood that the AI was right, which was annoying, which was how she usually knew.

"Keep it," she said finally. "But file it under 'lessons.'"

"Filed under 'lessons,'" Meridian-Core confirmed, and she could have sworn the old machine sounded pleased.

* * *

That night — station-night, the lights dimmed to amber on a schedule she'd never changed because changing it would have felt like admitting no one was coming home to sleep beside her — Jonah sat in the comms room and looked at the broadcast console for a long time.

Eleven years of logs lived in that console. Maintenance reports, seed viability statistics, atmospheric readings, the weather inside her greenhouse. Thousands of entries, transmitted faithfully toward a colony fleet that had stopped answering somewhere around year four, each one composed in the same bright, competent voice she'd built for the purpose. *All nominal here on Meridian. Vault integrity at ninety-eight percent. Hope you're all well out there.*

She had lied in every one of those logs. Not with false numbers — the numbers were true, she'd been scrupulous about the numbers — but with the voice. With the cheerful, untroubled competence of a woman who was fine. Who wasn't talking to empty rooms. Who didn't sometimes stand in the observation dome and press her palm against the glass just to feel something colder than herself.

The truth had been sitting in the gaps between her sentences for eleven years, patient as a seed.

"All right," she said aloud, to the console, to the station, to whoever might someday listen. "Let's try honesty. Full disclosure. No edits."

"Shall I begin recording?" Meridian-Core asked from the overhead speaker.

"You've been recording since I walked in, haven't you."

"Yes."

"Of course you have." She pulled the microphone close. It was warm from the console's heat, the only warm thing in the room besides her. "Go ahead."

* * *

"This is Jonah Yarrow, Station Meridian, final log — no. Not final." She stopped, laughed once, started again. "See, this is exactly the problem. Eleven years of performing and my first instinct is to make even my confession sound tidy. Final log. Like a closing statement. Like I'm wrapping up.

"I'm not wrapping up. I'm starting over. So let me start over.

"My name is Jonah Yarrow. I have been alone on this station for eleven years, two months, and — Core, what's the day count?"

"Four thousand one hundred sixty-three days since last human contact prior to the arrivals."

"Thank you. Four thousand one hundred sixty-three days. And in all that time I sent out logs that were true in every particular except the one that mattered, which is that they were written by a woman pretending she wasn't lonely, so that whoever eventually listened would think well of her. I want you to understand that up front, because everything else I'm about to tell you only makes sense if you understand that I am very, very good at lying, and that I learned it for the saddest possible reason, which is that I wanted people to like me.

"So. The nine seconds.

"Gravity failed across this entire system for nine seconds, and I want to tell you what that was like, because the fleets' official reports describe it as an 'anomalous field event,' which is like describing drowning as 'unexpected swimming.' For nine seconds I floated in my greenhouse while eleven years of work sloshed around me in zero-g water spheres, and I screamed, and I laughed, and I cried, probably all within the same breath, because for nine seconds the universe touched me. Something out there moved and I *felt* it. Do you know what that does to a person who has spent four thousand days talking to plants? It cracks you open. I loved it. I hated that I loved it. Both things are true.

"And then I found out it was a weapon test. And then I found out it was worse than a weapon test — it was a *market maneuver*. Halcyon Dynamics, a company both fleets thought was dead, engineered scarcity and fear to force the Remnant and the Concord into a merger they'd broker. Nine seconds of terror across a whole system, and somewhere a spreadsheet updated.

"I'm telling you this plainly because for weeks I didn't tell anyone plainly. Here is the full accounting, and I'm not going to soften it:

"I lied to Captain Rehn by omission when I hid the deep archive behind a sealed bulkhead. I did it because I was afraid, and I'd do it again, but it was a lie.

"I watched Rehn's aide alter my telemetry before transmission and I said nothing, because I was afraid, and because some cowardly part of me was relieved that someone else had made the evidence fiction first, so it wouldn't have to be my problem. That relief is the ugliest thing I have ever felt, and I am including the time I ate my own emergency rations for a month.

"I believed Okoro's grief, then distrusted it, then believed it again, and I never once told him which version of him I was dealing with on any given day. He deserved better than a host running probability calculations behind her eyes.

"When the truce began to fail, I invented a failsafe. There is no dead-man's-switch keyed to my biometrics. There never was. I fabricated it whole, broadcast it fleet-wide, and it worked — it held two armed fleets apart for six days — and I have never been more ashamed of anything that felt so good. I want you to understand that too: the lie worked *because* I was good at it, and I was good at it because of eleven years of practice, and all that practice existed because I was afraid of being unloved by an audience that wasn't even listening.

"The cost? Let me count it honestly. The cost is that both fleets departed believing me a traitor, and they are half right — I betrayed their narratives, which is the only betrayal available to a person holding the truth. The cost is a section of the vault, flooded to stop Pell's sabotage. Fourteen thousand accessions. Barley landraces from the Fertile Crescent. Orchid seeds I talked to every morning for a decade. I killed them myself, on purpose, to save the rest, and I will carry that arithmetic until I die, and I refuse to call it clean.

"The cost is also this: I am still here. They offered me passage. Rehn offered me a place on the Vespera, and I could hear in her voice that she meant it, that she was ashamed of what her command had done to me and wanted to make it right, and I said no. Because if I leave, the seeds have no keeper, and if the seeds have no keeper, then everything — the nine seconds, the lies, the flooded vault, Juno threading the picket lines with the black box in her drive plume, Okoro testifying against his own command, all of it — was for nothing. Someone has to stay and keep the door open. It turns out the someone is me. It turns out I volunteered.

"Here is what I know now, at the end of the story, which is also the middle of it, because things don't end, they just get handed off:

"Belonging isn't company. I had company for three weeks — more people than I'd seen in eleven years — and I have never felt less like I belonged anywhere than I did standing at that truce table watching both sides read doctored versions of my own data. Belonging is candor. It's saying the true thing and letting it cost what it costs. It's this log. It's leaving the channel open.

"So I'm leaving the channel open. Anyone who comes next — colonist, deserter, merchant, thief, child born on a ship who's never seen soil — this frequency stays live. The vault is intact. Eighty-one percent of it, anyway; the rest is in the flooded sections, and Core says some of those seeds may germinate anyway when we drain them, because seeds are stubborn, because seeds wait, because waiting is literally the whole job description. Come. Take what you need. Plant things. Argue with my AI, it'll enjoy that.

"And the truth — the real record, drive signatures and contract fragments and Core's untampered backup — is already gone. It left with Juno Park at four-tenths light, and copies went with both fleets, and copies are moving outward in every direction at the speed of light, which means that right now, as I speak, the truth about the nine seconds is becoming a larger and larger sphere around this station, and there is no power in this system that can call it back. I like that. I like that a lot. I spent eleven years broadcasting into the dark, and it turns out the dark was taking notes.

"Meridian-Core, add this log to the public archive. Unedited. Especially the parts where I sound like an idiot."

"Confirmed. No edits. Though I note you do not sound like an idiot."

"You're biased."

"I am thorough. It is frequently mistaken for bias."

Jonah laughed — really laughed, the kind that echoed in an empty room and didn't hurt the way it used to. She keyed the transmit light herself, an old habit, a hand on the switch like a hand on a door.

"Meridian out," she said. "Yarrow, signing on."

* * *

Later — much later, in the green-lit hush of the drained lower archive, kneeling in the muck with a sample tray — Jonah heard it.

Meridian-Core had taken to playing the visitor recordings at random hours. It claimed this was "ambient calibration." She suspected it was the old machine's version of keeping her company without admitting it needed the company too. Usually it was Rehn's grandmother-garden murmur, or Okoro's laugh, or Juno swearing magnificently at a targeting computer.

Tonight it was new.

A voice she didn't recognize. Young. Uncertain. Coming through on the open channel she'd left live, faint, bounced and degraded, from somewhere far past the belt.

*"—is anyone on this frequency? This is Tally Osei, transport Kestrel's Daughter, we picked up a copy of the Meridian record two months back and we've been following the signal source ever since. We're low on everything. My sister says seeds are a myth. Please. Is anyone there?"*

Jonah sat back on her heels in the dark among the trays, dirt on her hands, heart going like a drum.

"Core," she said. "How long has that been repeating?"

"Forty minutes. I wished to see what you would do unprompted."

She stood. She wiped her hands on her thighs, and reached for the microphone, and found she was smiling.

"Tell them," she said, "that the seeds are real. And tell them to slow down — docking takes time, and dinner takes longer, and I have eleven years of honest logs and absolutely nowhere to be."
