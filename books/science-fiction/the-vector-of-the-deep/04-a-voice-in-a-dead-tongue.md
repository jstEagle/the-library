## 4. A Voice in a Dead Tongue

The beacon woke me at 0347 station time, which is the hour the universe reserves for bad news.

I'd fallen asleep in the maintenance cradle again — a habit Tam calls "romantic" and Ilsa calls "a fire hazard." The cradle is a padded alcove behind the beacon's primary relay housing, just big enough for a person and her regrets, and it hums at a frequency that took me three months to stop finding ominous. That night the hum changed. It climbed half an octave and developed a stutter, like a singer forgetting lyrics mid-verse.

I was upright before my brain finished booting, one hand already on the diagnostic slate.

"Talk to me, old man," I said. The beacon doesn't answer, obviously. But after six years of patching its failing core with scavenged parts that no manufacturer has made since before my grandmother was born, I've earned the right to talk to it like a stubborn relative. You develop relationships with machines you keep alive past their warranty. The machine develops opinions.

The slate spat back a cascade of telemetry that made my stomach drop through the deck plating. Carrier wave detected. Source: deep vector, bearing 221 mark 14, beyond the shipping lanes, beyond anything with a reason to exist out there. Signal strength rising. Modulation pattern: *linguistic*.

Not noise. Not a pulsar hiccup, not a ghost echo off the ring's own traffic, not any of the dozen mundane explanations I ran through twice while my hands shook. Someone — or something — was talking.

And the beacon, my beacon, the derelict nobody ordered, was listening like it had been waiting two hundred years for exactly this call.

Because, I would learn, it had.

* * *

I should explain what I mean when I say the beacon is mine, because ownership on Meridian Station is a legal fiction and possession is a smuggling term of art.

Officially, Relay Node Kestrel-9 is decommissioned. Unlisted. A line item in a maintenance ledger so old the ledger itself is probably contraband by now. It hangs in a high, cold orbit where the ring's shadow falls long, and station traffic gives it a wide berth the way people give a wide berth to a house where someone died. Nobody ordered it built — or rather, whoever ordered it built arranged for the order to be forgotten, which is a trick I've since learned costs more than the construction.

Unofficially, I've spent six years keeping it breathing. Salvaged capacitors from a scrapped tug. Rewound its cooling coils with wire Tam swore he wouldn't ask about and never did. Named its last surviving maintenance drone Ptolemy, because it circles the core endlessly and corrects nothing, which struck me as honest. Ptolemy is dented, single-minded, and loyal in the way of things that have no other option, and I love him without embarrassment.

I found the scrubbed commissioning records in my second year. There should have been a plaque, a charter, a name. There was a hole in the archive shaped like intent. Somebody had gone to real trouble to make Kestrel-9 unremembered, and I told myself then that it was probably boring trouble — budget fraud, a dead contractor's liability, the ordinary sediment of bureaucracy. I told myself that because the alternative was interesting, and interesting things near Meridian have a way of getting people erased.

So I kept the beacon alive the way you keep a stray alive: quietly, off the books, telling yourself it's for the animal's sake and not your own.

At 0351, the stray started singing.

* * *

I recorded everything. First rule of salvage: capture before you touch. Second rule, learned from Sef over cards and cheap tea: *when something impossible happens, write down the exact shape of the impossibility, because later you'll want to disbelieve it and you'll need evidence against yourself.*

The signal wasn't a distress ping in any modern protocol. Modern distress is standardized to death — three tones, position packet, automated handshake. This was *speech*. Compressed, degraded, chopped into fragments by distance and decay, but structured the way language is structured, with pauses that meant something and repetitions that meant more. A voice, or something doing a very committed impression of one.

I sat in the cold dark of Kestrel-9's observation blister with my breath fogging the glass and listened to a dead tongue say the same broken thing over and over, and I felt the specific vertigo you feel when a story you tell yourself as comfort turns out to be load-bearing.

Here's the thing about myths on a station like Meridian: everyone has one. Ours was the Halcyon. Every smuggler knows the story — the generation ship that left during the Collapse carrying ten thousand sleepers and never arrived anywhere, the cryoship that became a campfire tale, a hazing ritual for green crew (*sure, kid, and if you fly the dark vector at 221 mark 14 you'll hear her singing*). I'd heard the story a hundred times. I'd told it twice. I believed in it the way you believe in ghosts: fully, and only when it was useful to be afraid.

The signal said coordinates.

Not all of them. Fragments. But fragments of coordinates are worse than none, because they imply the rest exists somewhere, waiting, and implication is the sharpest tool the universe owns.

I did what any competent professional does with an impossible thing at four in the morning. I copied it, encrypted it, hid the copy in three places, and took it to my family.

* * *

The Vesper was docked at Berth 12, running quiet between jobs, which meant Tam was elbow-deep in the port drive housing and swearing at it in a dialect only he and certain elderly engines understand. I came up the gangway with the data slate held against my chest like a wounded bird.

Sef was in the galley. Sef is always in the galley; it's where the light is good for reading. He's our archivist, which on a smuggler means he authenticates the memories we carry, prices them, and occasionally explains why the family heirloom some client paid us to preserve is actually a forgery wrapped around a genuine crime. He taught himself dead languages the way other men collect knives, and he taught me a few as a game — *here, Wendell, sound out this glyph; it's a prayer, or a recipe, or a threat, depending on the vowel.*

He looked up when I came in, and whatever my face was doing made him set down his book.

"Show me," he said, before I said anything. That's Sef. He reads faces the way he reads manuscripts.

I put the slate down and played him the raw signal.

I watched his hands first. People's faces lie; hands rarely do. His hands went still over his teacup, then began to move — small motions, tracing shapes in the air, following the cadence of the transmission like a man catching a rhythm he hasn't heard since childhood. His lips moved. Silently at first, then not.

"That's not possible," he said, in a tone I'd never heard him use, which frightened me more than the signal had. Sef's whole personality is the management of surprise. "Play it again."

I played it again. On the third pass he was whispering along, translating in real time, badly, the way you stumble through a poem you knew once in another life.

"It's Pre-Collapse Standard," he said. "The old common language. Before the Fragmentation split it into forty successor tongues and killed the root. Nobody has spoken it natively in—" He stopped. Recalculated. "Two centuries. Maybe longer. Everything we have is reconstructions, academic exercises, texts. This isn't a reconstruction. Listen to the stress patterns. Nobody teaching themselves from archives gets the stress patterns wrong like that. This is a *native speaker*, Wendell. Or a machine built by native speakers, which amounts to the same thing."

"What's it saying?"

He was quiet for a long moment. The galley hummed. Somewhere below us, Tam hit something with a wrench and apologized to it.

"Distress," Sef said finally. "It's a distress call. Automated, looping, degrading — the repetition intervals are lengthening, which means the transmitter's losing power. It identifies the source vessel." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet, which scared me worse than anything else that night. "It says its name is *Halcyon*. It says it woke early. It says—" his voice caught, steadied, the archivist reasserting control over the man— "it says the sleepers are dying. Life support failure across multiple bays. It's been calling for help."

"For how long?"

"The signal decay profile suggests..." He worked it silently. "Years, Wendell. It's been calling for years. We're hearing it now because your beacon finally got strong enough to listen."

My beacon. Built to listen. Commissioned by someone who wanted the order forgotten, hung in an orbit pointed at nothing, maintained by a smuggler who couldn't explain why she couldn't let it die.

I thought about the scrubbed records. I thought about the hole in the archive shaped like intent. And a cold thought walked into the room and sat down beside me without asking: *the beacon wasn't abandoned. It was hidden. There's a difference, and the difference is a somebody.*

"Sef," I said. "If the Halcyon is real — if there are really ten thousand people asleep on that ship — then someone built a listening post to catch exactly this call. And then someone else buried the listening post so thoroughly that even its own builders' names are gone. Both of those things can't be accidents."

"No," Sef agreed softly. "They can't."

"So either the burying happened after the building, because somebody didn't want the call answered." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the voice I use with customs inspectors. "Or the burying happened first, and the beacon was always meant to hear this and do nothing."

Tam chose that moment to come up the ladder, wiping grease on a rag, complaining about a fuel coupling with the passion of a man whose problems are still soluble. He stopped when he saw our faces.

"Okay," he said. "What did you two find, and how much trouble is it?"

I handed him the slate. He listened to thirty seconds of a dead language begging, and I watched the complaint drain out of his posture, replaced by something older and heavier. Tam's family came out of the Exclusion Zones. He has opinions about who history keeps and who it throws away, though he mostly expresses them by fixing things.

"That's not a myth," he said.

"No," I said.

"That's a *ship*."

"Yes."

He sat down heavily across from Sef. Neither of them looked at me, and both of them were thinking the same thing I was, because we're a crew and crews think in parallel: *coordinates mean a job. Coordinates mean Ilsa. Coordinates mean the last big job she's been hinting at for a month, the one that's supposed to buy us all out.*

And coordinates mean that somewhere out in the dark, ten thousand people have been dying slowly for years, within reach of a station that built a secret ear to hear them scream and then gagged the ear.

* * *

I went back to Kestrel-9 alone that evening. I needed to, the way you need to revisit a wound to confirm it's real.

Ptolemy circled the core, faithful and useless. I pulled the full signal log, cleaned it up, and listened to the whole loop end to end — all eleven minutes of it, every fragment, every lengthening silence between repetitions. Near the end of the message, buried under degradation, there was a section Sef hadn't gotten to yet, and I played it maybe twenty times trying to hear what it wanted.

Then I sent it to him with one line attached: *tell me I'm wrong.*

He called back in nine minutes. I could hear him breathing, fast, the way he breathes when he's found something in an archive that someone paid good money to lose.

"The fragmentary section," he said. "It's a manifest summary. Passenger counts. Two populations, Wendell. Two separate cryo-banks. The first block — Charter Wing, it's labeled — corporate colonists, contract holders, the founding investment. The second block—" his voice did something complicated— "the second block is labeled *Exile Wing*. Blacklisted refugees. People the Collapse governments stripped of standing and shipped out because dead in transit is cheaper than alive on the dole. Ten thousand souls total, give or take. And the ship's AI is asking — it's *begging* — for anyone receiving this to log the full manifest. To witness it. Because it believes, Wendell, it has been built to believe, that someone will try to make the Exile Wing never have existed."

I stood in the cold blister with the stars wheeling slow past the glass and felt awe curdle into dread in real time, a physical sensation, milk turning in the skull.

A myth with coordinates was a wonder. A myth with a manifest, a cover-up, and a hidden listener was a bomb with my fingerprints already on the casing.

Because here's what I knew, lying awake in the maintenance cradle afterward while Ptolemy hummed his idiot circle around the core: I hadn't found the beacon. Six years ago, the beacon had been *left where I would find it* — or left where anyone might, a landmine of truth waiting for whichever fool loved abandoned things enough to keep them alive. And whoever buried it was still out there, still powerful enough to erase commissioning records, still watching, perhaps, the same signal I was watching.

The Halcyon hung at 221 mark 14, dying loudly into a darkness it had been promised was empty.

And tomorrow morning I would have to decide whether to tell Ilsa Rook that the last big job of her life had just drifted into our reach wearing ten thousand heartbeats and a two-hundred-year-old lie.

The beacon stuttered. The loop repeated. Somewhere out there, something patient was still asking if anyone remembered.

I pressed my palm flat against the warm casing of the core, the way I do when I'm asking it to hold on a little longer, and for the first time in six years I wondered which of us had been kept, and which of us had been kept *for*.
