## 19. Both Wings Open

The assembly chamber had been a grain exchange once, back when Meridian Station pretended it grew things. Now the vaulted ceiling held dead chandeliers and the floor held four hundred folding chairs, and every one of them was occupied by people who, three weeks ago, would have sworn on their mothers' graves that the Halcyon was a ghost story.

I sat in the third row with my hands folded like a schoolgirl waiting to be scolded, which was more or less what I was. My name was on the agenda twice: once as witness, once as — I checked the printed docket again because I still didn't believe it — *petitioner for custodial appointment, Deep Relay Beacon 7.*

Somebody had finally given the beacon a number. It only took a mass psychic trauma and the collapse of a two-hundred-year oligarchy.

"State your name for the record," said the chair pro tem, a rail-thin woman named Okonkwo who'd been elected by acclamation at three in the morning when nobody better could be found. She had the eyes of someone who'd slept maybe six hours total since the broadcast, which put her ahead of most of us.

"Wendell Fairweather."

"And you confirm that the memory archive transmitted through Relay Beacon 7 was authentic and unaltered?"

"I compressed it. Sef did the compression, actually. But we changed nothing." I paused. "You can't alter a dying ship's logs without them noticing. The Halcyon has been keeping books for two centuries. It's very good at it."

A ripple of something moved through the chamber — not quite laughter, not quite grief. People here were still learning what to do with their faces. Ten thousand memories will do that. Everyone in this room had woken up three weeks ago holding a stranger's last message home in their head, or a child's face from a cryo-bay they'd never known existed, or — in the case of perhaps sixty families present — a signature. Their great-grandmother's signature, or grandfather's, on an order titled *Population Management Directive: Exile Wing, Post-Wake Disposition.*

*Disposition.* The word had done more damage than any of the founders' other crimes. You don't need to kill ten thousand people if you can just file them out of existence.

Okonkwo walked me through the rest of it. The cordon. The strike team. Tam's burns, which drew a low sound from somewhere behind me — Tam himself, bandaged and bored, sitting where he could see the exits, because some habits survive even vindication. Doss testified after me, flat-voiced, naming his own superiors with the enthusiasm of a man defusing bombs. Then came the hard part, the part everyone had come for.

The vote.

Not on guilt — guilt had been settled by arithmetic and signatures. On power. Station reactors ran at capacity; waking the Halcyon's cryo-systems meant rerouting enough energy to dim whole residential rings for months. Someone's lights were going to flicker. Someone's hydroponics yield was going to drop. The question before the assembly was whether ten thousand sleeping strangers were worth cold showers and brownouts until spring.

I'd prepared a speech. Sef helped me write it, all about shared humanity and historical debt. I never gave it. Instead Okonkwo asked if I had anything to add before the floor voted, and I stood up and said:

"The Exile Wing has been awake longer than you think. Not the bodies. The *waiting*. Two hundred years of it, logged and timestamped. If you vote no, you're not choosing comfort over rescue. You're choosing to be the last entry in their log." I sat down.

It wasn't eloquent. But the tally board went up forty minutes later and the numbers weren't close. Both wings. Rationed wake cycles, staggered over eight months, medical oversight, the works. When the result posted, nobody cheered. There was just a long exhale across four hundred people, like a station remembering how to breathe.

* * *

The Vesper led the first boarding party because there was no argument about it. Ilsa Rook put herself forward and nobody — not the assembly, not the press, not the founding families' lawyers circling like well-dressed vultures — could find grounds to object. She knew the approach vector. Her crew knew the hull. And, though the official record doesn't say this part, she wanted it. Needed it, maybe, the way a debtor needs to see the ledger.

We docked at Charter Wing Gate One on a Tuesday. The corridor beyond the airlock smelled of ozone and old frost, and the lights came up in slow amber waves as the ship's caretaker AI — hoarse now, running on fumes and stubbornness — recognized authorized personnel for the first time in its existence.

"Welcome," it said, and its voice cracked in the middle of the word, and Tam, who does not cry, had to go inspect a bulkhead for a while.

Sef walked the Charter Wing corridors reading the stenciled names aloud, translating the old Pre-Collapse Standard designations into living speech. Room 4-C: *Osei, Adaeze, age 61, charter agronomist.* Room 4-D: two brothers, ages nine and eleven. Sef read every plaque like a litany, and I understood then that this was a language lesson too — the last one, the reverse of all those games aboard the Vesper. Teaching the dead tongue back to the living.

The bulkhead between wings was still sealed. Of course it was. We opened it together, the whole first boarding party, Charter Wing volunteers and Vesper crew and two assembly observers who kept trying to take notes and kept failing because their hands shook. The door took eleven minutes to cycle. Nobody spoke. When it finally parted, the air that came through was colder and older and smelled faintly of disinfectant and time, and on the far side, in frost-silvered bays stacked like a library of the saved, lay the Exile Wing.

More of them than the Charter Wing. Nearly double. Families, mostly. The manifests Sef recovered listed trades instead of titles: welders, teachers, hydroponicists, a whole wing of people who'd been blacklisted for the crime of being poor and inconvenient and alive.

Tam put his palm flat against one of the frosted viewports, over a face none of us would meet for months yet. "Eight-month queue," he said. "They waited two hundred years. What's eight months?"

"An eternity," said Sef quietly. "But a counted one. That's different."

* * *

Ilsa surrendered on a Thursday.

She didn't announce it. She came down to the beacon — my beacon, officially mine now, though the paperwork wouldn't clear for another week — carrying two cups of coffee and wearing her dress uniform, the one from her security days, which I'd seen exactly once and never asked about. She set the coffee on my console next to the maintenance drone, who chirped at her with what I swear was suspicion.

"You're turning yourself in," I said. Not a question. The uniform answered it.

"The assembly's convening a tribunal. Closed session, but Doss told me the shape of it. Complicity in suppression of inquiry, obstruction, falsification of incident reports." She shrugged, the way she shrugs when a job goes sideways and there's nothing left but the walking-away. "All true. Every line of it."

"You could run. Half the outer ring would hide you. Tam would steal a shuttle."

"I know." She looked at the viewport, where the Halcyon hung in the distance, floodlit now, work pods swarming its flanks like bright lice doing holy work. "Wendell, I spent nineteen years making sure you three never had to look over your shoulders. That was real. I need you to hear that it was real. And I spent those same nineteen years making sure nobody looked *there*." She nodded at the ship. "Both things are true. That's the whole problem with me. Both things are true."

I thought about what to say, and what I thought was: *you smuggled memories for a living, and so did I, and the difference is yours were heavier.* What I said was: "The tribunal should hear about the boarding parties. About the burns Tam took getting the transmitter evidence out. About you leading every single shift since."

"They will. That's the point. Let them weigh it — all of it, both pans of the scale. If I flee, I get to keep the version of myself that rescued the sleepers, and lose the version that buried them. That's not justice, that's editing." She smiled, tired. "You of all people know what editing costs."

She finished her coffee, saluted the drone — who, traitor, saluted back — and walked out of my beacon toward the transit cars and the tribunal chamber and whatever the assembly decided she deserved.

* * *

The tribunal took nine days. I attended when my schedule allowed, which was often, because the beacon mostly listens and doesn't need me hovering. The prosecution was thorough and joyless. They played the old incident reports in her voice, young Ilsa Rook, twenty-six years old and already lying fluently for people who paid her in silence. They showed the inquiry she'd helped bury — a customs auditor named Pell who'd gotten too curious in the years before I was born, transferred, discredited, gone.

Then the defense did something I didn't expect. They didn't counter. They called Tam, who described the salvage contract and how Ilsa had planned, right up until Wendell's confession, to destroy the last proof of her own complicity — and then hadn't. They called Sef, who submitted the full linguistic archive of the Exile Wing's final messages, and noted, dry as dust, that preservation of evidence had been Ilsa's decision in the end, however accidental. They called Doss, who said four words: "She came back voluntarily."

And then they called the sleepers. Or rather, they called the first of them — because the rationing schedule had begun, and Bay One of the Exile Wing had come online eleven days into the tribunal, and the first woman to open her eyes in two hundred years had asked, in halting resurrected Standard, to address the court.

Her name was Maren Vashti. She was a structural engineer. She was ninety-one years old biologically and thirty-one in every way that mattered, and she stood in the tribunal chamber wrapped in a thermal blanket and looked at Ilsa Rook for a long moment.

"I don't know you," she said, through Sef's interpretation, though Ilsa understood more of it than she let on. "I know what you did. It was read to me. I have been asleep while my grandchildren's grandchildren were erased from the record, and this woman helped hold the lid on that." A murmur went through the chamber. "And I also know that when the doors opened, she was standing on the other side of them. Holding them open." Maren turned to the panel. "I am not asking you to forgive her. Forgiveness is mine to give and I haven't decided yet. I'm asking you to be precise. Punish what she buried. Credit what she dug up. Don't confuse the two, and don't pretend either cancels the other."

The panel deliberated four days. The verdict, when it came, was neither acquittal nor mercy: five years' station service, restricted, supervised — most of it assigned, in a piece of judicial poetry nobody planned, to the Halcyon restoration crews. Ilsa Rook, who had once helped erase a ship, would spend half a decade helping rebuild one. No absolution. Just work. Just continuation.

When they read it, she stood, thanked the panel in a steady voice, and caught my eye across the chamber. She didn't smile. She nodded once, the nod of a captain acknowledging a heading, and I nodded back, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

* * *

That night I went back to the beacon. The drone met me at the airlock, aggrieved at my absence, and I let it lecture me while I made tea. Through the viewport the Halcyon glowed bay by bay, a constellation assembling itself out of an old dark. Charter Wing, Exile Wing — the bulkhead between them had been cut out entirely last week, hauled away as scrap, and nobody had suggested keeping a piece of it for a museum. Some doors you don't preserve.

Sef pinged me on the local channel. "Bay Four wakes tomorrow. The Osei brothers. Nine and eleven." A pause. "I've been practicing the greeting. Want to hear it?"

"Read it to me."

Sef read it — the old words, the ones that had crossed two centuries of silence to reach a smuggler's patched-together beacon, now spoken slowly and carefully for children who would hear them as their first living sentence. I listened with my tea going cold and my throat doing something embarrassing.

"Tomorrow, then," Sef said.

"Tomorrow," I agreed, and cut the channel, and turned back to the console —

— where a light I had never seen before was blinking.

Low priority. Faint. From deep space, past the Halcyon's drift corridor, past everything. A signal, thin as a thread, repeating patiently in a language dead for two hundred years.

I sat down slowly. The drone chirped, questioning.

"Hold my tea," I told it, and opened a channel.
