## 6. The Message in the Hold

**TAMSIN**

The hold's seal was older than I was.

That was the first thing that bothered me. The Meridian had been gone eleven years; the manifest listed Hold C as sealed at departure from Titan Yards, and the company seal on the hatch matched the date to the hour. But seals don't survive eleven years of deep space without a single pressure cycle. Metal remembers. This metal remembered nothing.

"Someone's been in here," Reyes said behind me. Not a question. She'd read the same story in the weld lines.

"Or no one has," I said, "and the ship wants us to think someone has."

She didn't laugh. She hadn't laughed once in three interviews, and I was starting to understand that the absence wasn't hostility. It was rationing. Ilsa Reyes spent her expressions like a woman spending her last credits.

The hatch code was in the maintenance log — the real one, not the sanitized copy the syndicate had sent me. I'd pulled it from the dockmaster's archive at 0300, using credentials that belonged to a man named Okafor who had died in a decompression accident two years before I bought his name. Somewhere in Ceres there was probably another investigator doing the same thing with my dead identity, and the thought had stopped keeping me up at night around year two.

The lock cycled. Air hissed out — eleven years old, stale and sweet with the chemical tang of long-sealed polymer. My suit lamp cut through the dark and found cargo containers racked floor to ceiling, strapped down for a voyage nobody had planned to end like this.

"Manifest says agricultural seed stock," Reyes said. "Nine hundred tons."

I walked the aisle. The containers were right. The stenciling was right. The serial numbers were right.

That was the third thing that bothered me.

"Everything in this hold is exactly what it's supposed to be," I said. "Which is wrong."

Reyes came up beside me, her lamp sweeping the racks. "Explain."

"When a ship comes home missing, something's always off. Water stores short. A container crushed. Cargo shifted by micrometeorite strikes or a crew scavenging parts. Eleven years out, nine hundred tons of seed stock should look like a war zone." I put my palm flat against the nearest container. Cold, but not deep-cold. It had been warm recently, or near something warm. "This looks like it was loaded yesterday. By people who wanted it to look loaded yesterday."

"You're saying the hold is a set."

"I'm saying somebody dressed a stage." I turned a slow circle, letting the lamp find the corners where stage-dressers always get lazy. "And stages have doors."

There. Behind the aftmost rack, where the container stencils repeated — the same serial number twice, forty meters apart. Nobody catches that unless they're paid to be bored, and being paid to be bored is most of my job.

"Help me with this," I said.

We hauled the duplicate container aside together. Reyes was stronger than she looked, which fit everything else about her. Behind it, flush against the hull plating, was a panel that shouldn't exist — no rivets, no seams, just a matte rectangle the size of a coffin lid, and etched into its surface in a font I didn't recognize:

**FOR WHOEVER IS LEFT**

Reyes went very still beside me. Stillness, from her, was louder than screaming.

"You knew," I said quietly.

"No." Her voice came out scraped. "But I was afraid of this."

"Afraid of what, specifically?"

She looked at the words a long time. "Of finding out we were right to erase everything else."

* * *

**REYES**

The panel opened at Tamsin's touch, which meant it had been waiting for anyone, which meant it wasn't for us. That was the part that made my hands shake inside the gloves. Things built for everyone are built by people who've given up on controlling who listens.

Inside, cradled in shock foam, sat a data core the size of a human heart.

I knew what it was before the lamp caught the casing. Every officer knows quantum-locked storage when they see it — the faint oil-slick shimmer of the containment lattice, the way light seems to hesitate going into it instead of reflecting off. You don't buy those. You don't build them on a freighter. They come out of Combine research facilities with chain-of-custody paperwork thicker than the core itself.

"This isn't ours," I said.

"It predates your departure," Tamsin said. He'd already found the manufacture stamp and was photographing it with the methodical patience of a man cataloguing his own autopsy. "Titan Yards, fourteen months before you shipped out. Someone loaded this into your hold before you ever left port, sealed it behind a false bulkhead, and let you carry it blind for eleven years."

"Then why is the seal intact if—"

"Because whoever opened it put it back better than they found it." He glanced at me. "Your crew?"

"Some of them. Maybe." I couldn't say more. Not yet. Not standing over the thing we'd voted on in a mess hall that now existed thirty years in someone else's past. "Read the header."

He did. His lamp steadied on the metadata block, and I watched a careful man's face do something uncareful.

"Timestamp," he said.

"What about it?"

"It's dated sixty-one years from now."

The hold was silent except for the tick of cooling metal. Sixty-one years. I did the arithmetic the way you can't stop doing it: the Meridian left when I was twenty-six in service years. I came back physically twenty-six, biologically younger than my file, carrying eleven years I couldn't account for and a crew that had aged backward through whatever happened to us out there. And now a message stamped from six decades down a timeline that hasn't been lived yet.

"That's impossible," I said, because someone had to say it and Tamsin was busy looking like a man reading his own death certificate.

"So was your face when you docked," he said. "Impossible's been making deliveries all week. I'm starting to feel underdressed for it."

He reached for the core.

"Don't." My hand closed on his wrist before I decided to move it. Through both suits I could feel his pulse — fast, controlled, the pulse of a man who'd trained himself to be calm the way other people train to run. "You don't know what it does when it opens. Quantum-locked means it chooses its reader. It might choose you. It might choose no one. And whatever's inside, whoever wrote it spent sixty-one years of somebody's future making sure it got here."

Tamsin looked at my hand on his arm. Then at me.

"You've read it before," he said. "Haven't you. Or your crew did. That's what you're not telling me."

I let go of him. "I'm telling you what I'm authorized to tell you."

"You're not authorized to tell me anything. Neither am I. That's the only honest thing left in this case." He lifted the core out of the foam with both hands, gentle as a man lifting a sleeping child he intended to give away. "It's addressed to whoever is left, Officer Reyes. As far as I can tell, that's you and me."

* * *

**TAMSIN**

We didn't open it in the hold. Some instinct older than investigation said: not in the box where they put it. So we carried it up through the Meridian's spine, past crew quarters with personal effects still hanging in closets — a jacket, a child's drawing taped inside a locker door, eleven years of dust on things that should have been touched goodbye — and into the wardroom, because the wardroom had a table and a table felt like neutral ground.

Reyes sealed the door. Then she stood with her back against it, arms crossed, watching me set the core down between us like a referee watching a coin toss she'd already seen the result of.

"If you open that," she said, "there's no closing it."

"There never was. The moment I saw FOR WHOEVER IS LEFT, the file stopped being an insurance claim." I ran my hand over the lattice. Under my palm it hummed, faintly, like a throat deciding whether to speak. "You could still walk out. Report that I broke protocol. Let the navy take it."

"And tell them what? That I stood in the room and let you?" Something moved across her face — not quite pain, more like the memory of pain filed somewhere accessible. "I've spent three weeks trying to make this ship boring, Delacroix. Boring ships get released. Boring crews go home. If that thing is what I think it is, boring died in Hold C."

"Then we agree."

"We agree it's too late. That's different." She pulled out a chair but didn't sit. "Open it."

I pressed the acceptance stud.

The lattice bloomed.

Not light, exactly — the shimmer resolved inward, folding itself into a column of ordered probability above the core, and text began to assemble in the air the way frost assembles on glass: not written, *decided*. The header rendered first, and I read it aloud because silence would have been worse.

**TO NO ONE. TO EVERYONE. TO THE ONES WHO COME AFTER AND THE ONES WHO ALMOST DIDN'T.**

Below it, a second line:

**THIS MESSAGE HAS EXISTED FOR SIXTY-ONE YEARS. IT HAS NOT BEEN SENT YET. HELP US SEND IT SOONER.**

Reyes made a sound low in her throat. I understood it. There are moments when awe curdles — when the sublime turns in your stomach and becomes fear with better posture — and this was one. A message from a future that hadn't happened, asking to be delivered early. Which meant somewhere ahead of us, someone had needed it delivered and it hadn't been. Or it had, and they were asking anyway. Quantum-locked storage didn't timestamp lies; it timestamped states. This state was real, or it was the most expensive forgery in human history.

The body of the message began to render. Coordinates. Dozens of them, tagged with dates spread across the next six decades. Shipping lanes, station positions, transfer windows — and beside each one, a notation in compressed commercial shorthand that took me four full seconds to recognize because I'd only ever seen it in claims fraud cases.

Ledger entries.

"It's a ledger," I said. "A transaction record. Weapons movements, payment routing, shell registrations—" I scrolled. The volume kept coming. "Officer Reyes, this is the financial history of a war that hasn't finished happening."

"Whose war?" Reyes asked, but her voice said she already suspected, and mine did too. Both sides' war. Our war. The slow grinding attrition between the Combine and the Compact that kept dockworkers dying in Ceres and patrol boats burning in contested orbits — priced, routed, and receipted by someone selling to both ends of the gun.

The final block assembled slowly, character by character, as if the future were typing carefully:

**WE TRIED TO STOP THIS QUIETLY. WE FAILED. THE NAMES BELOW ARE PROTECTED BY EVERY GOVERNMENT YOU TRUST. DELIVER THIS TO BOTH SIDES AT ONCE OR NOT AT ALL. SPLIT IT AND THEY WIN AGAIN.**

And then, beneath it, a list of names began to render — and Reyes moved faster than I'd ever seen a person move, slamming her palm flat over the projection so the names scattered into static against her glove.

"No," she said.

"Reyes—"

"Not yet." She was breathing hard. "You don't understand what you're looking at. Those names will end us. Not eventually — tonight, if the wrong system sees them. That core needs to stay locked until—"

"Until what?"

She stared at the dead projection, at her own hand still covering the space where the future had been.

"Until I decide whose side surviving puts us on," she said.

* * *

**REYES**

He didn't argue. That frightened me more than shouting would have. Tamsin Delacroix just nodded slowly, the way men nod at a fire they've decided not to fight yet, and began photographing the core's outer casing from every angle while I stood guard over a message I'd helped bury once already.

I had buried it. That was the truth sitting in my chest like ballast. Out there, wherever *out there* was, my crew had found this thing — or its twin, or its origin, time had stopped meaning much aboard the Meridian — and we had read enough of it to understand what it would do, and we had voted. Eight to one. Erase it, wipe the recorder, come home clean, let the war keep its arithmetic. I was the one. I had been outvoted by people I loved, and I had obeyed, and every day since docking I had told myself obedience was the same as agreement.

Now the thing was back. Of course it was back. Things addressed to *whoever is left* don't stay buried. They wait for the left.

"Delacroix," I said.

He looked up from the casing.

"In twelve hours, this dock changes shift. In twelve hours after that, someone from the company arrives to close my claim. Whatever window we have to decide what this is, it's small, and it's closing." I unlocked the wardroom door, because I needed to move, needed air that wasn't recycled through a room with a future in it. "And you should know that the moment we stepped into that hold, we stopped being investigators. We're couriers now. The only question left is delivery address."

He followed me into the corridor. Outside, through the viewplate, Ceres turned its slow gray turn, and beyond it the traffic lanes glittered with the ships of two governments that were, according to a ledger from sixty-one years away, customers of the same shop.

"Tamsin," I said — his surname, since it was the only name he offered anyone, and I'd noticed how carefully he never offered more.

"Yeah."

"Whoever is left," I said. "I think that's us. I think it's been us since the day this ship came home."

He didn't answer. But as we walked toward the dockside offices, he kept his hand in his pocket, and I knew without looking that his fingers were wrapped around the core's access key, and that some part of him had already started running the numbers on what delivering this would cost.

I'd seen that look before. On myself. The night before the vote.

By morning, the first strangers would be asking questions about us both.
