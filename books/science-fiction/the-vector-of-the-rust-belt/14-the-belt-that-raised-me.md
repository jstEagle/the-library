## 14. The Belt That Raised Me

The habitat didn't have a name anymore. It had had one, once — New Vesta, or maybe just Vesta-Adjunct, one of those bureaucratic compromises the Belt Compact loved, a name that meant nothing and cost nothing. Then the war came through and the Compact abandoned it, and the Combine shelled its docking arms, and the four thousand people who stayed called it what it was: Home. Just that. Home, spoken flat, the way you'd say a fact about gravity.

I had left it nine years ago with a burn bag full of my old life and a fake identity sewn into the lining of my coat. I came back with a woman the universe had aged backward, a data core from a future that didn't exist, and a bounty on my head that used my real name.

Some homecomings you don't get to choose. You just survive into them.

* * *

We came in dark, the way I'd taught Reyes to come in dark, riding the debris wake of a dead ore hauler so the habitat's approach sensors would read us as junk. The tug — *Marguerite*, a name I'd never asked the origin of — coughed and shuddered the whole way down the approach vector, her drive plate scarred from Dock Nine, her life support wheezing like a man climbing stairs. Reyes sat in the second seat with the core strapped to her chest in a cargo sling, arms wrapped around it, and watched the habitat grow in the viewport from a smear to a scar to a place.

"That's where you're from," she said. Not a question.

"From near it," I said. "I lived two rings over, in the section that got vented in the second year. This place held. Somebody made the call to seal the arms and let the outer docks burn instead of the spin sections. Four thousand people are alive because someone did arithmetic and hated themselves after."

"You know who made the call?"

"I know everyone who lived in it afterward. That's the problem."

She looked at me. In the dim of the cockpit her face was young the way it always was now, twenty-six and wrong against the forty years she carried behind her eyes, and she had that expression she got when she was deciding how hard to push. She decided.

"You've been running from this place for nine years, and you're bringing us here."

"I'm bringing us here because every port with a Combine flag will scan the *Marguerite* and every port with a Compact flag will scan the *Marguerite*, and the places with no flags are all cartel-run now. That leaves exactly one habitat in contested space where the sensor grid is held together with prayer and the manifest office is a woman named Odera who owes me nothing and hates me slightly, which is the safest relationship in the Belt."

"That's not why."

I flew the last correction burn instead of answering. She let me. That was the arrangement we'd fallen into somewhere past Ganymede: she let me not answer, and I let her see that I knew she noticed.

* * *

The docking arm was a rib cage of scorched trusses and patchwork sealant, lit by work lamps strung on cable that had been work lamps when I was a teenager. A dockhand in a patched thermal suit flagged us in with hand signals older than either government, and the clamps caught the *Marguerite* with a bang I felt in my teeth, and then we were inside, breathing recycled air that tasted like hydroponics and hot metal and — I hadn't let myself remember this — cinnamon. Somebody on Ring Three still grew cinnamon. Somebody had grown it through the shelling, through the embargo, through the years I'd been gone.

"Stay with the ship," I told Reyes. "I'll find Odera."

"You're going to walk into a habitat full of people who knew you under your real name, three days after that name showed up on bounty notices, alone."

"Yes."

"And that's the plan."

"That's the whole plan."

She stared at me, and then she did something I hadn't seen her do: she laughed. One short, disbelieving breath of it. "You're going to get us both killed because you can't ask for help like a normal person."

"Reyes. In three years, the number of people who've done me a favor without a price attached is zero. I'm about to find out if that number stays zero. I'd rather find out alone."

She unstrapped the core and held it out to me. "Then carry it yourself. If they're going to decide what you're worth, let them decide with all the evidence on the table."

* * *

Odera Voss ran the manifest office the way a lighthouse runs a coastline: from a single room, with a single lamp, answering to no one. She was seventy now, maybe older — the Belt ages people in ways Earth never sees, low gravity and high radiation and work that eats the body — and when I pushed open her door she didn't look up from her ledger. She just said, "Docking fee's twenty, cargo fee's ten, and if you're here to sell me something I've already got it."

"Odera."

Her pen stopped.

She looked up. I watched her do the thing everyone from Home does when they see a face they buried: the half-second of arithmetic, the checking against the dead, the recalculation of the world. Her pen came down on the desk. She stood up slowly, the way old people stand up when they're angry, which is to say with terrible care.

"Tomasin Ashgrove."

The name went through me like cold water. Not Delacroix. Not the dead man I'd been wearing for three years like a coat. *Ashgrove.* My father's name, my grandmother's spelling, the name on the school rosters and the memorial wall and the purge list. Nobody had said it out loud at me in nine years.

"Hello, Odera."

"You're dead," she said. "You've been dead since the fall. I signed the memorial notice myself. I wrote *lost with the evacuation of Ring Two* and I spelled your name right because your mother taught me to spell it when you were nine and stealing cable from my stores."

"I know."

"There's a plaque."

"I know."

"There's a plaque, Tomasin, and I put flowers on it every Remembrance, and you're standing in my office wearing a dead man's face and a dead man's papers asking me for docking fees like we're strangers at a port." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about Odera; it never rose. "Sit down."

I sat.

She looked at me for a long time. Behind her, through the office's one window, the spin section turned its slow turn, and the cinnamon smell came in through the vent, and somewhere a child was running along the ring corridor the way I had run along it, and the habitat held four thousand people who had survived everything the war could throw at them by the simple method of refusing to leave.

"Three years," she said finally. "That's how long the bounty's had your name on it. My nephew runs the comm relay. He flagged it the day it posted. He's known you were alive for three years, Tomasin."

The floor did the thing it had been doing all month, the dropping-away thing. "He never said anything."

"He never said anything," she agreed. "Neither did I. Neither did the eleven other people on this ring who worked out who you were. You want to know why?"

"Why."

"Because you left." She sat back down, and the anger went out of her the way tide goes out, leaving something older underneath. "You left because your name was on the purge list and staying would have gotten your neighbors interrogated, and we understood that, and we let you go, and we mourned you. And then you spent nine years being no one, and every year at Remembrance we said your name out loud anyway, because that's what we do here. The Belt doesn't get to keep much, Tomasin. Names are what we keep."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that wouldn't come out as the thing I'd spent nine years not saying.

"So," Odera said, and pulled her ledger back, and clicked her pen. "Docking fee's twenty. Cargo fee's ten. You've got berth six, the one with the good power tap, because berth six is empty and I say it's empty. And whatever's in that sling under your coat stays on your ship until you decide who needs it, and when you decide, my nephew can put a signal into forty relays before the Combine finishes a cup of coffee." She wrote something in the ledger. "Welcome home. Don't make me update the plaque."

* * *

Reyes didn't believe it at first. I watched her not believe it, over dinner in the *Marguerite*'s cramped galley, eating actual grown food — real greens, real protein, a bread that a woman named Sefa had pressed into my hands at the dock with the instruction *tell your friend the war hasn't beaten my ovens* — and waiting for the invoice.

"They're feeding us," she said. "They know who you are. They know what's on your ship. There's a bounty that would rebuild this habitat twice over, and they're feeding us bread."

"Yes."

"Who do they think they're loyal to? You're not Compact. You're not Combine. You're not even legally alive."

"They're not loyal to me," I said. "That's what I keep trying to explain to you and you keep not believing, because you spent your career in navies and navies don't work this way. They're loyal to the fact that I'm from here. That's the whole transaction. That's all of it."

"That's not a transaction. A transaction has two sides."

"Then it's not a transaction." I turned the bread over in my hands. It was still warm. "That's the thing, Reyes. That's the thing I ran from. There's no ledger here. There's no arithmetic. Odera gave me berth six because berth six is the good one and I'm from here, and she'll never ask me for anything, and it will cost her, and she knows it will cost her, and she did it anyway. I spent nine years building a life where every kindness had a price tag so I'd never be caught owing anyone. And the whole time, four thousand people were saying my name once a year over flowers."

Reyes was quiet for a while. The core sat in the corner of the galley, quantum-locked, patient, full of a future that had already been erased and was still trying to save us.

"I had a daughter," she said.

I looked up.

"Before the Meridian. Before the aging. I've never — the file says forty, and the file's right, and there's a girl on Titan who is now older than her mother, and I haven't been able to say it out loud because saying it makes it a fact I have to live inside." Her hands were flat on the table, steady the way she kept them when she was anything but. "You said once that being no one was the only country you could live in. I know. I've been living there too. It's just — I chose the ship over her, back then. The long haul, the bonus, the years. And the ship took the years back and gave me nothing, and I keep thinking if I'd stayed —"

"You'd be older," I said. "And she'd have had a mother. And the cartel would still be arming both sides, and the war would still be eating habitats like this one, and none of it would be your fault."

"That's not how guilt works."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

We sat with that, two people in a tin galley orbiting a rock that had refused to die, and after a while she reached over and took a piece of my bread without asking, which I think was her way of saying something she couldn't say yet, and I let her, because letting people take things without invoicing them was apparently a skill I was learning at forty-one years old, nine years late.

* * *

The dreams came back that night. They always came back here — the venting of Ring Two, the sound a hull makes when it lets go, which is not a bang but a sigh, a long metallic exhale, two hundred people becoming vacuum in the time it takes to notice a draft. In the dream I was on the wrong side of the bulkhead. In the dream I always was. I'd been on the right side, in the seal party, turning the wheel that closed the arm off with two hundred people on the far side of it, and the arithmetic had been correct, and the habitat had held, and I had done the math and hated myself after, exactly like whoever made the call I'd told Reyes about, because it had been me. It had always been me. I'd never told anyone that. I'd left before the memorial, before anyone could look at me too long and see the wheel in my hands.

I woke in berth six's narrow bunk with the good power tap humming and went out onto the ring corridor because I couldn't be inside my own head anymore.

The corridor was night-dimmed, running lights only, and a kid was there — maybe ten, sitting cross-legged by the viewport with a slate, doing homework at an hour when homework was clearly an excuse. She looked up at me. She looked at me the way kids look, which is to say without any of the mercy adults pretend to.

"You're the ghost," she said.

"I'm sorry?"

"Tía Odera's ghost. From the plaque. My mom says the ghost came home on the junk ship." She shrugged. "You don't look like a ghost."

"What do ghosts look like?"

"Older." She went back to her slate. Then, without looking up: "My granddad was on Ring Two. He says whoever sealed the arm saved the whole ring, and he says that person carried it alone too long, and he says—" she frowned at her homework, hunting the exact words, "—he says the Belt doesn't ask its ghosts to be sorry. It just asks them home."

I stood in the corridor of the habitat I'd sealed and fled and mourned and been mourned by, and a ten-year-old went back to her math, and the spin section turned, and the cinnamon came through the vents, and for the first time in nine years I let myself stand still in a place where someone knew my name.

* * *

Reyes found me there an hour later, or maybe she'd never gone to sleep either. She leaned on the rail beside me and we watched the stars wheel past the viewport, the slow carousel of a home that had never stopped turning.

"Odera's nephew flagged something an hour ago," she said quietly. "A ship came through the outer relay asking for docking. Small, fast, no registry that holds up. And they asked for us by description." She paused. "Not by name. By description. Like someone told them what we looked like."

The cold came back, the old familiar cold, but it came back differently now. Before, fear had been a country I lived in alone. Now I had four thousand people between me and it, and a kid doing homework by a viewport, and bread in my pocket, and a name on a plaque that I was apparently allowed to take back.

"Cartel?" I said.

"Odera thinks so. She says the ship's pattern matches a fixer she's seen before, back in the smuggling years. She says his name is Corvane." Reyes's jaw tightened. "Tamsin. He didn't ask to dock at the office. He asked to dock at *berth six*."

I looked down the long turning corridor, at the doors of people who had kept my name alive for nine years without being asked, and understood exactly what was coming, and understood, for the first time, that I was not going to face it alone — and that this, more than anything the cartel could send, was the thing that terrified me.

"How long," I said, "until they're inside the arm?"

Reyes checked the relay feed on her slate. "Ninety minutes," she said. "Maybe less."
