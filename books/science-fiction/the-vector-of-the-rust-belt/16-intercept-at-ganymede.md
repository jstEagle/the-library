## 16. Intercept at Ganymede

**TAMSIN**

The trap closed at 0417 ship time, which is the hour when even smugglers sleep badly.

I'd been running dark for nine days — no transponder, no running lights, thermal bleed tucked behind a heat sink shaped like a piece of junked cargo skiff. Reyes had the watch. I was in the bunk behind the cockpit dreaming about nothing, which was the closest thing to peace I'd had since Ceres, when her hand on my shoulder came through the dark like an arrest.

"Tamsin. Wake up. We have company."

I was strapped into the crash couch before my eyes finished opening. The scope showed what she'd seen: three drive flares blooming out of Ganymede's shadow, arranged in the wide triangle that meant *we know where you are and we don't need to hurry*. Combine hulls. Corvettes, two of them, and behind them something bigger with a profile I recognized from casualty reports — the cruiser *Vigilant*, twelve hundred tons of diplomatic muscle that had spent the war escorting armistice delegations and shooting down anyone who objected to them.

"They've been waiting," Reyes said. "They didn't chase us here. They were already here."

"Which means someone sold us." I ran the numbers out of habit. Fuel: eleven hours of burn left if I wanted any margin. Options: one hole in the patrol line to the south, too far, too slow. "Or everyone's been sold since Ceres and we just found out."

"Does it matter?"

It did, actually. It mattered because knowing who sold you told you what they wanted. But there wasn't time for that kind of thinking anymore. The lead corvette lit me up with a targeting laser — polite, deliberate, a handshake made of light — and then the comm crackled with a voice that sounded like it had been sanded smooth by twenty years of giving orders nobody wanted to hear.

*Unidentified vessel, this is Captain Amara Osei of the CSS Vigilant. You are carrying property stolen from the Meridian. Power down your drive and prepare for boarding. Resist and we will not fire on you — we will simply stop being careful.*

"I like her already," Reyes said dryly.

"You would." I looked at the scope again. Three ships, one vector, zero exits. And underneath the fear, doing its quiet arithmetic in the back of my skull, the part of me that had been pricing lives for fifteen years noted something else: *they said stolen from the Meridian.* Not stolen cartel property. Not contraband. They knew exactly what we had, and they'd chosen to name it as the Meridian's — which meant whoever gave us up wanted the core treated as evidence, not loot.

That was either very good news or the most elaborate trap of my career.

I keyed the comm. "Captain Osei. This is the tug *Patience*, registry pending, cargo one item, provenance disputed. I'll power down. But I want it on record that I'm complying under protest."

A pause. Then, faintly amused: *"Noted, Mr. Patience."*

She knew. Of course she knew. I cut the drive and let the silence take us, and beside me Reyes watched the corvettes grow in the scope like three slow knives, and neither of us said the thing we were both thinking: that after ten days of running, being caught felt almost like rest.

* * *

**REYES**

They boarded us at 0500 with the kind of discipline that made me homesick for a navy that had never been mine. Six marines, weapons slung but hands near, faces professionally blank. They took the core without ceremony — sealed it in a transport case lined with Faraday mesh, tagged it, logged it. Watching it leave the tug's hold was like watching a piece of my own chest get carried off on a stretcher.

Eleven years ago I'd have fought them. Eleven years ago I was forty-one years old and a lieutenant commander with a crew of eight depending on me, and I would have put my body between that case and anything that moved it. Now I stood aside with my hands visible and let a marine half my apparent age carry away the only proof that my crew's deaths had meant anything.

Tamsin watched me watching. He didn't say anything. That was the thing about him — he'd learned when silence was a kindness, which is a rarer skill than courage.

They marched us across the docking collar into the *Vigilant* and down a corridor that smelled like recycled air and institutional coffee, and into a briefing room where a woman in captain's grays stood alone at the table. No adjutant. No recorder running. She dismissed the marine escort with a flick of two fingers, waited for the hatch to cycle shut, and then did something no Combine officer had done to me in this entire nightmare.

She sat down first.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. You look like you haven't slept since Jupiter, and I need you sharp, so this goes faster if you're not standing at attention for people who aren't going to give it to you."

Tamsin sat. I sat. Up close, Osei was fifty-something and gray at the temples and tired in a way that went all the way down, the way you get tired when you've been fighting your own side quietly for years.

"You're Ilsa Reyes," she said to me. "First Officer, Meridian. Biologically twenty-six, chronologically fifty-two, and officially dead along with the rest of your crew until nine days ago. And you—" she turned to Tamsin, and I saw him go very still, the stillness of a man hearing his own name coming down a corridor, "—are going to tell me your real name in a minute, or you're going to lie to me, and I'm going to let you finish the lie before I correct it. Your choice. I find people respect the courtesy."

The room hummed. Somewhere below us, in a Faraday-lined box, the future sat waiting.

"The core," Tamsin said carefully. "You called it Meridian property. That's an interesting choice of words for a Combine boarding party."

"It's an accurate one. The Meridian was contracted through Halcyon Deep Freight, which is a subsidiary of Corvane Logistics, which is—"

"—the arms cartel," I said. "We know. It's in the message."

Something moved behind Osei's eyes. Not surprise. Confirmation. "Then you've decoded more than my analysts have in six days of trying. The quantum lock defeated everything we threw at it until—" she paused, and chose her next words like a woman stepping between mines, "—until I stopped reporting our progress upward."

Tamsin and I looked at each other. Three weeks ago I'd have read that pause as a trap being set. But I'd spent three weeks learning to read Tamsin Delacroix, and some of it had rubbed off: you learned to weigh what a person risked by saying a thing against what they'd gain. A captain setting a trap doesn't confess to falsifying her own reports. A captain setting a trap keeps her lies boring.

"How deep does it go?" Tamsin asked.

"To the top," Osei said. "I've suspected for four years. Suspecting is worthless. Suspicion gets you reassigned to a weather station on Titan, or it gets you a funeral with full honors and no body. What I needed was proof, and proof is what's sitting in my hold right now, locked tighter than anything my cryptographers have ever seen." She leaned forward. "But I'm told you two can open it. Or at least that you've opened enough of it to know what's inside. So here's my situation, and then here's my offer, and then you can decide how much of yourselves you're willing to spend."

* * *

**TAMSIN**

Here is what I have learned about offers: the dangerous ones always come wrapped in honesty.

Osei laid it out flat, no varnish. In seventy-two hours, the armistice talks resumed at L4 — the third round in two years, both delegations exhausted, both publics sick of the war, both militaries quietly rearming for the round after. The talks were theater. She believed it and could prove maybe a tenth of it; the core, fully decoded, could prove all of it. The same hands feeding guns to the Compact fleet were signing the Combine's logistics contracts. The admiral chairing the ceasefire framework — Admiral Voss, the Peacemaker, the man whose face was on recruitment posters in three systems — was the cartel's senior partner.

"If I walk into those talks with suspicion," Osei said, "I lose my command and the war continues. If I walk in with that core authenticated and decoded, the entire framework collapses in front of both delegations, and whatever replaces it has to be built on ground that isn't owned by Corvane." She spread her hands. "I can't authenticate it. My analysts can't decode it. But the message addressed itself to *no one and everyone*, and somewhere in that index — according to the fragment your friend broadcast from the cruiser that took her — there's a verification protocol. A chain of custody. The message validates itself, but only if it's delivered by someone who can testify to how it was found, and who found it, and what was done to keep it from reaching this table."

"And that someone is me," I said.

"That someone is you." She didn't soften it. "On the record. Under your real name. Voice, face, biometrics, sworn testimony, entered into the permanent armistice record and published to both governments and every news service in human space. Forever."

There it was. Three years of running compressed into a single sentence, spoken aloud in a room with no recorder — ironic, that — while outside the viewport Ganymede turned slow and ice-colored and indifferent.

"You know what you're asking," I said.

"I know exactly what I'm asking. I pulled your file, Mr.—" she paused, deliberately, "—well. Your real file. The one from before. I know you came off a purge list in the fall of the Belt. I know the name you buried and I know why. And I know that if you testify under it, the Combine can't purge you twice without admitting the first purge happened, which is politically impossible once you're standing in front of the cameras. Testimony is armor, Mr. Delacroix. Ugly, heavy, permanent armor. But armor."

"My name isn't a suit I can put on," I said. "It's a grave I dug. There are people alive today because that grave stayed shut. Dockworkers. People who vouched for a dead man's papers. The moment I stand up and say *my name is*, every favor anyone ever did me becomes evidence, every kindness becomes a crime, and every person who helped me disappear gets audited by people who audit for a living."

Osei was quiet for a moment. "And if you don't testify?"

"Then the core stays locked, or it gets decoded in a lab by analysts working from your authority, and the cartel finds out, and the cartel owns enough of the Combine's security apparatus to make sure the decryption dies in an accident. You said it yourself. You can't open it. We barely can, and Reyes has been living inside its mathematics for three weeks."

"Then teach my analysts—"

"They'd need months," Reyes said. "Maybe years. The lock isn't encryption, Captain, it's a proof-of-history. It wants a witness. That's the whole design. Whoever built it understood that data can be stolen, forged, suppressed — but testimony, witnessed testimony, testimony entered into the public record with a living human being standing behind it — that's the one thing a cartel can't buy back after the fact." Her voice caught, just slightly, on the next words. "My crew read this message eleven years ago and voted to erase it. Eight to one. I was the one. And I have spent eleven years being wrong about it in a body that won't age past the day I made the mistake. Don't ask me to help you bury it again."

The room held still. Outside, Ganymede's terminator crept across the viewport, night rolling over the ice like a lid closing.

Osei turned back to me. "Seventy-two hours. The talks reconvene at 0900, station time, day after tomorrow. If the core isn't in the chamber with authenticated testimony attached, Voss closes the session, declares the Compact negotiating in bad faith, and we get another two years of war. Two years at current attrition rates—" she named the number, and it was the kind of number I used to write premiums against, the kind that looks abstract until you remember each unit is somebody's whole world. "That's the deadline. That's the price. I'm not going to pretend it's small."

* * *

**REYES**

They gave us a cabin — a real cabin, with a door that locked from the inside, which after three weeks on a tug felt like decadence — and an hour to decide, and then Osei left us alone with the biggest decision either of us would ever make and a pot of coffee that tasted like regret.

Tamsin stood at the viewport with his back to me, hands folded behind him, and I knew that posture. It was the actuary's stance. He was running numbers. He was always running numbers; it was how he prayed.

"Say it out loud," I said. "Whatever the ledger says. Say it."

He didn't turn around. "Option one: I testify. My name comes back from the dead, permanently, publicly. Everyone who ever helped me is exposed to review. I spend the rest of my life being a person with a history, most of which is classified, all of which is dangerous." A breath. "Millions live. Option two: I refuse. Osei tries anyway, fails, probably gets reassigned to Titan, the war runs two more years, and I stay invisible and alive and complicit. Option three: we run. Take the core, run dark again, hope we find another way. Odds of success, low. Odds of survival, lower. Odds of the core surviving contact with the cartel's hunters, negligible."

"And the fourth option," I said. "The one you're not saying."

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't. Then: "Option four is that I testify, and it works, and the war ends, and I have to live in the world afterward. As myself. With a name." His voice did something I'd never heard it do — cracked, slightly, like ice under load. "Reyes, I built the invisibility brick by brick. It cost me everything I was. And now someone's telling me the only way to save millions of people is to hand it all back, and the worst part — the genuinely obscene part — is that some small rotten corner of me *wants* to. Has wanted to since Ceres. Since the loader. Since the first small mercy I couldn't quite smother."

I got up and stood beside him at the viewport. Below us, the tug *Patience* hung in the docking cradle, small and battered and the closest thing to home either of us had.

"When I was forty-one," I said, "I had a daughter. Mariel. She was nineteen when the Meridian went missing. By now she's thirty. Or she was, before—" I gestured vaguely at myself, at the impossibility of my own face. "The point is, I know what it is to mourn a version of yourself that other people remember better than you do. And I know that the mourning doesn't end. It just gets quieter. But Tamsin — the vote. Eight to one. I've had eleven years to understand why the eight voted the way they did. They were afraid. It's forgivable. But the message didn't address itself to the brave. It addressed itself to *everyone*. Which means it was written for the afraid, too. For the ones who'd have to be scared and deliver it anyway."

He looked at me then, and I watched him arrive at the place I'd arrived at years ago, the place past fear where the math stops mattering because the answer was never in the math.

"The verification protocol," he said slowly. "When I trigger it — when I testify — it needs my identity confirmed against pre-fall records. Civil registries. Belt census data."

"Yes."

"Which means the Combine has to formally acknowledge that the purge lists existed. Publicly. On the record." A ghost of something crossed his face — not a smile, but the place where a smile might someday learn to live. "Osei didn't mention that part."

"She didn't have to. You figured it out in four seconds. That's why it has to be you." I touched his sleeve, lightly. "Your name isn't just testimony, Tamsin. It's a confession the Combine has to make to get what it wants. You'd be spending your grave to buy everyone else's pardon."

The intercom chimed. Osei's voice, stripped of command, almost gentle: *"Mr. Delacroix. Ms. Reyes. The corvettes report movement in the outer system — three unidentified drives, inbound fast, profile matches Corvane escort patterns. They know where we are. I need your answer now, or I need you off my ship in a lifepod, and I need to know which."*

Tamsin closed his eyes. I watched him say goodbye to a dead man's name — watched it happen in the set of his shoulders, the way a building settles when the last scaffold comes down.

He opened his eyes and keyed the comm.

"Captain Osei. Get me to the chamber. My name is Elias Vance Delacroix, born Thule Station, Belt Registry 7734-C, and I'm ready to say it on the record."

A pause on the line. Then, quietly: *"Welcome back from the dead, Mr. Delacroix. All hands — set course for L4. Best speed."*

Outside the viewport, the stars began to slide.
