## 1. Contraband of the Mind

The dead travel light. That's what I tell my clients, anyway, when they hand me a chip the size of a fingernail and ask if I can really get it through customs. The dead don't need oxygen, don't need water, don't need a berth on any ship with a legal registry. All they need is a smuggler with a soft heart and a good hiding spot.

I have both. Don't tell anyone about the heart.

The *Kestrel* hung in Meridian approach lane like a stray dog waiting outside a butcher's shop — not hopeful, exactly, but unwilling to leave. Around us, the orbital customs ring of Halcyon Station turned in its slow, bureaucratic orbit, all floodlights and scanning arrays and officers who had learned to look bored in a way that cost extra money to fix.

"Manifest's loaded," Juno said from the copilot's seat, not looking up from their slate. They never looked up when they were lying. Looking up, Juno maintained, was for amateurs and people with clear consciences. "Fourteen hundred crates of agricultural substrate, six hundred of medical polymer, and one very bored inspector named Okafor who takes his bribe in uncut caffeine and his dignity in private."

"And the ghosts?"

"Ghost class is clean. You want to do your magic, or do I get to watch you suffer?"

I unbuckled and floated aft toward the cargo bay, where fourteen hundred crates of nothing in particular waited to become something much more valuable. Juno calls it magic. It isn't. Magic implies mystery, and what I do is closer to surgery — delicate, repetitive, and the kind of work where one trembling hand ruins somebody's mother forever.

Here's how the trade works, for those of you who've never had anything worth remembering. Memory-chips are illegal in most of the inner system. Officially, it's a safety issue — unregulated neural recordings can carry malware, can carry compulsion loops, can carry, in the Consortium's own words, "destabilizing emotional content." What that phrase means in practice is: grief. Grief is destabilizing. Love is destabilizing. The recorded voice of your dead daughter saying your name is *extremely* destabilizing, and the Consortium would very much prefer you purchase an approved Consolation Package from a licensed provider instead — six standardized sessions of professionally moderated mourning, with a licensed grief counselor's voice and a licensed musical score and a licensed little blue light that pulses at a frequency proven to reduce attachment.

People don't want licensed grief. People want *their* grief. Their own, specific, irreplaceable dead, in their own voices, wearing their own terrible jokes. And since the Consortium won't sell them that, they come to people like me.

Hazel Eastwick, licensed to carry nothing, carrying everything.

* * *

The cargo bay was cold, which is good for the chips and bad for my hands. I pulled the first crate — agricultural substrate, according to the manifest, and technically true, because the false-bottom lining was molded from actual substrate polymer, and if you scraped it you could probably grow something sad in it.

Inside the lining, nested like eggs: forty chips in foam cradles, each one tagged with a client number and nothing else. No names. Names are dangerous. The chips don't get names until they reach the other side, where a broker I've never met face-to-face matches them to the people who've been waiting, sometimes for years, sometimes for a grief that has changed shape twice since they first paid.

I checked each chip against the intake log — serial, checksum, seal integrity. Forty chips. Forty little tombs. I held the fourth one up to the bay light for a moment longer than the checklist required, because the client number matched a file I'd read too many times: a woman on Thessaly Station whose husband had been a deep-belt miner. Thirty-one seconds of recording. That's all she'd asked for. Not his last words — he hadn't had any, the decompression had been too fast — just thirty-one seconds of him in their kitchen, complaining about the coffee, laughing at something she'd said off-camera. She'd told the intake clerk she played the licensed Consolation Package every night and it was like being read a menu when you were starving.

Thirty-one seconds of coffee complaints. That's what I was carrying past the guns.

I seated the chips into the manifest layer — a physical trick, not a digital one, because digital anything gets scanned and physical cleverness only gets searched, and searches cost bribes while scans cost careers. The manifest chips for the substrate crates were real, legitimate, boring as oatmeal. Each one had a hollow channel milled into its spine, and each channel held a ghost chip, and the whole assembly read as a single unremarkable file: LOT 4471, SUBSTRATE POLYMER, AGRICULTURAL GRADE, QTY 40, INSPECTED AND SEALED.

The dead riding inside the paperwork of the living. There's a metaphor in there I refuse to examine.

* * *

By the time I'd done two hundred chips, my fingers ached and my back had that specific complaint that comes from floating in a harness for four hours doing precision work. Juno drifted in with two bulbs of the terrible tea they insist on buying and sat on a crate like gravity was a personal favor they were doing me.

"Okafor's boarding in twenty," they said. "You're at two hundred and six."

"Two hundred and forty by the time he reaches the bay."

"Two hundred and forty by the time he reaches the bay," Juno repeated, in the tone of a person who has heard this promise eleven times and been disappointed twice. They sipped their tea. "New client batch came in while you were sleeping. Thessaly woman sent a message."

I didn't stop working. "Did she."

"Sent a recording, actually. Which, you know. On brand." Juno set their slate on the crate beside me, screen up, and drifted off to pretend to inspect the sealant guns, which is their way of giving me privacy without admitting privacy is a thing either of us needs.

The message was eleven seconds long. The widow of Thessaly, face filling the frame, older than her file photo because grief does that, holding a chip in her open palm like a bird she was afraid would fly. *"Received. It's him. It's — it's the kitchen, it's the bad coffee, he says the thing about the—"* and there her voice went, and the recording cut off, because whoever had sent it along to Juno had understood that some messages don't need to finish.

Eleven seconds. I'd carried thirty-one seconds of a dead man complaining about coffee for eight months across four stations, and it had arrived, and it had landed, and somewhere on Thessaly a woman was going to survive her husband now instead of merely outliving him.

That's the job. That's the whole job. Nobody pays me to feel this. The fee covers transport, concealment, and risk. The feeling is a gratuity I can't seem to stop including.

* * *

Inspector Okafor arrived at hour six, exactly as bribed. He was a heavyset man with the eyes of someone who had looked inside ten thousand crates and found his soul in none of them. He drifted through the *Kestrel*'s bay with a scanner wand he handled the way a priest handles a censer — ritually, performatively, with no expectation of revelation.

"Substrate," he said, in the voice of a man reading his own eulogy.

"Agricultural grade," I agreed.

"Lot 4471." He passed the wand over a crate. The wand chirped its contentment. Somewhere inside that crate, forty dead people held their breath, if dead people can be said to hold anything. "You know," Okafor said, and for one lurching second I thought the game was up, "I had an aunt on Callisto. Died three years ago. Her Consolation Package was very professional."

"I'm sorry for your loss," I said, which is the correct response, and also the only one permitted.

"Mm." He stamped the manifest without looking at it. "Clean ship. Clean papers. Clean everything." The way he said *clean* had a price tag hanging off it, and Juno materialized at his elbow with the second half of the caffeine bribe and a handshake that lasted a half-second too long and meant *yes, and also no, and also never speak of this.*

Okafor left. The airlock cycled. Juno and I stood in the bay among fourteen hundred crates and the two hundred and forty ghosts I'd finished with nine minutes to spare, and neither of us said anything, because there's a specific silence that follows a clean customs pass, and it's the closest thing to prayer either of us practices.

"Thessaly woman," Juno said finally. "You watched it eleven times."

"Twelve."

"You always do this."

"Do what?"

"Carry them twice." Juno pushed off the crate, drifting toward the cockpit, and threw the words back over their shoulder with the studied carelessness of someone who had learned the hard way what happens to smugglers who carry things twice. "Once for the client. Once for yourself. That's how people like us end up with cargo holds full of other people's dead and nothing of our own."

"I have plenty of my own," I said.

And that was true, and that was the problem. Because Juno was wrong about one thing, and right about everything else. I didn't carry other people's dead because I had none of my own. I carried them because I had exactly one, and he was eleven years gone, and no chip in existence held his voice, because he'd vanished before I ever thought to record him, before either of us knew that *vanish* was a thing a living man could do.

Theo Voss. Dead, officially. An accident, officially. A name I kept in the one place customs could never scan, which is to say nowhere at all, which is to say everywhere.

* * *

We made the Halcyon drop at hour nine. The broker's people came alongside in a maintenance skiff painted the gray of things that are officially not there, and I passed the crates through the lock one by one, and somewhere on the other end of that transfer, forty families were going to get phone calls or notes or nothing at all — just a chip appearing in a dead drop with a number that matched a number they'd been given years ago by a clerk they'd never see.

Payment cleared before the skiff undocked. Juno watched the account update with the small, sharp satisfaction they allowed themselves about money and nothing else.

"Half to the reserve," they said. "Half to the run fund. And you're eating something that isn't tea today, I can see it in your posture."

"I'm fine."

"You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you've adopted a ghost." Juno killed the docking clamps and the *Kestrel* shivered free of Halcyon's ring, falling away from the floodlights into the honest dark. "The miner's widow. You're going to think about her for a week."

"Three days."

"A week." Juno was almost always right about things like this, which was insufferable, which was why I kept them. Eleven years running the lanes together and they still couldn't sit through my silences without narrating them. "Next pickup is in six days. Broker's got something special, he says. Won't say what over comms. Wants a face-to-face at the Vantaa drift."

"A face-to-face." I frowned. Old Man Sable did face-to-faces for two reasons: extraordinary merchandise, or extraordinary trouble, and in my experience the two arrived holding hands. "What kind of special?"

"The kind he charges extra for." Juno fed the burn coordinates into the board and the stars stretched. "He said — and I quote, because I wrote it down because it was weird — *'Tell Eastwick I've got a manifest that never flew.'*"

The words went through me the way cold goes through a hull breach — fast, total, and without asking permission. A manifest that never flew. Somewhere in the back of my skull, a door I'd kept locked for eleven years rattled on its hinges, and I made myself smile, and I made my voice come out level, and I said:

"Then I guess we're going to the Vantaa drift."

Juno looked at me then. Looked up, for once. Whatever they saw in my face, they had the grace not to name it.

"Course is laid in," they said quietly. "Six days."

Six days. And somewhere ahead of us, in the drift between the lanes where honest ships don't go, a dead man's name was waiting on a piece of paper that should never have existed.

I carried other people's ghosts for a living. I should have known, sooner or later, one of them would be mine.
