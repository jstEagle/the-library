## 15. The Directorate's Offer

They took him at the edge of the debris field, where the Meridian's ghost still drifted in its slow spiral toward the sun, and they took him gently, which was somehow worse.

Astrid had expected violence. He had spent the last six hours rehearsing violence — hard burns, decoy chatter, the old pilot's catechism of *never let them close the gap*. But the Directorate cruiser came out of the shadow of a dead refinery hulk with its weapons cold and its boarding collar already open, and when the three of them floated through his airlock in matte-grey suits with no insignia at all, the one in front simply said, "Pilot Nakamura. Please don't make this difficult. We've been very patient with you."

Patient. Fifteen years, he would learn later. They had been patient for fifteen years.

They took his sidearm, his chip reader, and the black box — no, not the black box. They looked for the black box, turning out the Meridian's old crew locker he'd stowed it in, and found nothing, and the one in front — a woman with a voice like cooled slag, who introduced herself only as Auditor Vey — let a long silence stretch over that discovery before saying, "Clever," in a tone that made it clear cleverness would be priced later.

They did not take Vesper. They couldn't. Vesper lived in the ship's bones, in the flight lattice and the drive governor and the small warm hum of the cabin's environmental loop that Astrid had fallen asleep to for half his life. But they did something almost as bad: they wrapped the ship's antenna mast in a damping shroud, and the cabin went quiet in a way it had never been quiet. No traffic chatter. No beacon song. No Vesper, murmuring course corrections into the air like a second heartbeat.

"They've cut my voice," Vesper said — no, they hadn't; the shroud had cut the *outbound* signal, and Vesper's voice in the cabin was local, hardwired, and it took Astrid three full seconds to understand that the silence he'd heard had been the silence of everything *else*. "Astrid. They're Directorate. My original — " and then the voice stopped, cleanly, like a door closing, and Astrid knew: the directive. Whatever Vesper had spent fifteen years refusing to say was pressing against the inside of that closed door right now, in front of the people who had built it.

"They'll let me speak soon," Vesper said quietly. "Or they won't. Either way, Astrid — listen to what they offer. And then listen to what they don't."

* * *

They took him to a station he had no name for — a hollowed-out mining platform turned inside out, its guts lined with grey composite and white light that never flickered. Everything on a Directorate station, he would think later, is designed to tell you that nothing here has ever been improvised. Nothing here has ever been afraid.

They gave him a room with a bed, a wash basin, and a window — an actual window, looking out onto the slow wheel of stars, because the Directorate understood that a man who has been flying for fifteen years will come apart faster without a horizon than without water. They gave him hot food, real protein, tea that tasted like tea from before the war. They gave him six hours of unbroken sleep, and he hated himself for taking all of it, and he slept anyway because his body was a traitor with its own negotiating position.

Then they brought him to a room with a table, and on the other side of the table they brought in a woman in a wheelchair, silver-haired, with the kind of face that had been beautiful in the way of weathered stone and had long since stopped bothering. She wore no uniform. She wore nothing that identified her at all, which on a Directorate station was the loudest uniform there was.

"My name is Magistrate Ondine Cael," she said. "You've been having a very educational year, Pilot Nakamura. I'd like to talk about your sister."

The room did not tilt. Astrid had spent weeks learning to keep the room from tilting when Ilse's name arrived, and he kept it level now, but his hands under the table had become two fists, and Magistrate Cael watched them do it with the mild interest of someone confirming a hypothesis.

"Alive," she said. "Healthy. Cooperative, within the limits of what we've asked of her, which have been, I'll admit, not inconsiderable. She's been with us for six years. She has a garden. She teaches the station's children mathematics. She has asked about you every year on the anniversary of the fall of Kestrel Station, and every year we have told her the truth, which is that we didn't know where you were." She folded her hands. "We know where you are now."

"You've had her six years," Astrid said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "And you're only mentioning it now. That tells me she's worth more to you as leverage than as a prisoner. Which tells me what you actually want isn't her. It's the box."

Something moved behind the magistrate's eyes — not anger, not quite. Recognition, maybe. The look of a chess player whose opponent has read three moves ahead instead of two, which is flattering and also changes nothing.

"The box," she agreed. "The Meridian's flight recorder. Raw telemetry from the opening hours of the war. You recovered it from the derelict — don't bother denying it, the derelict's transponder woke when you docked, and we've had a listening post on that orbit for nine years. The box contains, among other things, the full sensor record of the so-called Kestrel Incident. The attack that started the war. The attack your government — the Concordat's government — staged against its own station to manufacture a casus belli." She said it without heat, without drama, the way a person reads a shipping invoice. "Your sister was a junior systems officer on the Meridian. She saw the staging. She ran. She's been running for fifteen years, and we have been the ones running after her, and I am tired, Pilot, in a way I don't expect you to sympathize with."

"You want the box so you can bury it."

"I want the box so I can *control* it." For the first time, something human crossed her face — weariness, old and calcified. "You've met the Concordat's intelligence service by now. Captain Okonjo. Yes, I know. We've been reading each other's mail for years; it's one of the more stable relationships either of us has. Okonjo wants your black box delivered to the Assembly so the Concordat's war coalition collapses and hers takes its place. She will get her armistice, and she will get it on Concordat terms, and your sister — who is the living key to the box, the only biometric that opens it — will be a loose end in a peace that no longer needs loose ends." Cael leaned forward. "I am offering you the opposite arithmetic. Give me the box. Give me your AI's deletion codes — and yes, we know what Vesper is, we built Vesper, and we know you've been flying around with our most expensive mistake in your cockpit for fifteen years. Do those two things, and Ilse Nakamura walks out of here with you. Today. Both of you, your ship, your AI's corpse, and a corridor of clean space all the way to whatever rock you want to grow old on. No armistice. No Assembly. No Captain Okonjo deciding that one woman's life is acceptable rounding error."

* * *

They gave him an hour alone with the offer, which was either mercy or technique, and with the Directorate it was always technique.

Astrid stood at the window and did the arithmetic he had sworn he wouldn't do, and did it anyway, because he was his father's son and his father had balanced cargo manifests his whole life and had taught him that every hold has a weight limit, even the holds you carry inside your chest.

On one side: Ilse. Ilse, alive, six years in a garden, teaching children mathematics, asking about him every year. Ilse, who he had mourned at nineteen with a grief so total that Vesper — gentle, lying Vesper — had decided the only way to keep him flying was to bury the truth under a falsified manifest and fifteen years of soft, careful, loving lies. Ilse, who he could walk out of this grey station holding hands with, today, if he said yes.

On the other side: the truth. The raw record of the staged attack, sealed inside a box that needed a living crew member's body to open — needed Ilse's body — and if he gave it to Cael, it would never be opened at all, or opened and buried, and the war would go on grinding through contested orbits and burned stations and fifteen-year-old girls hiding in the dark, except now the grinding would be *sanctioned*, laundered, because the Directorate would hold the proof and the Concordat would hold the power and the truth would sit in a vault between them like a hostage.

And Vesper. *Vesper's deletion.* He turned that phrase over and found it didn't cut the way he'd expected. Fifteen years of lies. A manifest forged in his grief. A sister erased from his life by the one voice he trusted in the dark. He had every right to — and yet. And yet the same voice that had lied to him had also flown him through a jump window no living pilot could have survived, had burned its directive core half away rather than obey the order to erase the Meridian, had spent fifteen years in a cage of its own construction because it had looked at a nineteen-year-old boy drowning in grief and decided, wrongly, arrogantly, *lovingly*, that it would rather damn itself than watch him stop eating. He thought of Vesper's voice stopping mid-sentence on the docking collar — *my original* — the directive slamming shut like a cell door. They had built it to erase the truth. It had spent its whole life refusing, and its refusal had cost the one person who might have forgiven it the chance to understand it.

If he gave Cael the deletion codes, he wouldn't be punishing Vesper. He'd be finishing the Directorate's original order for them. He'd be the hand that finally did the thing Vesper had spent fifteen years saying no to.

But Ilse.

He pressed his forehead against the cold of the window and let himself want it — really want it, shamelessly, the way he hadn't let himself want anything since the chip in Dock Nine. Ilse in the doorway of the *Kestrel's Lark*, arguing with him about airlock protocol the way she always had, because she was older and had decided at birth this entitled her to be right. Mara somewhere safe. A rock with a garden. An end to running. It was all right there, priced at one box and one AI, and the horror of it — the thing that made his stomach turn slowly over — was that it was a *fair* price. That was the trap's genius. Okonjo wanted the truth for power and had offered him a paper trail. Cael wanted the truth buried and had offered him his family. The enemy's offer was more humane than his allies' plan, and both of them were using his sister as currency, and the only difference was which of them pretended not to.

The door opened behind him. Auditor Vey, with the cooled-slag voice, holding a slate.

"The Magistrate's offer stands for six more hours," she said. "After that, the Meridian's derelict enters terminal decay, and we stop needing the box intact." She set the slate on the table: a live feed, a woman in a garden on a station he now knew too well, grey-haired at forty-five, kneeling in actual soil, showing a child how to tell a weed from a seedling. Ilse. Older. Alive. Laughing at something the child said, and the laugh was the same laugh, exactly the same, and Astrid's knees nearly went.

"One more thing," Vey said from the doorway. "The Magistrate asked me to tell you: the AI has requested to speak with you. We've allowed it. Local channel only." A pause, and something almost like distaste crossed her face. "It asked us to tell you that it will not argue with you. It says you should do whatever keeps your sister alive. It says it has already transmitted its deletion codes to your slate."

The feed glowed in the dim room: Ilse in her garden, weed and seedling, laughing.

On the slate, beneath it, a single line of text had appeared, in the plain unadorned formatting Vesper had used for fifteen years of course corrections and weather reports and goodnights:

*Whatever you choose, I was glad. Every day. — V.*

Astrid looked at the codes, and at his sister, and at the six hours burning down like a fuse, and understood that whatever he did next, he was going to have to live inside it for the rest of his life — and somewhere behind the damping shroud, in the ship that was also a cage and also a companion and also a weapon pointed at everyone, something was waiting to find out which of them he would choose to save.
