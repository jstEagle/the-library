## 11. Checksum

The paper lived in the sub-basement, three floors below the germination decks, in a room the original architects had labeled with the cheerful optimism of people who assumed someone else would maintain it: *ARCHIVE — DRY GOODS — DO NOT WET*.

Yara had not been down here in four months. She told herself this was because the trays upstairs needed her, which was true, and also because the sub-basement smelled like cold cardboard and the particular loneliness of things nobody had touched in decades, which was also true. She stood at the top of the stairs with a flashlight in one hand and a thermos of chicory substitute in the other and gave herself the same pep talk she gave reluctant seedlings.

*You're going down there, and you're going to be fine, and when you come back up you'll know something you didn't know before. Growth is uncomfortable. That's how you know it's working.*

The stairwell lights came on in reluctant sections, each bulb flickering awake like an old man being asked to help move furniture. At the bottom, the door was heavy, steel-cored, and sealed with a gasket that hissed when she broke it — a sound like the room exhaling after holding its breath for years.

Inside, the paper records of the Svalbard Annex — Earth's last seed vault, her vault, though *hers* was a word she used carefully, the way you'd use *mine* about a lighthouse you kept — sat in acid-free boxes on steel shelving, arranged with a precision that made her chest ache with fondness for the dead or retired archivists who had done it. Accession logs. Germination trial reports. Shipping manifests. Handwritten notes in the margins of printouts from an era when people still printed things out *because paper couldn't be hacked*, a phrase that had sounded paranoid in 2041 and sounded, in 2078, like scripture.

She pulled the first box and set it on the reading table under the humming fluorescent tube. Her breath fogged. The climate control down here was set for paper, not people: twelve degrees, thirty percent humidity. She'd last maybe two hours before her hands got too stiff to turn pages, so she'd better be efficient.

She opened her slate, woke the air-gapped terminal she'd carried down on a cart, and began.

* * *

The principle was simple enough that she'd once explained it to her brother over a laggy video call, back when Kepler-44 was still a shimmer of possibility and he'd been so excited he kept talking over her.

"Think of it like a recipe card," she'd said. "The network version of a record can be changed — anyone with access can edit it. But if you wrote the recipe on a card, and you kept the card, and someone later hands you a cookbook that claims the recipe always said *two cups of flour* when you know you wrote *one* — the card wins. The card is the checksum."

Tomas had laughed at her. "You're going to spend your whole life defending flour measurements."

"I'm going to spend my whole life defending *truth*, and flour is just where it lives."

She thought about that conversation now, turning the first brittle page of the 2063 accession log, and had to stop for a moment and press the heel of her hand against her eyes.

Not now. Grief was a weed; it would take every hour you gave it. She had work.

The method was tedious by design. For each record in the Meridian's official manifest — the beautifully typeset, seamlessly cross-referenced document Commander Rehn's quartermaster had provided "for reconciliation" — Yara needed to find the paper ancestor. The departure logs. The crew rosters. The supply requisitions filed before the Meridian ever left Earth, when the colony was alive and loud and answering, and every transaction left a paper shadow in this room.

If the digital records had been edited — and she was nearly certain they had; there was a *smoothness* to them, a lack of the small honest scars that real records accumulate, the misspellings and corrections and marginal notes of actual human administration — then the paper would show the seams. The paper couldn't be edited. The paper had been waiting down here in the cold for eleven years, patient as a seed, for exactly this.

She worked through the morning. Her fingers went numb. She drank the chicory substitute, which had gone lukewarm and tasted like punishment, and kept working.

The crew roster was the first crack.

The Meridian's manifest listed the original crew as forty-two. She remembered this because Rehn had said it herself, standing in the vault's atrium with her hands clasped behind her back like a woman touring a garden she intended to buy: "Forty-two of us went out, and forty-two of us came home. Not one lost." She'd said it with such warmth. Such *pride*.

The paper said forty-four.

Yara stared at it. The 2067 crew manifest, signed in ink by the mission registrar, a woman named Okafor whose handwriting slanted hard to the right as if she were always in a hurry to get somewhere. Forty-four names. Two had been struck through in the digital version Yara had compared that night — not deleted, *struck*, with the neat digital equivalent of a red pen, annotated *transferred to support rotation, Earth-side*. But there was no support rotation. Yara had spent eleven years reading every bulletin from every Earth-side program. There was no such rotation in 2067. She'd have known. She'd known everything else about that mission the way you know everything about a place your brother lives.

Two names, removed. And the two names were—

She checked the colony's last known personnel list, cross-referenced from her own vault archives, the ones that predated the silence. Dr. Amara Osei. And a life-support technician named D. Ferreira.

Both had been listed, in the colony's final transmissions, as Kepler-44 personnel.

Yara sat back on the cold metal chair and let that settle. It didn't prove anything. People transferred. Paper made mistakes. She was a scientist; she did not fall in love with her first hypothesis; she'd seen too many seedlings die of optimistic overwatering to trust enthusiasm.

But it was a thread. And she had spent eleven years doing patient, repetitive, unglamorous work, and if there was one thing eleven years of tending trays had taught her, it was that you pull one thread at a time and you keep your hands steady.

* * *

The distress call found her two days later, and it found her by accident, which was somehow worse.

She had graduated from the crew records to the communications logs — the vault's own logs, because the vault had served as a relay node in the early years, before the deep-space array at Alice Springs was finished and the traffic rerouted. Every transmission to and from Kepler-44 in the first three years of the colony had bounced through her antennas. Copies sat in her air-gapped archives, timestamped, checksummed, and — this was the part that mattered — mirrored on paper, because the relay system's original design specification required hardcopy confirmation of every priority-class message. A regulation written by a committee of paranoid engineers in the 2050s. Yara intended to build a small shrine to that committee if she survived what was coming.

She was looking for supply manifests. She was *not* looking for the message that arrived at 03:14 vault time on the 14th of March, 2074.

The paper slip was thin, thermal-printed, the ink already fading to a ghostly brown. Priority-class relay confirmation. Origin: Kepler-44 Colony Authority. Destination: Earth Coordination, Geneva. And the classification code—

Yara knew the classification codes. Every caretaker did. They were drilled into you in training, along with the joke that you'd never use them: *Code Green, all's well. Code Amber, trouble but manageable. Code Red—*

Code Red was never used. That was the joke. Code Red was the fire extinguisher behind glass you'd break and it would be a drill, always a drill.

The slip in her hands said Code Red.

Her own archive had no record of it. She checked. She checked three times, hands shaking, running the query on the air-gapped terminal with a patience she did not feel. The relay logs in her digital archive — her *own* logs, the ones she'd trusted, the ones that had never been touched because who would bother touching the seed vault's dusty mirrors — showed no Code Red from Kepler-44. Ever. The traffic log for 14 March 2074 showed routine telemetry, a supply request, a birthday message from a colonist named Okonkwo to her sister in Lagos.

But the paper said Code Red. Timestamped. Confirmed by relay. Signed out by the automated system with the machine equivalent of a signature, and countersigned — she squinted at the fading scrawl — by the night-duty technician, a name she didn't recognize, someone who had worked here before her time and gone, like everyone.

The distress call had come in. The vault had received it, confirmed it, forwarded it. And somewhere between this room and the world, it had been swallowed. Not lost — *removed*. Smoothed over, like the missing year in the Meridian's manifest, like the two struck-through names.

The colony had called for help. Earth had been told. And Earth — or someone speaking for Earth — had decided that the answer was silence.

Yara walked upstairs. She didn't remember deciding to; her legs simply took her, up the three flights, past the germination decks, into the atrium where the great glass wall looked out over the frozen Norwegian dark. She stood there a long time, watching snow move sideways across the floodlights.

Four hundred days. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. The Meridian had returned — had come down out of the sky with its impossibly young, impossibly charming crew — four hundred and some days *after* the colony screamed into the dark. Four hundred days in which someone had known. In which someone had held the message in their hands, this same thin thermal paper, and filed it in a box in a basement, and said nothing.

She thought of Tomas. She thought of him the way she tried not to, all at once and without armor: Tomas at nine, burying a time capsule in the yard and making her swear to protect it. Tomas at nineteen, waving from the departure shuttle with his palm pressed to the glass. Tomas at thirty-one, in the last clear transmission, holding up a tray of seedlings he'd grown from the varieties she'd shipped him, grinning like an idiot, saying, *They took, Yara. Everything took. You should see it here.*

Someone had known they were dying. Someone had let them.

* * *

She gave herself the night. That was discipline, not softness. She made dinner — lentils and the last of the real onions, hoarded like treasure — and she ate it slowly, and she let herself cry in the way she'd learned to, quietly and on a schedule, grief rationed like water on a long voyage. Then she washed her bowl, and she went back down into the cold, and she began to build a case.

Because that's what it was now. Not a suspicion, not a dread she carried around like a stone in her coat pocket. A case. And cases had structure. Cases had method. She was a scientist and a steward, and stewardship, it turned out, was not only for seeds.

She cleared the reading table and laid out her materials the way she laid out a germination trial: controls and variables, everything labeled, everything dated. The paper relay logs. The Meridian's official manifest. Her own air-gapped archives. A legal pad — actual paper, actual pen, because she was done trusting anything that could be edited from a distance — on which she wrote, in careful block letters at the top:

*WHAT HAPPENED TO KEPLER-44, AND WHO DECIDED THE WORLD WOULDN'T HEAR ABOUT IT.*

Underneath, she built the skeleton.

One: the colony transmitted a Code Red distress call on 14 March 2074. Confirmed by vault relay hardcopy. Absent from all networked records. *Therefore: deliberate removal, with access to relay archives.*

Two: the Meridian's crew manifest originally listed forty-four; the official version lists forty-two; the two removed — Osei and Ferreira — appear in colony records as colony personnel. *Therefore: the crew and the colony overlapped in ways the official record denies.*

Three: the Meridian's own records contain a smooth gap covering the colony's final year — a gap too clean, too plausible, the archival equivalent of a lawn with no weeds. *Therefore: edited by someone with time, skill, and motive.*

Four: the crew came back young. She wrote that one and stared at it. It was the strangest item, the one that made the whole list look like madness, and she made herself write it anyway, because a case that ignored its own strangest evidence wasn't a case, it was a comfort. *Whatever they did out there, it cost something. And the something is missing from every record on Earth except mine.*

She sat back and looked at the skeleton of it, and felt the thing she always felt when a trial came up strong and true: a terrible, exhilarating clarity. The trays upstairs had taught her this too. You could coax and hope and pray, but at some point the seed either had a living embryo in it or it didn't, and no amount of wishing changed the answer. The truth was like that. It was *viable* or it wasn't. And this truth had a living core. She could feel it under the husk.

The problem — she wrote this at the bottom of the page, underlined twice — was that viability meant nothing if you never got it into the ground. Evidence in a basement was a seed in a vault. It kept. It did not grow. To grow, it needed soil, light, witnesses, *reach*. And Commander Ilsa Rehn, who smiled like sunlight and whose crew did not age, currently owned every channel through which truth could travel.

Yara capped her pen. Her hands were stiff with cold and her eyes burned and somewhere above her, four hundred thousand seedlings slept in their trays, waiting for a spring she was no longer entirely sure the world deserved.

She was going to need help. Not many people — one or two, chosen with the care she gave to everything she planted. And she had an idea where to start: the young man with the old eyes who kept wandering her grounds like a man looking for a house he'd lived in before it burned.

She turned off the light, and in the dark of the archive, the paper records settled around her like a sleeping congregation, holding their breath, holding the dead's last words, waiting — the way seeds wait, the way she waited — for someone to finally give them water.

Tomorrow, she thought, climbing the stairs into the cold blue morning, I start planting.
