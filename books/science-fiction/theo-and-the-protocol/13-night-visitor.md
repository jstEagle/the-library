## 13. Night Visitor

Theo told himself it was insomnia.

That was the official story, anyway — the one he'd filed away in the part of his mind that still did paperwork. Insomnia explained why he lay awake in his bunk aboard the Meridian at 0300 hours, staring at a ceiling that hummed with recycled air. Insomnia explained why he kept dreaming of a corridor. Insomnia even explained, sort of, why he'd started walking in his sleep, because Dr. Vashti had said somnambulism was a documented side effect of neural recalibration, and Dr. Vashti had said it in the calm, rehearsed voice they all used now, the voice of people reciting answers they'd been given.

But insomnia did not explain the plants.

Three nights ago he had woken standing in the Meridian's hydroponics bay, barefoot, holding a basil seedling in both hands like something rescued from a fire. He hadn't remembered getting there. He hadn't remembered deciding to go. He only remembered the feeling — urgent, specific, *personal* — as if somewhere inside him a small stubborn gardener had seized the controls and marched him across the ship to save a plant that was in no danger whatsoever.

The gardener had excellent taste in herbs, at least. Theo had repotted the basil, watered it, and stood there for twenty minutes breathing in the green smell while his heart hammered like he'd run from something.

Tonight, the gardener wanted to go outside.

* * *

He didn't sneak off the Meridian so much as fail to be noticed leaving it. That was the strange thing about being regressed: everyone looked at him now the way you look at a convalescent, kindly and without seeing. The night watch nodded at him as he passed through the airlock corridor. "Can't sleep, Voss?" one of them asked, and Theo said, "No," which was true, and the guard smiled sympathetically and went back to his screen, apparently satisfied that a man wandering out into the dark toward an isolated facility was engaged in normal sleepless behavior.

Maybe it was. Maybe sleeplessness was contagious on this ship. Half the crew had shadows under their eyes lately, though none of them would say what they were dreaming about. When Theo had tried once — casually, over dinner, the way you ask about weather — the table had gone quiet in a way that wasn't silence exactly but more like a held breath, and Commander Rehn had touched his wrist and said, "Give it time, Theo. The mind is a garden. You can't rush the growing."

*A garden.* Everyone used garden metaphors around here lately, and Theo was beginning to suspect that was not a coincidence.

The vault sat two kilometers from the landing field, a low white arc against the dark hills, its windows lit the color of old honey. Theo walked the whole way without deciding to. His feet knew the route. That was the part that frightened him most, when he let himself think about it — not the dreams, not the gaps, but the competence of his body in places his mind had never been. His body remembered things. His body was keeping secrets from him, and it was a terrible collaborator, because it gave nothing away except at odd moments: a flinch at the sound of a pressure seal, a nausea that rose whenever he smelled ozone, and now this — a pull, gentle and gravitational, toward a building full of seeds.

The outer door was unlocked. Of course it was. Yara Almeida lived alone at the end of the world; she locked her seed cabinets and left her doors open, which told you everything about her priorities, and Theo found he liked her priorities very much.

She was awake, of course. She was sitting at a workbench under a lamp, sorting something tiny and brown into labeled envelopes, and when the door opened she looked up without surprise, the way you look up at weather.

"You're the one who's been walking past my perimeter sensors at night," she said.

"I— am I?"

"Twice this week. You get to the fence line, stand there for six or seven minutes, and then walk back to your ship." She set down her tweezers. "I assumed you were casing the place, but you seemed too apologetic about it. Even the way you stand is sorry."

"That's... accurate," Theo admitted. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be curious. It's a better use of the same energy." She studied him for a moment, and then, instead of asking why he was there — which he could not have answered — she jerked her chin toward a second chair. "Sit down. Do you want tea? I have real chamomile. I grew it. It's the only luxury in this entire facility and I will defend it with my life."

"Yes please," said Theo, who had not known until that moment how badly he wanted someone to offer him tea.

* * *

Her name was Yara Almeida, and she talked the way nobody on the Meridian talked — in digressions, in tangents, in warm looping circles that always came back around to seeds. While the tea steeped she gave him a tour of the workroom that was less a tour than a roll call. "This tray is sorghum, drought-line nine, very proud of her. These are potatoes that can grow in regolith if you insult them enough first. This one—" she paused at a small pot on the windowsill, a straggly tomato plant tied to a chopstick with red thread, "—this one is sentimental. Don't judge it."

"Who's it from?"

"My brother." She said it lightly, the way you set down something heavy with good technique. "He went to Kepler-44 eleven years ago. He sent me these seeds from the colony greenhouse before comms got... quiet." Her hands kept moving as she spoke, straightening labels that were already straight. "People assume a seed vault is about the past. It isn't. It's entirely about the future. Everything in here is a bet that somebody, someday, will want to grow something."

"And you're the one holding the bets."

"Somebody has to hold them," Yara said. "Most days I don't mind. Some days the silence gets loud." Then she looked at him directly, and her eyes were sharp and kind at once, which was a combination Theo had forgotten existed. "You didn't come two kilometers in the dark for my potatoes, Mr. Voss. Though you're welcome to some. Why are you here?"

And Theo opened his mouth to give the answer he'd been constructing all the way down the hill — *couldn't sleep, needed air, saw your lights* — and what came out instead was:

"I dreamed about your door."

Yara went still.

"Not this door specifically. A door. A habitat door. In the dream I'm standing next to it and there's a control panel and my hand is on the control panel and—" His throat closed. He pushed through it, because he had carried this alone for three nights and it was starting to corrode him. "Someone is on the other side of the door. In the dream I know them. And they're saying my name. Not *Theo*. My other name. My service name. And I wake up and I don't know what it is, and I've checked my own file twice, and my file just says *Voss, T., regressed, see attached*, and there is no attachment, and everyone on that ship smiles at me like I asked them about the weather." He heard his own voice crack and hated it. "I'm sorry. You don't know me. This is a lot of tea-adjacent trauma for one evening."

Yara was quiet for a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the hills, and somewhere in the vault's deep stacks a humidity system cycled on with a soft sigh, ten thousand seeds exhaling in their sleep.

"Theo," she said finally. "Do you know what I do every day? I send a maintenance report to a colony that stopped answering years ago. Every day. Into silence." She turned her chair to face him fully. "You learn things about silence, doing that. One thing you learn is that silence isn't empty. Silence is information someone is choosing not to give you." She tapped the table once, decisively. "Your crew is giving you silence. Your own head is giving you silence. But you're right about one thing — bodies keep records. Bodies are terrible liars and excellent archivists. So." She stood up. "Let's ask yours."

"Ask it what?"

"What it remembers." She was already moving toward the back of the workroom, past the germination trays, to a door marked ARCHIVE — LEGACY. "Fair warning: this may be unpleasant. Also fair warning: I have a bucket."

"...A bucket?"

"I'm a professional. Professionals prepare."

* * *

The legacy archive room was cold and dim and smelled of paper and dust and, faintly, of growing things — the smell of the whole vault seeping through the walls, green life pressing patiently against stored death. Yara woke a terminal older than either of them, its screen flickering up in amber characters.

"This array predates network synchronization," she said. "Everything in here came in raw, on the old bands, before anyone decided all records should live in one convenient editable place. Nobody bothers hacking systems like this. There's no glory in it. Which means nobody bothered to lie to it, either." Her fingers moved across the keys with the tenderness of someone greeting an old friend. "Kepler-44 transmitted on these frequencies for the first three years after settlement. Then they upgraded, synced up, joined the modern network. Standard practice. Efficient." Her mouth twisted. "Efficient is a dangerous word."

She pulled up a directory. Dates scrolled past. And then she stopped scrolling, and Theo watched her shoulders change — watched something settle in her, grim and ready, like a person picking up a weight they'd been training for.

"There's one transmission on the legacy band that came *after* the upgrade," she said quietly. "One. Four hundred days before your ship returned. It shouldn't exist. Someone forgot it, or couldn't reach it, or thought no one was listening anymore." She turned to look at him. "It's flagged as a distress call, Theo. Priority override. Full power. It came from the colony's primary transmitter, and it ran for ninety seconds before it cut off."

"Have you played it?"

"No." For the first time since he'd met her, Yara Almeida looked afraid. "Eleven years I've sat next to this file. I tell myself I haven't played it because I'm preserving evidence integrity. That's a lie. I haven't played it because my brother might be on it, and as long as I don't press play, he's still maybe alive somewhere in the not-knowing." She exhaled slowly. "But you came to my door tonight because your body is trying to hand you a delivery you won't sign for. And I think — I think we should sign for it together. Whatever it is."

Theo's mouth had gone dry. Somewhere below thought, below memory, below the smooth blank surface where his past used to be, something had begun to move — a pressure, a rising, the way water rises in a pipe before you know why.

"Play it," he said.

Yara pressed play.

Static first — the long breathless static of deep space — and then a voice, ragged, human, shouting over alarms:

*"—anyone on legacy band, this is Kepler-44 Primary, this is NOT a drill — they're in the habitat ring, they're cutting through the inner seals, they're taking people to the med deck — God, they're taking them to the med deck — if anyone receives this, the ship is the Meridian, I repeat, the vessel is the MERIDIAN, commander Rehn, they came for tissue, they're harvesting—"*

The voice broke into coughing. In the background, behind the words, there were other sounds. Theo's body understood those sounds before his mind did. His stomach understood them. His hands understood them — they had clenched into fists on his knees without consulting him.

*"—someone sealed section C, whoever did it, I know you could hear us, I know you could HEAR US—"*

Pounding. Faint, through the recording, through walls and distance and eleven years: fists on metal. A chorus of them. Rhythmic. Desperate. Diminishing.

The voice came back one last time, smaller now:

*"My name is Miguel Almeida. If my sister is alive, tell her the tomatoes made it. Tell her I kept them alive. Tell her—"*

The transmission ended. Static filled the room like water filling a lung.

*Almeida.*

Theo turned. Yara was sitting perfectly still, both hands flat on the table, her face composed into a calm so complete it was clearly costing her everything she had. She was going to be fine, her posture said. She was a professional. Professionals prepare.

Then Theo vomited.

Not gracefully, not partially — wholly, violently, into the bucket she had somehow already placed beside him, his whole body convulsing around a knowledge that had arrived in his gut before his brain could intercept it. Because he knew that pounding. He knew the rhythm of it. He had heard it not through a recording but through a door, through metal, through his own palms pressed flat against the wrong side of it, and underneath the pounding a voice had been screaming his service name — *Voss! VOSS! Open the door, Voss!* — and his hand had been on the control panel, and the panel had been glowing amber, and he had—

"I sealed it," he said, when he could speak. His voice came out in pieces. "Section C. I sealed Section C. They ordered me to and I — I remember the order, I remember the *tone* of the order, I remember—" He gripped the edge of the table. "Yara. I sealed the door. I sealed the door and I could hear them."

Yara moved. He braced for her to move away from him — he deserved to be moved away from — but instead she knelt beside the chair, took his shaking hands in her steady ones, and held them the way you hold something germinating: carefully, with both hands, believing in what it might become.

"Look at me," she said. "Theo. Look at me." He did. Her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely unafraid. "They took your memory because your memory is evidence. Do you understand? Everything they erased, they erased because it convicts them. Which means every piece you get back is a weapon." She squeezed his hands. "You didn't come here tonight by accident. Nothing grows by accident. Something in you has been trying to get back to the truth the way a root tries to get back to water."

"They'll re-treat me," Theo whispered. "If they find out I'm remembering. Amara already — I think they're going to do something to Amara—"

"Then we don't let them find out," Yara said. "Not yet. You walk back to that ship, you smile their rehearsed smiles, you sleepwalk beautifully. And you come here. Every night your feet try to bring you here, you let them. We'll rebuild it together — piece by piece, packet by packet. I have archives they've never touched. You have a body that keeps better records than they do." She almost smiled, fierce and cracked. "Between the two of us, we're a complete witness."

Outside, the wind picked up, rattling the vault's ancient eaves. On the windowsill, Miguel Almeida's tomato plant leaned against its chopstick, alive, absurd, patient.

Theo stood on legs that felt borrowed. At the door he stopped, because there was one more thing, and it wouldn't let him leave unsaid.

"The tomatoes," he said. "In the recording. He said to tell you they made it."

Yara closed her eyes. When she opened them again, the grief was still there, but it had company now — something harder, something planted.

"I know," she said. "I heard him." She rolled up her sleeves. "Now sit back down, Theo. We have four hundred days of history to reconstruct, and I make excellent tea, and neither of us is sleeping tonight anyway."

He sat back down. And far above them, in orbit, the Meridian turned silently through the dark — carrying, in its young and careful crew, the last people alive who knew exactly what the silence was hiding.
