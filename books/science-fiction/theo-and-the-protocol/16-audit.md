## 16. Audit

They came on a Tuesday, which Yara found insulting. Tuesdays were germination-check days. In eleven years she had never missed one, and she did not intend to start for a warrant.

The transport set down in the east meadow at 0700 local, kicking up a fan of dust that settled over her cold frames like an accusation. Six people climbed out. Four wore the slate-gray uniforms of the Reconciliation Authority — a name that had sounded benign when Rehn coined it and now sounded like a threat read in a soothing voice. The other two wore civilian clothes that were trying very hard not to look like uniforms: identical charcoal jackets, identical sensible boots, identical expressions of professional regret.

Yara was already outside by the time they reached the gate, secateurs in one hand and a tray of drought-stressed sorghum seedlings in the other, because if you were going to be audited, you were going to be audited mid-task, like a person with nothing to hide and a great deal of watering to do.

"Dr. Almeida." The lead auditor was a compact woman with gray-shot hair and a lanyard heavy enough to qualify as a weapon. Her badge said MERCER, T., DIRECTOR OF RECORDS COMPLIANCE. "Thank you for receiving us."

"You didn't ask," Yara said pleasantly.

"We're asking now." Mercer produced a tablet and turned it toward her with the ceremony of someone unveiling a portrait. "Warrant issued under the Records Integrity Act, countersigned by the Office of the Commander. We're here to inventory and secure all networked archival systems pending reconciliation between your holdings and the Meridian's official manifest."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we inventory them anyway, and you get to be difficult about it in front of witnesses." Mercer's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I've read your file, Dr. Almeida. Eleven years alone. I'm told you talk to the plants. I'd rather you talked to me."

"I talk to the plants because they don't interrupt," Yara said. "But fine. Come in. Wipe your boots. The vault has standards."

* * *

Inside, the audit took on the rhythm of a slow-motion burglary.

They fanned out through the archive levels in pairs, tagging cables, photographing server racks, running their handheld sniffers along every conduit like hounds at a fox trial. One of them — young, earnest, with the haunted look of someone who had joined a records agency expecting paperwork and gotten soldiers instead — kept apologizing each time he unplugged something.

"Sorry, ma'am. Sorry. This line goes dead for about ninety seconds while we image it."

"That's the irrigation controller," Yara said. "If it's dead longer than two minutes, the misting cycle fails and four hundred accessions start dying. But please, take your time."

He imaged it in forty seconds flat.

Yara made tea. It was, she had decided somewhere around the second hour, her primary weapon. She carried a battered thermos and a stack of ceramic cups — real ceramic, chipped, ancient — into every room where they worked, and she poured without asking, narrating as she went.

"This is Assam. Grown from my own stock, fourth generation. You won't taste anything like it off-world; the colony tried to replicate the cultivar and got something that tastes like regret and copper." She watched the young auditor's face flicker at the word *colony* and filed that away. "Careful, it's hot. So is the room you're standing in, incidentally. Rack seven runs warm. Has done since '39. I keep meaning to complain to someone, but there was no one to complain to until recently, and now the someone who came brought a warrant, so perhaps I'll lodge it with you."

Mercer appeared in the doorway of the main server hall, hands clasped behind her back, surveying the racks with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"You run this whole operation yourself?"

"Me and the plants. They're better at redundancy."

"No staff? No assistant? No remote contractor who handles maintenance windows?"

"It's a seed vault, Director. The entire point is that nothing happens here quickly." Yara topped up her cup. "Though I confess the last few weeks have been lively."

Something moved behind Mercer's eyes — a recalculation, quick and cool. "About that. Your daily transmission logs show anomalies. Burst uploads at irregular hours. Traffic to legacy bands."

"Do they?" Yara sipped. "I do run diagnostics at night. The bandwidth's free then. Also, I sleep poorly. Eleven years of talking to yourself will do that."

"Mm." Mercer let the silence sit, then consulted her tablet. "We'll need the keys to the networked core. All of it. Per the warrant, the systems will remain sealed under Authority lockout pending reconciliation. You'll retain physical custody of the facility, but remote access is suspended."

"Of course," Yara said, and handed over the key fob with the serene cooperation of a woman handing someone a live grenade and waiting to see which end they held.

Because here was the thing, the beautiful, ridiculous thing that Yara had been quietly thanking whatever gods presided over bureaucratic oversight ever since the Meridian landed: the networked core was not the vault. The networked core was the *gift shop*. It held the curated archives, the public-facing catalogs, the tidy summaries designed decades ago for funders who wanted dashboards, not data. It was accurate. It was even complete, mostly. And it was exactly what anyone who had grown up in a synchronized world would assume constituted the whole of a records institution — because in their world, there was only ever one copy, and it lived on the network, and whoever held the network held the truth.

But the vault had been built by people who remembered what it was to lose everything. Its founders had been old enough to distrust networks the way survivors distrust ice. So beneath the networked core, behind a door labeled COOLING INFRASTRUCTURE — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY, sat the air-gapped heart: eleven years of raw logs, every packet the colony ever sent before the silence, every byte of Meridian telemetry Yara had pulled from the deep-space array, checksums going back to the founding, and the paper ledgers — actual paper, acid-free, handwritten — that predated sync entirely.

Rehn's people could hold the networked core until the sun burned out. Yara intended to let them.

What she could not let them do was find the rest. Which meant the rest needed to stop sitting still.

* * *

The copying began that same afternoon, under cover of routine.

The vault's oldest trays used microfilm accession tags — a legacy system from the founding era, when the designers had decided that the only storage medium guaranteed to outlast every war,EMP, and format change was film and paper sealed in foil. Yara still had the tag printer. It lived in a corner of the workroom, humming to itself, and nobody had looked twice at it in years, least of all her, until three nights ago when she'd realized that a machine built to print tiny images onto tiny strips of film was also, functionally, a machine built to make evidence disappear into a garden.

She worked in the evenings, after the auditors withdrew to their transport, and again in the grey hours before dawn, when the corridor cameras — newly installed, newly networked, newly *theirs* — swept on a cycle she had already memorized. Ninety seconds of blind hallway. Enough.

The process was absurdly simple, which was its genius. She fed documents into the microfilm camera: the checksum manifests, the distress-call headers, the orbital tracking tables showing the Meridian's true arrival window, page after page of the paper ledger entries that proved her archive had never once synced with anything. Each strip of film went into a standard accession sleeve. Each sleeve got a ciphered number from the vault's own cataloging scheme — numbers so mundane, so deeply boring, that no auditor alive would pull them. TR-4471-B, *Triticum aestivum*, landrace, provenance: donor batch 12. Inside the foil, wrapped around the seed vial like a secret tucked into a coat lining, lay the truth about Kepler-44.

By the third night she had hidden the distress call among the barley. By the fifth, the Meridian's falsified manifest comparison lived in the amaranth. She caught herself murmuring to the trays as she worked — old habit — but the murmurings had changed. *Hold this for me,* she told a row of wheat. *Just for a little while. You've kept stranger things safe.*

On the sixth night, the young auditor found her in the workroom at midnight.

She heard his footsteps and had time to sweep the last film strip into her sleeve before he rounded the racks, holding two cups of the tea she'd given him days ago, refilled, steaming.

"You're up late, ma'am."

"Plants don't respect business hours." She nodded at the cups. "You came all the way back here to bring me tea?"

"You looked like you hadn't slept." He set hers down on the bench, careful not to disturb the seed trays. His badge said OKONKWO, D., JUNIOR COMPLIANCE OFFICER. Up close he couldn't have been more than twenty-five, with the particular exhaustion of someone doing a job whose shape didn't match the job description. "Can I ask you something? Off the record?"

"Nothing in this building is off the record anymore," Yara said. "But ask anyway."

"Why do you keep all this?" He gestured vaguely at the racks, the trays, the whole patient green dark of the place. "Eleven years. No audience. The colony stopped answering before I finished school. Everyone says it's — " he hesitated — "it's a memorial job. Custody of a grave. Why not just... maintain the minimum and go live somewhere with people?"

Yara considered him for a long moment. Then she picked up a tray of wheat seedlings — the ordinary kind, nothing hidden in it — and held it between them.

"Because a grave is for something that's finished," she said. "This isn't finished. Every one of these seeds is a sentence that hasn't been said yet. Wheat doesn't know it's forgotten. It just waits for conditions to change." She set the tray down. "Someone has to be here when the conditions change, Daniel. That's the whole job. Everything else is watering."

He looked at the seedlings for a while. When he spoke again, his voice was lower. "Director Mercer's report is due Friday. There's a section on anomalous activity. Unlogged access to the microfilm suite, after-hours movement in the archive corridors." He wasn't looking at her. "I have to include what I observed."

"Of course you do," Yara said calmly, though her pulse had gone loud in her ears. "And what did you observe?"

He met her eyes then, and for a moment he looked much older than twenty-five, older than the whole regressed crew of the Meridian put together, because he had earned his age honestly. "I observed the caretaker checking her germination trays. At night. Because plants don't respect business hours." He picked up his tea. "Goodnight, Dr. Almeida."

He was gone before she trusted her voice to thank him.

* * *

Friday morning, Mercer delivered the findings in the vault's front office, standing, formal, tablet in hand.

"The audit is concluded. Your networked systems are sealed under Authority lockout effective immediately. Remote access suspended. Any attempt to circumvent the seal will constitute obstruction under the Records Integrity Act." She paused. "Otherwise, the facility is in remarkable order. Frankly, better order than most institutions with full staff. Whatever else happens, Dr. Almeida, that's to your credit."

"You're too kind," Yara said. "Will I get a copy of the report?"

"You'll get a summary. Full findings go to the Office of the Commander." Mercer gathered her people with a tilt of her head, and they filed out past the seedling benches, past the tea things Yara had left out as a parting courtesy, past the rows of trays with their small white tags fluttering faintly in the ventilation draft — thousands of them, ordinary and extraordinary, wheat and barley and amaranth, and folded inside their foil sleeves, quiet as spores, the whole unedited history of what humanity had done to itself out among the stars.

At the door, Mercer stopped. Turned half around.

"One more thing. A personal observation, not in the report." Her eyes were sharp and unreadable. "You cooperated very easily, Dr. Almeida. People who hide things usually fight harder."

"People who hide things," Yara said, smiling as she handed over the last signed receipt, "usually have something worth hiding. I have seeds. Ask anyone."

Mercer held her gaze a beat too long — long enough that Yara understood the woman suspected exactly as much as she could prove, which was nothing — and then walked out into the dust and the morning light, and the transport lifted, and the meadow settled back into silence.

Yara stood in the doorway until the sound faded. Then she locked the front door, walked down to the workroom, and pulled the first foil sleeve from the wheat tray, checking it with the tenderness other people reserved for sleeping children. Intact. Legible. Safe.

Her networked core sat upstairs under an Authority seal, dumb and obedient, broadcasting nothing. It didn't matter. The vault had never lived in the servers. It lived here, in the cool dark, in foil and paper and living tissue, in the one archive on Earth that had spent eleven years learning how to survive being ignored.

She updated the log by hand, in ink, in the ledger that no warrant had touched:

*Day 4,113. Audit complete. Systems seized. Nothing lost.*

Then she turned to the window, where the eastern sky was doing something enormous and orange above the meadow, and allowed herself, for the first time in weeks, a single unguarded thought:

Sooner or later, Rehn would come for the air-gapped core too. And next time, Yara would not be playing caretaker.

She checked the misting cycle, whispered goodnight to four hundred accessions, and began, quietly, to plan.
