## 19. Rehn's Version

Ilsa Rehn had learned long ago that the truth was a perishable commodity. It spoiled in the open air. It curdled under scrutiny. But a well-made story — a story with grief in it, and sacrifice, and a cure — that kept for years. That could be canned and shipped to every corner of the system and opened on holidays.

She stood in the broadcast studio on the top floor of the Meridian's temporary command annex — formerly the Ministry of Planetary Logistics, formerly the Ministry of Agriculture, formerly, if you went back far enough, a granary, which Ilsa found poetically satisfying — and let the makeup artist dab the last of the fatigue shading under her eyes.

"Less," she said. "Leave some. People don't trust a commander who looks rested."

The artist, a nervous young man named Dev who had joined her staff three weeks ago and was already indispensable, tilted his brush. "You've been through something," he said, half to himself, working the shading into the hollows beneath her cheekbones. "That's what they need to see."

"That's exactly what they need to see." She smiled at him in the mirror, and he flushed and looked down, and she thought: twenty-two years old, this one. He would have been eleven when the Meridian launched. He was the future, and the future was going to love her, because she was going to give it everything it wanted and take only what it wouldn't miss.

Dev stepped back. On the monitor, the woman looking out at her was fifty-three years old and carried every one of them honestly: the gray coming in at the temples, the lines around the mouth, the faint tremor of exhaustion in the hands she'd fold on the desk. A woman who had gone into the dark for humanity and come back older than sin.

It was, Ilsa reflected, the single greatest special effect of her career. The Meridian had left with a crew whose average age was sixty-one. The crew standing before the cameras today averaged thirty-four. The official explanation — cellular regeneration achieved through the experimental Voss-Adler Protocol, at terrible cost to the crew's own bodies — was true in every particular except the ones that mattered. The protocol was real. The cost was real. The bodies it consumed were simply not their own.

"Two minutes, Commander," said Okonkwo from the door. Her comms chief, solid as furniture, holding a tablet with the rundown. "Feed goes to all terrestrial networks, all orbital relays, and the four settlement bands that still answer. Kepler relay is dark, obviously."

"Obviously," Ilsa said, and did not flinch, because flinching was a habit she had burned out of herself decades ago, along with most of the others. "And the vault?"

Okonkwo's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "Agricultural array remains outside our authority. Per the caretaker's... interpretation of her charter."

"The caretaker," Ilsa said warmly, "is a treasure. We'll get to her."

* * *

The studio lights came up like a small sunrise, and Ilsa Rehn began to speak.

She did not begin with the colony. That was the first rule, the one she'd learned giving eulogies early in her career: never open with the dead. Open with the living. Open with the faces in the room.

"People of Earth. People of the stations, the settlements, the ships still moving between us. My name is Ilsa Rehn, and eleven years ago I made you a promise."

Behind her, the screen bloomed with footage: the Meridian's departure, the crowds at the launch gantry, the banners. Children waving. She'd chosen the shot carefully — there was a girl in the front row, maybe seven, missing a tooth, holding a paper rocket above her head. That girl was now eighteen. Ilsa wondered idly where she was. Watching, probably. They were all watching.

"I promised we would find a way to save you from the slow catastrophe of aging. From the diseases that take your parents while you're still learning who they are. I promised we would go into the dark and bring back fire." She paused, and let the pause do its work. "We have kept that promise. And we paid for it."

Now the colony. Now the dead.

She told them about Kepler-44. She told it beautifully, because she had written it beautifully, over six drafts, cutting every sentence that raised a question and keeping every sentence that closed one. An engineered blight — agricultural in origin, weaponized by accident or by malice, the forensic teams were still determining which, though she knew which story tested better with focus groups and had already decided. A colony of nine hundred souls. A greenhouse district where the pathogen had first taken hold. Quarantine measures that failed. A final transmission, fragmentary, heartbreaking, which she played at low volume behind her voice so that the audience heard grief literally layered into the words.

What she did not tell them: that the blight samples in the Meridian's hold had been collected, not fought. That the quarantine measures had been imposed from outside. That the final transmission had been cut off mid-sentence by someone aboard her own ship.

"We arrived too late to save them," Ilsa said, and her voice cracked on the word *late*, precisely on schedule, because she had practiced the crack in a mirror until it stopped feeling like lying and started feeling like craft. "But we arrived in time to learn from them. Their sacrifice gave us the key. The Voss-Adler Protocol works. It works because nine hundred people on a world none of you will ever see refused to die for nothing."

Behind her, the screen changed: the crew. Standing in formation, young-faced, solemn. She watched the feed's picture-in-picture audience reaction — the public channel ran sentiment analysis in real time, a little graph climbing in the corner like a pulse — and saw the numbers spike when the camera found Dr. Amara Osei, who stood very straight and looked, God help her, genuinely stricken, which read to the audience as grief and was, in fact, grief, just not about what anyone thought.

"These are the people who carried your cure home," Ilsa said. "Some of them gave more than others. Some of them gave things I cannot yet explain publicly, because the science must be verified before it is shared. You have my word — my personal word — that when it is ready, no one will be denied. Not the wealthy. Not the connected. Everyone."

This was the sentence that would run in every headline, and it was also, she noted with professional admiration for her own work, the sentence that bought her the communications authority vote in the Assembly next week. Generosity first. Terms later. Terms buried so deep in warmth that by the time anyone dug for them, the ground would already be paved.

"To honor Kepler-44," she concluded, "we propose the Rehn Memorial Initiative: a research settlement, a medical archive, and an annual day of remembrance. Let their deaths mean life. Let their silence teach us to speak to each other better than we have."

She held the last frame — the crew, the flag, the soft-focus image of a greenhouse on a distant world — for a full five seconds before the lights went down.

In the dark, Okonkwo said quietly: "Sentiment index at ninety-one percent. That's a record. The old record was the moon landing anniversary."

"Ninety-one," Ilsa repeated. She exhaled slowly. Somewhere in her chest, beneath the satisfaction, there was a small cold weight that had lived there for eleven years and would live there forever, and she had made her peace with it the way you make peace with a stone in your boot: you stop walking, or you stop noticing. She had stopped noticing. Mostly.

"Get me the Assembly whips," she said. "And Dev — tomorrow, more of the gray at the temples. We're building trust, not a calendar."

* * *

The after-broadcast reception was held in the annex atrium, under the great glass roof, among plants that someone had imported at obscene expense to make the place feel alive. Senators and directors moved through the crowd with the particular hunger of people who had just watched ninety-one percent of the public fall in love and wanted some of it transferred by proximity.

Ilsa worked the room the way she did everything: efficiently, warmly, and with total attention. She remembered names. She remembered daughters' birthdays and sons' university applications. She asked Senator Vasquez about her hip and recommended, gently, that certain treatments might become available sooner rather than later, and watched Vasquez's eyes go bright with hope, and thought: *there*. Another thread in the web. Not cruelty — never cruelty first. Cruelty was a tool of last resort, crude and memorable. Favors were quiet. Favors compounded. A man who owed you his mother's extra decade would forgive you almost anything, and would hate himself for forgiving it, and would therefore need you even more.

"You were wonderful," breathed Undersecretary Hale, gripping her hand with both of his. "When you talked about the final transmission — I confess I wept."

"It was a hard thing to say," Ilsa agreed, "and a harder thing to have heard the first time."

All technically true. She was careful to build her lies entirely out of true materials. It was why they held.

She was on her third glass of water — she drank water at receptions; it read as discipline — when Okonkwo appeared at her elbow with the particular set of his shoulders that meant a problem he didn't want to say aloud in front of senators.

"Commander. The monitoring desk flagged something during the broadcast."

"Flagged what?"

"Viewership. There's a node in the southern hemisphere — remote, low-priority, an agricultural telemetry cluster — that logged the full broadcast. Standard. But the caretaker at the seed vault has legacy receiving equipment on the same band. Her station shows the broadcast was received, decoded, and..." He hesitated. "Replayed. Fourteen times."

Ilsa turned her glass slowly on the table. Around them the atrium glittered, laughed, toasted the dead of Kepler-44.

"Fourteen times," she said pleasantly.

"Yes, Commander. And she filed her daily maintenance log forty minutes late. First time in — well, the desk says ever. Eleven years of logs, never late."

Ilsa smiled at a passing director, nodded, waited until he'd moved on. Then, softly, to Okonkwo: "Eleven years of perfect punctuality, and tonight she's late. What does that tell you?"

"That the broadcast upset her?"

"It tells me she noticed something." Ilsa set down the glass. In her mind she laid the caretaker beside the other loose threads — Osei's haunted face on the broadcast, Theo's questions, the regressed crew dreaming in their bunks like a row of unreliable safes — and felt, not fear exactly, but the old hunter's attention, cool and clarifying, the feeling of a pattern beginning to insist on itself. "Nobody replays a eulogy fourteen times unless they're checking it against something."

"Against what? She wasn't there. Nobody was there."

"Exactly," Ilsa said. "So what is she comparing it to?"

Okonkwo had no answer. Ilsa picked up her water, lifted it toward the room in a small toast nobody was meant to notice, and sipped.

"Auditors," she said. "Routine records audit, the vault's networked systems. Nothing dramatic. Paperwork. Be gentle — she's a national treasure."

"And if the audit finds nothing?"

"Then we'll know she keeps her real records somewhere else," Ilsa said, "and we'll know exactly where to look, because there's only one somewhere else on that entire compound."

She looked up through the glass roof at the stars, at the invisible point of light that was Kepler-44, nine hundred graves wrapped in vacuum, and she raised her glass a centimeter higher — to them, privately, sincerely, because whatever else she was, she was not careless with the dead. They had given her everything. The least she could do was make sure no one ever asked them why.

"Beautiful lie travels fast," she murmured, to no one, to the stars. "Let's keep it ahead of the truth."

* * *

Fourteen hundred kilometers south, in a vault carved into bedrock and smelling of soil and lamp-oil and patience, Yara Almeida sat alone in the archive room with the broadcast playing again — fifteenth time — at low volume.

She wasn't listening to the words anymore. She'd stopped hearing the words around replay six. She was watching the screen behind Rehn. The B-roll. The greenhouse footage, soft-focus, meant to evoke loss.

Yara had spent eleven years memorizing agricultural layouts from every off-world settlement's public filings, because knowing what grew where was the job. Kepler-44's greenhouse district, per the last pre-silence filings, had run north–south bays with a distinctive curved truss design — her brother had complained about it in a letter once, *the trusses leak condensation right onto the tomato rows, I swear they built the whole colony upside down.*

The greenhouse in Rehn's footage ran east–west. Straight trusses. Tomato rows bone-dry.

It was a stock render. A generic greenhouse, pulled from some old promotional file, dressed with grief and shown to ninety-one percent of a weeping world.

"They didn't even use the real one," Yara said aloud, to the trays, to the dark, to her brother. Her voice sounded strange in the empty room — she realized she hadn't spoken since morning.

She pulled her chair to the air-gapped terminal, woke it, and opened the folder she had begun two nights ago, the one labeled with a boring accession number that meant nothing to anyone but her. Inside: timestamps, manifests, the smooth impossible gap where a year should be.

Above her, through meters of rock, the beautiful lie was traveling at the speed of light, arriving everywhere at once, settling into memory like seed into soil.

Yara cracked her knuckles and began, line by line, to plant something else.
