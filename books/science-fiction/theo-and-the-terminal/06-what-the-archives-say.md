## 6. What the Archives Say

The *Long Answer* had a name before Isandro owned her. Some previous captain had painted it on the hull in letters that flaked like dead skin, and when he'd bought the ship out of the wreckage of his academic career he'd decided the joke was on him and kept it. A vessel named for a question nobody asked anymore.

Now he sat in the cargo bay's converted office — a repurposed shipping container bolted to the deck plating, one desk, one chair, one terminal wired into the ship's archival core — and asked the question that had been eating him since Sera Venn said *we are still here* in a language two centuries dead.

He started where any scholar would start. Not with the enclave. Not with the signal. With the record.

"Archival query," he said, and his voice sounded wrong in the small space, too loud, like a man talking to himself in an empty church. "Vessic. Species designation VS-114. Full historical file."

The terminal hummed. The fan in the corner clicked through its familiar stutter — a sound he'd slept beside for twelve years, as comforting as a heartbeat. Data scrolled up the screen in the flat gray typeface of the Meridian Standard Archive, the great shared library that every port, every station, every schoolchild's tablet drew from. He'd contributed to it once. Peer-reviewed entries on Vessic phonotactics, published under a younger man's name, back when the work had felt like raising the dead out of love instead of curiosity.

The file loaded.

**VS-114: VESSIC. STATUS: EXTINCT (CULTURAL ASSIMILATION). LAST VERIFIED POPULATION: 0 (2190).**

He read the line three times. It hadn't changed. Of course it hadn't changed. But somewhere beneath the Kestrel Verge, inside a hollowed moonlet behind a corporate exclusion fence, three hundred people breathed and spoke and sang in a tongue this line declared impossible.

"Okay," he murmured. "Let's see how you did it."

He pulled the relocation records first. 2187. That was the year everything official happened to the Vessic, the way everything official happens all at once, in tidy bundles, so no single document ever has to carry the whole weight of a lie.

The file was beautiful. That was the word that came to him, sitting there in the cold light of the screen, and the beauty of it made his stomach turn. **Joint Resettlement Accord, Vessic Territorial Cession, ratified 14 March 2187.** Signatories: the Vessic Concordance Council — nine names, each with a biometric seal, each verified authentic by three independent certification houses. Halcyon Combine, counterparty. Neutral arbiters from the Calloway Compact. Even a humanitarian observer's endorsement from an aid federation whose name he recognized from donation drives at every port he'd ever docked.

Every signature real. Every seal valid. Every stamp in its place.

That was the first thing he understood, and understanding it felt like swallowing ice water: they hadn't forged the paperwork. They hadn't needed to. Somewhere in the last months of the Vessic nation, nine people had put their hands on a table and signed away a world — whether bought, threatened, deceived, or simply outvoted by events, the document didn't say and didn't care. And once that document existed, everything downstream of it was technically true.

Halcyon hadn't erased the Vessic with lies. They'd erased them with accuracy.

Isandro leaned back until the chair creaked. His breath fogged faintly in the unheated container; he'd been rationing heat again, the way he always did in the long slow weeks between contracts, and only now noticed the cold.

He kept reading.

The resettlement manifests listed transport allocations: forty-one chartered haulers over fourteen months, moving a registered population of 61,204 Vessic nationals to "designated integration zones" in the Halcyon coreward territories. Destination codes attached. Arrival confirmations attached. He opened the arrival logs with the dull mechanical efficiency of a man checking a wound to see how deep it went.

Destination code HX-9: received 12,880 individuals. HX-11: received 9,447. HX-3, HX-17, HX-22, down the list, numbers flowing in neat columns, summing almost exactly — within a rounding error of four hundred souls — to the registered total.

Almost exactly.

His hand stopped on the touchpad.

Four hundred. In a population of sixty-one thousand, a discrepancy of four hundred was statistical noise. An auditor would flag it, an auditor would be told it was transcription drift between census methodologies, and the auditor would initial the flag closed and go to lunch. Isandro knew because he'd been trained by people who audited things. You learned to see which discrepancies mattered, and four hundred scattered across fourteen months and forty-one ships was designed not to matter.

But if you were counting not passengers but *people who arrived alive* —

He made himself do the arithmetic anyway, there in the cold, because someone had to. If the enclave held three hundred descendants of survivors, and survivor populations multiply slowly across two generations of hardship, then the founding number had to have been larger. Larger than three hundred. Larger, probably, than four hundred.

The discrepancy wasn't noise. The discrepancy was them. Or most of them. The ones who never boarded, or never landed, or landed somewhere no manifest pointed to — a rogue moonlet inside an exclusion zone, behind a fence built to keep everyone out and, more importantly, to keep the truth in.

"Population zero by 2190," he said aloud, testing the words. "Voluntary relocation, completed assimilation."

The archive agreed with him serenely. The archive agreed with everyone. That was the horror of it — not that the record lied, but that it didn't. Every fact in it could be defended in court. Signatures genuine. Transports logged. Arrivals confirmed. Assimilation documented in employment registries, tax rolls, linguistic surveys showing declining Vessic usage year over year until the final survey in 2189 recorded, with scholarly regret, zero fluent speakers remaining.

Zero fluent speakers. Filed by whom? He dug into the metadata. The 2189 linguistic survey — the one that killed the language on paper — had been conducted by the Halcyon Bureau of Cultural Integration. Author of record: a Dr. E. Malloway, Department of Applied Demographics.

He searched the name. Dr. E. Malloway had a respectable publication history, a retirement pension drawn from Halcyon subsidiary accounts, and a death notice dated eleven years ago, peaceful, aged ninety-one, survived by grandchildren. Whatever Malloway had actually done in 2189 — walked through camps with a clipboard while people hid their children's names, or sat in an office inventing zeros from whole cloth — the record would never say. The record said what it needed to say. The record was a machine, and it had done its job, and its operator had died comfortably.

Isandro realized his jaw ached and made himself unclench it.

* * *

He worked through the night cycle without deciding to. The ship's lights dimmed around him on their timer, and he didn't override them, and the terminal glow carved his face out of the dark. Coffee gone cold twice. Once, distantly, the nav AI pinged him about a scheduled course correction and he dismissed it with a gesture that wasn't quite a wave and wasn't quite a slap.

There was a craft to erasure, he was coming to see. He'd spent his career studying languages that died naturally — worn thin by trade, by shame, by the slow gravity of bigger tongues. Natural death left scars everywhere. Loanwords stranded in neighboring vocabularies like fossils. Place names that no longer matched anything. Songs whose meanings had rotted out but whose melodies persisted, hummed by people who'd forgotten why. Natural extinction was messy, and messiness was evidence. You could dig through the debris of a natural death forever.

This was different. This was a curated death, and the curation showed if you knew how to look — showed precisely because it was so thorough. He ran search after search and found the same pattern repeating with a perfection no organic process could produce:

No Vessic loanwords in the trade pidgins of the Kestrel Verge. None. A regional language spoken by sixty thousand people for centuries, and not one word had leaked into the surrounding linguistic ecosystem? He checked his own doctoral fieldwork notes, the ones gathering digital dust in a personal archive he hadn't opened in years. There — he'd recorded it himself, back then, and filed it as a curiosity: an odd gap in the local lexicon, a cluster of concepts — certain kinship terms, certain words for land and debt — that every neighboring culture expressed with borrowed or invented vocabulary, as if a hole had been cut in the fabric of the region's speech and patched over. He'd written a footnote about it. A *footnote*. Younger Isandro had noticed the absence of an entire people and given it four lines and moved on.

The place names were worse. He overlaid a modern chart of the Verge against a pre-2187 survey, and watched the old designations dissolve: Vessic-named stations renamed, Vessic-named craters renumbered, a moon called something that translated roughly as *the place where we wait for the boats* redesignated as a Halcyon mineral survey marker, KSM-441. Two hundred years of human memory, scraped and resurfaced like a hull. Children born in the Verge today grew up on charts with no ghosts in them at all.

And the dreams — he thought of the dreams, and had to set the coffee down. For twelve years he'd told himself the Vessic phonemes in his sleep were residue, scar tissue from overstudy, a mind that couldn't let go of its favorite toy. He'd treated it as pathology. Something broken in him.

But what if the language hadn't wanted to die? What if a tongue that lived in sixty thousand throats, that had been murdered rather than faded, kept surfacing in the one mind foolish enough to keep listening — knocking, knocking, knocking on the door of anyone who'd ever learned to hear it?

He shook the thought off. Mysticism was a luxury. He had documents.

* * *

Toward the end of the night cycle he found the thing that finally made him get up and pace the cargo bay, fists opening and closing, breath steaming.

It was in the financial records. Of course it was. He should have looked there first; he'd known enough disgraced academics turned freight jockeys to know that money was the one language nobody bothered to disguise.

Cross-referencing the resettlement timeline against Halcyon Combine's public quarterly filings — filings required by interstellar charter law, available to any citizen, read by no one — he built a simple graph. On one axis, the registered Vessic population, declining month by month toward zero. On the other axis, Halcyon's extraction revenue from the Kestrel Verge concessions, rising.

The curves crossed in November of 2187.

The month the population curve hit zero — the month the last Vessic, on paper, ceased to exist — Halcyon's isotope revenue from the Verge jumped forty percent. Not gradually. Not seasonally. A step function. As if a constraint had been removed. As if something that had been slowing the machines down had stopped slowing them.

He stared at the crossing point for a long time.

A living people with a documented claim to a world is a legal obstacle. Treaties, charters, consultation requirements, compensation frameworks — the entire tedious architecture of indigenous rights that the interstellar community had built, mostly on paper, mostly honored in footnotes, but built. Sixty thousand living Vessic meant sixty thousand claimants standing on top of the richest isotope deposits surveyed in a century. Sixty thousand signatures required, or lawsuits filed, or protests staged, or at minimum a decade of expensive delay.

Sixty thousand people relocated voluntarily, per a properly ratified accord, meant the ground stood empty. Clean title. Clear conveyance. The machines could run at full burn.

The revenue curve didn't prove murder. He knew that. He was a scholar; he knew the difference between correlation and cause, and he knew that any Halcyon lawyer could stand up in any court and say *relocation is expensive and disruptive, of course output dipped during transition, of course it recovered after* — and the graph would fold like wet paper.

But he also knew what he'd seen with his own eyes tonight: a lie assembled entirely from true statements, seamless as poured glass, with a revenue spike sitting at its center like a fly in amber. And he knew what Sera Venn's face had looked like when she spoke of the Purge — the way grief can live in a face so long it stops being an expression and becomes architecture.

He thought about the four hundred missing from the manifests. He thought about a black box somewhere in a gutted warship, if the enclave's rumors were true. He thought about how much force it takes to make sixty thousand people disappear quietly, and what kind of ship delivers that force, and whose signature authorizes it.

The anger came up in him slowly, which surprised him. He'd expected shock, maybe, or fear. Instead it was a cold, patient fury, the kind he'd never had use for as a young academic — the kind that doesn't want to shout, doesn't want to smash, just wants to *document*. Wants to sit down with the whole seamless glass lie and find the flaw line and tap it, once, precisely, and watch the whole fabrication come apart in his hands.

They had erased a language so cleanly that even its absence had been tidied away. They had killed a people and filed the corpse under *assimilation, voluntary.* And the machine that did it was still running, still filing, still producing flawless paper — he was willing to bet his ship that somewhere in Halcyon's archives, right now, a form was being filled out about a cargo hauler who'd answered a distress call he shouldn't have heard.

Because that was the other thing the records taught him, the thing that turned his anger sharp-edged and urgent: erasure on this scale isn't an event. It's a maintenance program. Someone, somewhere, was still minding the store. Still watching for loose threads. And he was a loose thread now — a man whose ship had deviated from its filed flight plan toward an exclusion zone, whose transponder had gone quiet near a moonlet that officially contained nothing worth noticing.

He looked at the terminal, at the elegant, murderous columns of data, and made a decision the way he used to make them in graduate seminars: out loud, to an empty room, committing himself before caution could catch up.

"I'm going to need your testimony," he said to the archive, to the dead, to the four hundred. "All of it. Everything they wrote down."

Because here was the flaw line he'd already spotted, the scholar's instinct firing even through exhaustion: a lie built from accurate documents has one fatal weakness. Accurate documents can be *contradicted by better ones*. Somewhere in those forty-one transport logs, in those destination codes, in the internal correspondence that must exist behind the public filings — somewhere, the seam existed. No conspiracy is perfect at scale. Sixty-one thousand people generate paperwork. Ships log their routes. Crews talk. Someone signed something they shouldn't have, someone kept a copy, someone's conscience or greed or carelessness left a thread hanging.

He just had to pull it.

He saved everything — triple-redundant, encrypted, copies seeded into three separate data caches aboard the ship in case the terminal or the ship or Isandro himself failed — and then sat for a moment in the dark with his hands laced behind his head, listening to the fan click, letting the fury settle into something he could carry.

Then the cabin comm chimed.

He startled badly — twelve years of solo hauling had tuned him to expect silence — and keyed it. Sera Venn's voice came through, level and dry, in accented Standard:

"You've been awake thirty hours, linguist. Our sensors show your ship's power draw spiking all night. Either you're decrypting something, or you're dying." A pause. "If you found what I think you found, you should come hear the rest of it. From someone who was almost there. My grandmother's memory is longer than your archive."

Isandro looked at the screen one last time — at the crossing curves, the vanished names, the beautiful seamless lie — and reached for his coat.

"Yeah," he said. "I think it's time somebody told me what the paper left out."
