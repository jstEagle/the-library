## 10. Old Wounds, New Allies

Doran Vess had not slept in the Council Chamber in thirty years, and he did not intend to start tonight.

The chamber itself was older than anyone alive — a hollowed bubble of ice and salvaged hull plating at the heart of the enclave, its ceiling strung with heat-lamps that had once warmed cargo bays on ships whose names no one remembered. Three hundred people lived beneath the moonlet's skin, and every decision that touched all three hundred was argued here, under the lamps, in the language the galaxy believed dead.

Tonight the benches were full. Word of the outsider had moved through the tunnels faster than water through a cracked cistern, and by the time the horn sounded, every adult in the enclave who could walk had found a reason to be present.

Doran stood at the head of the crescent of elders and let the noise wash over him. He was sixty-one years old, broad through the shoulders from twenty years hauling ice, and he wore his authority the way he wore everything — plainly, without ornament. His grandmother had survived the Purge in a lifepod with eleven others and a sealed jar of seed-grain. He had been raised on the arithmetic of survival: how many calories, how many liters of recycled air, how many days until the next supply run. He thought in those numbers still.

And the numbers said: one stranger was a risk. One stranger with a ship, a transponder, and a debt to some nameless creditor was a wound in the enclave's skin that would not close on its own.

"The vote is simple," he said, and the chamber quieted because his voice carried whether he wanted it to or not. "The human stays three nights while his ship recharges. Then he leaves. He takes nothing with him but what he came with. No recordings. No coordinates. Nothing."

"Nothing," someone echoed from the back, and it wasn't agreement. It was a question wearing agreement's clothes.

Sera Venn sat two rows up, hands folded in her lap, her spine straight as a drill shaft. She was seventy-four and looked fifty, the way the long-lived always did out here where gravity was a suggestion and food was engineered rather than grown. She had been the enclave's memory-keeper for longer than Doran had been its warden, and between them lay forty years of rivalry that had begun as philosophy and calcified into something closer to doctrine.

She did not speak yet. She was letting him hang himself, as she always did. Doran respected it and hated it in equal measure.

"The man is a linguist," Sera said at last, when the silence had stretched exactly as far as she wanted it to. Not standing. Never standing; she made others come to her. "He decoded a distress call in a language two hundred years extinct. Do you understand what that means, Doran? There are perhaps four hundred people alive who can read our script, and this stranger taught himself from fragments. From *ruins*."

"He taught himself enough to find us," Doran said. "That's the problem, not the miracle."

"He taught himself because the language called to him." A murmur ran the benches. Sera let it run, then cut it off with a lift of one finger. "You want to talk about risk? Let's talk about risk. Halcyon Combine holds an exclusion charter over this moonlet worth more than the gross product of Calloway freeport. They patrol it twice a year with survey drones. Our population has held steady at three hundred for a generation because we do not grow, we do not broadcast, we do not *exist*. And now a hauler with a working ear has heard us scream."

"That's my point—"

"It is the opposite of your point." Sera finally rose, small and unhurried, and the chamber bent toward her like grass toward light. "Your plan is that he forgets us. That he flies back into the Verge and says nothing to anyone, ever, out of the goodness of a stranger's heart. You've met humans, Doran. You were married to one."

A ripple of laughter, quickly smothered. Doran felt his jaw tighten. The marriage was old ground and she knew it, and she plowed it anyway when it suited her, because Sera Venn had never once in her life declined an advantage.

"My plan," he said carefully, "is that we survive. That has been the plan for two hundred years. It has worked."

"Has it?" Her voice didn't rise. It never needed to. "We are three hundred people living in a tomb we dug ourselves, eating scrap-grown protein and telling our children their grandmother's name is a security risk. My granddaughter asked me last month why we can't say 'Vessic' above Level Four. She's nine. She thought it was a swear word." Sera turned, slowly, to face the whole chamber. "Survival is not the same thing as life. I have watched this enclave confuse the two for seventy years, and I am tired."

Doran let the silence sit. He'd learned long ago that the worst thing you could do against Sera was fill her silences for her.

Then he spoke to the room instead of to her. "Here is what none of you are saying out loud. The man wants to dig up the *Meridian's Wrath*. He wants to pull records out of a Halcyon graveyard and wave them at courts that have never once ruled in favor of a people who officially don't exist. And when it goes wrong — not if, *when* — the trail leads here. To us. To the children Sera is so worried about. Halcyon erased us once with orbital guns. What do you think they use the second time?"

"They won't need guns," said a voice from the hardliner bench — Maret Osk, third-generation tunnel engineer, a woman who had lost two brothers to a cave-in and trusted nothing she couldn't brace with her own hands. "They'll need a ledger. They'll buy the port authorities around us, flag the moonlet, seal the exclusion zone tighter, and wait. We suffocate politely. That's how they'd do it. That's how they've *always* done it."

"And how long does polite suffocation last?" This from Jossan Ree, youngest member of the council, twenty-nine, who ran the hydroponics and had the restless eyes of someone who had never seen open sky. "Another century? Two? We're already down to three hundred. Every year we lose more to the outside — kids who take a supply run and don't come back because out there they can be *anyone*, and in here they're a secret. Doran, I love this rock. But it is a rock. It is not a future."

"It's the only future you have," Doran said, "if you throw it away chasing justice from people who sold the law before we were born."

"Not chasing justice." Sera again. "Chasing *evidence*. There's a difference, and the difference is the black box of the destroyer that burned our world. Firing orders. Ship logs. Names. If that box exists, then what happened to us stops being a story grandmothers tell and becomes a fact with a serial number. Facts are harder to bury than people. Halcyon proved that — they buried *us* easily enough."

Doran looked around the chamber. He knew these faces better than he knew his own hands. Maret Osk, arms crossed, jaw set. Old Pell, who remembered the drone sweeps of '61 and flinched still at engine harmonics. Jossan Ree leaning forward like a plant toward a lamp. And scattered through the benches, the young ones — Tey the archivist with her ever-present data-slate, and behind her a cluster of the under-thirties who had grown up reciting the Purge as liturgy and were hungry for it to become something else.

He counted, the way he counted everything. And he did not like the number he arrived at.

"One question," he said, loud enough to still the side conversations. "For everyone voting yes tonight. When the enforcers come — and they will come, because a stolen war-record is not a whisper, it's a siren — how many of you are prepared to fight? Not argue. Not hide. Fight. Because that's what a yes means. A yes spends blood we haven't spilled in two hundred years, and I want every hand raised in favor to know it's raising a weapon along with it."

Silence. The heat-lamps hummed. Somewhere deep in the rock, the air recyclers breathed their slow mechanical breath.

It was Tey who broke it — the archivist, barely twenty-five, voice thin but steady. "My grandfather died in the second collapse without ever learning the word for 'homeland' in our own tongue, because his grandfather wouldn't teach it to children who might be caught with it. Two hundred years of swallowing our own names. Warden Vess, I'm prepared to fight for the right to say one word out loud. I think most of us are."

Doran closed his eyes briefly. He had argued this battle for forty years and he had always won it, because fear was a reliable ally and he had fed it well. But fear, he understood suddenly, sitting there under the humming lamps, was a currency that inflated. It took more of it every year to buy the same safety, and the vault was nearly empty.

"The vote," he said finally. "Those for sending the outsider after the Meridian's Wrath — knowing what it costs — raise your hands."

Hands went up in ones and twos and then in clusters. Jossan Ree. Tey. Old Pell, to Doran's genuine surprise, his spotted hand trembling but unmistakably raised. Half the hardliner bench stayed down — Maret Osk stared straight ahead, arms locked — but even among them, one or two lifted their fingers grudgingly, the way a man signs a contract he hates.

Sera voted last, of course. She raised her hand and held it high, and looked directly at Doran while she did it, and there was no triumph in her face at all. Only something tired and old and almost kind.

"Against," Doran said, and raised his own hand alone, because a warden who wouldn't count himself couldn't ask anyone else to trust the count. "Motion carries. Barely." He let his hand fall. "The salvage mission is authorized. One ship. The outsider pilots, and he goes with an enclave escort — Tey, since you're so eager, congratulations, you've volunteered. Comms blackout from departure to return. If his transponder pings anything larger than a relay buoy, we seal the outer tunnels and deny he ever existed. Those terms are not negotiable."

"Accepted," Sera said.

"And Sera." He waited until she turned back. "If this fails, if they trace it here — I will spend whatever it costs to protect these people. Including spending *you*. Remember that I told you so, in front of everyone, so that when it happens you can't say the warning never came."

"I remember everything, Doran," she said quietly. "That's rather the point of me."

* * *

The chamber emptied slowly, the way water leaves a basin — first the young, quick and chattering, then the old, moving in pairs, murmuring. Doran stayed. He always stayed. The chamber after a vote was the only place he was honest, because the benches couldn't testify.

Maret Osk found him there, as he'd known she would.

"You let it happen," she said.

"I counted honestly. It wasn't mine to stop."

"You could have swayed them. You've done it before. The water-ration vote. The antenna moratorium. One speech, Doran, and half those raised hands go back down."

"Maybe." He ran a thumb along the grain of the ancient table, wood-veneer over alloy, salvaged from a liner two centuries dead. "Maybe I'm tired of winning this particular argument."

Maret studied him with the flat patience of a woman checking a pressure seal for hairline fractures. "That's not what this is."

"No?"

"You looked at that linguist today. In the docking bay. You looked at him the way Pell looks at the sky-drone footage." She leaned in. "What did you see, Doran?"

He was quiet a long moment. The recyclers breathed. Somewhere above them, through forty meters of ice and rock, a stranger's ship sat cooling on their landing shelf, its hull patched with mismatched plate, its pilot presumably sleeping in a bunk he had no right to.

"I saw a man," Doran said at last, "who talks about evidence the way Sera talks about the dead. Like it's personal. Like he owes somebody." He shook his head. "I don't know who. I don't know what he's running from, or what he thinks digging up that wreck will fix in him. But men like that don't turn back, Maret. They either get what they're after or they get killed for it. And either way, the blast radius includes us."

"So we should have stopped him."

"We should have *used* him. Properly. Which is what we're doing now, whatever Sera thinks she's doing." He pushed himself up from the table, joints announcing themselves one by one. "Get the tunnel teams ready. If we're doing this, we do it clean — redundant comms, dead-drop protocols, the works. And put a watch on the outsider. Friendly eyes. Someone he'll trust."

"Tey?"

"Tey's going with him. Send Rennik. Quiet one. Good ears."

Maret nodded and turned to go, then paused at the chamber mouth. "One more thing. The signal he followed. The distress call." Her voice dropped. "It was ours, wasn't it? An old beacon. Something from the first generation."

Doran had wondered the same thing since the moment Sera had told him. Somewhere in the archive levels there were relics nobody powered up anymore — emergency transmitters from the founding, keyed to frequencies the founders had prayed someone, someday, might be listening on.

"Two hundred years," he said, "and it finally reached someone." He pulled his coat from the back of the warden's chair. "Let's hope he's worth the wait."

* * *

He found Sera in the archive level, three hours later, because of course he did. She sat in the lamplight among the memory-vaults, cataloguing something — she was always cataloguing — and she didn't look up when his boots rang on the deck plating.

"You voted against me," she said.

"I vote against you every time. It's tradition."

"Forty years of tradition." Now she looked up, and in the low amber light her face was stripped of the courtroom polish she wore in the chamber. Underneath it she just looked old, and tired, and afraid in the way only the very brave allow themselves to look. "Do you know why I pushed this, Doran? Truly?"

"Because you believe the dead deserve justice."

"The dead are past caring." She set down the slate in her hands — an old manifest, he saw, a shipping record from 2187, one of the forged ones, the paper trail of a genocide filed as paperwork. "I pushed it because of the girl. My granddaughter. Last week she drew our family tree for a school exercise, and she got four generations back and then drew a question mark. Four generations, Doran, and then a question mark, because that's all I've been able to give her. Names without places. Places without dates. A history with the middle burned out of it." Her voice frayed, just slightly, and she pressed on before it could tear. "If that black box exists, it has dates. It has orders. It has the *names of the men who did it*. That's not justice. Justice is for the living to feel better. That's a spine. You can build a people on a spine."

Doran lowered himself onto the bench across from her. For a while neither spoke. The vaults hummed around them, holding two centuries of grief in careful cold storage.

"If it goes wrong," he said finally, "I'll seal the tunnels myself. You know that."

"I know."

"And if it goes right?"

Sera almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that almost-smile — it contained the whole weight of seventy years. "If it goes right, then in a year or ten or fifty, some child stands on real soil under a real sky and says a name out loud that isn't a secret. And neither of us will be there. And that will be enough."

Doran stood. At the vault door he paused, hand on the frame, and said the thing he had come to say and hadn't planned to:

"The outsider. Isandro Montrose. When he wakes tomorrow — bring him to me. Before he goes anywhere. If my people are going to bet their lives on a stranger, I want to look him in the eye and hear his reasons from his own mouth. All of them. Even the ugly ones."

"He'll give you the ugly ones," Sera said. "That's the strange part about him. He seems to *want* to."

Doran grunted and walked out into the tunnel dark, and behind him the archive lights glowed on, patient as embers, keeping the dead company through another night of the long hiding.

Above, far above, beyond the ice and the rock and the exclusion zone's invisible fence, the galaxy went on not knowing they existed. For two hundred years that had been the whole of the plan.

Tomorrow, for the first time, it would also be the target.
