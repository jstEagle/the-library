## 16. The Hunter Assigned

The message arrived at 0347 station time, which told Ilsa Kreutz everything she needed to know before she read a word of it.

Halcyon Combine did not send urgent requests in the middle of the night. Halcyon sent memos, and memos waited. What came at 0347 was not a memo. It was a priority flag from the Meridian Yard audit team — the one that had gone dark for eleven minutes while a section of scrapyard lost power — and it was addressed directly to her personal queue with a routing code she had only seen twice in nine years of service.

She sat up in her bunk aboard the *Cormorant's Ledger*, let the cabin lights come up slow, and read it twice.

*Vault breach. Meridian's Wrath. Core missing. Audit team intact but shaken. Station logs scrubbed — intruder used a maintenance ghost. Transponder trace attached.*

Attached was a name.

*Isandro Montrose. Independent hauler. Vessel: Long Echo, registry Kestrel Verge. Cargo class: bulk ore.*

Kreutz read the name three times, then set the slate down on the blanket and looked at it the way other people looked at a photograph of someone they'd once loved.

"Montrose," she said aloud, to no one.

Her cabin was small and deliberately austere — a bunk, a locker, a work surface bolted to the wall, nothing that could be called decoration except a single object: a smooth black stone the size of a fist, taken from a riverbed on a world whose name had been removed from most maps. She picked it up now, turned it over in her hand, felt its weight settle into her palm like an old argument.

Then she got dressed, made coffee strong enough to strip paint, and went to work.

* * *

By 0500 she had Montrose's file open across three screens, and by 0600 she had stopped reading it as a file and started reading it as a man.

That was the mistake most hunters made, in her experience. They read targets as data points — transponder numbers, credit balances, known associates — and they missed the shape of the person underneath. Data told you where a man had been. Shape told you where he would go.

Isandro Montrose, age thirty-eight. Formerly Dr. Isandro Montrose, Department of Xenolinguistics, Calloway University Annex. Dismissed twelve years ago following the Aldervane testimony scandal — a case Kreutz remembered vaguely from the news feeds of the time, a translator's error or a translator's lie, an innocent man convicted, a career quietly ended. After that: cargo work. Ore runs through the Kestrel Verge. Debt markers with three different lenders. No political affiliations. No criminal record beyond two customs citations for undeclared personal effects. No family listed.

A nobody. A tired man hauling rocks through the dark because the alternative was worse.

Except a tired man hauling rocks did not walk into a Halcyon scrapyard under forged credentials, cut power to a secure section, and extract the flight recorder from a decommissioned destroyer. That required planning, nerve, and — critically — a reason.

Kreutz leaned back and closed her eyes and let the reason assemble itself, piece by piece, the way she'd been trained to do twenty years ago when she'd still worn a uniform instead of a corporate badge.

The Meridian's Wrath. Everyone at Halcyon knew what the ship was, even if no one said it aloud. It had a sister vessel, the Meridian's Grace, and between them they had done the work at Vessic Reach in '87 that the company's histories described, in a single bloodless sentence, as *the orderly relocation of an unviable settlement*. Kreutz had been nineteen when it happened. She had grown up, like everyone else, believing the sentence.

Then, four years ago, she'd been assigned to the internal security division proper — the division that didn't exist on any org chart — and she had learned what sentences were for. Sentences were load-bearing walls. You built them so the building stayed standing. And every so often, someone came along wanting to pull one out to see what fell.

Montrose wanted to pull out the wall.

She opened her eyes and began, methodically, to plan.

* * *

The first thing she established was that Montrose was still alive, which was not a given. The audit team's report noted that the intruder had triggered a cascade alarm on extraction and departed the yard under fire from automated defenses. Hull damage probable. But the *Long Echo*'s transponder had pinged a relay buoy eleven hours later, outbound from the Verge toward the Spinward lanes, which meant either Montrose was alive or someone else was flying his ship.

She pulled his traffic history. Ore contracts, fuel purchases, docking fees — the boring heartbeat of a working hauler's life. And there, threaded through it, something interesting: over the past six weeks, the *Long Echo* had made three unscheduled detours into deep space off any contract route. Each detour lasted between forty and seventy hours. Each ended with him returning to his scheduled lane as if nothing had happened.

Three visits. The same region of space each time — a patch of nothing inside the Halcyon exclusion zone around the rogue moonlet designated VX-9, a rock so worthless the company had never bothered to mine it, survey it, or name it.

Worthless rocks inside exclusion zones were never worthless. They were hidden.

Kreutz flagged the coordinates and sent a quiet query down the chain: *any assets, historical or current, associated with VX-9?*

The answer came back in ninety minutes, and it was the answer she expected: *no records found.*

No records found. In nine years, Ilsa Kreutz had learned that "no records found" was Halcyon's favorite phrase. It was the phrase they'd used about Vessic Reach. It was the phrase that appeared whenever someone asked what happened to the settlement, the people, the language, the graves. The company didn't destroy records; that left gaps, and gaps invited questions. The company *wrote* records — clean ones, seamless ones, records so complete that the absence of anything else looked like proof of nothing.

So somewhere inside VX-9, behind a wall of official nothing, there were either survivors or evidence or both, and Isandro Montrose had found them, and now he had the Meridian's Wrath's black box in his hold, and somewhere in his exhausted, debt-ridden, half-broken heart he had decided to do something about all of it.

Kreutz finished her coffee. She found that she respected him already, which was useful. Respect kept you careful.

* * *

She filed her operational plan at 1100, and by 1300 it had been approved by a committee that would deny approving it if anyone ever asked.

That was how Halcyon worked. Nothing was ever ordered; things were merely *enabled*. Her budget authorization came through as a routine security retainer. Her weapons requisition was buried in a port services invoice. If the operation went well, Director Roe's office would accept congratulations. If it went badly, Warden Ilsa Kreutz, private contractor, badge number KS-114, would be a deniable instrument that had acted alone.

She had accepted this arrangement years ago without bitterness. Bitterness was for people who still believed in alternatives.

Her plan was simple, because simple plans survived contact with reality. Three phases.

Phase one: containment. Quietly seed the ports along Montrose's likely route — Calloway above all, because everyone eventually came to Calloway; it was the largest freeport in contested space and the only place a man with no money and no friends could try to sell a secret. She would not detain him. Detention created martyrs and paperwork. She would watch, map his contacts, identify who he approached, and let Halcyon's money do what Halcyon's money always did: close doors before he reached them.

Phase two: isolation. By the time Montrose understood that no journalist, advocate, or magistrate in contested space would touch his evidence, he would be desperate. Desperate men made mistakes. Desperate men reached for allies, and allies could be bought, and bought allies reported back.

Phase three: recovery. The core, the contacts, the source. All of it, retrieved cleanly, with the enclave — whatever it was — left exactly as Halcyon had left it two centuries ago: unrecorded.

She reviewed the plan twice, adjusted two timings, and authorized herself a task force of one ship, eight operatives, and a budget line that could have fed a small moon for a year.

Then she did the thing she always did before an operation began. She took the black stone from its shelf, held it in both hands, and sat with it in silence for ten minutes.

She had taken it from a riverbed during her first assignment, on a world whose population had also become a sentence in a corporate history. She had never been able to explain to herself why she kept it. It was not guilt — she had long ago made peace with what she was. It was closer to *evidence*. Proof, held in the palm, that places existed before companies renamed them. That rivers ran before anyone measured their mineral content.

She put the stone away and went to brief her team.

* * *

They gathered in the *Ledger*'s wardroom: eight people, six of whom she trusted completely and two of whom she trusted to be exactly as mercenary as advertised. Dain Okoro, her second, ex-fleet security, patient as bedrock. The Ferro twins, signals and intrusion. Vashti, medic and interrogator, though Kreutz had never once needed the second skill — fear did the work faster. And the others, faces she knew down to their sleep-twitches.

She stood at the head of the table and gave them the shape of it without the soul of it.

"A cargo hauler named Isandro Montrose stole property from a Halcyon facility," she said. "The property is a flight recorder. Our job is to recover it and identify everyone he's been talking to. We do this quietly. Nobody dies unless somebody makes us."

Okoro raised an eyebrow. "And if he's already talked?"

"Then we find out who listened." She brought up Montrose's image on the wardroom display — a customs intake photo, six years old, a thin man with tired eyes and the particular posture of someone braced for bad news. "This is him. He's not a professional. He's not military. He's a linguist who drives ore. Which means he'll be clever in ways we don't expect and clumsy in ways we can use. He'll trust the wrong person, because lonely people always do. He'll hesitate at the moment we wouldn't, because he still thinks this is about being right instead of about winning."

Vashti studied the photo. "He doesn't look like a thief."

"He looks exactly like a thief," Kreutz said. "Thieves look like everyone. That's the whole trick." She killed the image. "We jump for Calloway at 1800. Questions?"

There were none, or none worth asking. She dismissed them and stood alone in the wardroom for a moment, looking at the empty space where his face had been.

*Lonely people always do.* She heard her own words come back to her and found, to her mild irritation, that they had lodged somewhere under her ribs. Because she had read the file again after the briefing, the way she sometimes read files after briefings, and she had noticed the thing the file didn't say: twelve years of solo runs. Twelve years of a man talking to his nav AI because there was no one else to talk to. Twelve years since the profession that had been his whole identity chewed him up and spat him into the cargo lanes.

She knew what that kind of loneliness built. It built conviction. It built a man who, having lost everything respectable, had nothing left to protect except being right. And a man like that, holding proof of a massacre in his cargo hold, would not sell it. He would spend it.

Which made him more dangerous than any professional, and easier to break than any fanatic, and — she admitted it to herself, alone in the wardroom, with the honesty she permitted herself in no other company — more worth breaking carefully.

* * *

The *Cormorant's Ledger* jumped for Calloway at 1758, two minutes ahead of schedule, because Kreutz believed schedules were promises you made to yourself.

During the transit she worked, and the work was patience itself. She built Montrose's likely route model, weighted by fuel costs, debt payments, and the psychology of a man carrying something hot. She cross-referenced every advocate, journalist, and minor magistrate in contested space against Halcyon's influence ledger — a document so comprehensive it functioned as a map of the entire region's conscience, and the conscience, as usual, was mostly for sale. Forty-one names came back green: reachable, buyable, already owned. Four came back amber: principled, expensive to flip, worth watching. Zero came back red.

Nobody in contested space was untouchable. That was the architecture. That was the point of it.

On the third day of transit, the signals section intercepted a fragment — a burst transmission on a pirate band, encrypted, origin uncertain, content unresolved. The Ferro twins flagged it as noise. Kreutz read the intercept metadata twice and felt the first cold thread of doubt go through her.

It wasn't the content. It was the timing. Someone, somewhere, was already trying to move information through channels Halcyon didn't own. It might be unrelated. It usually was.

But she had learned, over nine years, to treat coincidence the way sailors treated weather: not as an omen, just as a condition you planned around.

She added a directive to the operational file: *monitor pirate-band traffic for references to Vessic, Meridian's Wrath, or the term "Purge." Escalate immediately on match.*

Then she went to her cabin, took down the black stone, and sat with it while the stars stretched into lines outside the viewport.

Somewhere ahead of her, a tired man was making decisions she could almost predict word for word. He would go to Calloway. He would knock on doors. The doors would close. He would grow more isolated, more certain, more dangerous. And at some point — soon, if she managed it right — their paths would bend toward each other like light around a mass, and one of them would come out of that encounter changed forever.

She intended it to be him.

Kreutz put the stone away, checked her sidearm out of habit rather than need, and slept without dreaming, which was the only mercy her profession had ever reliably provided.

Two days out from Calloway, the pirate-band monitor matched.

One word, buried in a smuggler's chatter, repeated three times across six hours, moving outward through contested space like a spark through dry grass:

*Vessic.*

Kreutz read the report at 0347 station time — the same hour, she noted with a flicker of something that was almost amusement, that the original alert had reached her — and gave the order to increase speed.

The net was cast. Now it closed.
