## 32. Night Before the Signal

The enclave woke early on the last day, though no one had slept much.

Isandro came up from the comms bay at what his body still insisted was three in the morning, enclave time, and found the tunnels already lit. Not the usual amber of the work lamps — someone had strung pale blue filament along the main corridor, salvaged holiday wiring from a station that had died before Isandro was born, and it made the ice overhead glow like shallow water. Children were running beneath it, arms out, pretending to fly.

He stood in the corridor mouth and watched them for a while. Three hundred people. Two hundred years of hiding. In fourteen hours, give or take relay lag, every one of them would be named.

A boy — Dov, maybe seven, the one who'd once asked him if all offworlders dreamed in flat languages — skidded to a stop at his boots.

"Are you the antenna man?"

"I'm the antenna man."

"Can I see them?"

"After you practice your words." He crouched. "You have your words?"

Dov recited them, fast and breathless, the way children recite anything: a name, a date, a sentence in Vessic that had taken him two weeks to shape without swallowing the vowels. *We are the ones who remained.* His pronunciation was better than half the adults'. Isandro had checked.

"Good," he said. "Say it slower tomorrow. The whole galaxy's listening."

The boy's eyes went wide, and he ran back down the corridor shouting something to the other children, and Isandro realized with a small cold drop in his stomach that he hadn't been lying. The whole galaxy *was* listening. He just hoped it would be listening to testimony instead of to jamming static and gunfire.

* * *

In the long chamber the enclave called the Hearth — the widest tunnel, where the geothermal tap kept the air warm enough for the old people's joints — Sera Venn sat at a table covered in relics.

She worked alone, which was unusual; she was rarely alone anymore. But this task she'd claimed for herself. Laid out on cloth scavenged from cargo pallets were the objects: a child's shoe fused half to slag. A wedding ring recovered from a mass grave pit on the southern shelf, its band engraved in characters so fine you needed a lens. A fragment of door plating bearing a family name — *Venn* — burned into the metal by a heat so sudden the letters had vitrified rather than charred. Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother's door.

Elders came to her through the morning, one at a time or in pairs, each carrying something wrapped in their own hands. An old man named Pell set down a cooking pot dented by shrapnel and said nothing, just touched the rim once and left. A woman brought a lock of hair sealed in resin, her mother's, cut before the evacuation order that had never been an evacuation. Each object went onto the cloth. Each owner spoke a few words, or none. Sera wrote nothing down. She said the objects remembered on their own; writing was for people who'd forgotten how to listen.

By midday the table was full, and she stood over it a long time, her hands folded behind her back like a woman at a graveside, because that was what it was.

Tey found her there. The young archivist moved carefully these days — the bruised ribs from Calloway still caught when she breathed wrong — but she moved fast anyway, always fast, as if slowness might let the world change under her feet.

"They're asking me to catalog these," Tey said. "For the broadcast. Scan everything, tag it, push the images with the audio."

Sera didn't look up. "Will you?"

"I don't know how to tag grief."

That made Sera turn. She studied the girl for a moment — twenty-two years old, born down here, never having seen an open sky that wasn't rock, carrying more of the future than anyone should carry — and then she picked up the wedding ring and held it out.

"You hold it first," Sera said. "Then you'll know what to write."

Tey took it. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. She stood there turning it in her fingers until her jaw tightened, and then she said, quietly, in Vessic — her Vessic was accented, learner's Vessic, but it was real — *"I will say your names."*

Sera closed the girl's fingers over the ring. "There. Tagged."

* * *

Doran spent the day doing the thing Doran did best, which was arguing, except today he argued gently, and that frightened people more than shouting ever had.

He went tunnel to tunnel with the council roster under his arm, checking on the families who had voted against the broadcast — the forty-one households, nearly a fifth of the enclave, who believed with their whole bodies that light meant death. He didn't try to change their minds. That fight was over; the vote had been taken, the plan committed. What he did was listen.

An older couple, the Rassens, told him they'd be in the deep shelters tomorrow with their grandchildren whether the council liked it or not. Doran said the shelters were sensible and the council had stocked them himself. A young father asked, voice cracking, what happened if Kreutz's troops got into the tunnels before the miners arrived. Doran gave him the honest answer — some of us die — and then the truer one: fewer of us die than if we stay hidden another decade, waiting for Halcyon to find us by accident, the way they found everyone's grandparents.

"And if the tribunal does nothing?" the man asked. "If we burn our one chance and the charters stand?"

Doran was quiet a moment. Down here, in the blue-lit corridor, with the sound of children somewhere above, he looked older than his sixty years and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"Then we'll have spoken," he said. "And our children will know we weren't animals in a burrow. We were a people. That's not nothing. I spent fifty years telling myself it was enough to survive. It isn't. Survival is the floor, not the house."

He reported all of it to Sera that evening, standing in the doorway of the relic chamber, and she listened the way she listened to everything, completely, and then said only: "You've changed your mind about the boy's plan."

"The hauler's plan," Doran corrected, but without edge. Isandro Montrose had stopped being *the boy* to him sometime around the night he'd jettisoned twelve years of cargo to get home alive. "No. I still think he's gambling with lives that aren't his. But he gambled his own first, and he didn't flinch when the bill came due. So now it's our gamble too, and I'd rather lose it standing up." He paused. "Don't tell him I said that. He's insufferable enough."

Sera smiled, small and rare. "Your secret is safe with four hundred people."

* * *

The antennas were the problem.

They always had been. The enclave's transmitters were scavenged mining relays, adequate for whispering to friendly smugglers, laughable for shouting across contested space. The plan required forty synchronized bursts through forty relay nodes, most of them stolen bandwidth, some of them bribed station techs, two of them — Isandro refused to ask how — pirate arrays parked in the dark beyond the Verge. If the timing slipped by even a few seconds between nodes, Halcyon's jamming fleet could smother the signal node by node, and everything — the black box, Marta Oyelaran's documents, Sera's voice, the scans of the relics, the firing orders signed in Cassian Roe's own hand — would die in the dark unspoken.

So Isandro calibrated. He had been calibrating for six days, and on the last day he calibrated again, because there was nothing else his hands knew how to do with fear.

He worked in the antenna gallery, a natural chimney in the rock that opened to the surface through a shaft barely wider than his shoulders, where the array dishes clustered like pale flowers turned toward a sky they'd never been allowed to face. The cold up there bit through his jacket. He didn't mind. Cold meant clear signal.

Tey climbed up to him in the late afternoon, hauling a tool case and breathing hard around her ribs.

"Sync check," she said. "Node thirty-one dropped offline for eleven minutes overnight. Came back. Nobody knows why."

"Show me."

They knelt over the timing board together, and Isandro walked the chain node by node, listening to the handshake tones the way he'd once listened to phonemes — not hearing meaning exactly, but hearing *health*, hearing where the signal limped. Node thirty-one was limping. Its oscillator had drifted nine hundred milliseconds out of phase, probably thermal stress, possibly sabotage, and he rebuilt the sync lock with Tey holding the light and asking questions faster than he could answer them.

"Why does it matter if it's nine hundred milliseconds? That's nothing."

"It's everything. Forty voices saying one sentence. If one voice comes in late, it's not a chorus, it's a stutter, and stutters get stepped on." He seated the last connector and watched the phase trace flatten into a clean line. "There. Singing again."

Tey looked at the trace, then at him. "You talk about it like language."

"It is language. Everything transmitted is. That's the whole point of tomorrow." He leaned back against the rock, suddenly aware of how tired he was, tired in the marrow. "Forty relays are going to say, in forty directions at once: *these people exist, here is the proof, here are the names.* That's a sentence. Sentences can be denied. Choruses can't."

She was quiet for a moment. Then, awkwardly, the way she did everything sincere: "My words are ready too. For the broadcast. Tey Vessic-Orrin, archivist, fourth generation, born in hiding." She recited it. "I practiced in the mirror like an idiot."

"It won't feel like the mirror tomorrow."

"No," she said. "I figured."

* * *

After dark — after the hour the enclave called dark, though down here dark was a decision — the children gathered in the Hearth for the rehearsal.

It had been Sera's insistence and Doran's final concession: the youngest generation would speak. Not the elders with their eyewitness grief, not the adults with their careful statements. The children. Sixty seconds of the broadcast belonged to them, and in that minute they would each say their name, their lineage, and one sentence of Vessic, untranslated, daring the universe to claim the language was dead.

Isandro stood at the back while Sera led them through it. There was no stage; they simply stood in a ring around the relic table, the slagged shoe and the vitrified door plate and the resin-sealed hair, and one by one they spoke.

Mostly it went well. Mostly.

Dov rushed his line again, vowels swallowed, and Sera stopped him with a raised hand — not unkind, but absolute — and made him do it again. And again. On the fourth attempt a smaller girl started crying, not from fear but from the pressure of it, the weight of ancestors pressing down through a seven-year-old's throat, and her mother moved to lift her out of the ring, and Sera said, softly:

"Let her cry. Crying is also testimony. Let her finish."

The girl cried and finished, hiccupping her sentence in broken Vessic, and the ring of adults was absolutely silent, and Isandro found he had to look at the ceiling for a while.

When it ended, the children scattered to their bunks, and the enclave settled into the particular hush of a community the night before a thing that cannot be undone. Doors closed softly. The geothermal tap hummed. Somewhere a woman was singing, very low, a song whose melody Isandro recognized from the archive fragments — a harvest song, two centuries orphaned, sung tonight to a child who would sing it to the stars tomorrow.

He found Sera banking the hearth coals.

"They'll do it," he said. "The children. They're ready."

"They were always ready. They just didn't know what for." She straightened, pressed a hand briefly to the warm stone. "You should sleep, Isandro."

"So should you."

"I will. After." She looked at him across the banked fire, and her voice dropped into the register she used for things that mattered. "Tomorrow, when you press the key — whatever happens after, whatever comes through those tunnels or down from orbit — I want you to remember one thing. You didn't give us our voice back. We never lost it. You gave us *listeners*. That's different. That's the harder miracle, and it's the one that lasts."

He nodded, because his throat wasn't cooperating with speech.

He walked back toward the comms bay through the blue-lit corridors, past doors behind which three hundred people were lying awake holding their names like stones they'd finally been allowed to set down, and he was almost to the junction when Tey came running the other way, barefoot, still in her rehearsal clothes, and grabbed his sleeve.

"Node nineteen," she said, breathless. "It just went dark. Not drifting — *dark*. Dead handshakes. And Isandro—" she swallowed— "the failure signature matches what node thirty-one did last night. Someone's testing us. Someone's walking the chain."

Behind them, above them, through half a kilometer of ice and rock, the antennas stood pointed at a sky that had begun, quietly, to close.
