## 34. Defense of the Enclave

The first shell hit the outer ridge at 0347, and the whole moonlet rang like a struck bell.

Isandro felt it through the soles of his boots before he heard it — a deep subsonic thrum rolling up through the ice, through the rock, through the bones of the tunnels his friends had carved over two hundred years of hiding. The lights flickered once. Somewhere below, a child started crying, and somewhere above, Doran's voice came through every comm at once, calm as a man reading a cargo manifest.

"Contact confirmed. Four dropships on thermal. They're coming down the north face. Everyone to your stations."

Isandro was already running.

The transmitter chamber sat at the heart of the enclave, buried behind sixty meters of ice and a labyrinth of tunnels that had been dug narrow on purpose — too narrow for powered armor, too dark for drones without lights, too twisty for anyone who hadn't grown up memorizing them by touch. That had been the plan, such as it was. The broadcast was out; forty relays were screaming Sera's testimony and Cassian Roe's signature into every port in contested space. Halcyon couldn't unsend it. So they'd come to kill the source instead, and everyone who stood near it.

Kreutz's assault force: he'd counted the dropships on the scope. Four. Call it a hundred and twenty troops, exoskeletons, breaching charges, air support from the patrol ships still locked in orbit. Defenders: three hundred civilians, forty-odd adults with scavenged weapons, and whatever help the broadcast had bought.

The help had started arriving an hour ago.

* * *

They came in ones and twos at first, then in clusters — ore haulers with patched hulls sliding into the shadow of the moonlet, cutting engines, going dark. Miners from the Kestrel Verge who'd listened to the transmission on open bands while their drills chewed rock. Smugglers out of Calloway who'd been passing Tey's fragment of the firing order hand to hand for weeks, back when it was contraband gossip and not yet history. A trawler crew that had lost a daughter to a Halcyon debt-collection raid and said nothing about it, just docked and asked where they could stand.

By 0300 there were eleven ships hugging the ridge line and maybe ninety fighters crouched in the upper tunnels, and Isandro, hauling a crate of power cells down Junction Seven, understood that he was watching something he had no word for in either of his languages. Not an army. Armies had structure, supply lines, officers. This was ninety people who had heard a dead language speak and decided they would rather die awkwardly than live conveniently.

A broad woman in a vacuum-rated mining rig stopped him in the corridor, visor up, face slick with sweat. "You the one who sent it? The signal?"

"I helped."

She nodded slowly, like she was checking something off against a list only she could see. "My grandfather used to hum something. Wouldn't say what. Said it was nothing." She tapped her temple. "It wasn't nothing, was it."

"No," Isandro said. "It wasn't nothing."

She clapped his shoulder hard enough to stagger him and moved on toward the north galleries.

* * *

The defense had one shape and one shape only: make them come to you, and make every meter cost.

Doran ran it like the engineer he'd been before engineering became a crime. The outer tunnels were rigged with collapse charges — not to kill, Doran insisted, but to seal, though Isandro knew better than to believe the distinction would hold once people started dying inside them. The middle galleries were kill zones of darkness and choke points, defended by miners with fusion cutters repurposed into something between a tool and a verdict. The inner ring around the transmitter chamber was held by the smugglers, who fought dirty and knew how, and by the enclave's own — quiet men and women who had spent lifetimes practicing for a day they'd prayed would never come, and who now moved through the tunnels with the terrible competence of the long-prepared.

Sera commanded the inner ring. She'd refused a weapon. "I've hidden for eighty years," she told Doran when he pressed one into her hands. "I'm done hiding, but I'm not done being what we are. Someone has to remember why we're fighting while it's happening."

So she stood in the second gallery with the children and the elders behind her, and she sang.

Isandro heard it over the local net as he ran supplies forward — the old Vessic mourning cadence, the one from her testimony, the one that had gone out across forty relays and into a million ears. It rose through the comm channels and through the rock itself, and he saw hardened smugglers go still when it reached them, saw a miner cross himself with a gesture from a religion two centuries dead in this sector, saw people find their feet again after the shells knocked them down.

The Vessic had a term for it. He'd catalogued it twelve years ago in a dissertation nobody read: *sennavar* — the act of singing so the enemy must hear what they are destroying. It was considered a sacred burden, never a strategy.

Tonight it was both.

* * *

The breach came at the north face, exactly where Doran wanted it.

He watched it on a cracked tactical display salvaged from a wreck, huddled in the relay alcove with Tey, who had appointed herself his shadow and refused all orders to the contrary. The display showed heat blooms blooming through the ice like flowers made of fire — breaching charges, then the slow crawl of troop signatures down Tunnel One.

"They're moving careful," Tey murmured. Her fingers flew across her deck, rerouting power, spoofing sensor ghosts, doing the electronic equivalent of throwing gravel in pursuers' eyes. "Too careful. Kreutz trained them."

"She trained them for stations and corridors," Isandro said. "Not for a hole in the ground full of people who know every centimeter of it."

The first contact lasted eleven seconds.

Later, survivors would describe it differently — the flash of the cutter, the scream of armor, the sudden dark. What the display showed was simpler: six troop signatures entering Tunnel One, and four stopping permanently. The other two fell back, and the tunnel mouth went quiet, and somewhere in the dark a miner's voice said, almost apologetically, "Sorry. Sorry. I didn't—" and another voice, older, steadier, answered: "You did what you had to. Rest. I'll take the watch."

Ordinary people choosing to stand. Choosing, each one, alone, in the dark, without speeches.

* * *

By 0530 the pattern had set in and begun to grind.

Halcyon's troops were good. They adapted fast — brought in drones, flooded the tunnels with light, learned to fear the junctions. But the enclave's defenders were better at being home. They fought from memory, from muscle, from childhood games of hide-and-seek played in these same corridors. They collapsed Tunnel Three behind the third push and drowned Tunnel Five in coolant from a ruptured line, and when a squad tried to flank through the old ventilation shafts they found the shafts already rigged, because Doran had spent thirty years preparing for this night the way other men prepare for retirement.

And still the line bent. Inward, always inward.

Isandro carried ammunition. It was the only thing he was good for in a fight and he knew it — a linguist, a hauler, a man whose hands shook. He ran cells and charge packs from the armory to the front galleries, and each trip the front was closer. He passed a smuggler named Okonkwo holding a junction with a stuttering plasma torch, passed a pair of Vessic twins barely older than Tey dragging a wounded miner backward by the arms, passed a wall scorch-marked in the shape of a man who hadn't moved fast enough.

On his fourth run, the ceiling came down.

A shell punched through thirty meters of ice directly ahead of him and the corridor turned into white noise and white powder and a pressure wave that picked him up and set him down against the far wall like a doll. His ears screamed. Dust filled his mouth — rock dust, ice dust, and under it, faintly, the copper taste he'd learn to recognize as blood, though none of it was his.

Through the ringing, he heard singing. Still singing. Closer to the surface now, but steady.

He got up. He picked up the crate. He kept walking forward, because the people at the front needed the cells more than the people at the back needed him alive, and because Sera was singing, and you did not let down a woman who was singing *sennavar* while shells fell.

* * *

Tey found him at 0612, at the armory, loading the last crate.

"They're pulling back to regroup," she said. Breathless, lit up, alive in a way that frightened him. "Doran says we've got maybe twenty minutes. And Isandro — the relay net's degrading. Two relays went dark, jamming ships are chewing through the rest. The broadcast is still propagating but the *source* — if they take the chamber, they can prove provenance, discredit everything, claim we fabricated—"

"I know."

"And the backup transmitter's offline. Power coupling burned out during the first strike. I can fix it but I have to be *at* it, in the chamber, and—"

"No."

"—and it's my transmitter," she finished, chin up, seventeen years old and immovable. "I built half of it out of scrap. Nobody else knows the coupling array. You need me there."

"Tey." He took her shoulders. Through her jumpsuit he could feel her heart going like a bird's. "You're the archivist. You're the future. You don't stand in the room everyone is trying to destroy."

"You're in the room everyone is trying to destroy."

"That's different."

"It really isn't." She pulled free, gently, the way you'd pull away from someone you loved and had already decided to disobey. "You told me once that testimony needs witnesses. Who do you think is going to write down what happens here tonight? Who keeps the record, Isandro?" She backed toward the corridor, grinning, terrified, magnificent. "Twenty minutes. Fix the coupling. Keep them off us."

Then she was gone, running toward the loudest place in the world, and Isandro stood in the armory with a crate of power cells and the sudden cold understanding that he had just been outmaneuvered by a teenager, and that she was right, and that rightness was no comfort at all.

* * *

The second push came at 0640, and it came everywhere at once.

Kreutz had stopped being careful. The display showed troop signatures flooding three tunnels simultaneously, drones ahead of them, breaching teams blowing junctions rather than clearing them. The elegant defense began to come apart into something rawer — fights in the dark, blade and torch and boot, defenders falling back gallery by gallery, trading meters for minutes.

Isandro fought at Junction Nine, badly, with a cutter he'd never trained on, beside a Verge miner named Halloway who fought beautifully and died quietly, folding down against the wall with a look of mild surprise, and Isandro dragged him out of the fire line because someone should, because the record mattered, because Tey's voice was in his head saying *who keeps the record.*

At 0715 the inner ring closed. The surviving defenders — fewer than half of those who'd stood at midnight — sealed themselves into the last three galleries around the transmitter chamber, and the singing stopped, because Sera had finally taken up a weapon after all: not a gun, but a cutting torch, standing at the barricade beside Doran, an eighty-year-old woman in a borrowed environment suit daring the century to make her hide again.

And in the chamber itself, Tey worked.

Isandro heard her over the net, narrating to herself in the low rapid mumble of total concentration — *coupling's slagged, bypassing, don't need it pretty, need it loud* — while outside the chamber door the world ended in installments. He was ten meters away, hauling barricade plating, when the door blew.

Not the main door — the maintenance hatch, the one route Doran had sworn was unwatchable, because it was unwatchable, because it was suicide. Which meant the men coming through it weren't following doctrine. They were following Kreutz.

Three of them, in black, moving with a speed the tunnel fighters couldn't match. One went down to Doran's torch. One went down to a smuggler's shotgun blast at conversational distance. The third cleared the barricade, leveled his weapon at the transmitter housing — at Tey, kneeling inside its guts with a spanner in her hand — and fired.

Isandro was moving before the thought formed. Later he would not remember deciding. He remembered only the distance — five meters of open floor, an eternity — and his own body arriving like a thrown object, and the impact, and the burn across his side and shoulder as the energy bolt meant for the transmitter housing and the girl inside it tore through him instead.

He hit the deck. The pain arrived a half-second late, enormous, white. Through it he heard Tey screaming his name, heard the trooper's boots, heard a shotgun answer and silence follow. Then small hands under his arms, dragging him behind the housing, and Tey's face above his, streaked with grease and tears.

"Stay. Stay awake. Coupling's fixed, it's fixed, we're live, just stay—"

"Broadcast," he managed. Blood in his throat, tasting of iron and of words. "Still going out?"

"Every relay we have left." She pressed her jacket against his shoulder, hard, and he gasped. "They can't stop it. They're too late. All this — they're too *late*, and they keep coming anyway."

"Yes," Isandro said, and laughed, which hurt so much he nearly passed out, and which felt, in some way he couldn't explain and would spend years trying to, like hope. "That's what guilt looks like from the outside."

* * *

The attack broke at 0830, not because the defenders won but because the sky did.

Halloway's trawler crew — the ones with the dead daughter — had spent the whole battle sitting on their ships at the ridge, and at 0829 they decided they were done sitting. Eleven hulls rose out of the shadow of the moonlet in a ragged, suicidal, glorious line and put themselves between Kreutz's orbiting patrol ships and the dropships trying to extract her ground force. No weapons worth the name. Just mass, and nerve, and transponders broadcasting one phrase on every channel, in English and in flawless Vessic:

*We heard the testimony. We are witnesses now.*

Kreutz's commander, facing eleven civilian hulls laced with refugees and a battle he was losing meter by meter and a legal situation mutating beneath his feet with every minute the broadcast propagated — withdrew. The dropships lifted. The shelling stopped. The tunnels went quiet except for groaning rock, crackling fires, and voices calling names into the dark, hoping for answers.

Isandro lay behind the transmitter housing with Tey's jacket soaked red against his shoulder and listened to the silence arrive, and thought it was the loudest thing he'd ever heard.

* * *

They came for him at 0910 — medics, then Sera, then Doran, all wearing the same expression: relief stretched over grief, grief stretched over exhaustion. The casualty count came in pieces through the afternoon and each piece had a name. Forty-one defenders dead. Nineteen of the volunteers. Six enclave-born. Halloway. The twins' uncle. A boy of nineteen who'd never fired a weapon before last night.

Sera knelt beside Isandro's stretcher and took his hand, the one not bandaged, and held it the way you hold something you're afraid will be taken.

"The broadcast is still spreading," she said. "Calloway has risen. Three independent ports have suspended Halcyon charters pending review. Marta Oyelaran has gone public — she's demanding tribunal protection on open channels." A breath. "We did it, Isandro. It's working."

But her eyes went past him, down the ruined gallery, to the transmitter chamber where techs swarmed around the smoking relay array, and her grip tightened.

"What is it?"

"Kreutz didn't leave." Sera's voice dropped. "Her ship withdrew with the others — but our sensors caught a single suit signature dropping from high orbit during the chaos. Small. Silent. It entered the ice through the south shafts." She looked at him, and eighty years of survival were in her face. "She's inside, Isandro. She's been inside since the fighting started. And the only thing left in this enclave worth reaching alone is the final relay — and the man who runs it."

Down the corridor, in the direction of the antenna chamber, a door hissed shut.

Isandro sat up despite the medic's protest, despite the blood, despite everything, and reached for his boots.
