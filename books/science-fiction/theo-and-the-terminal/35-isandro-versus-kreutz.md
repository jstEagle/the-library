## 35. Isandro Versus Kreutz

The antenna chamber sat at the top of the enclave's tallest spire, a dome of scavenged hull plating and prayer, and it shook with every detonation that came up through the rock.

Isandro knelt at the transmitter console with his hands moving faster than his fear. The final relay was the hardest one — a deep-band array cobbled from three generations of salvage, its coupling coils held together with wire and faith. Tey had calibrated it yesterday. Tey was downstairs now, bleeding, because Kreutz had come for the transmitter first and the children second, and the defenders had chosen to spend themselves buying minutes.

He had minutes. Maybe two of them. Maybe less.

The uplink status crawled: *carrier lock — 61 percent.*

Through the dome's single viewport he could see the battle laid out like a diagram of everything wrong with the universe. Kreutz's dropships crouched on the ice shelf outside the main tunnel mouths, running lights strobing. Muzzle flashes stitched the trench lines where miners and smugglers and three hundred people who officially did not exist were holding ground that had never appeared on any map. Somewhere in those tunnels Sera was singing — he could hear her through the relay's open channel, the old mourning cadence, *we were here, we were here, we were here* — and the song was being broadcast on the same carrier wave he was building, syllable by syllable, into forty relay stations across contested space.

Thirty-eight relays already carried it. The black box audio. Marta Oyelaran's documents. Sera's testimony, raw and untranslated and undeniable. Two relays had gone dark under Halcyon jamming, and it didn't matter, because thirty-eight was past the threshold where suppression became archaeology. You could burn a message from the network. You could not burn it from forty thousand minds.

The last relay was the deep-band burst to the outer colonies — the places where Halcyon's writ ran thinnest and its money ran deepest, the places where the story needed to arrive loudest.

*Carrier lock — 74 percent.*

The chamber door was three meters behind him. It was a scavenged bulkhead with a manual wheel, and it would stop a bullet and nothing else.

He heard the wheel turn.

He didn't stop typing.

* * *

Ilsa Kreutz came through the door the way she did everything: precisely, without hurry, as if the universe were a filing system and she had already located his drawer.

She was alone. That was the first thing he understood, and the second thing was worse — she was alone because she didn't need soldiers for this. Her sidearm was holstered. Her hands were empty. Her armor bore the scuff of the tunnel fight, one pauldron scored by something that had nearly ended her, and she wore the marks the way other people wore medals.

"Montrose." She said his name like a case number. "You've made an extraordinary amount of noise for a man with one ship."

"Thirty-eight relays," Isandro said. His fingers kept moving. "You're late."

"I'm exactly on time." She walked toward him across the grating floor, unhurried, her boots ringing softly. "The outer-colony burst is the only one that matters. The inner relays are noise. Halcyon owns the courts in the inner systems — we'll have injunctions filed before your signal finishes propagating. But the outer colonies." She stopped two meters from the console, close enough that he could see the fine burn scarring on her jaw from some old op, far enough that his hands would never reach her. "The outer colonies still have juries. So that's the one we're going to stop."

"Then stop it."

"I'd rather buy it."

*Carrier lock — 81 percent.*

She let the number hang between them, then gestured at the viewport, at the burning trenches below. "Do you know what I admire about you, Montrose? You're not an ideologue. Ideologues are tedious. You're a man who lost his career and found a cause, and I understand lost careers. I've buried several people's." She tilted her head. "Here's my offer. You kill the burst. You walk out of here under my protection — actual protection, not the kind I sell. Halcyon restores your academic standing. Full reinstatement. Meridian University, your old chair, funding, tenure. Your name cleared of the Osei affair — expunged, sealed, gone. You'll never haul ore again."

The name hit him like cold water. *Osei.* Twelve years, and she'd dug it up. Of course she had. It was the first thing he'd searched for about her too, in the beginning, back when he'd still believed she might be a person and not a function.

"You did your homework," he said.

"I always do my homework." She stepped closer. "There's more. Amnesty for the enclave. Legal recognition — a protected settlement charter on their own world, signed, notarized, real. Halcyon will even fund the relocation. Three hundred people come out of the dark into the light, with land and law and a future. No more tunnels. No more scavenged air. Their children grow up under an open sky with their name on it." She paused, and for one unguarded instant something almost human moved behind her eyes. "That's not a bribe, Montrose. That's the best deal they will ever be offered. It's more than they've had in two hundred years. All it costs is one signal."

*Carrier lock — 89 percent.*

His hands had gone still on the console. He hated that. He could feel her registering it — the small betrayal of his own body, the pause that was an answer forming.

Because it *was* a good deal. That was the obscenity of it. Sera in the sun. Tey's wounds treated in a real hospital instead of a tunnel. Doran's hardliners getting the survival they'd voted for, wrapped in a charter with Halcyon's seal. And all it required was that the massacre stay a private matter between the murdered and the murderers. That the firing orders stay in one man's memory. That the truth arrive everywhere except the places it would do the most good.

"That's the trap," Isandro said quietly.

"It's an offer."

"It's the trap you've been building since Calloway. The trade for Sera. The amnesty terms. You've been teaching me the price of the truth the whole way here." He turned in the chair to face her fully. "You know what my problem is, Kreutz? I spent twelve years believing my word was poison. One bad testimony, one innocent man, one career ended — and I told myself I was finished as a witness. That anything I touched would rot." He looked down at his hands, the hands that had decrypted the black box, that had held Sera's recordings, that had typed every word of this transmission. "But I finally understand something. It was never my word that mattered. It was theirs. Sera's. Marta's. The dead in the box. I'm not the witness. I'm the wire."

"Poetry," Kreutz said. "Courts don't accept poetry."

"This isn't going to a court." He turned back to the console. *Carrier lock — 94 percent.* "It's going to everyone."

Her hand came off her hip and moved toward the holster, and he saw her decide — not to shoot him, he understood that even then, but to shoot the console. The relay. The one thing left.

He hit transmit.

* * *

The deep-band array fired with a sound he felt in his teeth, a rising harmonic that was almost — almost — a Vessic vowel. On the viewport's edge, the dome's antenna mast lit with discharge, throwing blue fire against the ice, and the last relay went screaming outward at lightspeed toward the outer colonies, carrying the firing orders of Meridian's Wrath and the signature of Cassian Roe and the voice of an old woman singing *we were here* in a language the universe had been told was dead.

Kreutz fired anyway. The round took the coupling housing and the console died in a shower of sparks, and it didn't matter, it didn't matter at all, because the signal was already gone — already past recall, already propagating through the dark faster than any warrant, any injunction, any bullet.

Isandro started laughing. He couldn't help it. It came out of him ragged and disbelieving, the laugh of a man who had been holding his breath for a year.

Kreutz stood over him with the pistol still level, and her face did something he had not expected. It didn't harden. It *emptied*. The professional mask stayed, but behind it he watched the mission die — watched her run the arithmetic and arrive at the only answer left. Forty relays. Forty thousand minds and climbing. A director's signature on a massacre, in every port from here to the Rim.

"You understand what you've done," she said.

"I know exactly what I've done."

"Not to Halcyon." Her voice dropped, and for the first time since the asteroid dock, since the scrapyard, since the debris field where she had crippled his engines and taken everything he owned, she sounded tired. "To them. Three hundred people in a hole in the ice, and now every shareholder, every board member, every man with money in those charters knows where the evidence lives. I had orders to contain this quietly. My replacement won't have orders. My replacement will have a budget." She holstered the pistol. "You didn't save them tonight, Montrose. You made them famous. Famous and *located*."

The chamber door burst inward behind her.

They came fast — four of Kreutz's soldiers in breach armor, weapons up, and behind them, impossibly, Doran, with a scavenged mining laser cradled in his arms and blood on his face that was not his own, and half a dozen enclave defenders fanning into the chamber with the desperate coordination of people who had never fought together before tonight.

Kreutz didn't reach for her weapon. She raised her hands a few centimeters, a gesture of professional courtesy, and stepped aside from Isandro with the unhurried grace of someone leaving a meeting that had simply run long.

"Get him out of here," she told her soldiers — and then, to Isandro, as they closed around him, as Doran's weapon tracked her and she ignored it completely: "The offer stands for one more day. After that, the people I answer to stop making offers." She moved to the door, then paused, and looked back at him with something that in a better woman might have been respect. "You chose who you are. I hope it was worth the price. It always costs more than you think — that's the one thing they don't put in the archives."

She walked out through her own soldiers, through the smoke, down toward the ice, and no one stopped her, because stopping her would have meant a fight the enclave could not afford, and because some part of all of them understood that she was right about what was coming.

* * *

Afterward there was the tunnel, and the smoke, and Sera.

She found him at the mouth of the main gallery, where the defenders were dragging their wounded toward the medical bay and the dead toward a wall where someone had already begun chalking names. She was unharmed. Her voice, when she spoke, was raw from the singing, from hours of testimony broadcast into a war.

"It's done?" she asked. "Truly done?"

"It's done. Everywhere. All of it."

She looked at him for a long moment, and then she did something she had never done in all the weeks since the asteroid dock, since the tests, since the wary circle of two languages learning each other again. She took his hand. Her grip was dry and strong and old.

"Then we stop hiding," she said. "All of us. Tonight."

Below them, through the gallery, the enclave was already gathering — three hundred people who had spent two centuries being no one, standing in the light of work lamps and fires, listening to the signal echo back at them from relay after relay, from port after port, their own voices returned to them by a universe that had finally, finally been made to listen. Somewhere in the crowd a child was teaching another child the mourning cadence. Somewhere Doran was weeping and pretending he wasn't. Somewhere out in the dark, Kreutz's dropships were lifting from the ice, and her warning hung over everything like weather.

They know where we are. They will come again, and worse.

But tonight, for the first time in two hundred years, the Vessic were not alone in the dark — and across contested space, in forty thousand ears at once, the first replies were already coming in.

Not silence.

Voices.
