## 3. Calliope

The ship docked itself at 0347, station time, and Wendell Fairweather watched it happen from the observation blister with a mug of tea going cold in his hand.

He had spent the hours since the return vector appeared doing what he always did when the universe misbehaved: running diagnostics, checking assumptions, looking for the boring explanation. A ghost echo off the Kessler debris field. A corrupted sensor. His own equipment lying to him the way old equipment will, sweetly and without malice. He ran the vector solution four times through the nav computer and got the same answer each time, which was the problem. The answer didn't change because it was true.

The ship came up the approach corridor at a crawl that no living pilot would choose—too slow, too precise, correcting for drift with a patience that felt less like competence and more like grief. It was a mid-range expedition hull, maybe forty years of wear baked into its silhouette, and Wendell knew every class of hull that had passed through Vesper in eleven years. This wasn't one of them. Its lines were older than his posting. Older than the truce. The registry plate on its flank had been scorched to illegibility except for three letters that survived like bones after a fire:

**CAL**

He set down the tea. His hands were steady. That surprised him later, how steady they were, as if some part of him had already understood what the rest of him refused to say out loud: *ships don't come back from places ships don't come back from.*

The docking clamps engaged with a sound he'd heard ten thousand times—a soft triple thunk, hydraulic breath, the station accepting a visitor—and then the corridor between the airlock and the ship went quiet in a way that made his ears ring. No knock on the inner hatch. No voice on the comm asking permission. Just the hum of Vesper's life support and, faintly, from inside the derelict, something that took him a full minute to identify because it had no business being there.

Singing. Thin, tuneless, a child's voice humming a scale that wandered and never resolved.

Wendell stood in the airlock antechamber for a long moment, one hand on the pressure equalization lever, and let himself have exactly five seconds of honesty. Five seconds where he admitted that whatever was on the other side of this hatch, his life as he had built it—the long route, the rituals, the careful invisibility—was over. Then he pulled the lever.

* * *

The Calliope's airlock cycled open onto darkness and cold. Not lethal cold—the environmental systems were limping along at some minimal setting—but the cold of a house where nobody has been home for a very long time. His breath fogged. His headlamp beam swept across bulkheads furred with frost ferns, and the frost was wrong, crystallized into spirals instead of feathers, as if the water molecules themselves had been twisted on their way down.

"Vesper Station to unidentified vessel," he said, and his voice sounded enormous in the dead air. "This is Wendell Fairweather, station keeper. I'm coming aboard. If anyone can hear me, respond."

The singing stopped.

Silence, then, but not empty silence. The silence of people listening. He felt it against his skin like a draft.

He clipped his tether to the guide rail and moved aft through the access spine. The ship's interior told a story in reverse: emergency lighting functional, gravity plating at two-thirds, personal effects scattered not in panic but in *order*—boots paired by bunks, tools racked, mugs washed and inverted. A crew that had tidied up before something happened. Before they'd gone somewhere, or been taken somewhere, or become something else.

The main cabin hatch was half-open. Warm light leaked through the gap, amber and unsteady, and the humming had started again, joined now by a second voice, lower, and a third. He pushed the hatch wide.

Thirty children looked back at him.

They were arranged in the acceleration couches and on the deck in loose clusters, wrapped in thermal blankets, and they were children—seven, eight, nine years old by his best guess, small faces and knobby wrists and the enormous watchful eyes of kids who have learned that adults are weather, unpredictable and worth tracking. But they wore adult crew uniforms, expedition-grade, the sleeves rolled six and seven times, name tapes stitched across chests too narrow for them. One boy near the viewport had *ORELLANA — FLIGHT ENG* on his chest and was wearing the uniform like a bathrobe, swimming in it, his feet not reaching the floor of the couch he sat in.

Nobody screamed. Nobody cried. They just watched him, thirty pairs of eyes tracking his headlamp the way birds track a hawk, and Wendell felt the vertigo hit him all at once—not fear, exactly, but a structural wrongness, the sensation of standing on a floor that has quietly changed its mind about being a floor. Because he knew these uniforms. Not these specific ones, but the pattern, the cut, the mission patch on the shoulders: a stylized fold-spiral around a single star.

The Meridian Deep Range Expedition patch. Discontinued eleven years ago. Retired along with the thirty-one crew members officially listed as lost with all hands when the experimental fold vessel *Calliope* failed to emerge from transit and both fleets, after eighteen months of mutual accusation, agreed to stop looking.

Both fleets had agreed the ship was gone. Both fleets had built a fragile peace on top of that agreement, each secretly certain the other had destroyed her.

And she was docked at his station, humming with children.

"You're real," said the nearest child, a girl, testing the words. "You're an actual person."

"I'm real." His voice came out rougher than intended. He crouched down to their level, the way you do, the way his body remembered before his mind caught up. "I'm Wendell. I keep the beacon station you've docked with. What's your name?"

She considered him with a seriousness that belonged to someone much older—it flickered across her face like lightning under a door, there and gone. "I don't—" She frowned, pressed her fingers against her temple. "I had a name. I know I had a name. It's..." Her hand drifted to her chest, to the name tape there, and she read it upside down as if it were a stranger's. "*ADEYEMI.* Is that mine?"

"It's on your shirt," Wendell said gently. "That seems like good evidence."

"It doesn't feel like mine." She said it without drama, the way you'd report a malfunction. "It feels like a coat."

Around the cabin, other children were doing the same thing—touching their name tapes, mouthing the syllables, hunting for themselves in the stitched letters. A small boy in the corner rocked slightly and recited, rhythmic as a prayer: "Coolant loop nominal, coolant loop nominal, coolant loop nominal." A girl with a braid coming undone was drawing the fold-spiral mission patch over and over on frosted metal with her fingertip, tracing it like a signature she couldn't remember making.

Wendell counted them twice, because the first count couldn't be right. Thirty. The expedition manifest, the one sealed into the accident record both fleets had signed, listed thirty-one souls.

His headlamp found the last couch, the one set slightly apart near the engineering bulkhead, and the vertigo that had been building in him since the airlock crested into something close to falling.

A girl sat there alone, smaller than the others, maybe six years old, wrapped in a blanket with only her face showing. She wasn't watching him. She was holding something in both hands, cupped against her sternum the way you hold a candle flame in wind, and she was rubbing her thumb across it in slow, absent strokes.

A technician's badge. Fleet-standard issue, eleven years out of date, the lanyard wound twice around her small fingers.

Wendell crossed the deck without deciding to. Later he would not remember walking; he would remember only arriving, dropping to one knee on the frozen deck plating, close enough to see the badge clearly. Close enough to read it.

The photo showed a woman in her thirties with dark hair cut short and a smile that leaned crooked, the left side higher than the right. The name beneath it was printed in the old fleet typeface, the one they'd discontinued after the merger standardization:

**FAIRWEATHER, ADA — SYS TECH, 2ND CLASS**

The deck tilted. Or he did. He put a hand flat on the plating to steady himself and felt the cold of the ship come up through his palm like a message.

Ada. His sister Ada, who had volunteered for the deep range program the autumn after their parents' funeral, who had stood in the departure hall in this exact uniform and made him promise not to make it weird, who had sent him exactly one transmission from beyond the fold boundary—eleven seconds of static and, buried in it, a fragment of her voice saying *tell Wendell the stars here are* before the signal collapsed forever. Who was listed dead. Whose death certificate bore both fleets' seals, whose memorial plaque hung in the Concord Hall of Service, whose name he had carried for eleven years like a stone sewn into his coat.

That woman's badge was being held by a six-year-old girl who looked up at him now with dark eyes, and in the set of those eyes, in the slight upward tilt at the left corner, he saw something that made his chest seize.

"That's my sister's," he heard himself say. His voice sounded like it was coming from the far end of a long corridor. "That's Ada Fairweather's badge."

The girl looked down at the badge, then back at him. And something moved behind her eyes—not recognition, not memory, but the *shape* of memory, the negative space where a person used to be. Her mouth trembled.

"She said somebody would come," the girl whispered. "I don't remember who said it. But she said somebody would come, and we should hold on to things. So I held on." She lifted the badge toward him, offering it with both hands, solemn as a sacrament. "Is she you? Are you the somebody?"

Wendell could not speak. Thirty children were watching him, and somewhere in the walls the environmental system ticked and sighed, and the girl's hands stayed extended, the badge glinting under his headlamp, and the impossible was no longer an anomaly on a sensor plot or a ghost vector in a nav solution. It was warm and breathing and six years old, and it had his family name stitched on a shirt too big for it.

He took the badge. His fingers brushed hers—small, cold, alive—and the contact broke something loose in his chest that he hadn't known was still pressurized.

"No," he managed. "I'm not her. I'm—" He swallowed. "I'm her brother."

The girl nodded slowly, filing this away, and then, with the terrifying adaptability of children, simply accepted it and asked the question that mattered to her:

"Are you going to feed us? We're really hungry. Orellana says the food printers only make crackers now, and they taste like the wall."

Behind him, thirty children stirred, and thirty voices began at once—questions overlapping, complaints, a burst of crying from somewhere aft, the boy chanting about coolant loops rising above the din—and Wendell Fairweather knelt on the deck of a ship that had been declared dead for eleven years, holding his dead sister's badge, surrounded by the lost expedition's crew aged backward into childhood, and understood with total clarity that nothing in his careful, invisible, well-ordered life was ever going to be simple again.

He stood up. He cleared his throat. And he said the only true thing available to him:

"Yes. First, we're going to feed everyone. Then—" He looked down at the badge in his fist, at the crooked smile in the photograph, at the name that meant the universe had just handed him a debt and a mystery in the same motion. "Then we need to talk about what happened to your ship."

The girl named-not-named Adeyemi tugged his sleeve. When he looked down, she was pointing past him, toward the engineering bulkhead, toward a sealed hatch marked with hazard striping he'd catalogued automatically and dismissed.

"That door stays shut," she said. "The bad quiet is behind it. *She* told us. The one on the badge."

Wendell turned. Behind the sealed hatch, faint but unmistakable, something was still running. A low, cycling hum, patient as a heartbeat.

The fold drive hadn't gone dark.
