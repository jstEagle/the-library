## 11. The Inspector

The ping came at 0417 station time, which was a time Wendell had chosen precisely because no one else would be awake to choose anything.

*Relay Station Vesper, this is Concord survey vessel Meridian's Eye. Requesting emergency docking for routine compliance audit. Investigator Sefa Oyelaran, Fleet Inspectorate, Fourth Division. Please acknowledge.*

Wendell read the message three times while his tea went cold beside him. Routine compliance audits did not arrive unannounced at four in the morning. Routine compliance audits were scheduled eleven months in advance and arrived with cargo manifests and courtesy gifts and inspectors who wanted nothing more than to finish their paperwork and get back to somewhere with weather.

He looked at the acknowledgment field for a long moment. Then he typed: *Vesper acknowledges. Docking collar two is pressurized and ready. Welcome to the long route, Investigator.*

His hands were steady. He noted that with detached professional interest, the way he'd note a fluctuation in a beacon's output. Steady now. The shaking could come later, when there was time for it.

Below him—two decks down, behind a pressure door that officially led to a failed water reclamation module—thirty children slept in bunks he'd built from spare parts over six weeks of nights. Thirty heartbeats. He'd counted them on the medical scanner more times than he'd ever admit, watching the slow green pulses stack up on the display like a constellation he'd invented.

One deck below that, hidden in a sensor shadow that had fooled Concord satellites for six weeks, sat the *Calliope*, cold and dark and patient as a held breath.

*They're here about the energy readings,* he told himself. *The flare cover story held. It has to have held.* He'd filed it through proper channels, cross-referenced it against solar observatory data from three other relays, even included the kind of tedious technical appendix that made inspectors stop reading by page two. Boredom was camouflage too.

But nobody sent Sefa Oyelaran for boredom.

He pulled up her file while the *Meridian's Eye* made its final approach. Fleet Inspectorate, Fourth Division—the division that audited stations near contested space, which meant the division that audited for signs of unauthorized contact between the Concord and the Reach. Eleven years of service. Two commendations. One disciplinary note, sealed, which meant it was either trivial or terrible.

Her personnel photo showed a woman in her fifties with close-cropped gray hair and the particular stillness of someone who had learned that waiting was a weapon.

Wendell spent the forty minutes before docking doing what he always did before company: cleaning things that were already clean, straightening the console cables, wiping down the galley table, checking that every surface of his life said *nothing to see here*. The station had one personality and it was his—orderly, sparse, faintly monastic. A man who lived alone with machines. That man had been true for eleven years. Now it was a costume, and the zipper was already digging into his back.

At 0502, the airlock cycled.

Sefa Oyelaran came through the docking collar like weather moving in—not fast, not slow, just inevitable. She was shorter than her photo suggested, broad-shouldered, wearing an inspectorate jumpsuit with no rank braid because rank was for people who needed to be impressed. Her eyes did a full sweep of the entry bay in the time it took to say hello, cataloging everything, committing it somewhere permanent.

"Fairweather, Wendell. Station master, Vesper relay chain." She offered a hand. Her grip was dry and brief. "You've been out here a long time."

"Eleven years."

"That's what the file says." She let the sentence sit there, unfinished, the way you'd leave a tool on a table within reach. "Eleven years is a long time to be the only voice on a channel."

"Someone has to be."

"Mm." She set down a single case—no entourage, no security detail, which somehow made it worse—and looked around the bay again. "Your station runs clean, Fairweather. I'll say that up front. Telemetry's beautiful. Logs are meticulous. Almost too meticulous, some of my colleagues would say." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "I'm not some of my colleagues. I believe people who care about their work write good logs. Shall we?"

"Shall we what?"

"The audit. That's why I'm here." She tapped her case. "Standard Fourth Division review. Power consumption, life support cycling, communications integrity, personnel wellness." A pause, calibrated to the millisecond. "Personnel wellness being a category of one, in your case."

"One," Wendell agreed. "Population of Vesper: me."

"Then let's make sure you're well."

* * *

She started with the power systems, which was either genuine thoroughness or a feint, and Wendell couldn't decide which possibility frightened him more. She read schematics faster than he could produce them. She asked questions that sounded casual and weren't:

"This auxiliary draw here, cycle 4472. What was that?"

"Spare parts fabrication. I print my own gaskets."

"You printed gaskets for nine hours?"

"I'm bad at gaskets."

She wrote something down. Everything got written down; he began to understand that the notebook wasn't for evidence, it was for rhythm. She was learning his cadence, his tells, the shape of his answers, so that when she asked the real question she'd know what a lie looked like coming out of him.

So he gave her rhythm. He talked about beacon maintenance the way he actually felt about it—with a quiet, unglamorous love—and found, to his surprise, that the truth was the best material he had. Every fact about the last eleven years except the last six weeks was solid bedrock under his feet. He stood on it deliberately.

"Life support," she said, two hours in. "Show me your atmospheric telemetry."

He brought it up. She studied the curves—the CO₂ scrubber cycles, the oxygen generation rates, the humidity—and Wendell watched her watch them and understood, with a cold drop in his stomach, exactly what she was looking for.

Thirty additional metabolisms left fingerprints. Even children. Even small ones. They breathed, they ate, they exhaled, they used water. He'd compensated—he'd adjusted the scrubber duty cycles, quietly increased the O₂ generator baseline, logged it all as component aging—but compensation was a story told in numbers, and numbers could be read aloud.

"Your oxygen generation is running twelve percent above your logged baseline," Oyelaran said.

"Yes. Generator three's efficiency has degraded. I filed a work order—" He pulled it up. Dated. Signed. Boring beyond measure.

She read it. All of it. Including the appendix.

"Hm," she said.

That was all. *Hm.* And then she moved on to communications, and Wendell followed her down the corridor feeling like a man walking across ice that was holding—for reasons he couldn't verify and couldn't control.

* * *

The communications audit took them past the corridor junction where the maintenance hatch to Deck Two stood behind its fake inspection tag. Wendell had walked this route ten thousand times without thinking. Today every meter was a country to be diplomatically managed.

Oyelaran stopped at the junction. Not at the hatch—at the junction, a meter short of it, as if drawn by some instinct that told her exactly how far she could go while remaining deniable.

"Failed water reclamation," she said, reading the tag. "How long?"

"Three years. Parts are on backorder. The Reach controls the manufacturing hubs for membrane filters, and after the Halcyon incident—" He shrugged, letting the politics finish the sentence for him, because everyone in the corridor between two fleets knew how that sentence ended.

"Inconvenient."

"It's the whole job. Inconvenience."

She touched the tag with one finger. Just touched it. Then turned away, and Wendell's heartbeat resumed something like a normal schedule.

In the comms room, she ran her own diagnostics against his relay array, and he watched her do it and hated how competent she was. She checked signal latency, encryption health, the handshake protocols with the next relay down the chain. And then, without any change in tone at all:

"Run me your outbound traffic log for the last sixty days."

There it was.

Sixty days. The exact window. The *Calliope* had come home fifty-two days ago, and everything Wendell had built since then—the fabricated freighter pings, the ghost traffic, the plausible little economy of lies moving through his section of the dark—lived inside those sixty days like a tumor inside an organ.

"Sure," he said, and pulled them up, and discovered that his hands were finally shaking, very slightly, where they rested on the console edge, out of her sightline. So there it was. Later had arrived early.

She scrolled. He watched her scroll. Each page turn was a coin flip performed in public.

The traffic logs were good. He knew they were good because he'd built them the way he built everything—obsessively, redundantly, with the tedium that made inspectors stop reading by page two. Ghost freighters with real registry numbers borrowed from ships confirmed on the far side of the chain. Maintenance bursts scheduled against actual maintenance. A pattern of noise so ordinary it was practically a lullaby.

Oyelaran read every page anyway.

Halfway through, she said, without looking up: "Do you ever get lonely out here, Fairweather?"

The question landed sideways, from nowhere, aimed at the part of him that wasn't a log entry.

"Sometimes," he said. Truth. Bedrock.

"What do you do about it?"

"Talk to the beacons. They don't talk back, but they're good listeners." Truth again, and he heard his own voice do something it hadn't done in weeks—warm slightly, at the edges. He let it. Let her see the lonely man, because the lonely man was real, and real was the only armor that didn't show seams.

She looked up at him then, for the first time in twenty minutes, and her eyes were not unkind. That was the worst part. They were sharp and tired and not unkind at all.

"My daughter's station-master on Ceres Gate," she said. "Third year alone. She says the same thing about her beacons." She closed the log. "Everything checks out so far."

"So far."

"So far." She stood, stretched her back with a small crack, and picked up her case. "I'd like to see the lower decks before I go. Standard structural survey. Ten minutes, then I'm out of your hair."

The floor did not open up and swallow Wendell Fairweather, though he briefly resented it for the oversight.

"Lower decks are mostly storage," he said. "And the dead reclamation module. There's a low-grade coolant leak down there—respirator recommended, and honestly it's nothing to look at."

"All the same."

"All the same," he agreed, and heard, distantly, as if from another life, thirty sleeping heartbeats keeping time beneath his feet.

* * *

Deck Two smelled the way he'd engineered it to smell: faintly of coolant, of disuse, of honest decay. He'd spent three nights making decay honest—staining bulkheads, seeding dust, propping a genuinely broken pump in the open where a flashlight beam would find it first. Lies, he was learning, were ninety percent set dressing.

Oyelaran walked slowly. She ran her hand along a pipe. She looked into the ruined reclamation bay and nodded at the broken pump as if greeting an old colleague.

"Storage," she said, and opened the nearest locker.

Rations. Spare seals. A crate of beacon transponders, serial-numbered and logged. She checked serials against his manifest with the speed of long practice, and every number matched, because Wendell had spent a week of his life making sure they would.

Second locker. Third. A fourth, deeper in, past the junction—

A sound. Small. High. The unmistakable acoustic signature of a child shifting in sleep, or waking, or turning over in a bunk built from salvaged frame members, two rooms away, through a door that was rated to muffle machinery and had never been tested against a five-year-old's dream.

Oyelaran's head tilted. Barely. A degree of arc, maybe less.

"Coolant leak," she said mildly. "You mentioned."

"Resonance in the plumbing," Wendell said. "When the pumps upstairs cycle, the dead lines sing. Drives me half mad at night."

"Mm." She looked at him for a long moment—long enough that he could feel his own pulse in his teeth—and then she smiled her smile that went nowhere, and closed the locker. "Structural integrity's fine. Your decay is very convincing, Fairweather."

Something in his chest went very still. "Investigator?"

"Decay." She was already walking back toward the lift, case in hand. "Stations rot unevenly. This rots evenly. Somebody's been maintaining the ruin." She stepped into the lift and turned, and for a moment she was just a tired woman in her fifties who missed her daughter. "I'm going to note in my report that Vesper passes audit. Clean logs, sound structure, one healthy, slightly eccentric station master."

"And the other thing?" His voice came out lower than he intended. "The thing you're noting privately."

Oyelaran considered him. The lift doors waited, patient as everything else about her.

"I note a lot of things privately," she said. "It's most of the job. Get some sleep, Fairweather. You look like a man who hasn't slept in fifty-two days."

The doors closed on the number, and Wendell stood alone in the honest-smelling dark, doing the math on exactly how much she knew, exactly what she'd trade for it, and why an inspector who passed him clean would bother telling him she'd seen through the ruin.

Above him, the *Meridian's Eye* undocked at 0931 and folded away toward Concord space, gone in a smear of light.

Wendell rode the lift down to Deck Two, keyed the hidden door, and stood in the warm lamplight of the bunkroom watching thirty children breathe. A girl with Ada's eyes was awake, watching him back.

"She knew," the girl said. Not a question. Children always knew.

"Yeah," Wendell said. "She knew."

He checked the docking clamps, the hull sensors, the long-range scopes. Nothing. Empty sky. And then, sweeping the exterior hull on a hound's instinct he couldn't have named, his scope caught it: a hair-thin seam on the ventral plating, a component that hadn't been there that morning, transmitting on a frequency so tight and so quiet it was almost a whisper.

Something had stayed behind.
