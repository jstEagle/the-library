## 7. People Who Raised Me

The *Long Answer* had a galley the size of a coffin and a table that folded out of the wall like an apology. Six chairs for a crew of five — the sixth had come with the ship, and nobody ever sat in it, because the sixth chair was where Captain Ilsa Reyes put her boots up when she thought no one was looking.

Tonight everyone was looking.

Vesper sat with their back to the bulkhead, the way they always did, and watched the crew argue about their life. That was the strange arithmetic of it: the data on the chip was Vesper's inheritance, Vesper's dead original, Vesper's impossible name on an impossible manifest — and it was being discussed over reconstituted stew like a salvage claim.

Because that's what it was, legally. Salvage. The derelict freighter sat in contested orbit with no registered owner, its logs wiped, its hull cold for eleven years. Under colonial maritime law — such as it was, out here where the law arrived by courier and left by the same route — finders kept what they could carry off a dead ship.

What they could carry off this one was worth more than the ship.

"Three hundred thousand," said Dov, not looking at anyone. He was turning his spoon over in his bowl, over and over, like he was trying to find the side of it that made sense. "Minimum. The Trust pays finder's fees for records this old. More if they're sensitive."

"More if they're *embarrassing*," said Priya Chandra from the engineering corner, where she'd wedged herself between a coolant recycler and the wall. She was small and quick and had hands that were always moving, always fixing something that wasn't broken yet. "Sensitive is a euphemism, Dov. You know it. I know it. The Trust doesn't pay three hundred thousand for paperclips."

"The Trust pays for quiet," Ilsa said.

That landed the way she meant it to. Ilsa Reyes didn't talk much, and when she did, the words came out pre-weighted, ballast dropped exactly where she wanted them. She'd run the *Long Answer* for nineteen years, hauling cargo through orbits other captains wouldn't touch, and she'd kept five people alive and paid through two recessions, a quarantine, and the year the fuel cartel tried to squeeze the outer routes dry. Nobody on this crew had been recruited. They'd all been *collected* — picked up broke and stranded somewhere, given a bunk and a job and, eventually, without anyone saying it aloud, a home.

Vesper had been collected eleven years ago. Age six, clone-certified, ward of the colony, unclaimed by any family because the family that might have claimed them was a murder file. Ilsa had signed for them the way you sign for a delivery, and then spent a decade pretending she hadn't done anything remarkable.

"Quiet," Priya repeated now. "Right. And how long does quiet last? We sell them the manifest, they bury it deeper, and in ten years some other kid finds another ghost ship and we're back here. Meanwhile we've got—" she nodded at Vesper, not quite meeting their eyes, "—we've got a person sitting right there whose whole origin story is on that chip."

"I'm in the room," Vesper said.

"I know you're in the room."

"Then say it to me."

Priya finally looked up. Her eyes were tired in the specific way of someone who'd been awake forty hours keeping a dying reactor polite. "Fine. I think we should look before we sell. That's all. I'm not saying don't sell. I'm saying a manifest from a ship that never flew, listing a woman who died before it launched — that's not cargo, Vesper. That's a door. And I don't like selling doors I haven't opened."

* * *

Dov took first watch after dinner, which meant he sat alone in the cockpit with the running lights off and the traffic feed murmuring, watching nothing happen in the black. It was his favorite hour of any day, the hour when the ship was just a room and the void was just weather, and he could pretend his life was as simple as it looked from outside.

It lasted four minutes.

"You knew," Vesper said, from the cockpit doorway.

Dov didn't startle. He'd raised this kid — gods, *raised*, there was no other word for it, taught them to fly on the short hauls, taught them cards, taught them which station cops took bribes and which took offense — and you didn't startle someone you'd taught to read your breathing. "Knew what?"

"You went quiet when I showed them the name. Not thinking-quiet. *Recognizing*-quiet." Vesper came forward and leaned against the console, arms crossed, twenty-two years old and wearing Ulyana Eriksen's frown like a borrowed coat. "You knew the name before I said it."

Dov turned his spoon habit over in his mind and set it down. "Sit down, sprout."

"Don't call me—"

"Sit down anyway."

They sat. The traffic feed murmured. Somewhere below them, the colony turned through its slow night, half a million people asleep or pretending to be, and somewhere beyond that, a dead freighter held its secrets like a breath.

"Dov." Softer now. "Please."

He looked at them. Clone-certified, ward of the colony, and underneath all that paperwork, just a kid who'd shown up on his ship with dead eyes and inherited grief and slowly, year by year, learned to laugh at his terrible jokes. He had lied to better people than Vesper and slept fine. This one was going to cost him.

"I recognized the passenger name," he said. "Marek Osei. Because Marek Osei was my cousin."

Vesper went very still. It was eerie sometimes, how completely they could stop — the clone stillness, the body forgetting to perform being alive.

"He died eleven years ago. Officially. Reactor accident on a hauler out of Meridian Yard. Closed casket, closed file, compensation paid to his mother, who is my aunt, and who cried at a funeral with no body in it because the Trust told her the vacuum had—" He stopped. Started again. "I never questioned it. Why would I? People die out here. But when you said his name was on a manifest for a ship that launched *after* his death date, I—" His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with detached interest. "I did some checking. Quietly. And Vesper, the compensation payment came from a discretionary Trust account. Not insurance. Discretionary."

"Which means what?"

"Which means somebody wanted his death to be official enough to pay for, and unofficial enough to never explain." Dov exhaled. "There are maybe forty names on that manifest. If even a few of them are like Marek — dead on paper, gone in fact — then this isn't a clerical error. It's a pattern. And patterns like that have owners."

"So we open the door."

"So we *don't*." He turned in his chair, and let himself say the thing he'd been carrying since the archive, the thing that had curdled in him for days. "Vesper. Listen to me. I love you. Everyone on this ship loves you, and none of us — *none of us* — signed up to be martyrs for a mystery. The Trust buried these people once already. They will do it again, and they will not be gentle about the digging. You want to pull that thread? Fine. But understand what unravels. Not just the lie. Us. This crew. Everything Ilsa built. Everything we—" his voice caught, and he hated it, "—everything we made for you."

Vesper was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was careful, level, and older than twenty-two.

"You think I don't know what this crew cost? I've watched Ilsa skip her own medical checks for six years so the rest of us could keep ours. I've watched Priya eat ration-grade protein for a month to cover a fuel shortfall. I know exactly what we are, Dov. We're five people holding a rope over a very long drop, and every one of us is holding on for the others." They stood. "But Marek Osei had a mother. And she cried at an empty grave. And if I walk away from that because it's expensive, then I'm not holding the rope anymore. I'm just hanging on."

They left the cockpit. Dov sat alone with the traffic feed and the black, and understood, with the clarity that only arrives too late to be useful, that he had already made his choice — days ago, in an archive terminal, in a message to an old contact — and that everything since had been him arguing with a decision he couldn't take back.

* * *

Ilsa called the vote the next morning, because Ilsa believed in votes the way she believed in torque ratings: as the only honest way to know what a structure could bear.

She laid it out flat, no rhetoric. Option one: sell the manifest to the Trust, take the fee, wipe local copies, move on. Option two: sit on it, sell later to whoever paid most, same silence, better price. Option three: open the door.

"Before we vote," she said, "I want it on record that option three has killed smarter crews than ours."

"On record," said Tam, the loadmaster, who was seventy and had opinions like scar tissue. "And I want it on record that I'm old, and that men my age get sentimental, and that my vote is three anyway." He shrugged at their looks. "I hauled sleepers' supplies for thirty years. Food, medicine, letters. You know what's in those cryo bays down there? People who wrote letters to a morning they were promised. Thirty years I carried mail *to* the future. I'd like to know what happened to it."

"Priya?"

Priya chewed her lip. "Three. But staged. We verify before we broadcast anything, we keep copies in three places, and we do not — *do not* — go anywhere near the derelict without a exit plan."

"Dov?"

The pause stretched. Vesper watched him across the table and saw him decide, in real time, to lie with his silence rather than his mouth — to vote three, to mean it, and to hope his contact in the Trust moved slower than the truth did.

"Three," Dov said.

"Vesper?"

Everyone looked at them. Vesper had prepared something — they always prepared something, the clone's habit of arriving with the right words pre-loaded — but sitting there, under the sixth chair with Ilsa's boots propped on it, surrounded by the only people who had ever chosen them rather than merely received them, the prepared words dissolved.

"I vote three," they said. "Not for the truth. Truth's cheap, everybody says they want it. I vote three because if there's even one name on that list that belongs to somebody's mother, somebody's cousin—" a glance at Dov, who flinched and covered it, "—then we're the only ones left who can say it out loud. That's the whole job. That's what this crew has always been. People who say things out loud when it costs."

Nobody spoke. Then Tam reached over and cuffed Vesper gently on the shoulder, the way he'd done since they were small enough to fit inside a cargo net, and Priya muttered "*gods help us*" and started listing parts she'd need, and Ilsa said, "Motion carries. Dov, plot us back to the derelict. Quietly."

*Quietly* turned out to be the operative word, though none of them knew yet how much it would matter.

* * *

They made the burn toward contested orbit that evening, and the ship settled into its transit rhythm — watches rotated, systems hummed, Priya sang badly to the coolant pumps. Vesper stood in the hold for a long time, among the ordinary freight, holding the chip that had started everything, feeling the strange doubled weight of it: theirs, and not theirs. An inheritance with a body count.

Dov found them there. He didn't say anything at first. Just stood beside them in the dim cargo lighting, shoulder almost touching shoulder, the way they used to stand at observation windows when Vesper was small and the stars were still new.

"Whatever's on that ship," Dov said finally, "whatever we find — you're not doing it alone. I need you to hear that before anything else happens."

"I hear it."

"And Vesper?" He was looking at the deck plating. "If it turns out knowing me was part of what hurt you — if anything I did, anything I am, turns out to be tangled up in this —"

"Dov."

"— I need you to know the rest of it was real. All of it. The cards, the flying lessons, all of it real."

Vesper looked at him — at the man who'd taught them to hold a hand of cards and hide their face, who'd carried them out of a burning docking bay when they were nine, who was lying to them right now with every line of his body — and felt the ground of their belonging shift by a single degree, so slightly they couldn't yet name it, only feel it, like a ship changing heading so gradually that the passengers only notice when the stars are wrong.

"I know it was real," they said.

In the cockpit, the comm board lit with an incoming transmission on a frequency the *Long Answer* hadn't heard in years — a Colonial Trust handshake code, priority-flagged, addressed not to the ship but to one crew member by name.

Dov's name.

The board chimed again. Patient. Certain. Like something that had already decided how this ended.
