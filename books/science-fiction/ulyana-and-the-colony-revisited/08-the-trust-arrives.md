## 8. The Trust Arrives

The *Marrow* was eleven hours out of the debris field when the hail came.

Vesper felt it before they heard it — a change in the ship's breathing, the way the comm array's idle hum sharpened into something purposeful. They'd been in the galley, eating reconstituted noodles out of the pot because washing a bowl felt like a luxury the fuel budget couldn't afford, and they set the pot down slowly, the way you set something down when your body knows what your mind hasn't caught up to yet.

Dov was already at the console. Of course he was. Dov lived at that console the way other people lived in their own skins.

"Talk to me," Vesper said.

"Inbound contact, bearing one-nine-seven. It's been shadowing us for six hours." Dov's voice was flat, which was how Vesper knew it was bad. Dov got loud when things were fine. "I picked up the drive signature an hour ago and told myself it was a hauler running the same lane."

"And now?"

"Now it's hailing us on a Trust frequency." He turned in his chair. His face had gone the color of old paper. "Vesper. It's a *Corvid*-class enforcement cutter. That's not a patrol ship. That's a ship they send when they already know what you have."

The galley smelled like cheap broth and coolant. Vesper stood very still and let the moment arrange itself around them — the hum of the recyclers, the tick of the hull expanding as it shed heat, Dov watching them with an expression that wanted to be calm and wasn't.

They'd known this would come. Somewhere in the back of their skull, behind Ulyana's memories and their own, they'd known it from the moment they pulled that manifest off the derelict. A ship that never flew doesn't stay secret for thirty years by accident. Somebody kept it secret. And somebodies with secrets buy cutters.

"Answer it," Vesper said.

"You sure?"

"They've been following us for six hours, Dov. They know where we are. Silence just tells them we're scared."

Dov hesitated, then opened the channel.

The face that resolved above the console was middle-aged, silver at the temples, wearing the charcoal-grey uniform of the Colonial Trust with the collar pin of a regional director. Behind him, the bridge of the cutter was clean and bright and full of people who were not looking at the camera. He had the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since been promoted into authority.

"*Freighter Marrow,*" he said. "This is Director Anselm Reiss, Colonial Trust Enforcement, vessel *Candor*. You are operating in a restricted survey corridor without a declared cargo manifest. Power down your drives and prepare for boarding and inspection."

Vesper stepped into frame. "Director. We're a cargo hauler. Our manifest is grain substrate and water reclamation parts. You're welcome to inspect the hold, but I'll need to see your warrant first — corridor seven isn't Trust jurisdiction unless there's a declared emergency."

Something flickered across Reiss's face. Not anger. Interest.

"You're the clone," he said.

The words landed in Vesper's chest like a dropped tool.

"I'm sorry?"

"The Eriksen clone. Vessel registration puts a V. Eriksen on your crew list. The Trust keeps records." He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced, warm at the edges. "I imagine that must feel strange, being recognized. Being *known.*"

"It feels strange when strangers know my paperwork better than I do."

"We're not strangers, Vesper. May I call you Vesper?" He didn't wait for an answer. "You've had a difficult few weeks. Your crewmate filed a report with our archive office — routine data discovery, nothing alarming — but it flagged some irregularities. A freighter ID that shouldn't exist. Records access patterns consistent with someone digging into sealed colonial history." He spread his hands, a man offering reasonableness. "We're not here to punish anyone. We're here to help. That manifest you found — it's a clerical artifact. A ghost entry. Thirty-year-old database corruption. We'd like to recover it, verify it, and put everyone's minds at ease."

Behind Vesper, Dov made a small sound. Vesper didn't turn around. They didn't need to. They could hear the confession in it — *your crewmate filed a report* — and they would deal with that later, or they wouldn't, because right now there was a Trust cutter matching their velocity at four kilometers and closing.

"Clerical artifact," Vesper repeated. "And it takes a *Corvid*-class cutter to collect corrupted data."

"Data is the colony's most valuable resource, Vesper. More valuable than ore. More valuable than—" he paused, delicately, "—than people, some would argue. We take its protection seriously." The smile again. "Transmit the manifest and the associated files, and we'll be on our way. No boarding. No inspection. No disruption to your schedule. Everyone goes home happy."

"And if I say no?"

Reiss sighed, genuinely regretful, a man burdened by the incompetence of others. "Then I board your vessel under emergency provisions, seize the data, and detain your crew pending review. Detention review can take... months. Sometimes years. The Trust's legal apparatus is thorough." He tilted his head. "You're a clone, Vesper. No offense intended, but your legal personhood is already conditional. Do you understand what I'm telling you? If this goes badly, you won't be detained. You'll be *reclaimed.* Property disputes go through a different office entirely."

There it was. Said plainly, finally, after a lifetime of it being said sideways: *you are not a person, you are an asset, and assets get audited.*

Vesper had spent twenty-eight years being underestimated. Teachers who graded their inherited knowledge as cheating. Employers who paid them in room and board and called it generosity. Doctors who scanned their implant and wrote *standard clone architecture, no anomalies* without ever really looking. People looked at a clone and saw a copy — a thing made, not born, a second printing of a book whose first edition had been murdered.

They never saw what a clone actually was: someone who knew exactly how much of their life belonged to someone else, and had decided, day by day, what to do about the rest.

"Let me consult my crew," Vesper said. "We're a cooperative vessel. Decisions go through the whole roster."

"Of course." Reiss's smile didn't waver. "You have ten minutes. Then I stop asking."

The channel closed.

* * *

The galley was too small for a war council, so they had one anyway.

Sefa arrived first, drawn up from engineering by the comm traffic, her dark hair tied back with a strip of cargo strap, grease on her knuckles. She took one look at the tactical display — the *Candor*, patient and huge in its orbit, its weapons cold but present the way a loaded gun is present — and said, "How much did they offer?"

"Nothing," Vesper said. "They offered nothing. That's worse."

Ryn came last, from the cockpit, where she'd been running evasion scenarios she hadn't told anyone about. She was the oldest of them, sixty-one, ex-Trust herself, discharged under circumstances nobody asked about twice. She poured coffee from the dented thermos and drank it standing up, eyes on the display.

"A *Corvid* has two shuttles and a marine complement of twelve," she said. "We have five people, a hold full of grain substrate, and engines that top out at point-three g under load. We cannot run. We cannot fight. So somebody tell me why I'm not powering down right now."

"Because of what's in the data," Sefa said.

"We don't know what's in the data," Ryn said. "We know there's a fragment. A capacity figure. Half the sleepers. That's a rumor until it's verified, and rumors aren't worth getting reclaimed over."

"It's not a rumor," Vesper said quietly. "It's a checksummed fragment from the cryo hub's own maintenance logs. The bays sustain four thousand sleepers. There are eight thousand in the ice. Someone did that math thirty years ago and killed my original to keep it quiet."

Silence. The recyclers hummed. Somewhere below them, the grain substrate shifted in its tanks with a sound like slow sand.

"They'll wake half," Sefa said. "Their half. Their people, their debtors, whoever's on whatever list exists. And the other four thousand stay frozen forever, or worse — the bays fail, the power draw gets reallocated, and those people die in their sleep without ever knowing there was a decision made about them." Her voice cracked on the last word. "My father worked in that hub. He used to say the hardest part of his job was that nobody ever asked him the question. *Which* half. Nobody ever asked."

"Because asking makes it real," Ryn said. She set down her coffee. "I spent nineteen years inside the Trust. Let me tell you what happens next if we hand it over. Reiss takes the manifest, takes the fragment, takes every copy, and then he takes statements. And somewhere in those statements, someone mentions that the Eriksen clone accessed sealed records using credentials derived from a dead investigator's neural imprint. That's not a clerical matter anymore. That's *misappropriation of colonial heritage assets.* That's the charge they use when they want to disappear someone legally." She looked at Vesper. "They will not reclaim you in public. They'll do it quietly. A transfer order. A medical review. You'll go into a facility and a different version of you will come out, one that signs off on everything, and none of us will be able to prove there was ever another one."

"That's cheerful," Dov said. It was the first thing he'd said since the galley filled up. His voice was rough. "Thank you, Ryn. Very comforting."

"It's true, which is better than comforting."

Dov sat down heavily on the bench. He looked older than his forty years, suddenly, the way men look when a bill comes due. "I have to say something," he said. "Before this goes any further."

"Dov—"

"No. I have to." He wouldn't look at Vesper. He addressed the table, the deck, the middle distance. "The archive report. The flag Reiss mentioned. That was me. I sent it. Three weeks ago, after you started pulling the colonial records, I sent a summary to a contact I have in the Trust's archive division. Marta Osei. I've known her since we were kids on Halcyon Ring. I thought—" His hands opened and closed. "I thought if the Trust already knew, they'd leave you alone. I thought being transparent was protection. I thought I was keeping you safe."

The galley went very quiet.

Vesper looked at him — at Dov, who had taught them to fly, who had held their hair back when the implant migraines hit, who had never once called them *it* even in the early days when everyone else slipped — and felt something crack and something else settle, both at once.

"You reported me," Vesper said.

"I tried to protect you. It's not the same thing. I know it's not the same thing." Finally he looked up. His eyes were wet and furious, mostly at himself. "I grew up with these people, Vesper. The Trust isn't a monster to me, it's Marta's laugh and subsidized school lunches and my mother's pension. I thought the system worked if you talked to it honestly. I thought wrong. I'm sorry. I am so sorry."

Sefa put her hand on his shoulder. Ryn didn't move, but her expression shifted from contempt to something more complicated — recognition, maybe. Everyone in that room had loved an institution that didn't love them back.

Vesper took a breath. Later. This was later-problem. Right now there was a clock.

"Dov. Does Marta know what the data says? The capacity figures?"

"No. I only sent her the discovery notice. Ship name, record IDs, access pattern. No content."

"Then she flagged it up the chain without knowing what she was flagging, and it landed on Reiss's desk, and now Reiss knows there's a ghost ship and a dead detective's clone in the same coordinate history, and he wants it buried before anyone reads it." Vesper turned to the display, to the *Candor*'s patient grey silhouette. "Which means he doesn't have orders to destroy us. He has orders to make a problem go away quietly. Quiet means no marines unless he needs them. Quiet means he'd rather we just... comply."

"People don't comply when you threaten to reclaim them," Ryn said.

"People comply all the time," Vesper said. "That's the whole system. But he gave us ten minutes because he thinks ten minutes is generous. He thinks we're frightened little people on a grain hauler deciding whether to be sensible." They felt the old familiar heat rise in their chest, the one that used to feel like shame and had recently started feeling like fuel. "He thinks I'm a copy. Copies comply."

"What are you thinking?" Sefa asked.

Vesper touched the base of their skull, where the implant sat under the skin — the standard clone architecture, the neural bridge that carried Ulyana's imprinted memories, the thing every doctor had scanned and shrugged at. Standard. Unremarkable. Never searched, because searching a clone's implant required a heritage-assets warrant, and nobody had ever thought a clone worth the paperwork.

"He wants the manifest and the files," Vesper said. "The files live on the ship's server. The decryption key lives somewhere he can't reach without a warrant he doesn't have and won't risk." They met Ryn's eyes. "Can you stall him? Legally stall him? You were Trust. Give him procedure. Give him forms. Give him the most boring hour of his career."

A slow grin spread across Ryn's face, the first one Vesper had seen from her in weeks. "Oh, I can give him *forms.*"

"Sefa, I need the implant interface open and isolated. Air-gapped from the ship's network. If he scans the hull and sees medical-grade neural activity, fine — clones think, it's what we do. But nothing traces from the key to anywhere external."

Sefa was already moving. "Isolated bus, local buffer, manual wipe. You'll have maybe ninety seconds of window while it writes. After that it's just... part of you."

"That's the idea."

Dov stood. He looked wrecked and resolute, both at once. "Tell me what to do. Whatever it is. I owe you that much and a lot more."

Vesper looked at him for a long moment. "You're going to answer the hail with me. And when Reiss asks who filed the discovery report, you're going to tell him the truth — that you did, that you're the crew's records officer, and that the report contained no data content. Because it's true, and because a man like Reiss smells fear, and guilt isn't fear. Guilt with a straight spine looks like innocence."

"And if he boards anyway?"

"Then he finds a hold full of grain substrate, a server with a corrupted ghost file we can't decrypt, and a crew that cooperates completely." Vesper allowed themselves a thin smile. "He'll search everything except the one place he'd never think to look. Nobody searches the copy for the original's secrets. Why would they? As far as the Trust is concerned, I'm just an echo."

They walked back to the console together, all five of them, which surprised Vesser — no, Vesper — which surprised Vesper, because they'd expected to walk alone. Ryn took the secondary station and began composing a request for warrant documentation that ran to fourteen pages. Sefa settled at engineering with her hands hovering over the isolation rig. Dov stood at Vesper's shoulder, close enough that his guilt was almost touchable.

"Nine minutes left," Dov said. "Maybe less. He'll come in angry."

"Good," Vesper said. "Angry people talk. Talking people reveal. And everything he reveals—"

They touched the base of their skull.

"—goes into the echo."

The channel opened. Reiss's face returned, and behind his composure Vesper could see the first hairline fracture of a man realizing the grain hauler wasn't groveling.

"Director Reiss," Vesper said pleasantly. "Before we discuss the manifest, my crew's legal officer has some procedural questions about your boarding authority. She's prepared a document. It's quite thorough."

Behind them, Ryn hit transmit on fourteen pages of pure bureaucratic vengeance.

Reiss's smile stayed exactly where it was, which was how Vesper knew they'd already won something. And beneath their skin, in the dark standard architecture of a clone's implant, the decryption key waited — small, invisible, and entirely theirs.

What Vesper didn't know — what none of them knew — was that the *Candor* carried a second passenger besides Reiss. Someone who had read the original Ulyana's file. Someone who recognized the name Eriksen the moment it appeared on the intercept list.

Someone who was, even now, walking toward the *Candor*'s shuttle bay.
