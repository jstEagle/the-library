## 1. The Inheritance of a Dead Man

The first thing I ever felt was grief.

Not hunger, not the cold ache of cryo-thaw, not even the particular indignity of waking up in a body you didn't used to own. Grief. It arrived before my name did, a great grey weight settled on my chest like a sleeping animal, and for eleven seconds—someone told me later it was eleven seconds—I lay in the recovery berth of the transport *Charon's Ferry* and mourned a woman I had never met.

Her name was Amara Chen. I knew that the way I knew my own hands, which is to say: completely, and without having done anything to deserve it.

"Vitals stabilizing," said a voice above me. A med-tech with tired eyes and a coffee stain shaped vaguely like the Kuiper Belt on her tunic. "Can you tell me your designation?"

I opened my mouth to answer and discovered the second strange thing about being me: there were two answers queued up, and both of them felt true.

"Silas Okonkwo," I said. Then, because honesty seemed important at the moment of one's birth: "The second one."

She wrote something on her tablet without blinking. Med-techs who work clone transports stop blinking at anything around year two, I'd guess. "Welcome back, Mr. Okonkwo."

*Back.* As if I'd been here before. As if the man whose memories were currently rearranging themselves behind my eyes like furniture movers with a grudge had simply stepped out for a while and I was house-sitting.

I sat up too fast and the cabin tilted. The graft headache arrived on schedule—a hot wire pulled tight from temple to temple, the neural surgeons' parting gift. Somewhere behind it, a dead man's life was unpacking itself into my skull, box by box, and I lay back against the pillow and let it happen, because there was nothing else to do. You don't fight a memory graft. You'd lose. Ask anyone who's tried; ask the three clones they had to retire from the program before the ethics board got squeamish.

Eleven years of a man's life, decanted into mine over ninety minutes of surgery I slept through. His childhood in Lagos Orbital. His law degree earned by lamplight because his mother couldn't afford the full-tuition track. His twenty years as an estate executor for Halcyon Terraforming Consortium, the corporation that owned him the way you own a very expensive pen. And threaded through all of it, dense as a vein of ore:

Amara Chen. Amara laughing in a hydroponics bay on Meridian Station, pollen on her sleeves. Amara arguing stellar cartography at two in the morning. Amara's last transmission, cut off mid-sentence, eleven years ago. Amara not existing anymore—not just dead, but *unrecorded*, scrubbed from every manifest, every personnel file, every photograph, so thoroughly that when I searched her name in the transport's database this morning, the search engine asked me if I meant *Amara Chenko, freight handler, deceased 2087*, a woman who shared nothing with her but nine letters.

They hadn't killed her twice. They'd done something more thorough than that. They'd made it so she could never have existed at all.

And then, apparently, they'd made *me*, so that a dead man could sign some paperwork.

I lay there in the hum of the ferry's systems and thought about how most people get to be sad in order. You meet someone, you love them, you lose them, you grieve. I'd been handed the whole package pre-assembled, shrink-wrapped, no assembly required. Love, loss, mourning—all installed before I'd drawn my first independent breath. Somewhere in Lagos Orbital there was a regulatory clerk who considered this efficient.

"First graft?" the med-tech asked, catching my expression.

"Is it that obvious?"

"You've got the look. Like you're listening to two radios at once." She adjusted something at the base of my skull—a port I decided not to think about. "It settles. Give it a week. The trick is to stop asking which memories are yours. None of them are. That's the point."

"That's supposed to help?"

"No," she said cheerfully, and left.

* * *

The *Charon's Ferry* had four days left to Meridian Station, and I spent them the way Silas Okonkwo—the first Silas Okonkwo, the original, the deceased—would have spent them: working.

That was the strangest part, those first days. Not the memories themselves but their *gravity*. I would reach for a datapad and my hand knew exactly where he kept his stylus. I'd read a clause in a contract and a complete opinion would arrive fully formed, worded in his particular dry cadence, before I'd decided what I thought. He was opinionated, my original. He believed contracts were moral documents wearing arithmetic as a disguise. He believed the Consortium's probate division was where good lawyers went to be forgotten. He drank his tea bitter enough to strip paint and considered this a personality trait.

He was, I realized somewhere over the second sleep cycle, easy to like. Which made it worse.

Because here was the arrangement, laid out in the legal brief I now owned along with everything else he'd owned: Silas Okonkwo, executor of the estate of Silas Okonkwo, was dispatched to Meridian Station to conduct a final probate audit and certify the disposition of assets. Standard procedure. The original had died—killed, though the file said *industrial accident* in the confident tone of a document that has never once been questioned—and under Consortium Clone Continuity Statute 12, a successor instance inherits the decedent's outstanding obligations. Including, in this case, one unfinished audit.

One audit of a station listed, in the same breathless prose as everything else in the file, as *non-viable, unpopulated, scheduled for decommissioning review*.

I read that phrase forty times over four days. *Unpopulated.* And each time, something in the borrowed architecture of my mind snagged on it, the way a sleeve catches on a nail. Because the original's notes—the ones the Consortium had deigned to include in my briefing packet, anyway—said otherwise. Fragmentary, encrypted, annoyingly incomplete. But clear enough: *Meridian headcount discrepancy. Manifests say zero. Supply requisitions say otherwise. Two hundred caloric units daily, minimum. Who's eating?*

Two hundred people, living unlisted on a station the paperwork insisted was empty. And a murdered auditor who'd noticed, and a clone dispatched to finish his work, and nobody at Probate Division seeming to grasp—or caring—that they might be sending the replacement down the same hole the original fell into.

Or maybe they grasped it perfectly. That possibility kept me up during third shift, staring at the ceiling of a cabin that still smelled of antiseptic and someone else's life.

* * *

On the second day I found the letters.

They were in the estate archive, buried in a subdirectory labeled *PERSONAL — DO NOT INDEX*, which in the Consortium's filing culture meant only that a middle manager hadn't bothered to delete them yet. Forty-one messages, drafts mostly, unsent, addressed to Amara Chen at an address that no longer resolved. Composed over the last three years of his life. The earliest ones were ordinary—missed you, the tea here is criminal, the board rejected the water reclamation proposal again, come back soon. The later ones changed. The later ones were afraid.

*I checked the personnel archives again today. Your quarters have been reassigned on paper to a Dr. E. Vashti who does not appear anywhere else in the system. It's like watching someone paint over a window. I know what I saw through it. I know who stood there.*

*They've offered me a promotion. Division counsel, Earthside. It comes with a full identity refresh—new registry number, new housing, the works. I think it's a door out. I also think it's a door that locks from the outside.*

*I'm going to keep asking. If something happens to me, I want it on record that I was going to keep asking.*

The last draft was dated six days before his death. It read, in its entirety:

*Amara. I found her locket. I'm sending it to someone I trust. If you're somehow reading this—if any of this survives—know that I never stopped looking, and I never once believed the reports.*

There was no report. There was no incident summary, no investigation, no inquiry. There was a single line in the station log—*atmospheric processor malfunction, Deck 4, one casualty*—and then a gap, and then a new facilities contractor, and then silence. Eleven years of seamless, immaculate, well-funded silence.

I closed the folder and sat in the dim cabin with my hands flat on the table, breathing the way the meditation modules had taught the original to breathe after Amara's death, and understood that I was doing it—using his breathing exercise, counting in his rhythm, grieving in his posture—and that there was no version of this where I came out clean. I wasn't mourning a stranger. I was mourning with a dead man's lungs.

The med-tech had said: none of them are yours, that's the point. She was wrong, or the Consortium was wrong, or the whole idea was wrong. The memories were mine now. That's what inheritance means. You don't get to choose whether the debt is yours. You just wake up owing it.

* * *

Third day, I did what executors do: I inventoried.

The estate of Silas Okonkwo, deceased, as compiled by Silas Okonkwo, executor, aboard the *Charon's Ferry*, transit to Meridian Station, in the presence of no witnesses whatsoever because the Consortium had seen fit to send me alone:

One apartment, Lagos Orbital, lease expiring. One bank account, modest—he'd been paid well and spent little, and what he'd spent it on was mostly transmissions to Meridian Station, back when the address worked. One collection of physical books, paper, absurdly heavy to ship, annotated in margins in a handwriting I recognized from the inside. One pending lawsuit against Halcyon Terraforming Consortium, filed eight months before his death, alleging falsification of station records, status: *stayed indefinitely*, which in legal terms means *buried alive*. One unfinished probate audit. One encrypted data cache, location unknown, referenced in his notes but never recovered.

And one murder. Unfiled, uninvestigated, unwitnessed—except by whoever had arranged the atmospheric processor malfunction, and by a station AI named Vesper, whom the file mentioned exactly once, in a maintenance footnote: *Station intelligence functional, restricted partitions per corporate directive.*

Restricted partitions. I turned that phrase over for a long time. An AI with locked rooms inside its own head. A witness with amnesia installed by the suspects.

I am, whatever else I am, a good lawyer—it came with the graft, along with the tea preferences and the grief—and a good lawyer knows the difference between a messy crime scene and a clean one. Messy scenes happen when people panic. Clean scenes are built, deliberately, by people with time and resources and institutional cover. The file on Silas Okonkwo's death was the cleanest thing I had ever read. Every question answered before it was asked. Every anomaly explained before it was noticed. It read like a document written backward from its conclusion, and the conclusion was: *nothing happened here, and no one will look, and if anyone looks, we were prepared for that too.*

They had not, however, prepared for the Clone Clause cutting the other way. I don't think they truly understood what they'd built when they drafted Statute 12. A successor instance inherits obligations—yes, liabilities, yes—but also *knowledge*. Everything the original knew, I know. Every suspicion he harbored, I harbor, with the added advantage of being legally invisible: a clone isn't a person to the Consortium, it's an asset, and assets don't get surveilled the way enemies do. They'd sent a copy of their problem back to the scene of the crime and expected it to countersign the cover-up.

Maybe I would have. I want to be honest about that, sitting here at the start of things, before the story hardens into legend. For two days I genuinely tried. I reviewed the audit scope as assigned: verify asset inventory, confirm zero resident population, certify decommissioning readiness, close the estate. Clean work. Bounded work. Sign the forms, collect the settlement, walk away with a completed obligation and a life that belonged to no one's ghost. There were moments—reading the certification forms, pre-filled, awaiting only my signature—when the temptation had a real, physical texture to it. Warm. Soft. The shape of a door out.

Then I reread the last unsent letter, and I thought about a woman who had been erased so completely that even her murderer's paperwork couldn't remember her name, and I thought about two hundred caloric units a day going to mouths that officially did not exist, and I took out a fresh datapad and started a new file.

I titled it *Estate of S. Okonkwo — Supplementary Findings (Unofficial)*, which is lawyer humor, the bleakest kind, and beneath it I wrote the first line of the only document that mattered:

*Item one: the decedent did not die in an industrial accident. Neither, I suspect, did anyone else on that station. Begin at the beginning: find out who is eating.*

* * *

Last night—fourth night, final watch before orbital insertion—I dreamed his dream. I know it was his because I'd found it described in his journal, in an entry dated three years after Amara's death: *the dream where she's in the observation dome and the aurora filters are running and she turns around and says my name, and I wake up before she finishes saying it, every time, and I would give a year of my life to hear the rest.*

In the dream she turned around. Her mouth began to move. And I woke—my heart slamming, the graft wire singing behind my temples—in the grey dark of the ferry cabin, alone, with the rest of the sentence still waiting somewhere ahead of me like a debt coming due.

The intercom chimed. The pilot's voice, bored and tinny: "Meridian Station on approach. Docking in ninety minutes. Mr. Okonkwo—you awake?"

"I'm awake," I said. Both of us, I didn't add.

"Whatever's down there," he said, with the elaborate casualness of a man who'd hauled cargo past that rock for years and didn't like talking about it, "the beacon's older than the charts say it should be. And somebody answered our handshake ping manually. Stations that empty don't answer pings."

I looked out the viewport at the thing growing in the dark—a rust-brown spindle wrapped in solar wings, turning slowly, lit along its spine like a candle in a jar. Listed as non-viable. Listed as empty. Answering pings with human hands.

"Good," I said, and got up to dress in a dead man's clothes.

Somewhere below, two hundred people who did not exist were waiting to find out what I was. I was starting to wonder myself.
