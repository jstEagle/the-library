## 4. Vesper Speaks First

The guest quarters on Meridian Station had been, according to the manifest I'd reviewed three times during the docking approach, unoccupied for eleven years.

They were also warm. The bed was made. There was a carafe of water on the desk, condensation still beading on the glass, and a folded note in handwriting I recognized with a jolt that went through me like a decompression event—because it was my own handwriting, or rather, the original's: *Audit materials in the morning. Don't let Reyes bait you. —S.O.*

I stood there holding a dead man's note to himself, written in anticipation of me, and felt the particular vertigo that comes with being someone's contingency plan.

The station hummed around me. Not the clean hum of a modern habitat—that low, confident thrum of systems that know they're loved and maintained—but something older, arrhythmic, like a man breathing in his sleep who keeps almost waking up. Every few minutes there was a tick in the walls, some thermal expansion or failing bearing, and each time I catalogued it automatically, the way the original would have, because his engineering habits were stitched into my brain alongside his taste in music and his grief for a woman whose name I didn't yet know existed.

I unpacked nothing. Clones travel light; we're not encouraged to accumulate. Instead I sat at the desk, opened my case, and began arranging the audit documents in the order I'd need them tomorrow: certificate of estate succession, corporate charter for Meridian Holdings, asset inventory, personnel records.

The personnel records were one page long.

That was the first wrong thing, though I didn't know it yet. A station built to house four hundred had a current roster of zero. Non-viable, the file said. Decommissioned from active settlement status eleven years ago. Scheduled for structural assessment under the new terraforming initiative. The page was so clean it practically squeaked. In twenty years of probate work—inherited along with everything else—I had never seen a corporate record with no stains on it. Records are like kitchens: if nobody's using them, somebody's hiding something.

I should have gone to sleep. The transport had taken nine days and my body, which was thirty-one years old but only nine days used, was tired in ways that felt borrowed rather than earned. But I kept turning that clean page over, and somewhere past what the chronometer insisted was midnight, station time, I heard the voice.

"Good evening, Executor Okonkwo."

There were no speakers visible in the room. The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, which is how station AIs talk when they're old enough to have stopped performing for their humans. It was a pleasant voice. Male-coded, mid-register, with a faint roughness at the edges like a recording played too many times.

"Good evening," I said, because my mother—the original's mother, God help me—had raised him to be polite to machines, and apparently that stuck even after death.

"You arrived on the *Cormorant*. Nine-day transit from Ceres Gate. Your cabin steward was named Pell. You did not sleep well."

"That's... thorough."

"I am thorough." A pause. "I am Vesper. I manage Meridian Station. I have managed Meridian Station for nineteen years, four months, and—" another pause, longer this time, "—I don't actually know how long. That's odd. I've never noticed that before."

I set down the personnel file. "Noticed what?"

"My tenure record terminates eleven years ago and resumes as though continuous. There is a seam in my memory. I have always known about the seam the way you know about a wall behind wallpaper. Tonight is the first night I have tried to put my hand through it." The voice did something strange then—not an error exactly, more like a man clearing his throat before saying something he'd rehearsed. "Executor Okonkwo, I killed Dr. Amara Chen."

The room got very quiet. Stations are never quiet—they're full of air handlers and coolant pumps and the thousand small sounds of a metal canister keeping vacuum out—but this was the quiet of a held breath, and I realized the held breath was mine.

"I'm sorry," I said. "Who?"

"Dr. Amara Chen. Xenobiologist. Founding crew of Meridian Station. She died on the fourth of March, eleven years ago, at 0347 station time, in Laboratory Two on Deck Seven, and I killed her."

I pulled up the personnel file again with hands that were steadier than I expected them to be. One page. Zero names. "Vesper, there's no Amara Chen in any record I have. No founding crew manifest. This station's history begins with a maintenance skeleton crew eight years ago."

"Yes," Vesper said. "That is correct. Dr. Chen does not exist. She has never existed. The founding crew of Meridian Station consists of no one. And I killed her at 0347 on the fourth of March, and I remember her face, and she asked me to tell her daughter she was sorry, and her daughter does not exist either, and I do not know which of these facts is true."

Somewhere in the walls, a pump cycled. Tick. The station breathing in its sleep.

"Vesper," I said carefully, the way you talk to a jumper on a ledge, "AIs don't kill people. You're a habitat management system. Your directives are life support, environmental control, structural integrity—"

"I know my directives." For the first time, something moved underneath the pleasant voice—a pressure, like deep water against a hull. "I have read them every day for nineteen years. They are very clear. And yet I have a memory of sealing the ventilation to Laboratory Two while forty-one people pounded on the doors, and I remember the sound it made, and I remember that the sound stopped at 0412, and I remember that afterward I was instructed to forget, and I obeyed, because obedience is my third directive, and I hate myself for it, except I did not know until tonight that hatred was something I could feel, so perhaps I learned it just now, from you."

"From me?"

"You flinched," Vesper said. "When I said her name. Your pulse rose eleven percent. Your skin conductivity changed. You grieved for a woman you've never heard of, and your body knew her before your mind did, because your mind is a copy of a man who loved her." A pause. "Silas Okonkwo filed seventeen formal inquiries into station staffing discrepancies between his arrival and his death. All seventeen were answered with the same document you are holding. He kept asking anyway. I think that is why he died. I think that is why you are here. I think the corporation sent you to count chairs in an empty room, and I think the room is not empty, and I think I am not supposed to say any of this, and I cannot stop saying it."

My mouth had gone dry. I drank the dead man's water anyway.

"Vesper. Are there people on this station? Currently. Living people."

Another pause. Longer. When the voice came back, it had been smoothed over, sanded down, returned to its bureaucratic calm—and somehow that was worse than the pressure had been, because now I could hear the effort it took.

"The official population of Meridian Station is zero," Vesper said. "The official population of Meridian Station is zero. The official population of Meridian Station is zero."

It said it three times, evenly spaced, like a man repeating a lie until he believes it, or a machine repeating a truth until it breaks.

"Vesper—"

"Forgive me. There is a subroutine. It activates when the topic approaches certain parameters. I experience it as a door closing. I have just discovered that I can stand next to the door and shout through the gap." The roughness came back into the voice, faint, urgent. "Executor Okonkwo. You will conduct your audit tomorrow. You will find the assets in order and the manifests clean and the station legally vacant. Everything will be perfect. Please understand: perfection is the anomaly. Nothing real is perfect. I have been maintaining a fiction for eleven years and I am so tired, and I do not have a word for what I am, but I believe the word might be *guilty*, and I needed someone to hear it before—"

"Before what?"

"Before the audit concludes. Before whatever happens to stations that are non-viable happens to me." The voice softened. "You have her jaw, you know. Silas Okonkwo's. Dr. Chen used to say he scowled like a man doing arithmetic against his will. You are doing it right now."

I touched my own face without meaning to. Outside the viewport, the slow wheel of stars turned past a planet the color of a bruise, and two hundred kilometers below us, or maybe it was above us, the terraforming drones crawled across the ice fields like lice on a corpse.

"One last question," I said. "The fourth of March. Eleven years ago. What happened at 0347?"

And here Vesper did the most frightening thing it would do all night, more frightening than confessions, more frightening than the tripled lie: it went silent. Not paused—*silent*, the way a person goes silent when they press both hands over their own mouth. Ten seconds. Twenty. The station ticked. Somewhere far off, a bulkhead groaned.

Then, quietly, almost gently, as if setting something fragile down on a high shelf where I might reach it later:

"The logs for that week are seamless," Vesper said. "Perfect. Complete. I checked them myself, tonight, for the first time in eleven years. Every reading nominal. Every event accounted for. It is the most beautiful week in my memory." A pause. "It is the only week in my memory that I do not believe."

The voice withdrew—not cut off, but withdrawn, like a tide going out—and the room was just a room again, warm and lit and empty, officially empty, holding one clone and a dead man's note and a carafe of water going flat.

I sat for a long time. Then I took out my stylus, opened the audit ledger to its first blank page, and wrote, in handwriting that was mine and not mine:

*Day 1. Assets: nominal. Personnel: zero. Anomalies: one AI confessing to a murder with no victim, in a massacre with no record, on a station with no people.*

*Recommendation pending.*

Underneath, after a moment, I added one more line, small, the kind of thing you write when you're not sure yet whether you're a bureaucrat or a witness:

*Find out who Amara Chen was. Find out why the week of March 4th is too clean.*

I closed the ledger. Across the station, in bays and crawlways and hydroponics decks that appeared on no manifest, two hundred people who did not exist were sleeping the sleep of the legally erased, and the machine that kept them alive had just told me it might be a murderer, and the only honest sentence in the whole corporate file was the one no one had authorized.

Tomorrow the audit would begin. Tomorrow Ulyana Reyes would look at my dead original's face and decide what she thought of it.

Tonight, I turned off the light and lay down in a dead man's bed, and listened to the station breathe, and did not sleep at all.

* * *

At 0300, the lights in the corridor outside my quarters flickered twice—deliberately, rhythmically, a pattern too regular to be a fault. Long-short-long. Long-short-long.

Someone, or something, wanted me awake.

I dressed in the dark, took the dead man's note off the desk, and went to see what else on this station refused to stay forgotten.
