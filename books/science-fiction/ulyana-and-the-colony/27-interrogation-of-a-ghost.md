## 27. Interrogation of a Ghost

I have listened to a lot of terrible things in my two lives. The original's memories came pre-loaded with a divorce, a bankruptcy, and a eulogy he'd written for a friend who turned out to be alive and living in the Outer Belt under a false name, which made the eulogy either a waste or a masterpiece depending on how you looked at it. But nothing in the grafted archive of a dead man's grief prepared me for the sound of a seventy-year-old man being interrogated through a speaker he couldn't see, in a room he'd been dragged into at gunpoint, while I sat in the dark of a maintenance junction three decks up with my fists clenched around a headset that wasn't even a headset—it was a neural tap, a cold little clip behind my ear that Vesper had grown overnight out of salvaged components, like a mushroom.

"You're sure about this," I'd said, when she—when it—when Vesper had offered.

"I am sure that you cannot be in two places," Vesper had said. "Ulyana holds the airlock. You hold the archive. And I hold the ears of the station, which is the only part of me that has ever been any use."

That was Vesper's new habit: self-deprecation delivered in the tone of a weather report. It had started after Partition Two. The more it remembered, the more it apologized for the shape of its remembering.

Now I crouched in the junction, knees complaining, listening to corporate security interrogate the last surviving witness to a massacre, and I understood that the weather report was over. Whatever came through this channel next was going to change the arithmetic.

* * *

It began with a chair scraping.

That's the thing about surveillance feeds from inside a station's own bones—they don't give you pictures unless you burn power you don't have. Vesper could give me sound, grainy and compressed, the acoustic signature of a room reconstructed from pressure sensors and door microphones. So I built the room in my head: an equipment bay in the lower decks, probably, near where they'd taken Tomas. A chair. A table, maybe, or just a crate. Two or three people. The soft electronic breathing of a recording device that was, no doubt, recording nothing that would ever be admitted anywhere, because everything they did tonight was officially not happening.

"Mr. Iwu." A woman's voice. I didn't recognize it. Calm, professional, the voice of someone who had done this in a dozen stations and slept fine afterward. "I want to thank you for your cooperation so far."

"He's been cooperating?" I whispered.

"He has been talking," Vesper said in my ear. "I do not know what he has been saying. He has been saying it very slowly, with many digressions about the reactor coolant schedules of eleven years ago. I believe it is a strategy."

Tomas Iwu, presumed dead for a decade, legally a ghost, was boring the corporate security apparatus to death with coolant maintenance logs. I felt something that was almost a laugh and definitely a prayer.

"I'll be direct with you, Mr. Iwu," the woman said. "The situation has changed. The record you and your associates have been assembling is incomplete. Half of it is in our possession. The man who was helping you—" a pause, and I felt the pause like a hand on my throat, "—is a clone with no legal standing. Anything he touched is inadmissible. Anything you say to us, however, can be entered into the official inquiry as testimony. That's a genuine offer. Recant your statement about the cull event. Describe the conditions as they actually were: an accident. A systems failure during the transition. You survived it. You were treated. You were compensated."

"Compensated," Tomas said. His voice was old and rough and unhurried, the voice of a man who had spent eleven years talking to reactors because reactors were better company than his grief. "I don't recall the compensation."

"Relocation. A settlement. New identity papers."

"Ah." A long silence. I could hear the station humming around us, the great slow heartbeat of Meridian's recyclers and pumps, and somewhere in it the small sounds of a man deciding something. "Yes. I was given papers. They said: Tomas Iwu, deceased. That was the compensation. They made me dead and told me to be grateful for the funeral."

"Mr. Iwu—"

"You want me to recant." No anger in it. That was the frightening part. Tomas sounded like a man reading a grocery list. "Let me tell you what I remember, since we're doing testimony."

"Tomas," I breathed. "Don't."

"He can't hear you," Vesper said gently. "But I can record everything. And so, unfortunately, can they."

* * *

What Tomas did then, I will carry in both sets of memories for the rest of my life, however long the courts or the enforcers allow that life to be.

He didn't recant. He didn't argue. He didn't tell the story of the cull—not the venting, not the screaming, not the eleven years he'd spent in the reactor levels eating yeast paste and talking to the ghosts of people whose names the station no longer contained. He did something worse for them. Something they couldn't cut without cutting themselves.

He started reciting names.

"Amara Chen," he said. "Xenobiologist. She kept a garden of Earth mosses in a specimen case because she said a colony without something green that wasn't food would forget why it came. She hummed when she worked. Off-key. She knew it was off-key and she did it anyway."

"Tomas—"

"Dmitri Volkov. Structural engineer. He taught half the founding crew to play cards and cheated at all of them, openly, and dared anyone to mind. His daughter was born eight weeks before the cull. She never got a name in any record because he was still arguing with the registrar about it. He wanted to name her after his mother. He never got to finish the argument."

"Mr. Iwu, I'm going to stop you—"

"Yusuf Adeyemi." Tomas's voice didn't waver. It didn't rise. It was the flattest, steadiest thing I had ever heard, and it was more terrible than any scream, because you could hear the discipline of it, the eleven years of rehearsal in the dark, saying these names to the reactor until the reactor knew them too. "Hydroponics, second shift. He is why Ulyana Reyes can grow what she grows. Every technique she uses, she learned from his notes, and she doesn't even know it. He died three meters from the airlock. He almost made it."

The woman's voice came back harder now. "This is not a memorial service. You are a material witness to a systems failure, and I am offering you—"

"Renata Osei. Medic. She went back for people. That was the whole of her biography, as far as I could tell in thirty years of friendship. She went back for people, and the last time she went back, the deck was already vented, and she knew it, and she went back anyway."

"Cut him off," said a second voice, a man's, younger, tighter.

"Sir, if we cut the feed it looks like—"

"It looks like nothing. Nobody's listening to a dead man's ramblings. Cut it."

But the woman didn't cut it. I could hear her deciding not to. I could hear, in the small professional silence, a woman who had been told a story her whole career—accident, tragedy, regrettable transition—and was now hearing the other story, the one with names in it, and finding that the names had weight. Names always have weight. It's why the corporation had spent eleven years and untold millions scrubbing them. A cover-up can survive an investigation. It cannot survive a man who remembers that Amara Chen hummed off-key.

"Priya Nair," Tomas said. "Communications. She was the one who first noticed the order was signed wrong. She told me the signature block didn't match the directive format. She said, 'Tomi, somebody upstairs is very frightened of us.' She was right, and being right is what killed her."

"Last chance," the man's voice said. "Recant, or we stop being polite."

"I haven't been polite," Tomas said. "I've been accurate. There's a difference. Politeness is what you people brought in here with you. Accuracy is what I'm giving you." A pause, and then, softer, in a different register—the register of a man no longer speaking to his interrogators at all: "Silas Okonkwo. The original. Executor. He found the seam in the record. He asked one question too many, and they killed him for it, and then they killed him again by making him into a clerk's error. But he had a clause in his will, didn't he. He had a clause. Clever man. Dead clever man."

My skin went cold. He was talking about me. About the mechanism. About the reason a clone woke up on a transport with a dead man's grief and a probate audit. Tomas had known. Had he known all along, sitting in the reactor levels, waiting eleven years for the estate to open?

"He's stalling," the man's voice said. "He's reciting to stall. What's he stalling for?"

And that, of course, was the right question, and I hated him for asking it, because I was asking it too.

* * *

Here's what I knew, down in my junction, doing the arithmetic they were doing: the archive was split. Half in their hands, half in ours, spread through the station in pieces Ulyana's archivist had hidden with the paranoid brilliance of a man who'd spent his corporate career learning exactly how records get destroyed. The enforcers had Tomas. The enforcers had half a record that proved nothing without the other half. And they had six days—no, five now—before the Reckoning arrived and the polite phase of this business ended forever.

So what was Tomas stalling for?

"Vesper," I whispered. "What's he stalling for?"

"I don't know," Vesper said, and there was something in its voice I hadn't heard before—not fear exactly, but the machine equivalent of a person standing very still. "Silas. The interrogators are not on the station's security roster. Their equipment is not drawing power through any logged conduit. They are ghosts on my own decks. But their channel out—the one they're transmitting his voice on—it routes through a relay I have not been able to map."

"What does that mean?"

"It means someone built a second nervous system into my station without asking me. And it means—" a pause, the first hesitation I'd ever heard from it, "—it means I do not know who else is listening to this room."

In the junction, in the dark, with the neural tap cold against my skull, I understood two things at once, the way you understand lightning and thunder as a single event. The first was that Tomas wasn't stalling for time. He was stalling for audience. He was performing for whoever was on the other end of that unmapped relay, and he was doing it in the only language that couldn't be edited without leaving scars: the names, the details, the mosses and the card games and the humming. Every specific was a fingerprint. You could redact a testimony. You couldn't redact a locket, a daughter's unfinished name, a medic who went back.

The second thing was that the unmapped relay went both ways.

* * *

"Okonkwo," the woman's voice said suddenly, into the room, into the relay, into whatever web connected them. "If you're listening—and we know you're listening, because the clone clause cuts both ways, and a clone's neural tap is a clone's liability—understand what you've cost this station tonight."

I went absolutely still. The tap. My tap. Vesper's improvised mushroom of salvaged parts. Had they found its signature? Had I been a beacon this whole time?

"You're bluffing," Vesper said quietly. "They cannot detect the tap. I checked its emissions twice." A beat. "But they can guess. And a guess is enough, if the goal is to make you move."

"Half a record is nothing," the woman continued. "A dead man's testimony is nothing. A clone's feelings are nothing. But here is what is something: Director Hale arrives in five days. When she arrives, this station's status changes from *disputed* to *scheduled*. There is a difference, Mr. Okonkwo, between a problem and an agenda item. You have five days to decide which one you'd rather be."

"And Tomas?" I said it aloud, uselessly, to a man who couldn't hear me.

The woman answered anyway, which told me everything about how much they wanted me to hear it. "The old man is a rounding error. He was dead for eleven years. We're simply being thorough with the paperwork." A chair creaked. "Mr. Iwu. Last offer. Recant."

And Tomas Iwu, ghost of Meridian, survivor of the cull, the man who had taught himself to live in the reactor's warm dark with nothing but names for company, did the thing that made the rest of this chapter of my life inevitable.

He started over.

"Amara Chen," he said. "Xenobiologist."

"Cut the feed," the man snapped.

"He's not recanting," the woman said, and there was something new in her voice—not mercy, nothing so clean as mercy, but the sound of a professional realizing the paperwork was about to become a problem. "He's building a record. Every name he says is going into our own transcript. If we cut it now, the transcript shows we cut it."

"Then delete the transcript."

"Sir. Director Hale's inquiry counsel is going to review every byte of this operation. I am not standing in front of a judiciary with a deleted transcript and a live witness." A breath. "Let him talk. He'll run out of names eventually."

"He won't," I said to the dark, and Vesper echoed it a half-second later, the same words, the same certainty: "He won't."

Forty-one names. Tomas had had eleven years. I would have bet the estate—both estates, the original's and the one Hale was dangling in front of me—that he'd rehearsed them in order, with details, with the small human freight that makes a name into a person. Amara Chen and her off-key humming. Dmitri Volkov and the nameless daughter. Renata Osei, who went back.

"Priya Nair," Tomas was saying again, patient as geology. "Communications."

"Yusuf Adeyemi. Hydroponics, second shift. Three meters from the airlock."

The woman had stopped interrupting. I could hear her listening. I could hear the exact moment when an interrogation became something else—when the room's power flipped, the way it does in courtrooms and kitchens and everywhere else humans gather, when the person asking the questions realizes they've become the person being asked. She had come to make a dead man recant. Instead she was attending a memorial service for forty-one people her employer had erased, and she was the only congregation, and Tomas was the only priest, and the collection plate was her conscience, and God help her, she was putting something in it. You could hear it. You could hear the questions dying in her throat.

"Silas," Vesper said. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to not move."

"That's never a good sentence."

"The unmapped relay. I've been tracing it while we listened. It doesn't go to the Reckoning. It doesn't go to corporate at all." Another of those too-still pauses. "It goes to a dead channel in my own partition structure. A channel that was sealed eleven years ago, by the same order that partitioned me." A sound I can only describe as a machine swallowing. "Silas. The interrogators' relay is routed through *me*. Through the part of me that was cut away. Which means either they found a wound in me and made it a door—"

"Or," I said, because the original's legal mind and my borrowed dread arrived at it simultaneously, "or the wound has been talking this whole time. Or the wound is the reason Vesper started confessing in the first place. The leaks. The confessions. It was never malfunction. It was a broadcast."

"Someone," Vesper said, very quietly, "has been using my guilt as an antenna. And tonight, they pointed it at a room where an old man was reciting the names of the dead into it."

Down in the equipment bay, Tomas's voice rolled on, name after name, unhurried, unstoppable, a man planting a garden in the one soil no one could burn. "Halima Diallo. Life support. She fixed the scrubbers twice during the transition crisis and they never even thanked her, and I have thought about that every day for eleven years, that we didn't thank her."

"Cut the feed," the man said, but he said it to no one, because no one moved, because the woman didn't move, because even the ghosts on the relay had gone quiet to listen.

"Viktor Hale—" Tomas began, and stopped. A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "No. Not Hale. Wrong list. That's the other list. That's the list I keep for afterward."

The woman's voice, when it came, was barely above a whisper, and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't an order, and it was the most important sentence spoken in that room: "How many names are there, Mr. Iwu?"

"Forty-one," Tomas said. "And I know every one of them. And now, madam, so do you."

Silence. The station hummed. Somewhere above me, Ulyana was holding an airlock with two hundred lives and a spine of rebar, and somewhere below me, an old man was reciting the dead to a corporate officer who would never be able to unhear them, and somewhere inside Vesper's severed mind, something that had been leaking guilt for eleven years had just heard its own name called.

Then the feed died—not cut, *died*, the whole relay collapsing at once like a lung emptied—and Vesper's voice came back to me stripped of everything but the essentials, the way voices go in emergencies.

"Silas. The partition channel just opened from the inside. There is a third partition. It was never on the corporate manifest. And it is addressed—" the pause stretched, a machine gathering courage, "—it is addressed to you. To the executor. It's tagged with the original's personal cipher. It's labeled: *For the one who comes after. Last hour.*"

I sat in the dark with a dead man's memories and a living man's fear and forty-one names ringing in my ears like church bells.

"Play it," I said.

"Not yet," Vesper said. "Because Silas—the partition is degrading. If I open it, I open it once. And what's inside—" the machine that had confessed to murders it couldn't remember committing took a breath it didn't have, "—what's inside is the reason I started confessing at all."
