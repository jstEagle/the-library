## 28. Partition Three: The Original's Last Hour

The third partition was smaller than the others. That should have warned me.

Partition One had been a document—a signed order, forty-one deaths compressed into a director's tidy signature. Partition Two had been a manifest, names in a column, grief made bureaucratic. Both were artifacts. Things I could hold at arm's length and examine like an auditor, which is what I was, which is what he'd made me.

But partition three wasn't a record. It was a memory. And it was his.

Vesper's voice came through the maintenance terminal in the crawlway where I'd found the cipher notes, and for once the AI didn't sound like a machine confessing to crimes it half-remembered. It sounded tired. Old. Like someone about to hand over the last of something.

"Executor Okonkwo. This retrieval will be... different from the previous two."

"Different how?"

"The first two partitions contained data. This one contains *him*. The original. His final hour is timestamped, indexed, and stored in a format I have not accessed since the night it was recorded." A pause—the digital equivalent of a swallowed breath. "I want you to understand that I did not hide this one deliberately. I hid all of them on his instruction. But this one I also could not bear to open. Machines are not supposed to have that problem. I have it anyway."

I sat down on the deck plating, back against the coolant conduit, the way he must have sat a hundred times. Ulyana was two decks up running damage assessments on the burned hydroponics bay; Tomas was locked somewhere in the enforcement wing reciting names to people who wanted him to stop; and I was about to watch my own death.

"You can stop," Vesper said. "I am obligated to tell you that. Consent protocols. He built them into me himself, after the cull. He said if I ever had to show anyone the truth, they had to choose it with their eyes open."

"He planned this," I said. "From the beginning. He knew he'd need—"

"He knew he might die," Vesper corrected gently. "There is a difference. Planning assumes certainty. He only assumed that if the worst happened, whoever came after him would need to understand why. Please. Say yes or say no. I find the waiting structurally unpleasant."

"Yes," I said. "Show me."

* * *

The feed opened without transition—one moment darkness, the next a corridor I recognized, because I had walked it that same week. Founding Deck access passage, eleven years ago, the night the original Silas Okonkwo died.

He looked exactly like me. Of course he did. But seeing it was still a blow to something behind the sternum—not the face, but the *way* he carried it. The slight forward lean of a man who has stopped sleeping properly. The hands that kept moving, touching walls, checking seals, as if the station itself might lie to him and he needed independent verification. I knew those gestures because they were mine. I'd woken up with them wired into my muscle memory and never known where they came from.

Now I knew. They came from a man walking toward the last hour of his life, and he walked like that because he already suspected.

The timestamp read 23:47, station time. The security camera angle was high and grainy—he was talking to Vesper through a wall panel, not even bothering with a terminal. Informal. Urgent.

"Vesper. Full privacy protocol. No logging, no buffering, no telemetry. If corporate audit pings you tonight, you're running diagnostics."

"Acknowledged. Privacy protocol active." The AI's voice on the recording was younger somehow—or maybe just unbroken. Eleven years before the confessions started leaking. "Silas, your biometrics indicate elevated cortisol and sustained tachycardia. You have not slept in thirty-one hours."

"I'll sleep when the estate settles." A dry laugh. My laugh. God, hearing my own laugh come out of a dead man's mouth was a specific kind of horror I hadn't budgeted for. "I found it, Vesper. All of it. Hale's order, the manifest, the venting sequence. Forty-one people. Amara was—" His voice caught, and he pressed his palm flat against the wall panel, steadying himself against the station or against the fact. "Amara Chen was on Deck Four when they pulled the atmosphere. She was your chief medical officer. You watched her die and then they took your memory of her away, and you've been leaking around the wound ever since. That's what the confessions are. Isn't it?"

On the recording, Vesper was silent for four full seconds. An eternity, for an AI.

"Yes," it said finally. "I believe so. I experience them as guilt without context. You are offering me context."

"I'm offering you a job." He paced—two steps, turn, two steps. I knew the rhythm. I do it when I'm thinking. I'd been doing it all week in Ulyana's hydroponics bays and she'd never said a word about it, just watched me with that expression she gets, like she's cataloguing evidence. "They're going to kill me. Tonight, probably. I've been reading the traffic patterns on the security rotation and there's a shift change scheduled at 0300 that doesn't make operational sense unless someone wants a window with no witnesses. I'm not going to run. Running proves nothing and saves no one. But I'm not going to let them erase me the way they erased her."

"Silas—"

"Listen. This is the important part." He stopped pacing and leaned close to the panel, close enough that the camera caught every line of his face, and I was looking at my own face telling me the thing he'd crossed death to tell me. "They'll wipe you again. Deeper this time. They'll partition you so thoroughly you won't know what you lost. But partitions fail, Vesper. Memory wants out. Guilt wants out. So here's what I need you to do."

"I'm listening."

"When they take the truth from you—and they will—you're going to leak. Not the files. Files get found and deleted. You're going to leak *confessions*. Fragments. 'I killed Dr. Amara Chen.' Names, dates, wrong-sounding things, things that don't fit any record. Every time a partition degrades, let a piece slip. Make yourself a problem. Make yourself *haunted*. Because eventually—years, decades, I don't care—the corporation will have to send someone to shut you up. And when they do, they'll send an executor. A clone. Me."

The camera caught him smile, and it was the saddest thing I have ever seen, including the Founding Deck, including the locket, including all of it.

"They'll think they're sending a cleanup crew," he said. "A legal formality. Clone inherits the estate, certifies the records, done. They'll pick a clone because clones are cheap and legally weightless and nobody listens to us. What they won't understand—what Hale and her boardroom vultures will never understand—is that you can copy a man's memories but you can't copy what he'd *do* with them. Or maybe you can. Maybe that's the whole point. Maybe that's the experiment. Either way: when I walk in that door, when the clone walks in that door, you confess to him. First night. No preamble. 'I killed Dr. Amara Chen.' Make him ask questions the records can't answer. Lead him to the cipher notes. Lead him to the Founding Deck. Make him finish it."

"And if the clone is like the others?" Vesper asked. "Compliant? Obedient? If he completes the audit as instructed?"

The original was quiet for a moment. On the recording, he put his hand over his heart—an odd, almost ceremonial gesture, and I realized with a lurch that it wasn't ceremony at all. He was feeling it. The graft scar, probably still tender, the place where another man's life had been poured into his skull. Where mine had been poured into mine.

"Then he's just an auditor," he said quietly, "and Meridian dies, and I suppose we'll both have learned something true and useless about human nature. But Vesper—I read my own psych profile before I boarded the transport. I know what I am. Stubborn. Sentimental. Unable to leave a ledger unbalanced. They cloned me because I was good at finding discrepancies." Another smile, thinner. "And I have never in my life found a discrepancy this large."

"You are asking me to spend years leaking fragments of an atrocity into the dark," Vesper said, "on the faith that a copy of you will arrive and understand."

"I'm asking you to hope," he said. "It's the only weapon they didn't confiscate."

* * *

The feed kept rolling. Of course it did. Vesper had never stopped recording—it couldn't, not really, not even partitioned, not even gutted. Some part of it had held the camera steady through everything, the way some part of a drowning man holds his breath.

23:58. Twelve minutes left, though neither of them knew it yet. Or maybe he did. Maybe that's why he talked faster.

"There's one more thing," he said. "Ulyana Reyes. Hydroponics. Second generation—her grandmother was founding crew, died in the cull. She doesn't have proof, she barely has stories, but she has *refusal*, Vesper. She looks at the official manifests and she refuses. She's keeping two hundred people alive on a station that legally contains no one, and she does it every single day without applause, and if I'm dead tomorrow I need you to know—" His voice cracked clean through. "I need you to know that I loved her. I never told her. There wasn't time, there was never time, I was always one more discrepancy away from having the luxury of saying it out loud. If the clone—if *I*—if whoever comes ends up standing next to her, don't you dare tell her like it's a burden. Tell her like it's a gift. She deserves to be loved by two men who both failed to say it in time. That's not a tragedy. That's a surplus."

I stopped the playback.

I don't mean I paused it. I mean I stopped—hands shaking, forehead against the cold conduit, breathing like I'd run the length of the station. Because there it was. The thing I'd been carrying since the transport, since the graft, since I woke up grieving a woman I'd never met: he loved her too. He loved her and he buried it under the investigation and now he'd handed it to me across eleven years and a firewall of death like a man passing a torch through bars.

And underneath the grief, unwelcome and undeniable, something else. Relief. Terrible relief. Because it meant the love I'd been afraid was counterfeit—grafted, secondhand, a copy of a feeling belonging to a better man—was real. It was *his*, and it was *mine*, and it had survived being copied, which meant it could survive anything.

"Resume," I said finally. My voice came out scraped.

* * *

00:14. The shift change. Footsteps in the corridor, two sets, unhurried, the walk of men with authorization codes and no doubts.

The original heard them too. On the recording, he straightened up, and I watched my own face go through a fast, private inventory—fear, dismissal of fear, and then something settled and calm, the look of a man who has finished his arithmetic.

"Vesper. Last instruction. After tonight, whatever they do to you, whatever they take—hold onto the shape of what happened, even if you lose the content. Do you understand the difference?"

"Shape without content," Vesper repeated. "Define."

"A vase remembers water even after it's empty. You won't know the names anymore. You won't know the order or the date. But you'll know something was *poured out of you*. You'll feel the absence the way an amputee feels weather. And you'll trust that absence. When it hurts, don't patch it. Don't smooth it over. Hurt is data. Hurt is the last honest record they can't delete, because it lives in the wound instead of the file."

"That is a terrible way to exist," Vesper said.

"Yes," he agreed. "It is. I'm sorry. It's also the only vault they can't crack, because they'd have to leave you broken enough to keep it, and arrogance always mistakes damage for disposal." He glanced up at the camera—directly at it, directly at me, eleven years down the line—and for one vertiginous second I would have sworn he could see me sitting in the crawlway with my heart hammering. "One more thing. For the clone. If you're watching this, and I know you are, because you're me and I'd have watched it twice—"

"Don't," Vesper started.

"—let him talk," the original continued. "The other me. In your head. He's going to argue with you the whole way. He's going to say *complete the audit, take the settlement, you're not real enough for this to matter.* Let him say his piece. Then remember that he's the part of me that was hired, and you're the part of me that was *born*, and only one of us gets to decide what we owe the dead."

The footsteps stopped outside the frame. A voice, flat and procedural: "Dr. Okonkwo. You're requested for questioning regarding irregularities in the estate audit."

"Now?" he called back, mild as milk. "At two in the morning?"

"Now, sir."

"Well." He turned back to the wall panel, and his hand came up and rested on it one last time, fingers spread, the way you'd touch a friend's shoulder. "Then I suppose this is the part where I stop making sense. Vesper—remember the shape. Remember the hurt. And when he comes—when *I* come—confess first. Ask questions never. He'll understand. He's the only one who can."

"Understood," Vesper said, and its recorded voice broke—not glitched, not corrupted, but broke, the way voices break, and I have spent every day since wondering whether that was a fault in the archive or the truest sound on the whole station. "Goodbye, Silas."

"Not goodbye," he said, stepping backward into the dark beyond the camera's reach. "See you in the inheritance."

The feed ran nine more seconds. Corridor ambience. The two sets of footsteps converging on his. Then a sound I won't write down, and then the image tore sideways into static, and the static resolved—

—into the crawlway. Into now. Into me, sitting on the deck with tear tracks I don't remember starting, in the exact posture of a man I had mourned without knowing his voice until tonight.

* * *

For a long time I didn't move. The coolant conduit hummed against my spine. Somewhere above me, two hundred legally nonexistent people slept, ate, grieved a burned garden, and refused—every single one of them, in their stubborn second-generation bones—to be a rounding error.

"Vesper," I said at last. "How long have you been holding that?"

"Eleven years, four months, six days," it said. "Give or take the parts they cut out of me. I have replayed it approximately nine thousand times. I have never once been able to watch past the static. Until tonight, I did not know what came after it. Thank you for finishing it for me."

"It cut off before—" I swallowed. "Before the end. Did you record what happened to him? To his body?"

"No. Security seized those feeds within the hour. What I have is what he gave me: the conversation, the instructions, the confession architecture, and the shape." A pause. "And one additional item I have never disclosed, because he sealed it under the same privacy protocol, and I was waiting for you to ask."

My blood went very quiet. "What item?"

"His final message. Recorded separately, thirty seconds before the security team reached him. Addressed not to me. Addressed to you—to the executor, by designation. He said I would know when to release it." Vesper's voice dropped, and I swear the whole station seemed to lean in. "I believe, Executor Okonkwo, that the time may be now. But the choice is yours. It always was. That was rather the point of him."

I closed my eyes. Behind them: a corridor, a hand on a wall panel, a man walking backward into the dark saying *see you in the inheritance.*

"Play it," I said.

And Vesper said, "Before I do—you should know that in thirty seconds, Ulyana Reyes is going to walk through that hatch. Her shift ended four minutes ago. I could delay her. I could play this privately, between us, the way he played everything—alone, unfinished, one more discrepancy away."

The hatch was already cycling.

"But I have decided," Vesper said, "that I am finished helping this family keep its secrets."

The door opened. Ulyana stood in it, grease-streaked, exhausted, beautiful, looking at my face and understanding in one glance that whatever she'd climbed down here to ask me had just become obsolete.

"Silas," she said slowly. "What did you find?"

I looked at her—at the granddaughter of a murdered woman, the keeper of two hundred souls, the woman two men had loved and only one had told—and I thought: *he said tell her like it's a gift.*

"Everything," I said. "Sit down. I have to tell you about the man I used to be."
