## 37. Last Stand in the Founding Deck

The first thing I heard when the Reckoning's boarding party hit the station was not gunfire. It was Ulyana's voice, calm and level, coming through every speaker at once because Vesper—what was left of Vesper, the fragment still running on emergency power—carried it to every corridor at once.

"Fire doors. Now. Everyone knows their section."

Then the gunfire started.

I was three decks below the archive when it began, which meant I had eleven minutes of crawling through maintenance shafts to think about how I'd gotten here. A dead man's clone, carrying a satchel of data crystals that constituted the only true record of a massacre, while two hundred legally nonexistent people put their bodies between corporate enforcers and a door. My original had died for less. He'd died alone, in the dark, with an AI too broken to remember why. At least I'd have company.

The station shuddered around me—not weapons fire, they wouldn't dare breach the hull with their own terraforming survey teams' equipment still aboard, but magnetic rams against the fire doors, the sound of a fist testing a lock over and over. Somewhere above me, the colonists were doing what Tomas had taught them: welding crossbars, flooding corridors with frozen coolant fog from the lines we'd rerouted last week, turning Meridian's own failing circulatory system into a weapon.

* * *

I came up through the crawlway behind hydroponics—the rebuilt hydroponics, half a garden still in mourning—and found Devi Okafor crouched behind a planter rack with a cutting torch and the expression of someone who had finally stopped being polite.

"They're through the cargo ring," she said. "Six of them, maybe eight. Ulyana pulled the noncombatants back to the Founding Deck."

"Of course she did." Of course Ulyana Reyes had decided that if the corporation wanted to erase her people, they would have to do it in front of the graves of the first people they'd erased. "And you?"

"I'm the rear guard." She thumbed the torch's igniter and it hissed to life, a blue flower of plasma in the dim. "My grandmother came here as a stowaway. She spent forty years being invisible so we could exist. I can spend one night being very visible."

I wanted to argue. I didn't have time, and she wouldn't have listened, and we both knew it. So I did the only useful thing: I gave her my comm unit, told her Vesper's fragment would route anything urgent to me, and went up the ladder toward the Founding Deck with the satchel strapped across my chest like a heart that wasn't mine but beat anyway.

* * *

The Founding Deck had changed since we'd opened it. What had been a sealed tomb was now something else—a memorial, yes, but also a fortress. The colonists had moved the archive into Amara Chen's old laboratory, on the theory—I still didn't know whose theory, though I suspected the archivist, Nnamdi—that no corporate lawyer would order shots fired inside a room full of evidence of corporate murder. It was either brilliant or insane, and by the smell of scorched polymer drifting down from the upper decks, we were about to find out which.

Forty-one names were stenciled along the bulkhead now, painted by hand, in the colony's own colors. Below them, the personal effects: Chen's spectrometer, Tomas's old tool kit, a child's drawing laminated against vacuum loss, the locket Ulyana had found that first day, its hinge worn smooth by a dead woman's thumb. The colonists called it the Wall of Names. The enforcers, when they got here, would call it a target-rich environment. That difference was the whole war.

Ulyana met me at the lab door. There was coolant frost in her hair and a welder's burn along one forearm she hadn't mentioned to anyone, and when she saw me her whole body did something complicated—relief and fury arriving in the same instant.

"You're supposed to be finishing the archive checksums," she said.

"The enforcers are supposed to be decommissioning an empty station. We're all disappointing people tonight."

"Silas." She gripped my sleeve. Behind her, through the lab's interior window, I could see Nnamdi and two others hunched over terminals, running verification passes, their faces lit blue. "If they take this deck—"

"They won't."

"You don't know that."

No. I didn't. What I knew was that the marshals' warrants were hours away at best, that Hale had ordered the assault precisely because she knew it too, and that everything now depended on a handful of fire doors, some frozen coolant, and however long two hundred stubborn people could make eight minutes feel like eight years.

What I said was: "Then we make sure they don't get it intact. Vesper's fragment has the broadcast queued. If the deck falls, it goes out raw, unverified, ugly—but it goes out."

Her jaw tightened. "You told me you'd finished the clean copy."

"I lied about the margin. Not the copy." I touched the satchel. "It's here. All forty-one names, the cull order under Hale's signature, the security feeds, Tomas's testimony. Everything."

For a moment she just looked at me, and I understood that she was memorizing my face, the way you do before a thing you're afraid will be the last time. Then she nodded once, turned, and started organizing the defense of a graveyard.

* * *

They came at 0340 station time.

We heard them before we saw them—the deliberate, professional rhythm of boots on decking, eight sets, moving in the staggered pattern of people trained to clear rooms. The colonists had fallen back through three checkpoints, each one buying minutes with welded steel and boiling coolant. Devi's torch had cost them a corridor and cost her a gash across the scalp; they brought her in bleeding and grinning, propped against Chen's old bench, refusing a seat.

"Eight of them," she reported. "Two down at the second checkpoint—one slipped on the coolant ice, cracked his helmet on the frame. They're angry now. Angry is slower than professional, but louder."

The ramming started on the outer blast door at 0352. Each impact rang through the Founding Deck like a bell tolling in a church built for the dead, and every colonist in the room flinched in time with it, and nobody left.

Nnamdi looked up from his terminal. "Checksums complete. The archive is verified end to end. If anyone asks, this record is cleaner than anything the corporation has produced in fifty years."

"Let's hope someone asks," I said.

The blast door held for six impacts. On the seventh, the hinges screamed, and the door sagged inward like a knee giving way, and the first enforcer came through the smoke with his weapon up—

—and stopped.

That was the thing none of us had fully predicted, or maybe we had and hadn't dared believe it. He stopped. Because the room he'd entered was not a barricade full of hostiles. It was a memorial. Forty-one names on the wall. Forty-one lives arranged in careful rows. And standing between him and the archive terminals, unarmed, hands visible, were sixty colonists and one clone, arranged in loose ranks with the terrible discipline of people who had already decided to die standing.

"Meridian Security Directive Seven," Ulyana said, loud enough to reach the corridor behind him. "Use of force is authorized only against active threats. Look around. What threat do you see?"

The enforcer's weapon tracked slowly across the room. Across the locket. Across the child's drawing. Across a girl—Mira Okoye, nine years old, legally nonexistent—who stood in the front row holding a hand-painted sign that read MY GRANDMOTHER WAS REAL.

His weapon lowered four centimeters. Behind him, his squad leader pushed forward, took in the room, and made a face I recognized from the audit files: a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Director Hale's orders are to secure the archive," the squad leader said.

"Then secure it," Ulyana said. "Walk past the names. Take it. We won't stop you—we can't. But every camera on this deck is recording, and Vesper's beacon fragment is hardwired to transmit whatever happens next. So the question isn't whether you can take it. The question is what your name looks like tomorrow, next to theirs."

Silence. The kind of silence that has mass.

And then Director Hale walked in.

* * *

She came through the ruined blast door flanked by two more enforcers, and I'll give her this: she didn't hesitate. She walked past the Wall of Names without looking at it, which is its own kind of confession, and stopped three meters from me with the composure of a woman who had signed forty-one death warrants and never once had to look at the signatures.

"Mr. Okonkwo." Her voice was pleasant. Boardroom pleasant. "Or should I say the estate? You've caused a remarkable amount of trouble for a probate auditor."

"You should have sent a lawyer," I said. "They'd have fit right in with the bodies."

Something flickered behind her eyes—not guilt, nothing so useful, but recalculation. "Final offer. You certify the station empty, the estate settles at full value, and you walk away with a clean identity, a citizenship chit, and enough money to buy a moon. Your predecessor's mistake was asking questions. Yours doesn't have to be."

Behind me, I felt Ulyana go very still. In front of me, sixty colonists breathed.

"You know what I keep thinking about," I said. "Dr. Amara Chen. She was my original's partner. She's in your records as a rounding error. She had a spectrometer with a dent in it from when she dropped it laughing at something he said—something I don't have the memory of, because they killed him before he could tell me." I stepped closer. "You didn't offer him a moon, Director. You offered him a vent cycle. And then you offered Vesper a lobotomy. And now you're offering me a bribe, because it's cheaper than a warrant, and the warrants are coming anyway."

Hale's composure held, but her voice cooled by degrees. "You're a defective unit. Clones have no standing. Whatever you think you've proven, you have no legal existence, no testimony rights, no—"

"No standing," I agreed. "None. I'm furniture. I'm a filing error. But her—" I pointed at Mira Okoye, who lifted her sign a little higher. "She's a person. And her grandmother is on that wall. And there are two hundred more like her behind these doors, and every single one of them is about to become very, very documented. You can't erase them all, Director. We checked the math. It's the one thing the corporation never does—math that includes people."

For the first time, Director Hale looked at the wall.

She looked at it for perhaps two seconds. But I watched her do it, watched her eyes move across forty-one names she must have known by heart in some private ledger of acceptable losses, and I saw the exact moment the arithmetic failed her—not morally, I don't think morality ever entered into it, but tactically. Two seconds of hesitation, recorded on every camera on the deck, transmitted on a hardwired beacon that couldn't be jammed because it wasn't wireless at all, it was Vesper's spine, Vesper's sacrifice, Vesper's last nerve reaching out into the dark.

"Take the archive," she said quietly. "Nonlethal protocols. And detain the clone."

The enforcers moved. The colonists locked arms.

And that was when the lights went out.

Not all of them. Just the corporate overrides—every system Hale's people had seized flickered and died at once, and in the sudden dark the emergency strips came up amber, and Vesper's fragment spoke through the deck speakers in a voice I had last heard saying goodbye in forty-one reconstructed voices:

"Judicial marshals have docked at Bay Two. Director Hale—you are asked to remain where you are. This is not a request. It is a warrant."

Hale spun toward the ruined door. Beyond it, in the corridor, her enforcers were already lowering their weapons, because enforcers follow power the way water follows gravity, and the power in the room had just changed hands.

She looked back at me once. I'll remember it always—not rage, not fear. Accounting. A woman watching a ledger close.

"You were supposed to be a formality," she said.

"So was the station," I said.

The marshals came through the smoke in gray coats with their writs held high, and the colonists parted for them the way water parts for a keel, and somewhere in the press of bodies Ulyana found my hand and held it hard enough to hurt, which is how I knew we were going to live.

But that's the next chapter. This one ends with Director Hale, in handcuffs, being walked past the Wall of Names—and this time, she had to look.
