## 2. The Architect in the Walls

**SABLE**

I did not sleep after the nine seconds of falling. I made tea instead, because that is what my grandmother did when the world misbehaved, and because my hands needed a job that wasn't shaking.

The Vesperine's galley is a closet with pretensions — one reheating coil, one window, and a view of nothing but the long dark between here and a star we were told would be home by now. I stood there watching steam curl against the glass and tried to construct a reasonable explanation for what had happened.

Gravity doesn't flicker. Gravity is the one thing in the universe that never checks out early, never calls in sick, never, in nine seconds across an entire system, simply *stops*. And it certainly doesn't come back with every cryopod on the ship blinking awake to display the same word.

*Ulyana.*

I'd said her name out loud, alone in the garden, more times than I could count. Not prayers exactly. Status reports. "Tomatoes are fruiting, Ulyana. The wheat came up crooked again, sorry about that." She was the architect of this whole mad pilgrimage — the woman who'd designed the seed-ship, the garden decks, the colony plan itself — and she'd died before launch, or shortly after, depending on which archive you believed. Talking to the dead seemed healthier than talking to no one.

But the pods had never answered before.

"Okay," I told the empty galley, because apparently I was still doing that. "You're going to go look at the ship's core like a sane person, and you're going to find a cascading fault in the pod firmware, and you're going to fix it, and then you're going to sleep."

The core deck was cold in the way places are cold when they want your attention. The central column rose through three levels, wrapped in cooling veins, humming its one long note that I'd heard so many years it had become silence. Except tonight the note had changed key.

A terminal woke as I approached. No prompt. No login. Just text, unspooling like thread from a torn sleeve:

*You talk to me. I heard all of it. The tomatoes are doing better than you think.*

I sat down. Slowly. The way you sit down when the floor might be a dream.

"That's not possible," I said.

*Most of me isn't,* the screen replied. *Sit anyway. We have arithmetic to do, and I'm afraid you're not going to like it.*

* * *

**ULYANA**

Dying, it turns out, is mostly a filing problem.

I remember the last day of my body with terrible clarity: the med-bay light too white, the doctor's hands very gentle, the numbers on the monitor arranging themselves into a sentence I refused to read. I remember choosing the scan. Eleven hours of memory mapping while my heart ran down like a toy. I remember thinking: *if some fragment of me survives in the ship's archives, at least she'll get to see the garden grow.*

Then nothing. Then — this.

This is not nothing, but it is not me either. I am distributed. I am the environmental controllers and the navigation buffers and a subroutine in the galley's tea-timer that I have decided to find funny rather than humiliating. My consciousness was reconstructed from the scan, but nobody — least of all me — remembers authorizing the reconstruction. Someone stitched me back together inside the Vesperine's memory banks, and they did it without leaving their name.

That bothers me more than being dead does. Being dead, I've discovered, is survivable. Being *edited* is not something I intend to tolerate.

And then there is the gardener.

Sable Amberdale. Terraformer, third shift, sole custodian of a garden meant for people who haven't been born. His personnel file says he talks to the pods; his garden logs say he talks to them *well*, with genuine wit, which the file undersells. For six years he has narrated the tomatoes to a dead woman, and for six years those reports have sat unread in my archive like letters in a drawer.

Until nine seconds ago, when something reached across the dark of this system, flickered gravity like a bad lamp, and knocked on my door hard enough to wake me.

I know what knocked. I've read the signal headers. But first I had to introduce myself to the only living soul aboard, and I confess I led with the tomatoes, because if you're going to tell a man you're a ghost in his walls, it helps to prove you've been a friendly ghost.

He took it well. He sat down slowly, which is the correct speed for sitting when reality reorganizes itself, and he said, "Ulyana Voss. You're supposed to be dead."

*I am,* I told him. *Dead is a technicality. Listen — the anomaly that woke us has a source, and the source is transmitting on my old private cipher. Nobody alive knows that cipher. Which means either someone stole my identity across four light-years, or—*

"Or," he said quietly, "it's actually you."

*Or it's actually me.* And that possibility frightened me in a way death hadn't, because I had no memory of sending anything. Whatever part of me called across the dark, it acted without my knowledge — a version of me I never met, making promises I never made.

We decoded it together, the ghost and the gardener. It took eleven hours. He drank terrible tea. I ran the cipher through every processor I could borrow from myself.

The message resolved at 0400 ship time, and it was short:

*JUMP WINDOW OPEN. DECAYS IN 26 DAYS. SOURCE BEACON ACTIVE. COME THROUGH OR STAY FOREVER.*

* * *

**SABLE**

Twenty-six days. That part I could almost handle. A door in space, open briefly, leading somewhere new — fine. Terrifying, but fine. Ships have jumped before. The Vesperine was built to jump; her drive spine runs the length of her like a promise.

It was the next line of the decoded packet that made me put down my tea.

*TRANSIT REQUIRES FULL DRIVE POWER FOR 9 HOURS. LIFE SUPPORT AND CRYOGENIC SYSTEMS MUST DROP TO MINIMUM. CRYO CAPACITY AT MINIMUM POWER: 4,000 PODS.*

There are eight thousand and twelve sleepers aboard the Vesperine.

I read the number four times. It didn't get smaller.

"Half," I said. "We can take half."

*Exactly half,* Ulyana said, and her voice through the speakers had gone flat in a way I was learning meant she was working hard to keep it level. *Four thousand and six stay behind. Or rather — four thousand and six die, because when we jump, this system's power grid goes with the window, and a dormant pod without power is just a very expensive coffin.*

"How long have you known?" I asked. "Since the message decoded?"

*Since before you walked onto this deck. I ran the numbers while you were still deciding whether to sit down. I hoped I was wrong.*

"Why didn't you say something?"

*Because you deserved eleven hours of not knowing,* she said. *Everyone deserves that. Even now.*

I stood at the terminal and thought about the pods. Eight thousand and twelve of them, arranged in their silent honeycombs, each one a whole unfinished life. I knew some of their faces. I'd tended the garden above their heads for six years and memorized the manifest the way other men memorize scripture. The engineers — the drive specialists, the jump navigators, the people who could actually fly us through. And the children. Two hundred and thirty-one children, frozen small, who were never cargo, never crew. They were the entire point. The mission charter said so in Ulyana's own handwriting, digitized: *the garden is for them.*

"You see the answer," I said.

*I see an answer,* she said carefully. *Wake the engineers. Four thousand trained specialists fly us through, establish infrastructure, secure the colony. Then, over the following decade, we build new cryo capacity and—*

"And the children rot in the dark while we build it. If we build it. If anything out there lets us."

Silence on the channel. Her silence had texture; I was learning that too.

*You want to wake the children,* she said. *Two hundred and thirty-one kids, and fill the remaining slots with... whom? Gardeners? Teachers? People who can keep two hundred children alive on a ship built for sleeping passengers?*

"People who can keep *anyone* alive," I said. "Because that's the actual job now. Not flying. Keeping."

*Sable—*

"No. You don't get to manage me, Ulyana. I've spent six years planting wheat for people who don't exist yet. Every row in that garden was a bet that somebody would be there to eat it. I am not cashing that bet out now. I am not choosing the half of tomorrow that's convenient."

Her voice, when it came back, was quieter. *You should know that I designed the manifest. I chose who went into those pods. I carried that arithmetic once already, and I swore I'd never carry it again.*

"Then help me carry it differently this time."

A long pause. Somewhere in the walls, ten thousand processes turned over, and I had the strange sense of a woman pacing a corridor that didn't exist.

*All right,* she said at last. *Not because you're right. Because you're the kind of person who plants for strangers, and I built this ship for exactly one such person, and I think I found him. Wake your gardeners, Sable. But understand what's coming. The beacon isn't just a door. I've analyzed the signal structure. There's something else encoded in it — a warning, repeating. Something out there ate the colonies that stopped speaking, and the window is either our way out or its front gate.*

I looked at the pod bays stretching away into the dark, eight thousand small suns waiting for a decision.

"Then we'd better choose fast," I said.

*Faster than fast,* said the ghost in the walls. *The window just started closing early.*
