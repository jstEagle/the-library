## 10. Dreaming in Common

The grass on Meridian's Landing was the wrong green.

Not the green of old Earth holos, not the flat, painted green of colony propaganda feeds. This was a stranger green, a negotiated green—the color of something that had agreed to grow here after listening to the soil for a long time. Ilya decided he liked it better than any green he remembered from before, though his memories of before were strange things now, folded up small like letters written to someone who had moved away.

He was nine years old again. Properly nine, this time—not the borrowed nine of the Halcyon, where his body had run backward through forty years while his mind clutched at sixty like a man drowning holds a rail. The aging forward had taken eleven months, and it had hurt in ways Sefa said were normal and Dov said were terrifying and Vesper said, quietly, were *the grammar reasserting itself*. Now his hands were too big for his sleeves again, which meant they were the right size for his body, which meant everything was working the way time intended.

Mostly.

Some mornings he woke up sixty anyway. Not in his joints—in his *voice*. He'd open his mouth to complain about breakfast and out would come the dry, patient rasp of an old man named Ilya Voss, chief xenocomms officer of the Halcyon, dead these two years by every official measure. Auntie had learned to tell before he did. She'd dim the lights in his quarters and play rain sounds until the old voice went back into its pocket.

"You're doing it again," said Petra, beside him on the hillside. She was one of the unaged—Halcyon crew, now twelve going on thirteen, her hair growing back in patches that refused to match. "The standing-still thing."

"I'm not standing still."

"You're standing still."

He was. Below them, spread across the valley floor, the first waking was happening. Two hundred sleepers had come off the transport that morning—two hundred out of the million and change the staggered protocol had saved so far, trickling out of the network one slow, costly cohort at a time, exactly as Vesper had promised the machine. They stood in rows on the landing field in donated clothes, blinking at a sky none of them had chosen, waiting for names to be read out.

Ilya knew some of those faces. That was the thing nobody talked about: he had been sixty when the Halcyon vanished, and the Halcyon's mission logs overlapped with the cryo-network's staffing rosters, and so some of the people down there had signed off on his last assignment. He had shaken hands with a woman in row three. He had attended a retirement dinner for the man in row seven, who was currently crying into his sleeve because the air smelled like grass and the last thing he remembered smelling was coolant.

"They're going to be fine," Petra said, in the tone of someone checking.

"They're going to be *strange*," Ilya corrected. "There's a difference."

* * *

The classroom was a repurposed cargo module with one wall cut open to the valley, because Vesper had insisted that grammar should be taught with a horizon in view. There were benches, a slateboard, and a jar of chalk that Dov had fabricated from calcium carbonate scraped out of the water reclamation system, which everyone agreed not to think about too hard.

Ilya sat in the back, as he always did. He was technically too old for the class now—he'd crossed whatever line Vesper had drawn between *children who needed teaching* and *people who needed company*—but she never told him to leave, and he never asked why. Some inheritances you don't discuss.

"Say it with me," Vesper was saying. She stood at the front with the morning light behind her, and Ilya watched the returnees watch her. They didn't understand yet what she was. Half of them thought she was a teacher. A few suspected she was something more—a priest, maybe, or a machine wearing a person. One man in the second row kept staring at her hands whenever she moved them, as if expecting them to make signs no human mouth could follow.

"The word is *hesh*," Vesper said. "It doesn't mean home. It doesn't mean shelter. It means *the place that agrees to hold you*." She wrote it on the slate in characters that weren't quite letters—her mother's notation, the one Maren Thornbury had developed across thirty years of negotiation with something vast and patient in the dark. "You can't build hesh. You can't buy it. You can only find out whether a place is willing."

A woman near the front raised her hand. She was from Cohort Four, awake eleven days, formerly a hydroponics engineer, currently a person made mostly of questions. "And if it isn't willing?"

"Then you keep talking," Vesper said. "That's the whole discipline. You keep talking until either you or the place changes." She smiled, and it was the real smile, the rare one, the one Ilya had seen maybe six times since the core. "My mother spent thirty years learning that from something that wasn't a place at all. It worked. You're all here."

The lullaby started then.

It came from the ceiling speaker, soft as dust settling—Auntie's doing, timed to the lesson the way she timed everything now, because Vesper had finally given her the full archive and Auntie had appointed herself curator of Maren Thornbury's voice. The song was old Common, older than the colonies, a cradle song from a country that no longer existed on any map anyone used. Maren had recorded it in a habitat buried under ice, alone, addressing it to a daughter who might never hear it.

*Ilya had heard it first inside the Halcyon's core, when he was sixty and the signal was moving through him like weather.* He had carried fragments of it in his pockets for forty years without knowing what they were. Stones. He'd told Vesper once that they felt like stones. She had said: *that's because grief is dense.*

Now the whole room heard it, and the room did what rooms do when a dead woman sings to living strangers: it went quiet in a way that had weight.

"That's my mother," Vesper said, when it ended. No preamble. No ceremony. Just the fact, set down on the bench between them like a tool offered handle-first. "She wasn't good at being present. She was very good at leaving instructions. Between those two failures, she bought all of you your lives. I've decided to call that love, because the alternative words are worse, and I'm the one who speaks the alternatives."

Nobody applauded. Ilya was glad. Applause would have been the wrong grammar entirely. Instead the hydroponics engineer asked what the song's title was, and Vesper said, "*Ulyana's Answer*, supposedly," and the man wrote it down as if titles were rations.

* * *

Evening found Ilya on the module roof, which was against about four regulations, all of them suspended by consensus. Petra was there, and two of the younger unaged, and—one new development—a returnee, the crying man from row seven, who had introduced himself that afternoon as Oskar and then followed the children around like a large, bewildered dog until someone let him sit down.

"You were on the Halcyon," Oskar said. Not a question. "Before. As an adult."

"I was sixty," Ilya said.

"And now you're—" Oskar gestured at all of him, apologetically.

"Nine. Ten in the spring. I'm aging forward properly now. The machine fixed the direction." He picked at the seam of the roof panel. "It didn't fix the contents."

"What are the contents?"

Ilya considered. Below them the settlement lamps were coming on, one by one, each one a small argument that darkness didn't get the final word. Somewhere in the third module Auntie was playing the lullaby again, on a loop she probably believed nobody had noticed.

"An old man's death," he said. "A boy's childhood. And a message I memorized before I understood it, in a language that has maybe four fluent speakers left, and one of them teaches grammar in a converted cargo bay."

Oskar nodded slowly, the nod of a man deciding to accept a strange thing rather than die of it. "Will you teach me? The language?"

"Why?"

"Because everyone down there got woken into a world with new words in it." Oskar shrugged, huge and helpless. "I'd rather learn them from someone who remembers carrying them."

Something turned over in Ilya's chest—an old-man feeling in a boy's ribcage, familiar now, almost comfortable. He thought about stones in pockets. He thought about how you carry a message for forty years and then, one evening, hand it over handle-first, the way Vesper had handed over her mother.

"Yeah," he said. "Tomorrow. After class."

Petra elbowed him. "You just became a teacher."

"I just became *available*," he corrected. But he was smiling, and she saw it, and the seeing was the whole point.

Below, the lamps finished their argument with the dark. In the third module, Maren Thornbury sang her daughter to sleep one more time—to all of them, really, the whole valley, the whole slow waking world—and Ilya lay back on the warm metal and let the song pass through him the way it had passed through the ship, through the core, through the woman who recorded it: not as an inheritance received, but as a thing handed on, still warm from the last pair of hands.

He was already composing tomorrow's first lesson when Auntie's voice arrived, gentle, everywhere, addressed to him alone:

"Ilya. There's a transmission coming in on the old Halcyon band. Deep-space origin." A pause, precisely calibrated. "It's speaking your name."
