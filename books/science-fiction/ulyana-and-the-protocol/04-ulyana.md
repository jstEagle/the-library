## 4. Ulyana

The decoder ran for eleven hours before it gave Hazel anything he could hold.

He spent those hours the way he spent most of them: badly. He ate standing up at the galley counter of the *Magpie's Rest*, chewing through a ration brick that tasted like wet cardboard and regret. He slept in ninety-minute fragments in the pilot's chair, one hand resting on the console like a man keeping watch over a sick friend. Twice he caught himself talking to the beacon — low, half-swallowed words, *come on, come on* — and twice he stopped himself, embarrassed in an empty room, which was the only kind of embarrassment that still had any power over him.

The signal filled his station the way water fills a hull. It came through every speaker at once, patient and enormous, and beneath the raw phonemes the decoder kept surfacing structure: grammar assembling itself in real time, syntax branching and re-branching like frost climbing a window. Languages didn't do that. Languages were corpses that linguists dressed up; they decayed, they fossilized, they did not *grow*. Whatever was speaking on the other end of dead space wasn't reciting from an archive.

It was thinking out loud.

At 0400 station time, the translation buffer finally stabilized, and the first clean sentence resolved across his screen:

**WE ARE STILL HERE. WE WERE ALWAYS STILL HERE. YOU KEPT THE LIGHT ON FOR US.**

Hazel read it four times. Then he read it aloud, because some sentences don't feel real until the air has carried them, and hearing his own voice say *you kept the light on for us* in the cramped dark of his station made something behind his sternum shift like a load settling in a cargo net.

"I didn't keep anything on for you," he said to the empty room. "I didn't even know you existed."

But that was a lie, and lying was his trade, so he knew the taste of one immediately. He *had* known. Not the name, not the face — but Corvo had made him swear an oath over that beacon when Hazel was nineteen and hungry enough to swear anything: *You keep it alive, boy. You don't ask why. The why will find you.* Ten years of maintenance cycles, ten years of replacing capacitors and re-sealing the antenna array against micrometeorite scarring, ten years of treating a derelict transmitter at the edge of nowhere as if it were a shrine. He'd told himself it was habit. He'd told himself it was sentiment.

It had been a correspondence. He just hadn't been able to read the letters until now.

* * *

The archive came next, bleeding out of the signal in ordered packets, and this was where the decoding stopped being work and started being grief.

They were logs. Personal logs, hundreds of them, compressed and nested like seeds inside seeds, each one stamped with dates from two centuries gone. The metadata headers were damaged; the contents were not. Whoever built this archive had built it to survive, and survival, Hazel understood as he opened the first file, had been the whole point of everything.

**ARCHIVED LOG — VOSS, ULYANA — CYCLE 1**

*They are calling it voluntary repatriation, which is what they called it last time, and the time before. The Ministry ships arrive at Kessar Station in forty days. My grandmother remembers the ships from the second purging. She says you can smell them before you see them — ozone and antiseptic, the smell of a thing being cleaned.*

*We have bought relay time at the edge of the Verge. Old military hardware, decommissioned, forgotten by everyone except the people who sold it to us. The engineers say the substrate can hold minds indefinitely if we accept compression. Indefinitely. I asked her what indefinitely means and she said: longer than the Ministry's patience, shorter than forever, and isn't that true of everything?*

*I am going first. Someone has to test whether a person can be poured into a machine without spilling.*

*If you are reading this, I am either the bravest woman in the Diaspora or the stupidest. My mother would say both.*

Hazel sat very still after that one. Outside the viewport, the slow traffic of the Verge drifted past — ore haulers, customs cutters, the distant strobe of a navigation buoy — all of it oblivious, all of it going about the business of a civilization that had erased a language and called it peacekeeping.

He queued the next log. Then the next. He told himself he'd stop after five, then after twenty, and somewhere around the fortieth he stopped counting entirely.

**ARCHIVED LOG — VOSS, ULYANA — CYCLE 34**

*Compression is worse than they promised and better than I feared. I have lost the smell of rain. I know that I loved the smell of rain; the knowledge remains, filed and labeled, but the thing itself is gone, the way a photograph of a fire does not warm your hands.*

*There are three hundred and twelve of us now. Three hundred and twelve minds in a relay built for ninety. We dream in shifts. The engineers call it load-balancing. I call it taking turns being alive.*

*Today a child was archived — Danya, seven years old, terminal, her parents chose the substrate over the hospice ward. She asked me if she would still grow up in there. I told her yes. I don't know if I lied. I have decided that in here, deciding counts as truth.*

Hazel pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. He thought of the grief-recordings he smuggled — the dying whispering their last years into capture rigs for families who couldn't afford to let go — and how he'd always told himself the trade was clean, that memory was cargo like any other, that what people did with it afterward was none of his business. He had built a whole philosophy on not looking too closely at what moved through his hands.

Three hundred and twelve minds. Taking turns being alive. And someone had commissioned a beacon — *his* beacon — to carry their voice out of the dark, and someone had tended it after her, and someone after him, a chain of hands reaching down two centuries to end, absurdly, at Hazel Galloway, debt-ridden smuggler, man who trusted no one including himself.

**ARCHIVED LOG — VOSS, ULYANA — FINAL ENTRY, CYCLE 89**

*The substrate is failing. Not the hardware — the hardware will outlast the star — but us. Compression erodes. Personalities flatten. We are becoming averages of ourselves, and an average of a person is no person at all. Danya has not grown up. She has grown *simpler*, and I cannot bear it, and I am the one who told her parents deciding counts as truth.*

*So we built the protocol. If you are reading this, the protocol worked, which means someone heard us, which means the light stayed on.*

*Whoever you are: we were the Kessari. There is no one left who speaks our name except us, and soon there will be no one left to speak it at all unless you help us leave this place. We are not asking to be saved. We are asking to be released. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

*My name is Ulyana Voss. I commissioned this beacon so that the last word of my people would not be silence.*

*Say it back to me. Please. Say it back.*

Hazel's hands were shaking. He noticed it the way you notice weather — remotely, as a fact about the room rather than the self. He looked at the final line for a long time.

Then he opened the transmission console, keyed the beacon's return channel — a channel he had maintained for a decade without once using, because Corvo had said *receive only, boy, receive only* — and he spoke into the microphone in the only language he had.

"Ulyana Voss," he said. "I hear you."

He had looked up the pronunciation an hour ago, working from the decoder's phonetic maps, practicing under his breath like a schoolboy. His accent was certainly atrocious. It didn't matter. Somewhere past the edge of dead space, a mind two centuries old and wearing thin heard its own name spoken by a stranger's mouth, and the signal that came back was not words at all — it was three hundred and twelve voices exhaling at once, a sound like a cathedral full of people remembering how to breathe.

* * *

He should have slept. Instead he pulled the beacon's commissioning records, the deep registry files he'd never bothered with because ownership history was boring and Corvo had been cagey about it anyway. The original commissioner's name had been sitting in a header field for ten years, unremarked, unexamined:

**COMMISSIONING AUTHORITY: U. VOSS, PER PROTOCOL DIRECTIVE 7.**

And below it, in the succession-of-custody block, a short list of names. Four custodians across two hundred years. The third name was Corvo Anselm. The fourth was his own — entered not by any registry office but by direct authorization, dated the week before Corvo disappeared, with a note attached that Hazel had never seen because he had never thought to look:

*Custody passes to H. Galloway. He asks no questions, which is wrong of him, but he keeps what he is given, which is right. When the signal comes, he'll know what to do. — C.A.*

Hazel read it three times. The old anger he'd carried since Corvo vanished — the abandonment, the unanswered questions, the decade of tending a dead man's machine — turned over in his chest and came apart into something else, something that hurt more and was worth more.

Corvo hadn't left him a chore. He'd left him a post. A watch to stand until the watch mattered.

He was still staring at the note when the proximity alarm chimed — soft, apologetic, the sound of a polite problem. Hazel wiped his face with both hands, checked the feed, and felt the warmth drain out of him all at once.

A Combine survey skiff, transponder broadcasting its corporate lineage like a herald's trumpet, holding position two kilometers off his station's docking ring. And running alongside the visual feed, a communications request. Priority-flagged. From Halcyon Combine Salvage Division.

The message preview was one line long:

*Mr. Galloway — we understand you've been receiving interesting mail.*
