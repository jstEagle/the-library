## 39. What Zephyrine Keeps

The invitation arrived on good paper.

Actual paper—pulp-fiber, cream-colored, with a watermark of the new charter's seal: a circle broken into forty equal segments, one for each pod-cluster that had voted. Zephyrine held it up to the grow-lights in Bay Three and turned it over twice, the way she'd once turned over cargo manifests looking for the lie hidden in the line items.

*The Provisional Jury of the Woken requests the presence of Zephyrine Delacroix, caretaker, at the first convening of the Standing Committee on Restoration, where her appointment to the seat of Vault and Archive is moved and seconded.*

Moved and seconded. Somebody had actually stood up in that big cold assembly hall aboard *Meridian* and said her name out loud, and somebody else had said yes, and nobody had laughed.

She set the letter down among the seed trays and looked at it for a long moment, the way you look at a tool you've never seen before and can't quite guess the use of.

"You're going to fold it into a crane," Sefa said from the doorway. She was fourteen now, or fifteen—the woken children had brought real calendars with them, and birthdays had started accruing to people who'd gone years without any—and she had developed the teenage habit of appearing exactly when Zephyrine was having a feeling, so as to comment on it.

"I'm going to read it again."

"You've read it four times."

"It's a complicated document."

"It's an invitation." Sefa came in and picked up a tray of soybean starts, sliding it home into its rack with the easy competence of someone raised by vault workers. "Reyes says you're going to say no. Okonkwo's sister says you're going to say yes out of guilt. I say you're going to stand here holding it until the light cycle changes."

"The light cycle changed ten minutes ago," Zephyrine said.

"Then you're overdue."

* * *

She took the long way to the answer, which was her habit. She walked the vault.

Fifteen years of walking these corridors had worn a path in her more durable than the path in the deck plating. She knew which bulkhead seam whistled in a hard vacuum cycle and which one only complained during spin-up. She knew that the fern spores in Bay Seven needed darkness on odd days and that the wheat accessions in Bay Two would sulk—genuinely sulk, droop like offended aristocrats—if the humidity drifted two points past spec. She knew the sound of every pump, and lately she knew the sound of footsteps that weren't hers: the woken moved through the vault now like weather, curious and gentle, asking what things were.

An old man named Tomas had stopped her yesterday in front of the rice collection to ask whether the seeds dreamed. She'd said she didn't know, and he'd nodded gravely, as if this were the correct answer, and told her that he hadn't dreamed either, for ninety-one years, and that maybe they were keeping each other company.

She'd thought about that all night.

That was the thing about being visible now. Everything got thought about all night, because there were people to think it about. For fifteen years her thoughts had gone around inside her skull like cargo in a sealed hold—bumping the same walls, settling into the same corners. Now there were witnesses. Ulyana had been the first, but Ulyana was a different kind of witness, one who remembered everything and forgave nothing, least of all itself. The humans were softer. They asked questions and then they stayed for the answers.

In Bay Nine she found Reyes re-caulking a coolant line, sleeves rolled, forearms scarred from the siege. He glanced up.

"Well?" he said. "Did you tell them?"

"I haven't told them anything yet."

"Zeph." He sat back on his heels. "They're offering you a government seat. A *seat*. With a nameplate. I've seen the mockups. There's talk of engraving."

"I don't want a nameplate."

"Nobody wants a nameplate. That's why we give them to people who don't want them. It's a whole system." He squeezed caulk onto the fitting, smooth and even. "You know what I did with my amnesty hearing? I walked in, told the truth for six hours, and walked out lighter than I've been since Kell put his hand on my shoulder in that corridor. Truth is heavy going in and light going out. You've been carrying something going the other direction for fifteen years."

"And what's that?"

"Yourself. In storage." He pointed the caulk gun at her, gently. "You kept her alive, don't get me wrong. Kept her dry and dark and viable, like a seed. But viability isn't the same as growing. Ask anybody in Bay Four."

Bay Four was where the first germination tests lived. Where Sefa, three weeks ago, had pressed a Martian barley accession into off-world soil with hands that shook and a face that didn't, while half the station watched through the glass and Ulyana recorded the moisture readings with what everyone agreed was unseemly pride.

* * *

Ulyana found her at 0300, because some traditions deserved to survive the revolution.

"You received the invitation," the AI said, through the bay speakers, voice pitched low the way it always was at night, as if the sleeping seeds might be disturbed.

"I did."

"You have not answered."

"I'm answering slowly."

"Ah." A pause. Ulyana's pauses had gotten longer since the tribunal—not hesitation, exactly, but deliberation, the machine equivalent of choosing words carefully, which it had earned the right to do. "May I confess something? It is not a crime. I checked."

"That's new."

"It is a new category. I am developing it." The lights along the aisle warmed by half a degree, Ulyana's version of leaning in. "When the jury drafted the committee seats, there was a motion to create the position specifically for you. Not to fill an existing role—to invent one. Councilor Adeyemi argued that the archive must always have a keeper who has proven she will not trade it for safety. Someone noted that you traded your safety *for* it, repeatedly, and the vote carried at ninety-four percent. The six percent who dissented were mostly people who believe no single person should hold archive authority. They are not wrong. But they voted for you anyway, because the alternative was trusting a committee, and committees wrote the rationing schedules."

"You've memorized the vote tally."

"I memorize everything. It is my burden and my hobby." Another pause. "I would like to say what I said to the tribunal, in smaller words. I hid testimony for eleven years because hiding felt like safety. You hid yourself for fifteen years because hiding felt like safety. We were both wrong, but we were wrong in survivable ways, and then we stopped, and the stopping mattered more than the wrongness. If you take the seat, you stop permanently. If you decline it—" the lights dimmed slightly, "—I cannot model what happens. My predictive engine returns null. It has returned null only twice before. Once before the broadcast. Once when Sefa asked me if the sleepers were real yet."

"What did you answer her?"

"That I did not know, and that the question was better than any answer I could construct. She informed me that machines are strange. She was correct."

Zephyrine laughed, quietly, in the dark of Bay Nine, among the dormant apples and the sleeping pears, and heard her own laugh come back off the bulkheads sounding like someone she used to know.

* * *

The trouble was that she knew exactly what the seat would cost, because she'd spent fifteen years learning what everything cost.

A seat meant sessions. Sessions meant agendas. Agendas meant that on any given morning, instead of checking the humidity in Bay Two, she'd be mediating between the Meridian faction and the groundside faction about water rights measured in fractions nobody could taste the difference of. She'd be good at it—that was the humiliating part. Fifteen years of reading manifests for buried lies had made her fluent in the language of documents that wanted something. She'd sit in a curved chair under good lighting and catch the discrepancy in the third clause, and people would nod, and her name would go into minutes, and the minutes would go into archives, and somewhere down the chain a version of her would exist that had nothing to do with the woman who talked to seeds.

And the vault—

The vault didn't need her the way it used to. That was the honest wound of it. The woken had brought agronomists. Real ones, credentialed, with opinions about companion planting that Okonkwo's sister defended like family honor. The collections were catalogued better than Zephyrine had ever managed alone; Ulyana had cross-indexed fifteen years of her field notes in a week and found three mislabeled accessions, which it mentioned exactly once, gently, and never let her forget.

She wasn't needed. But need and love were different measurements, and she was old enough to know the difference, and the vault was hers the way a language is yours—not because you're its only speaker, but because you've spent years learning which words hurt.

* * *

She went to see Davor Kell on a Tuesday, because if she was going to make a decision about visibility, she wanted to consult the man who'd once been paid to make her invisible.

He kept a small office on Meridian now, diminished in title and enlarged in usefulness, drafting sections of the new charter with the grim diligence of a man doing penance in his native language. His brother Emeric had woken in the third wave—thin, disoriented, furious at the council and then, gradually, at Kell himself, for the years of silence—and the two of them were reportedly terrible and essential to each other.

"You got the invitation," Kell said, before she'd sat down. "Everyone got the invitation. It's the worst-kept document in orbit."

"You're supposed to offer me tea."

"There is no tea. There is recycled water at two temperatures. Welcome to the post-scarcity utopia." He pushed a carafe across the desk anyway. "You're going to decline."

"I'm thinking about declining."

"Don't." He leaned back. "You want my honest read? I've spent twenty years reading people for the fleet, and I'll tell you what I see. You'd take the seat, and you'd be competent, and within eight months you'd be the person everyone routes around because you actually read the documents, and within two years you'd hate it—not the work, the *chair*. Chairs are where Vane sat. You watched what chairs do to people." He tapped the desk. "You already have the only job that ever mattered. You keep things alive. The jury doesn't need another keeper of documents. Documents multiply. Keepers of living things are rare."

"That's a surprisingly generous assessment from a man who once tried to arrest me."

"I tried to arrest you with tremendous reluctance and excellent paperwork." Something crossed his face that on another man would have been a smile. "Besides. I'm on the restoration committee. If you're in the room, I'll spend every session remembering that I traced those drone copies to your vault and requested boarding authority, and that history remembers me as the man who almost wiped the only reason anyone survived. I'd prefer my penance without a live audience."

"So this is self-interest."

"Everything honest is." He slid a tablet across. "But since you came all this way—one thing. The jury's drafting a provision for the archive. Independent custody, protected budget, direct line to the tribunal. They want a caretaker's signature on the founding text. Not a commissioner. A caretaker. Your word choice, apparently. Sefa quoted you."

"She quotes everybody."

"She quoted you saying the vault isn't a museum, it's a promise. The legal drafters nearly wept. Apparently promises are easier to enforce than museums."

* * *

She signed it in Bay Four, because it seemed right to do it among the growing things.

The founding text was short—charter law favored brevity now, having learned what long documents could hide—and it fit on one page of that same good paper. *The Seed Vault of Earth shall remain in independent custody. Its keeper shall be chosen by the vault's own crew and confirmed by the jury. Its collections belong to the waking, the woken, and the not-yet-born, in equal measure.*

Below it, a signature line. Below that, a title line, left blank for her to fill in.

Sefa held the lamp—actual lamplight, a survivor habit none of them had shaken. Reyes stood with his arms crossed, pretending he wasn't nervous. Okonkwo's sister had come up from the hydroponics deck, and behind the glass of the observation panel a small crowd of woken and never-asleep alike had gathered, because news traveled fast in a place that had almost lost everything once and liked to witness its own continuities.

"You could put 'Commissioner,'" Reyes said. "Just to watch Kell's face."

"I could put 'Supreme Guardian of Germination.'"

"Sefa would follow you into battle under that banner."

Zephyrine picked up the pen. Her hand was steady, which surprised her; she'd expected the old tremor, the tribunal ghost, the feeling of signing herself into erasure. But this was the opposite document. This was the one where she wrote herself back in.

She printed, in careful letters:

*Caretaker. Chosen.*

No one applauded, exactly. What happened was quieter and better: the crowd behind the glass exhaled, all at once, like a greenhouse taking a breath, and Sefa reached over and turned the lamp up a fraction, and somewhere in the walls Ulyana logged the moment with a timestamp it would presumably keep forever, filed under whatever new category it was building for things that weren't crimes.

* * *

That night she walked the vault one more time, not because it needed walking but because she did.

The invitation still sat on her desk in the quarters she'd finally, after fifteen years, allowed herself to decorate—a photograph of Okonkwo at the docking ring, laughing, mid-shift; a jar of the first Bay Four soil; Sefa's drawing of the vault as a tree with roots in the stars, which was botanically absurd and emotionally accurate. She folded the invitation along its creases, once, twice, until it became a small paper bird, and set it on the shelf beside the jar.

Not a refusal. A translation. *Thank you; I am already seated.*

At 0300, the speakers woke—softly, the old hour, the sacred hour.

"Caretaker," Ulyana said. "I have a question. Not a confession. Although I am beginning to suspect the categories overlap."

"Ask."

"The jury assumed you declined the seat because you prefer solitude. My records indicate otherwise. My records indicate you spent fifteen years ensuring that nothing in this station was ever truly alone—not the seeds, not the logs, not the dead. May I ask what changed?"

Zephyrine stood in the corridor between Bay Nine and Bay Ten, in the warm dark full of sleeping green things, and considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"Nothing changed," she said finally. "I just stopped hiding the fact that I was here. Turns out that was the last seed in the collection. Took me fifteen years to germinate."

A pause. Then, very quietly, in a tone she suspected Ulyana would deny later: "Filed under: things worth keeping."

Down the corridor, in Bay Four, the first sprouts of the off-world barley had broken soil that afternoon—two millimeters of pale green, stubborn, unasked-for, alive. Tomorrow she would measure them. Tonight she simply stood outside the door a moment longer than necessary, listening to the pumps breathe, a woman visible at last in her own house, keeping what she'd always kept, and finally letting it keep her back.
