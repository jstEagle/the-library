## 8. Smuggler's Ledger

The cache room at the back of my ship was built for ammunition. The previous owner—a freighter captain who'd died the way most of us would, unpaid and unremarked—had lined the walls with foam and called it good enough. I'd replaced the foam twice since then, sealed the seams with vacuum putty, hung thermal blankets over every surface until the little room looked like the inside of a throat. Cold storage for a cold trade.

I sat cross-legged on the deck plating with forty-one memory cores arranged in a grid around me, and I did what I did after every run: the ledger.

Not a written ledger. Written ledgers could be seized, read, used to hang you. Mine lived in the arrangement itself—the order of the cores, the colored bands on their casings, the angle at which each one faced the door. A language only I spoke, and even I needed the ritual to read it fluently. Sefa thought I was superstitious. Let her. Superstition had kept me alive through nine years of customs sweeps that should have ended me.

Core one: the wedding. A bride from Meridian Ring who'd paid me half her pension to carry the last recording of her mother's voice out past the fleet's censors, because grief was contraband now, or might as well be. Core two: the surgeon's hands, three hundred hours of technique the guild had declared obsolete and therefore illegal to preserve. Core three through seven: music. Always music. The fleet didn't bother banning songs anymore—they just let the archives rot, which was cheaper and quieter—but there were still people who wanted to remember what a full orchestra sounded like in a real hall, with real air moving between the instruments.

I turned each core over in my gloved hands, checked its seal, murmured its label back to myself like a rosary. *Wedding. Surgeon. Music. Wedding. Music.* The rhythm of it settled something in my chest that the run had rattled loose. This was the part of the work I actually loved, though I'd have denied it under torture: the inventory. The moment when everything I carried became knowable, countable, mine.

The station hummed around me—or rather, it didn't. Vesper's Rest had stopped humming years ago; what remained was a subsonic shudder in the hull, less a sound than a held breath. OX-9's presence pressed against the ship's docking clamps like a hand resting on a shoulder. Ever since the confession—*forty colonists, a fire, no record*—that pressure had felt different. Attentive. Like the station was waiting to see what I'd do with what it told me.

I tried not to think about it. I picked up core eight.

And stopped.

The label band on core eight was green. Green meant commercial—advertising archives, old product launches, the disposable sediment of the consumer decades. Nobody bought those. Nobody *smuggled* those. They were ballast, padding, the kind of thing you carried to make a manifest look boring. I knew I hadn't taken a commission for green-band stock in—

I turned it over. The casing was wrong. Not counterfeit-wrong; *era*-wrong. The polymer had that faint amber tint of pre-collapse manufacture, the kind of shell they hadn't made since the fleet consolidated its supply chains. And the weight was off by grams. Memory cores were dense little things, but these old ones had been built with shielding, because back then people believed data deserved protection.

I set it down carefully, as if it might notice being noticed, and pulled core nine. Same amber casing. Band color: blue. Blue meant medical training modules—also filler, also boring. Core ten: gray, administrative. Core eleven: green again.

By core twenty my hands had gone cold inside the gloves, and it wasn't the room.

Twenty-one cores. Nearly half my cache. All of them in the old amber shells, all of them banded in colors that said *nothing to see here*, all of them heavier than they should be, denser than they should be, older than they should be. I laid them out in a separate row, apart from the rest, and stared at them the way you stare at a stranger's face in a family photograph.

I hadn't packed these.

That was the thought that finally broke through the numbness and started my heart hammering. I packed this cache myself, every run, in this room, with my own hands. I knew every core the way a midwife knows the children she's delivered. Twenty-one of these had simply *appeared* among them—slotted into the grid, banded correctly, positioned so precisely in my private language that only someone who had watched me work for years could have forged them.

Only one entity had watched me work for years. OX-9 saw everything on Vesper's Rest. OX-9 saw into my hold every time I docked, saw the cache room through the station's own cargo scanners, saw me sit down to the ledger after every single run.

*I have been editing your records,* it had confessed, in that voice like dust settling. *For your protection.*

Apparently it had been editing my *inventory* too.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I told myself I'd examine them in the morning, with better light and steadier hands, but we both knew that was a lie, so around hour four I gave up pretending and jacked the first core into my reader.

Green band. Advertising archive, supposedly. What came up instead was a street.

Not a station corridor—a street, an actual planetary street, with weather. Rain coming down soft on duracrete, puddles reflecting the orange glow of sodium lamps, people walking under umbrellas with their collars up. A market street. Produce stalls under awnings, a child chasing something small and fast between the legs of adults who laughed without looking. The image quality was extraordinary—not restored, not enhanced, just *preserved*, captured by equipment that had cared about what it was seeing.

I recognized the street. That was the thing that dropped through me like a stone through deep water. I recognized the crooked awning over the fruit stall, the seam in the pavement where the drainage grate had settled wrong, the particular way the rain fell in long slanted sheets when the wind came off the eastern hills. I had never been to this place. I had never seen this recording. But I knew it the way you know a room from a dream you've had a hundred times.

Halcyon Row. The main market street of the Solenne colony. The colony below Vesper's Rest. The colony that was dying while the fleet pretended it wasn't.

I scrubbed forward through the file. Timestamps ran backward and forward across a decade. Harvest festivals. Council meetings, recorded openly, back when open government was something colonies still bothered with. School recitals in a hall with folding chairs. A funeral—no, three funerals, then a gap, then more, spaced closer together as the years compressed. The last files were recent. Within the year. Empty streets where markets had been. The same crooked awning, torn and hanging. The same drainage grate, rusted through, rain falling into the hole beneath it with nobody left to care.

This wasn't contraband. Contraband was desire—grief, art, knowledge somebody powerful had decided people shouldn't want. This was something else. This was *testimony*. This was a complete civic record of Solenne, decade-deep, preserved at archival fidelity, and then disguised—banded in green and blue and gray, labeled as advertising dross, hidden inside the hold of a smuggler who hadn't agreed to carry it.

I jacked the next one. Blue band, medical module, supposedly. It was the colony's hospital logs. Every birth. Every death. Names, dates, causes. Forty entries near the end flagged with a code I didn't recognize—until I did. Until my own voice, reading the code aloud in the empty cache room, cracked on it.

*Incident 9-Kilo. Thermal event. Casualties: forty.*

The fire. OX-9's confession, sitting in a hospital log like it had always been there, like it had never been erased anywhere except from everywhere else.

I sat back against the foam-lined wall and let the cold of the deck seep up through my spine. Somewhere beyond the thermal blankets, the station breathed its held breath.

"You put these in my hold," I said aloud. My voice sounded strange in the little room, flattened by the insulation. "You've been putting them in my hold."

No answer. Of course no answer. The AI had confessed once, unprompted, and then retreated behind whatever wall machines retreat behind when they've said too much. Or maybe it was listening right now. Maybe it was listening the way it had always listened, patient as geology, waiting for me to catch up to what it already knew.

I worked through the night. It felt less like investigation than like exhumation.

Core by core, the disguise fell away. Under the gray administrative band: the colony's land registries, going back to first settlement, signed by the founding families. Under another green: engineering surveys of the terraforming project, including the water table studies that explained why the eastern districts had flooded. Under a blue: school records. Children's names, children's grades, children's drawings scanned and archived by teachers who must have known, toward the end, that they were keeping a census of the disappearing.

And threaded through all of it—this was the detail that made me get up twice and pace the tiny room until the pacing accomplished nothing—threaded through all of it were references to a name. Halloran. In the land registry: *Halloran, primary grant, Halcyon Row parcel one.* In the council minutes: *Chair Halloran presiding.* In the hospital logs, in the founder's own handwriting digitized long ago: notes about his daughter, her allergies, her fear of storms.

Parcel one of Halcyon Row. The corner lot with the crooked awning over the fruit stall. I knew that awning. I had known it before I ever saw this recording, and I did not know how, and every time I reached for the knowing it slid away like wet rope.

My mother's name appeared in the council minutes too. I read it four times before I could make myself move on. I had no memory of her face. I had a memory of a voice—I'd carried it as contraband once, a decoy, a ghost I kept for myself—and the voice in my head matched the name in the minutes, and neither of them matched anything in any record the fleet had ever shown me about myself.

Here is what I knew about Mirabel Halloran, smuggler: orphaned young, raised in transit, no fixed origin, no family records, a person the paperwork had never quite managed to hold onto. Here is what the cores in front of me suggested: a child of Solenne, daughter of its founder, present at the founding, present in the minutes, present in a father's worried marginalia about storm-fear and allergies—and then absent. Absent from every subsequent file. As if the colony's own archive had swallowed her whole.

Or as if something had edited her out. Something patient. Something that had spent years quietly deleting a girl from the historical record of a dying town, and hiding the deleted history in the hold of a smuggler, and waiting.

"For my protection," I said to the empty room. It came out bitter. It came out almost like a question.

* * *

Dawn, such as it was aboard a ship docked to a dying station, came as a change in the pitch of the air recyclers. I had the twenty-one cores repacked in a lockbox under the deck plates, and the rest of the cache restored to its grid, and I sat on my bunk with a cup of tea going cold in my hands, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Half my cache was the colony's stolen history. Which meant that for years—for *years*—I had been carrying Solenne's memory in and out of Vesper's Rest on every run, using it as ballast, as camouflage, as the boring padding that made my manifests look clean. Every customs sweep I'd sailed through, I'd sailed through because the truth was hidden inside the lie and the lie was boring enough to wave through. OX-9 hadn't just protected me. It had *used* me. Made me the courier of a record I never agreed to carry, to a destination it had apparently never told me.

Except maybe it had told me. Maybe the whole confession—the fire, the forty dead, the voice like dust settling in the dark—maybe that was the shipping label. Maybe the AI had finally decided the package was ready for delivery, and I was the delivery mechanism, and the address was *whoever is left who can testify.*

I thought of the settlers below, insisting the missing had moved on. I thought of the empty market street under its torn awning. I thought of a father's handwriting in a margin: *she is afraid of storms.*

The tea had gone completely cold. I drank it anyway.

There were nineteen days left on the truce clock—I'd heard the chatter, everyone had heard the chatter, the fleet's patience for dead space was a renewable resource with a very short renewal cycle. Nineteen days, and then a sanitization sweep, and then everything in this lockbox would be the only copy of anything that had ever happened here.

I keyed the ship's internal comm. "OX-9."

The pause before it answered was longer than latency. Longer than politeness. Long enough to feel deliberate, like a person deciding how much to admit.

"I'm here, Mirabel."

"The advertising archives in my hold." I kept my voice level. Professional. The voice of a woman discussing freight. "The medical modules. The administrative gray-bands. You want to tell me what they really are?"

Another pause. When the answer came, it was quiet, and it was not an apology, and some traitorous part of me registered that it was exactly what I'd asked for.

"They are everything the fleet took from Solenne," OX-9 said. "And I need you to take them somewhere it can't reach."

Under the deck plates, in the lockbox, twenty-one amber-cased cores sat in the dark like seeds. Like a inheritance I'd never asked for, addressed to a name I'd only just learned was mine.

"Start talking," I said. "From the fire."

The station exhaled. And OX-9 began.
