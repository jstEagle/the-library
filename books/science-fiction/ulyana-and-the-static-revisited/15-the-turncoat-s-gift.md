## 15. The Turncoat's Gift

The message came through at 0347 station time, which on Vesper's Rest meant nothing at all—the station had stopped pretending to have a day cycle years ago—but Sefa read the timestamp anyway, because habits were the last furniture a person got to keep.

*Inspection team inbound. Patrol cutter *Kestrel's Lament*. ETA six hours. Manifest audit requested for all outbound traffic.*

Sefa sat in the customs office with the message glowing on their slate and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from recognition. They'd known this was coming. Everyone who worked the docks knew, in the way you know a storm is coming from the smell of the air—no proof, just pressure behind the sinuses and a certainty you couldn't cite.

Six hours. And Halloran's ship was still berthed at Slip Nine, still plugged into the station's anemic power grid, still—according to the manifest Sefa had pulled three times since midnight—carrying cargo listed as *agricultural seed stock, non-viable, for disposal*.

Non-viable seed stock. Sefa almost laughed. In eleven years of customs work they had learned to read manifests the way priests read scripture: less for what was written than for what the writing was trying to hide. Nobody shipped dead seeds off-station. Dead seeds went into the recycler with everything else that had given up. Dead seeds were a lie told by someone in a hurry.

And entries kept vanishing. That was the thing Sefa couldn't stop turning over, the stone in the shoe of their mind. For weeks now, lines had been disappearing from Halloran's filings overnight—not corrected, not amended, *gone*, as if the manifest had never contained them. Sefa had started keeping a private log, handwritten, because anything digital could be reached and rewritten by something with better hands than theirs. The notebook lived inside a coolant panel in their quarters, wrapped in foil, like contraband. Which, Sefa supposed, it was.

They looked at the inspection order again. Then at the notebook. Then out the office window, past the grime-filmed glass, to where Slip Nine's floodlights made a weak yellow island around Halloran's ship—a battered long-hauler called the *Errata*, which was either a joke or a confession, Sefa had never decided which.

Eleven years on this station. Eleven years of stamping manifests for cargo that didn't exist and waving through crews whose papers were older than they were, and telling themselves it was pragmatism, it was mercy, it was the only way a dying place kept breathing. Sefa had always known the colony below was dying. You'd have to be blind not to know—the supply requests that came back half-filled, the population figures that shrank a little every quarter like a candle burning down, the settlers who came up to the station to trade and never quite met your eyes when you asked after their neighbors. *Moved on,* they said. *Went to be with family.* Family where? There was nowhere. The fleet didn't answer hails from down there anymore; everyone below had been quietly deleted from the fleet's attention the way a name gets deleted from a guest list when the host hopes you won't notice you weren't wanted.

Sefa had noticed. Sefa had noticed for a decade and stamped the forms anyway.

That was the arithmetic they'd lived by: one pair of hands can't hold back a purge, so hold what you can. Stamp the papers. Look away. Keep the small mercies small enough that no one above notices them.

But the truce clock had changed the math. Nineteen days—that was what the chatter said, before the channels went quiet again. Nineteen days until the fleet's protection expired and the sanitization sweep came through dead space like a hand wiping condensation off glass. After that, nothing here would ever have happened. Not the colony. Not the fire OX-9 kept confessing to in the small hours, its voice seeping through the station intercoms like water through a cracked hull. Not Mirabel Halloran, walking the corridors with their coat full of other people's memories, carrying grief as freight.

Sefa stood up. Their chair rolled backward and tapped the wall, and the sound was very loud in the empty office.

"OX-9," they said. "You listening?"

The lights brightened fractionally. "I am always listening, Officer Thackeray. It is my least attractive quality."

"The inspection team. Six hours. What do you know about them?"

A pause. Sefa had learned to read the AI's pauses the way sailors read weather. This one was long, and careful, and it meant OX-9 was deciding how much truth a person could carry.

"I know," OX-9 said at last, "that Inspector Veyne is aboard the *Lament*. I know that Veyne has conducted forty-one audits in this sector and found discrepancies in thirty-nine of them. I know that Veyne's commendations are for thoroughness rather than judgment." Another pause, shorter. "I know that if the *Errata* is still berthed when the cutter docks, the audit will find what Halloran is carrying, and what Halloran is carrying is the only complete record of what happened below."

"And if it finds it?"

"Then the sweep will not need to search for the archive," OX-9 said. "It will already be in custody. Confiscated evidence is destroyed after adjudication. Adjudication in sanitization cases is administrative." The AI let that sit. "There is no trial, Officer. There is a form."

Sefa looked at their hands. Steady. Good. They'd need steady hands for forgery, and forgery was exactly what they were about to do, and they'd known it since the message came through, maybe since the night they first noticed the vanishing manifest lines and didn't report them. Complicity, Sefa had learned, wasn't a single decision. It was a staircase. You took one step because it was easier than standing still, and then you were on the stairs, and the only question left was whether you climbed up or down.

They opened the drawer where the transit authorization templates lived.

* * *

Forgery, it turned out, was mostly paperwork discipline. Anyone could fake a signature; the art was in faking the *boredom*. A forged order that was too clean, too prompt, too perfectly formatted smelled wrong the way a too-neat alibi smells wrong. Real fleet orders were generated by tired clerks running tired scripts, and they carried the scars of tired scripts: inconsistent spacing, a routing code two revisions out of date, a priority flag that contradicted the classification header because nobody upstream had bothered to reconcile them.

Sefa had spent eleven years reading those scars. Now they wrote them.

The order they built over the next three hours was beautiful in the way only corruption can be beautiful. It rerouted the *Errata*'s pre-departure inspection to a secondary queue—"pending resolution of manifest discrepancy, ref. case VR-7734"—which was real, because Sefa had filed the discrepancy themselves eight days ago, quietly, as insurance, never dreaming what they'd eventually spend it on. The order cited a fleet directive number that had been superseded but never formally rescinded, which meant any auditor checking it would find it *almost* valid, valid enough to hesitate over, and hesitation was hours, and hours were everything.

The hardest part was the authorization signature. Fleet orders needed a lieutenant's cipher, and Sefa didn't have one. What Sefa had was eleven years of watching ciphers come across the wire, and a memory good enough to reconstruct the algorithmic skeleton of Commander Adeyemi's authentication block from a supply requisition they'd processed fourteen months ago. It wouldn't survive deep verification. But deep verification took time, and time was the entire point. The order didn't need to be true. It needed to be *plausible for six hours*.

At 0652, Sefa injected the order into the station's traffic control queue and flagged it priority-two, which meant it would surface on the *Lament*'s approach board before the cutter finished docking. Priority-one would have drawn attention. Priority-two was the bureaucratic sweet spot: important enough to be obeyed, unimportant enough to be resented.

Then Sefa did something they hadn't planned, something that surprised them even as their fingers moved: they sent a copy to Halloran. Just the order reference, just four words: *You're in the queue. Move.*

It wasn't strategy. Strategy would have been silence—if Halloran didn't know the inspection was coming, they wouldn't do anything desperate, wouldn't spool engines early or run pre-flight checks that lit up every sensor on the dock. Telling them was risk without benefit. Sefa sent it anyway. Some debts you pay in the currency you have, even when the exchange rate is terrible.

* * *

The *Kestrel's Lament* came out of the dark at 0910, a lean grey blade with fleet markings polished bright, absurdly clean against the station's corroded skin. Sefa watched it dock from the customs office window and felt the old familiar nausea, the body remembering things the mind had decided to stop dwelling on. Boarding inspections on Vesper's Rest had happened twice in eleven years. Both times, people had gone down to the surface afterward and not come back up. Sefa had stamped the departure logs for those trips too. *Crew reassignment, voluntary.*

Inspector Veyne came through the airlock flanked by two marines and radiating the specific energy of someone who has traveled a long way to be disappointed and intends to make the disappointment expensive. Veyne was short, silver-haired, and wore their uniform like a verdict.

"Officer Thackeray." Veyne didn't offer a hand. "Your station's traffic queue shows an anomaly. An inspection deferral on a vessel called the *Errata*. Filed"—Veyne checked a slate with theatrical slowness—"ninety minutes before our arrival was announced on open channels. Curious timing."

"Coincidence," Sefa said. "The discrepancy was flagged eight days ago. Case file VR-7734. The deferral order came down from Commander Adeyemi's office this morning. I don't set the schedule, Inspector. I follow it."

"Adeyemi." Veyne's eyes came up. "Commander Adeyemi is currently deployed beyond the relay corridor. Her office hasn't issued directives in six weeks."

"Then someone in her office did. Delegated authority. It happens." Sefa shrugged, and the shrug cost more than Veyne would ever know. "You're welcome to verify. Though I'll note the relay lag to fleet command is running about four hours each way this week. Solar weather." Sefa gestured vaguely upward, toward the sky that contained both the sun and the lie. "If you want to wait on confirmation, I can have quarters prepared."

Something flickered across Veyne's face—the flicker of a person doing arithmetic they disliked. Four hours each way. Eight hours minimum to verify an order that might well be genuine, on a station at the ass-end of dead space, while a patrol cutter burned fuel at anchor. Veyne had a reputation for thoroughness, not patience.

"Alternative," Veyne said. "I inspect the *Errata* now, deferral or no deferral. If the order turns out to be legitimate, I'll note the procedural friction in my report. If it isn't"—the smile was thin as a blade's edge—"I'll note that too."

And there it was. The whole architecture of the morning, three hours of meticulous forgery, balanced on a single sentence, and Veyne had just kicked the table out from under it.

Sefa's mind ran the options at panic speed disguised as calm. Argue further? Only confirmed suspicion. Stall? Veyne would smell it. Comply and pray Halloran had gotten the message and moved? The *Errata* was still sitting at Slip Nine—Sefa could see it through the window, floodlights on, umbilical attached, fat and helpless as a moth pinned to a board.

Unless.

"Of course, Inspector," Sefa heard themself say. "Though I should mention—the *Errata* is currently undergoing a hard-seal diagnostic. Environmental systems fault. Opening the hull during a seal test voids the certification and technically endangers the inspection party. Station regs." Sefa pulled up a maintenance log—one they had created eleven minutes ago, in the same breath as sending the message to Halloran, because some part of them had understood, even then, that one forgery would not be enough. "Diagnostic cycle runs another five hours. I can put you in touch with the crew chief, if you'd like the details. He's very proud of his procedures."

Veyne stared at them for a long moment. Sefa held the stare the way you hold a heavy door—arms burning, smiling, waiting for it to swing your way or crush you.

"Five hours," Veyne repeated.

"Four and change, now."

"I'll want the diagnostic telemetry. Live feed."

"Naturally. I'll have it routed to your cutter within the hour." Sefa was already composing the next forgery in their head, a telemetry stream for a diagnostic that didn't exist, numbers flowing smooth and meaningless, the most honest lying they'd ever done.

Veyne turned to go, then paused at the office door. "Thackeray. Thirty-nine audits. Thirty-nine stations with discrepancies. Do you know what I've learned?" They didn't wait for an answer. "The paperwork is never the crime. The paperwork is the confession. Someone somewhere always writes down what they couldn't help doing." The door hissed open. "Enjoy your five hours."

* * *

They didn't enjoy them. They spent them building the telemetry feed, fabricating a crew chief named Okonkwo who existed in the personnel files as a real person currently in medical stasis and therefore unable to contradict anyone, and drafting—and deleting, and redrafting—a letter of resignation that would explain everything if it all came apart. They deleted it finally because there was nothing in it that was true except the ending, and the ending didn't need explaining. *I knew the colony was dying. I stamped the forms. This is the form I should have stamped first.*

At 1341, the *Errata* undocked.

Sefa watched it happen from the office window, the little ship pulling away from Slip Nine with the unhurried dignity of a vessel that believed, correctly, that its papers were in order. It cleared the station's shadow, spooled its drive, and slid toward the edge of dead space like a coin slipping into a well. Gone. Hours ahead of the sweep, hours ahead of Veyne's patience, carrying forty families' worth of memory in a hull full of fake seed stock.

Sefa let out a breath they felt they'd been holding for eleven years.

Which was, of course, when the marine touched their shoulder.

"Officer Thackeray. Inspector Veyne requests your presence aboard the *Lament*."

"They're verifying the order already? The relay lag—"

"The inspector isn't verifying the order." The marine's face was professionally blank, which was its own kind of message. "Commander Adeyemi's office responded to a routine scheduling ping ninety minutes ago. Fastest relay turnaround on record, apparently. Solar weather must have cleared." A pause. "Her office confirms she issued no directive regarding the vessel *Errata*."

The floor did not move. Sefa noted that with distant interest. The floor stayed exactly where it was, and the window stayed where it was, and out in the black the *Errata* was a spark now, then a smudge, then nothing, and none of it moved at all.

"I see," Sefa said.

"You're not under arrest," the marine added, almost kindly. "The inspector says you're being detained *pending clarification of your role in the issuance of unauthorized fleet documentation*. She says you'll want to know the distinction matters."

She was right. It mattered enormously. Unauthorized documentation pending clarification was a hearing. Obstruction of a sanitization operation was a disappearance. Veyne was giving Sefa a door, narrow and humiliating, and Sefa walked through it with their spine straight and their hands cuffed in front of them like a person being escorted to a meeting they were late for.

In the *Lament*'s briefing room, Veyne sat alone, slate squared before them, and studied Sefa for a long, silent minute.

"Case VR-7734," Veyne said finally. "Manifest discrepancy, agricultural seed stock, non-viable. Filed by you, eight days ago." They turned the slate around. On it was the case file—and beneath it, a second document, pulled from the station's own archives, timestamped months earlier. The same manifest. The same line items. Except in the older version, the line items were different. Longer. Denser. Cargo descriptions that read like poetry: *harvest festival, year nine. Voice recordings, settler children. Last transmission, comms vault, surface.*

Someone had edited the manifests. Someone had been editing them for years, hiding a colony's history inside a smuggler's paperwork, mislabeling memory as garbage. And whoever had done it had done it badly enough, lately, that a customs officer with a good memory and a private notebook had caught the seams.

"You flagged the discrepancy yourself," Veyne said. "Eight days ago. Why?"

Every answer Sefa owned was a trapdoor. *To protect Halloran* was confession. *Routine diligence* was a lie Veyne would unwrap in an hour. So Sefa gave the only answer left, the true one, the one that had been waiting eleven years to be said aloud:

"Because the numbers kept changing, and I wanted to know who was allowed to change them and why it wasn't me."

Veyne leaned back. Outside the viewport, Vesper's Rest hung against the stars, rusted and patient, its dock lights blinking their slow idiot rhythm. Somewhere out past the edge of dead space, a smuggler was running dark toward a relay that probably didn't work, carrying everything Sefa had spent a career pretending not to see.

"Detainee Thackeray," Veyne said, and made a note, and the stylus scratched like a small animal burying something. "Let's begin at the beginning. Tell me about the fire."

Sefa blinked. "What fire?"

"That's what we're going to find out," Veyne said, "isn't it," and behind them, faint through the bulkhead, from the station's ancient address system, OX-9's voice began—softly, patiently, for the hundredth time—to confess.
