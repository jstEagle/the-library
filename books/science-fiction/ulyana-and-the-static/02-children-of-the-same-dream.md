## 2. Children of the Same Dream

The message arrived at 0300 ship-time, which on the *Halcyon* meant nothing and meant everything—nothing, because the ship had no day; everything, because only the desperate and the dying sent messages at 0300.

Marguerite Vasquez read it twice, standing in her narrow quarters with the lights at their lowest permitted setting, the way she liked them, the way that made the bulkheads feel closer and the ship feel smaller and therefore, somehow, safer. The sender was a name she half-recognized from the education rosters: Ilsa Reyes-Kwan, primary instructor, Ring C school annex. The subject line was four words long, and those four words were the reason she read the message twice.

*They are all drawing it.*

Forty children. One image.

Marguerite dressed, pinned her identification chit inside her collar where it wouldn't catch the light, and took the long route through the ship's spine, past the observation blister where the void hung black and patient, past the memorial wall where the names of the honest dead were etched in polymer that would outlast everyone who remembered them. She walked the way she always walked: unhurried, unremarkable, a woman the corridors had long since learned to ignore. Forty years of practice. Invisibility was not a gift. It was a discipline, maintained daily, like stretching.

The school annex smelled of chalk substitute and warm bodies and the faint chemical sweetness of the air scrubbers working harder than they should. Ring C was old. Ring C was always old; the ship's maintenance algorithms had opinions about which rings mattered, and Ring C had never been among them.

Ilsa Reyes-Kwan met her at the classroom door. She was younger than Marguerite had expected, with the particular exhaustion of someone who had stopped sleeping and started enduring. She held a tablet against her chest like a shield.

"You came," she said. "I half thought you wouldn't."

"You filed through the proper channel. That's rarer than you'd think." Marguerite stepped past her into the classroom. "Show me."

The drawings were pinned to the walls in three uneven rows, and Marguerite stood before them for a long time without moving, because moving would have meant admitting what she was seeing, and some admissions deserve a moment of silence first.

A door. Underwater. Blue-green light filtering down in slanted columns, the way light does through liquid, through pressure, through the particular filter of a dream. The door itself was heavy, paneled, absurd—a wooden door, on a generation ship where no child had ever touched wood, standing at the bottom of an ocean no child had ever seen. And on every drawing, in every one, a figure at the door. Small. Knocking. Sometimes rendered in careful pencil strokes, sometimes in furious crayon, sometimes as little more than a smudge—but always there, always at the door, always knocking.

Forty children. Forty hands. One image.

"Same composition," Marguerite said quietly. "Same light angle. Same door."

"Same door," Ilsa confirmed. "I checked. I thought I was losing my mind, so I checked. The perspective is consistent across all forty. Down to which side the hinges are on." Her voice thinned. "Children this age can't agree on which color the sky is, Ms. Vasquez. They drew the hinges."

Marguerite moved along the rows. In the corner of one drawing, a child had written, in the careful broken letters of early literacy: *HE IS COLD.* In another: *LET HIM IN.* A third had drawn the door ajar, a sliver of darkness beyond it, and the darkness had been colored with such pressure that the crayon had torn the paper.

"When did this start?"

"Nine days ago. One girl drew it first—Amara Osei. Then two more. Then I stopped counting because counting stopped meaning anything." Ilsa lowered the tablet. "I reported it to the psych division. They scheduled an assessment for three weeks out. Three weeks. So I filed with you, because—" she hesitated, and Marguerite watched her decide whether to say the next part, and then watched her say it anyway, "—because my grandmother used to talk about you. She said if the numbers ever stopped adding up, there was a woman who would still count them."

Marguerite said nothing. Her grandmother's grandmother, possibly. The Vasquez name had a long tail.

"I'd like to speak with the children," Marguerite said. "Individually. Starting with Amara Osei."

* * *

They gave her the reading nook, a half-circle of cushions beneath a window that looked out on nothing—on the hull, on the dark, on the long route. Marguerite sat on a cushion that smelled faintly of other children, other years, and let each of them come to her one at a time, the way Ilsa arranged it, so that it felt like a privilege and not an interrogation.

They were small. That was the thing that undid her, every time, the thing no amount of procedure prepared her for: they were so small, and their feet didn't always reach the floor, and they spoke about the door with the flat certainty of children reporting weather.

*It's under the water but you can breathe there,* said a boy named Tomas, seven years old, picking at the seam of his sleeve. *You breathe slow. Slower than slow. And the door is warm even though the water is cold.*

*The knocking isn't scary,* said a girl named Priya, six, matter-of-fact, swinging her legs. *It's polite. He knocks four times and then waits. He never gets mad. He's been waiting a really long time so he's good at it.*

*My mom says it's just a dream,* said a boy named Dev, eight, with the careful tone of a child repeating an adult's reassurance he didn't believe. *But dreams don't have hinges.*

Marguerite asked each of them the same question, in the same gentle voice: *What's behind the door?*

And each of them, every one, gave her the same answer with the same puzzled patience, as if she were the one being slow:

*The rest of us.*

* * *

Amara Osei came last.

She was small even for six, with her hair in careful rows and a smudge of marker ink on her left wrist, and she climbed onto the cushion across from Marguerite with the solemnity of someone arriving at a scheduled appointment. She looked at Marguerite the way the others had not—directly, steadily, with an attention that felt older than her body.

"You're the counting woman," Amara said.

"I count some things," Marguerite said. "Amara, I want to ask you about your drawing. The first one."

"The door."

"The door."

Amara folded her hands in her lap, an oddly adult gesture, and recited—not described, *recited*—in the cadence of something learned rather than invented:

"You go down through the water and it gets heavy on your chest like a hand, but it doesn't hurt, it just holds you. You breathe slow. Slower than slow. There's a light above you but it's very far away, like a coin at the bottom of a well, and you know if you look at it too long you'll forget which way is up, so you don't look. The door is brown and it has a ring for a handle and the ring is cold but the door is warm. He knocks four times. Then he waits. He's been waiting longer than the ship has been a ship. And behind the door is the rest of us, and we're all sleeping in rows, and we're so cold, and we're dreaming the same dream so we won't be lonely, and the dream is the water, and the water is the dream."

The reading nook went very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor a door cycled; the annex breathed its scrubbed, sweetened air; the ship hummed the note it had hummed for two hundred years.

Marguerite did not move. Inside her chest, something with her mother's voice turned over in its sleep.

Because Marguerite knew that recitation. Not its words—its *shape*. She had carried its shape inside her for forty years, from a dream she'd had at the age of five, in the year her mother went under, a dream she had never told anyone because she had understood even then that it was not a thing you told, it was a thing you kept, the way you keep a key you no longer remember the lock for.

*The water heavy on your chest like a hand. The coin of light. Don't look, or you'll forget which way is up. Four knocks. The rest of us, sleeping in rows.*

She had thought, all her life, that it was her dream. Hers alone. The private inheritance of a child whose mother had chosen the cold.

"Amara," Marguerite said, and her voice came out steady, which she counted as the day's single professional triumph. "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody taught me." The girl shrugged, small shoulders rising and falling. "It's just what the dream says. Everybody's dream says it. That's how we know it's true—because it says it the same to everybody." She leaned forward, and lowered her voice, and what she said next she said the way children say the things they have decided adults most need to hear:

"He's not knocking to get out, counting woman. He's knocking to see if anybody's still awake out there. Because if nobody answers soon, he's going to stop knocking." A pause. "And then who lets in the rest of us?"

Marguerite walked back through the ship's spine without remembering the route, which had never once happened in forty years of disciplined, deliberate walking. Her hands were cold. The observation blister passed on her left, the void pressing its face to the glass, and she thought: *underwater. They said underwater. There is no water on this ship. There has never been water on this ship.*

But there had been water in the dream. Her dream. Forty years ago. Before she had words for pressure, for suspension, for the slow cold breathing of the cryo bays her mother had walked into and the manifest had recorded and—

And.

Marguerite stopped walking. The corridor hummed around her. She stood very still in the middle of the long route and did the thing she did best, the thing she had been built for, the thing her name meant in her family the way *smith* had once meant things to older families: she counted.

Forty children, dreaming of sleepers in rows.

A door, underwater, on a ship that was a sealed steel lung.

And underneath the school annex's report, underneath the crayon and the cushions and the polite knocking, the first number of a new sum, the sum she had been dreading her whole life without knowing its shape: *how many sleepers does the *Halcyon* carry, and who has been counting them, and what happens to the ones whose names stop appearing in the totals?*

She turned—not toward her quarters, not toward her office, but toward the records deck, walking now with a purpose the corridors had never seen from her, and if heads turned in her wake, for the first time in forty years, she did not notice.

The numbers, she suspected, were about to stop adding up entirely.
