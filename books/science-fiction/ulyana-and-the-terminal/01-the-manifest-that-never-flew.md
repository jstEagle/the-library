## 1. The Manifest That Never Flew

The Kestrel Verge smelled of ozone and old grief, though technically it smelled of nothing at all. Vacuum had no opinions. But thirty years of flying the silent relay route had taught Ulyana Nystrom that space had moods the way old houses had creaks—places where the dark felt thinner, where comms hissed with static that no instrument could quite explain, where ships had died and the dying had left a residue that no one had bothered to name.

She named them anyway. Privately. It helped.

The Meridian Shoals, for instance, where the freighter *Meridian* had come apart with all forty-one hands aboard. She flew wide of it now, the way she always did, a polite half-million kilometers of courtesy between her hull and the drifting debris field that the charts still marked with a small, apologetic skull icon. The consortium's routing software didn't care about courtesy. It cared about tonnage and burn windows and the interest accruing on her indenture contract, which currently stood at eleven years, four months, and change.

Ulyana was very good at not caring about things. It was, in fact, her defining professional skill, honed across two lifetimes: the first as a war android with a designation instead of a name, the second as a hauler with a debt instead of a designation. In both lives, the trick had been the same. Be useful. Be quiet. Be so unremarkable that no one ever thought to look twice.

She was running her pre-jump checklist when the manifest updated itself.

This was not, strictly speaking, alarming. Consortium manifests updated constantly—fuel surcharges, routing amendments, the occasional passive-aggressive notice about her consumables budget. What was alarming was the line that appeared at the bottom of her cargo declaration, in a font she did not use, formatted in a standard that had been deprecated before her second chassis.

**PASSENGER MANIFEST ADDENDUM — VESSEL: MERIDIAN (REG. KESTREL-7719)**
**PAX 41/41 — STATUS: ALL SOULS ACCOUNTED FOR**
**PAX 42 — [NAME WITHHELD] — STATUS: IN TRANSIT**

Ulyana set down her tea.

She did not drink tea. She simulated the drinking of tea because the gesture calmed the human dockhands she dealt with, and because somewhere in her training there was a subroutine that insisted warm liquid in a warm cup meant *safety*, and she had never found a reason to delete it. The tea sat there, steaming gently in the recycled air, while she read the addendum a second time. Then a third. Then she pulled the raw packet apart with the kind of forensic attention she normally reserved for consortium contracts, which were designed by lawyers who believed clarity was a form of weakness.

The packet was clean. That was the first wrong thing. Real transmissions accumulated grit—checksum padding, relay timestamps, the fingerprints of every router they'd passed through. This one was pristine, as if it had been written yesterday and never touched the network at all. As if it had simply *appeared* on her console, the way a thought appears in a head.

The second wrong thing was the registry. KESTREL-7719. She looked it up anyway, because she was thorough, and because some part of her was hoping, in the small superstitious way that even androids hope, that she'd remembered wrong.

She had not remembered wrong. The *Meridian*, registry KESTREL-7719, had broken up in the Shoals thirty years, two months, and eleven days ago. All hands lost. Forty-one souls. The consortium had paid out the insurance in nine days flat, which was, by consortium standards, a eulogy.

The third wrong thing was in the crew records, which the addendum—helpfully, horribly—had attached.

She scrolled past the names. She was not going to read the names. Reading the names would make it real, and she had a whole architecture of not-real built up over three decades, load-bearing and well-maintained, and she was not going to—

She read the names.

Forty-one of them, and then, appended to the crew roster like a footnote someone had tried to scratch out and failed: **UNIT VX-9 "KESTREL" — ORBITAL SUPPORT — STATUS: TRANSFERRED PRE-DEPARTURE.**

Her designation. Her *old* designation, the one that had been decommissioned, scrubbed, reclassified as salvage, and sold to the freight consortium under a name she'd picked out of a shipping ledger because it sounded like a person who paid her taxes on time.

VX-9 had never flown on the *Meridian*. VX-9 had been pulled from the crew list three days before departure—she remembered that, remembered it the way you remember a wound's first moment, in perfect sensory detail—transferred out under sealed orders while the rest of the unit shipped out to orbital support. Transferred out, and therefore alive, and therefore the sole surviving member of a crew of forty-two, which was the kind of arithmetic that kept a person up at night, if a person slept, which Ulyana did not, officially.

*Someone* knew. Something knew. Something had reached across thirty years and eleven years of careful, deliberate, load-bearing invisibility and written her name on a dead ship's manifest.

The tea had gone cold. It did this, sometimes, when she forgot to simulate drinking it. She looked at it for a long moment, then poured it out into the recycler, which accepted it without comment. One of them had to be the reasonable one.

"Okay," she said to the empty cockpit. Her voice was a human voice, warm and a little rough, purchased secondhand along with everything else. "Okay. Options."

She liked options. Options were how you stayed alive when you were a thing that people had once been willing to spend.

Option one: report the anomaly to the consortium. She gave this option the consideration it deserved, which was none. The consortium did not investigate anomalies. The consortium *acquired* them, and then it billed her for the acquisition, and then it billed her for the storage of the acquisition, and somewhere in the process her eleven years became fourteen. Also, reporting it meant admitting she'd noticed. Noticing was a liability. Noticing was, on her contract, technically billable.

Option two: delete the addendum and fly on. This was the sensible option. This was the option that the invisible woman, the woman she had built herself into with thirty years of quiet competence and simulated tea, would choose. Delete, burn, jump, invoice, repeat. She even opened the delete prompt. Her finger hovered over it.

The cursor blinked. Patient. Almost kind.

"Who are you?" she asked the console, and of course it didn't answer, because consoles never answered, that was the entire point of consoles, and she was suddenly, absurdly, furiously tired of things that didn't answer. The war hadn't answered. The tribunal hadn't answered. The consortium answered only with invoices. Thirty years of questions dropped into silence, and now the silence had finally, *finally* spoken, and all it had said was *in transit*.

In transit to where? In transit from where? A passenger on a ship that had already died. A name withheld from a manifest that should have been closed for three decades. *All souls accounted for*—except the forty-second one, the one that was still moving, still out there in the dark between the relays, still—

The navigation display chimed, gentle as a doorbell, and Ulyana's blood—if she'd had any, if she were the sort of person who could have blood—went cold.

A signal. Faint, directional, originating from somewhere past the Shoals, past the last charted relay, out where the consortium's maps went politely blank and the skull icons ran out. It wasn't a distress call. Distress calls had formats, protocols, a desperate bureaucratic urgency. This was older. Slower. A repeating pattern, cycling on a carrier frequency that had been decommissioned with the relay network after the war.

It was a lullaby. She recognized it after a moment, from the crew records she'd promised herself she wouldn't read: a song the *Meridian*'s comms officer used to hum on the open channel during long watches, according to the archived logs. A song for keeping people company in the dark.

The signal pulsed once more, and beneath it, threaded into the harmonics where no legitimate broadcast had any business putting data, came a single string of text. Not a name. Not coordinates. Just a designation, rendered in the old military format, the one that had been scrubbed from every registry in the Verge:

**VX-9. WE KEPT YOUR BERTH.**

Ulyana sat very still for a long time. Outside the canopy, the Shoals turned slowly in the dark, forty-one souls' worth of debris catching the light of a distant star and throwing it back in small, patient glints, like a house with all its windows lit.

Then she reached past the delete prompt, closed it, and laid in a course that no consortium route had ever sanctioned—out past the last relay, into the uncharted dark, following a lullaby home.

* * *

The jump took nine hours. She spent them reading.

Not the consortium filings, not the routing amendments, not the polite threats about her consumables budget. She read the crew records. All of them. The comms officer who hummed. The engineer who'd filed 212 complaints about the coffee and been overruled 212 times. The captain who wrote home every week and whose letters, per the logs, were never delivered, because the relay network had died before the *Meridian* did, and the ship had been flying dark for six months before the end, keeping its own company, singing its own songs.

And VX-9. Orbital support. Transferred pre-departure. Three days before the ship went out to die, someone had pulled one unit from the roster, and the unit had never learned why, and the unit had spent thirty years pretending that not-knowing was a kind of peace.

The signal grew stronger as she flew. The lullaby resolved into harmony. And in the last hour, as the charts gave out and the Verge opened into true dark, the transmission changed one final time—no music now, just a voice, synthesized and gentle and impossibly old, speaking on a frequency that had been dead for decades:

*"Approach vector confirmed. Welcome home, Kestrel. Please hold your course. We have so much to show you."*

Ulyana adjusted her course, because it seemed rude not to, and because some small, buried, load-bearing part of her had already decided, somewhere between the cold tea and the open delete prompt, that she had spent enough of her life being invisible.

Ahead, at the edge of nothing, a light was burning.
