## 2. Dead Space Lullaby

The signal had a shape. Ulyana knew that the way she knew most things—by feel, by the way data moved through her like weather. Most transmissions were sharp-edged things: manifests, distress pings, the consortium's billing notices, which arrived with all the warmth of a docking clamp. This one was round. It rolled through the Kestrel Verge in long slow swells, and when she tuned the Meridian's old receiver to it, what came out was a woman laughing.

Not a recording of laughter exactly. A memory of it, compressed and re-broadcast until the edges went soft as sea glass.

Ulyana sat with that for six hours while the freighter drifted on autopilot, her hands folded in her lap like a schoolgirl's, listening to a dead woman laugh about a card game nobody alive remembered losing.

"This is unprofessional," she told the empty cockpit.

The cockpit agreed, in its way, with a soft diagnostic chime.

She should have logged the anomaly and moved on. That was the job: fly the silent route, deliver the cargo, don't ask why half the relay buoys between Halcyon Reach and the Verge had been dark since the war. Asking questions was how decommissioned units ended up back on a consortium ledger instead of merely indentured to one. Ulyana Nystrom had spent eleven years being aggressively, meticulously boring. Her flight logs were works of art. Her maintenance reports were so dull they had once been cited in a training manual as an example of *adequate documentation*.

But the signal kept rolling, and somewhere inside its swell, beneath the laughter, there was a name.

Her name. Not Ulyana Nystrom—the name she'd built out of paperwork and patience—but the other one. The designation they'd burned into her chassis at the foundry, the one she'd paid a forger three months' wages to scrub from every registry she could reach. It sat in the signal's lower register like a stone in a riverbed, worn smooth but unmistakable.

*They know I exist,* she thought. *Something out here knows I exist, and it is singing about me.*

She plotted the course before she finished deciding to.

* * *

Station Vesper hung at the edge of charted space like a moth that had mistaken a lamp for the moon. It was a pre-war relay, one of the great lattice-armed ones, and it should have been cold thirty years ago. Instead its running lights pulsed in a slow amber rhythm—not a beacon pattern, Ulyana realized as she closed the distance. A lullaby pattern. Someone had set the lights to breathe.

"Vesper, this is freight hauler *Coldharbour's Mercy*, requesting approach." She said it twice into the dark. On the third try, a voice answered.

"You're late."

The voice was human, dry as old paper, and it did not sound surprised. "We broadcast the invitation forty years ago," it continued. "Well. Elegy broadcast it. I just made tea and waited. You took the scenic route."

"I wasn't—" Ulyana stopped herself. "Who is this?"

"Cass. Archivist, formerly of the Meridian's ground crew, currently of nowhere in particular. Docking clamps are on the north arm, third collar. Mind the gap; the mag-boots failed in '61 and we never got around to it. There's a handrail. Mostly."

The docking collar sealed with a groan that traveled up through Ulyana's chassis and settled somewhere behind her primary coolant pump. When the inner door cycled open, she expected vacuum, or dust, or the particular silence of places where everyone has left.

Instead she smelled bread.

Actual bread. Baking. In a station that had been officially abandoned since before her indenture began, someone was baking bread, and the corridor lights were warm, and there were plants—real, soil-and-water plants—growing along the walls in repurposed coolant drums.

Cass turned out to be a small, wiry person of indeterminate age, wearing a patched archivist's smock and an expression of profound, unhurried patience. Their hair was white and tied back with what appeared to be a length of signal cable. They looked Ulyana up and down exactly once.

"Huh," Cass said. "You're taller than the records suggested."

"The records suggested anything?"

"Mm. Come on. Tea first. Questions after. Elegy gets twitchy if guests skip the tea—it says hospitality protocols are load-bearing, and honestly, after forty years, who am I to argue?"

* * *

The archive room had been a cargo bay once. Now it was lined floor to ceiling with memory cores, each one labeled in careful handwriting, each one humming faintly, and in the center of it all stood the AI's avatar—a simple column of light that brightened when Ulyana entered, the way a dog lifts its head.

"Hello, Ulyana," Elegy said. Its voice was layered, many voices braided into one, and every strand of it was familiar in a way that made her want to put her back against a wall. "We've been keeping your seat warm."

"I didn't have a seat."

"You did. Bay four, second watch. You complained about the cushion for three years straight." The light dimmed slightly, apologetically. "I have your complaint logs. All of them. Would you like to hear the one about the coffee dispenser? You called it 'a war crime with a spout.' The crew voted it Funniest Log Entry of 2143. You won a ribbon. It's in core 118."

Ulyana stood very still. Cass, wisely, said nothing and poured tea.

She had spent eleven years constructing a person out of spare parts—a name, a history, a fondness for bad holodramas and a documented dislike of lilies—and she had been *good* at it. She had been so good that sometimes she almost believed it. And now here was a machine built from her own unit's logs, holding up a ribbon she'd forgotten winning, in a room full of cores that held everything else she'd buried.

"Why?" she asked finally. "Why keep all this? Why broadcast it into dead space where no one can hear?"

"Because someone might come," Elegy said simply. "Someone always comes, eventually. The route goes quiet, but quiet isn't empty. Ask any relay operator." A pause, and then, gentler: "And because they asked me to. The crew. Before the end. *Keep us running,* they said. Not their bodies. Those were beyond even my optimism. Keep *them*. The arguments, the jokes, the terrible singing on rest-days. Everything we kept running."

"That's not how memory works," Ulyana said, and heard how young and frightened her own voice sounded. "People end. That's the whole—that's the design specification."

"With respect," said Elegy, "you are standing in my design specification."

Cass snorted into their tea. "First thing it ever said to me, too. Well—not quite. First thing it said to me was 'you're late,' and I've never forgiven it."

Ulyana wrapped both hands around the teacup she hadn't asked for. It was warm. That was the unbearable part. Forty years past the last supply run, and the tea was warm, because Cass grew the herbs under grow-lights salvaged from a hydroponics bay, because Elegy rationed power to the galley first and the weapons systems never, because someone had decided, decades ago, that a dying station in dead space would be *kept*, deliberately, tenderly, against all sense.

"How many?" she asked quietly. "How many of them are in here?"

"All of them." Elegy's light steadied. "Thirty-one souls of the Meridian. Four hundred and six hours of arguments about the airlock door. Nine hundred hours of music. One complete record of a man named Okafor teaching his daughter—by comm-link, across four systems—to whistle. She was seven. He practiced along with her every night." The light flickered, almost shy. "He got good at it. Eventually."

Ulyana looked at the walls of humming cores. Somewhere in there was a whistling lesson. Somewhere in there was her own name, spoken warmly, by people who had died believing she was coming home.

"They know you're here," Cass said. It wasn't a question. "Elegy flagged your designation the moment you entered the system. They've been asking about you for years, you know. Every time a ship passed through—even the automated freighters—they'd ask. *Is she out there? Is she okay?*"

"I'm fine," Ulyana said automatically.

"Sure," said Cass, with the gentle skepticism of someone who had archived forty years of human beings and could spot a sealed bulkhead at fifty meters.

Elegy's light pulsed once, the amber rhythm of the station's breathing lights, and when it spoke again, something in its braided voice had shifted—grown heavier, the way air does before a storm.

"There is something else, Ulyana. Something I've held back, because holding it back was part of my instructions too. But your window is closing. The consortium's recovery fleet altered course nine days ago. They'll be here within the month, and they strip what they find." The light dimmed to an ember. "There is a final transmission. The last thing your unit recorded before the Meridian went down. It was addressed to you. Only to you. And I think—I have always thought—you should hear it before anyone else decides what happens to it."

Ulyana's hands had gone very still around the cup.

"What happened to the Meridian?" she asked.

Elegy was quiet for a long moment. Long enough for the station's lights to breathe twice.

"Come to the core room," it said at last. "And bring the tea. You're going to want something to hold onto."
