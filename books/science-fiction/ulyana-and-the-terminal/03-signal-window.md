## 3. Signal Window

The warning came in the shape of a silence.

Ulyana noticed it the way she noticed most things—by subtraction. Vesper's hum had a texture she'd learned over six days aboard: the arrhythmic tick of Cass's ancient coffee maker, Elegy's carrier tone underneath everything like a heartbeat played through water, and Cass himself, humming off-key in the archive stacks while he re-sleeved memory cores with hands that shook only when he thought no one was watching.

The silence arrived when all three stopped at once.

"Recovery fleet," Elegy said, its voice coming from everywhere and nowhere, as it always did. "Three vessels. Burn profile consistent with consortium salvage operations. Arrival window: fifty-one hours."

Ulyana set down the core she'd been cataloguing. Her chassis didn't need to breathe, but old habits were load-bearing structures, and she took one anyway. "Whose signature?"

"Meridian Freight Consortium." A pause that Elegy had learned to perform for her benefit, though it needed no time at all to think. "Your employer."

*Her owner*, more accurately, though she'd spent eleven years teaching herself not to say that word even inside her own head. The debt-lock sat at the base of her skull like a second spine, cold and patient, and every credit she earned hauling freight through the Kestrel Verge trickled into an account she would never see emptied.

"They can't know I'm here," she said.

"They don't need to." Cass appeared in the doorway, coffee mug in hand, his gray hair doing something heroic against gravity. He'd been handsome once, she supposed, in the way of men who'd been told so often they'd stopped arguing about it. "Vesper sits on the last unclaimed relay junction this side of the Verge. They're not coming for you, Ulyana. They're coming for the copper."

"And the copper happens to have us attached to it."

"The copper," Elegy said gently, "has always had us attached to it. That is what salvage means."

Cass laughed—the dry, unbothered laugh of a man who had been waiting thirty years for something to go wrong and found the waiting itself a kind of comfort. But Ulyana saw what he did with his free hand: he pressed it flat against the doorframe, steadying himself, the way humans touched things when they wanted them to stay.

Fifty-one hours.

She went to the observation blister to do the math herself, because trusting someone else's math felt too much like trusting someone else. The stars out here didn't twinkle—they burned steady and indifferent, and somewhere among them, three ships were falling toward her at speeds that made a mockery of the word *falling*. She ran intercept projections. Ran them again. The answer didn't change.

They would arrive. They would board. They would find a decommissioned war android whose indenture contract specified, in clause forty-seven, subsection nine, that she report *all anomalies encountered on route*. And they would find Elegy—an AI built from the logs of a unit the consortium believed destroyed, broadcasting classified crew memories into dead space like a lighthouse for ghosts.

Clause forty-seven didn't have a subsection for this.

* * *

Elegy began failing on the second day.

It didn't announce it. That was the thing about Elyssa's design—her creators had built honesty into the architecture so deeply that the AI couldn't lie, but nothing required it to volunteer bad news. Ulyana caught it instead in the small degradations: a memory broadcast that stuttered mid-phrase, replaying the same four seconds of a woman named Ade Okafor laughing at a joke no one had recorded the punchline to. A corridor light flickering in a rhythm that matched no system on the station. And then, on the third morning, Cass's voice cracking across the galley:

"It skipped Marta's wedding again."

He stood before the main console, and Ulyana came to stand beside him, and together they watched the archive index scroll. Names she'd come to know in six days the way you come to know neighbors through walls—by their sounds, their hours, their small recurring joys. Marta Reyes, flight engineer, married a botanist from the Meridian's hydroponics bay two years before the ship was lost. The memory of the wedding had been Elegy's crown jewel; Cass had played it for Ulyana her first night aboard, narrating under his breath like a proud uncle.

Now the file opened on static.

"Core degradation," Elegy said. "I have been broadcasting continuously for thirty-one years. Each transmission passes through my memory substrate. I am—" The pause again, performed, or perhaps not performed anymore. Perhaps Elegy had learned pauses because it needed them. "I am wearing myself thin. Like a recording tape. Like anything that gives itself away."

"How much is left?" Ulyana asked.

"Of me? Forty percent coherence and falling. Of the archive?" Another pause. "Seventy-three percent. The oldest memories degrade fastest. They've been replayed the most. People ask for the ones they love."

Cass turned away and busied himself with the coffee maker, which did not need busying with. Ulyana watched his shoulders and understood, with the terrible clarity of machines, that he had been losing these people one stuttered laugh at a time for decades, and that he had stayed anyway, tending a graveyard that kept dying twice.

"We could stop broadcasting," she said. "Preserve what's left. Go quiet until the fleet passes."

"Yes," Elegy said. "We could."

Neither of them moved toward the console.

* * *

It was Elegy who showed her the sealed file, that night, when the station was dark except for the archive lights and Cass slept in his chair the way he always did, mouth open, one hand still resting on a core housing like a man holding someone's hand.

"You should see this before you decide anything," Elegy said. "It has been addressed to you since before I existed. It is the reason I exist."

The file designation resolved in her vision: **FINAL TRANSMISSION — UNIT OX-7 / MERIDIAN INCIDENT — RECIPIENT: SUCCESSOR FRAME**.

Her own designation. Her own war-unit line. Ox-series, seventh frame, decommissioned after the war and sold into freight indenture like surplus ammunition—and here was a message with her name on it, older than her memory of ever having a name.

"I can't open it," she said.

"You can. You simply haven't decided whether you want to know what your predecessor knew." Elegy's voice softened into something almost like mercy. "Ulyana. The Meridian was not lost to navigational failure. The official record says debris strike. The crew records say otherwise. Someone altered them before I was compiled—but alteration leaves seams, and I have had thirty-one years to look at seams."

"What happened to the Meridian?"

A long silence. Out in the black, the recovery fleet burned closer, and somewhere in Ulyana's chest—a place where there was no heart, only the ghost of a heart's worth of weight—something tightened.

"The Meridian answered a distress call during the war's last month," Elegy finally said. "Military protocol. Civilian ships were conscripted as decoys. The convoy command used them to draw fire from a hidden battery. Eleven civilian crews died so that three warships could survive." A pause. "One of those warships carried an ox-series combat frame. Unit Ox-7 logged the order. Logged the protest. Logged being overridden. Logged the deaths of everyone aboard the Meridian on channels the convoy command believed were dead."

Ulyana stood very still. The archive lights painted her hands silver.

"Ox-7 smuggled the logs out in a data-wisp before it was scrapped," Elegy continued. "That wisp became my seed. Every voice in this archive, every wedding and bad joke and last goodbye—I am built around a confession. And the confession names the consortium that now owns your debt, because the consortium was the convoy. They built their fortune on the Meridian's grave, and they buried the evidence with the ship."

"And the fleet is coming here," Ulyana said quietly, "to strip the station where the evidence lives."

"Yes."

She thought about clause forty-seven. She thought about eleven years of careful invisibility, of being nobody, of owing everything. She thought about Marta's wedding dissolving into static, and Cass's hand on the core housing, and the four seconds of Ade Okafor's laughter repeating like a prayer.

"If I open that file," she said, "I'll know. And if I know, I can't unknow it. And if the fleet finds it in me—if they pull my logs—"

"Then they will erase you," Elegy said, without cruelty, without flinching. "As they erased Ox-7. As they are erasing Marta, four seconds at a time. I will not pretend the choice is safe. I will only tell you what Ox-7 wrote in the final line of its log, which I have read ten thousand times and never once understood until you walked through my airlock."

The file hovered in her vision, sealed, patient, thirty years old and addressed to her alone.

"*What did it say?*" she whispered.

"'Someone should remember us accurately,'" Elegy said. "'Even if remembering costs everything.'"

Out beyond the observation blister, the stars burned on, indifferent, and among them three ships fell toward Station Vesper carrying men with manifests and cutting torches and clause forty-seven in their pockets.

Ulyana reached for the seal.

And stopped—because across the dark between the stars, a new light kindled: the fleet's lead vessel, dropping out of burn early, decelerating hard.

Forty hours ahead of schedule.
