## 6. The Belt Remembers

The trail was a rumor wearing another rumor's coat.

We'd followed it for eleven days—Ulyana cross-referencing supply manifests that didn't exist against purchase orders that had been scrubbed, me doing what I did best, which was asking the wrong people slightly wrong questions in the kind of places where wrongness was a currency. The trail kept almost dying. It would go cold for two days, then warm again like something breathing under snow.

"It's being fed," Ulyana said on day nine, watching me nurse a cup of recycled coffee that tasted like it had been recycled through someone first. "Somebody out here is keeping these people alive. Somebody with money."

"Somebody with guilt," I said.

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

She was right. They never are.

The trail ended—or rather, stopped pretending to be a trail—at a chunk of rock catalogued as 4-Vesta-Adjunct-Kilo, which is bureaucratic language for *a rock we couldn't be bothered to name*. No atmosphere worth mentioning, no minerals worth mining, no reason for anyone to be there at all. Which is exactly why someone was.

I brought the *Magpie* in slow and dark, running cold except for life support, and set down in a crater that some ancient impact had scooped out of the rock's flank like a thumbprint in dough. Ulyana watched the scopes while I watched the dust settle around our landing struts in that slow, dreamy way vacuum has, unhurried, like even physics out here had decided to take its time.

"Nothing on passive," she said. "Which means nothing wants to be found, or nothing's home."

"There's a third option."

"Don't say it."

"Somebody's home and very good at not wanting to be found."

She gave me the look she'd been perfecting since the dive bar—the one that said *I used to audit people like you*—and unstrapped from her seat anyway. That was the thing about Ulyana Reyes. She could look at you like you were a rounding error and still follow you into the dark.

We walked out onto the rock with suit lamps low, following a seam in the surface that wasn't natural. Someone had cut it—cut it carefully, the way you'd cut a doorframe if you wanted the door to look like geology. My smuggler's heart did a little pirouette. This was craftsmanship. This was somebody who understood that the best hiding place isn't darkness; it's plausibility.

"You're smiling," Ulyana said over comms.

"I'm appreciating."

"You're grinning like a man looking at his own funeral arrangements."

"Also possible."

The seam opened into a tunnel, and the tunnel opened into warmth. Not metaphorical warmth—actual heat, radiating off the walls, which meant reactors, which meant people, which meant we'd either found Meridian's ghosts or walked into a trap with excellent insulation.

Then the lights came on.

Not all at once. In sections, one after another, marching toward us down the corridor like a polite interrogation. And standing at the end of them, framed in the last pool of light, was an old woman in a patched environment suit with the chest plate painted over—painted over, I noticed, where a corporate logo would have been. She held a cutting torch the way other people hold opinions: firmly, and ready to share.

"You're the beacon man," she said.

Her voice came through on our channel, which meant she'd already sliced into our suits, which meant we'd been hers since before we landed. I felt Ulyana tense beside me, her hand drifting toward the sidearm she'd started carrying somewhere between the refinery and now.

"How—" I began.

"The burst. Nine seconds of dead gravity and then your little transmitter screaming into the dark like a lighthouse keeper having a nervous breakdown." She lowered the torch, but only to rest it on her shoulder. "Every off-grid ear in the belt heard it. Most of them thought it was bait. I said, no, that's a lonely man's signal. Bait doesn't sound lonely." She looked at us both, and her eyes behind the faceplate were tired in the specific way of someone who has counted losses and keeps recounting them to make sure they haven't shrunk. "I'm Marta Okonjo-Vey. I was Meridian's chief hydroponics engineer. Come inside before your suits do something embarrassing, like keep you alive longer than I want to stand here."

* * *

There were sixty-three of them.

They lived inside 4-Vesta-Adjunct-Kilo the way roots live inside soil—invisible from above, holding everything up. They'd carved out habitation rings from the rock's interior, grown food under lights that flickered just slightly out of sync so the plants wouldn't notice they were orphans, raised three children who had never seen an open sky because their parents had decided the sky was a lie told by corporations.

Marta fed us real food—actual greens, actual protein grown in vats that smelled honest—and sat us down in a common room whose walls were covered in photographs. Printed photographs, physical ones, hung on hooks and wires. Faces. Hundreds of faces.

"We print them," she said, following my gaze. "Paper doesn't get edited."

Ulyana went very still beside me. I understood why. We'd come here hoping for witnesses. What we'd found was a memorial that happened to breathe.

"You saw the recording," Marta said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Part of it," I said. "Enough."

"Then you know how it ended." She turned her cup in her hands—a real ceramic cup, chipped, older than the colony probably. "What you don't know is how it started. Meridian was a research station. Materials science, mostly. Gravity lattice work. Four thousand souls, give or take the children." Her voice caught on *children*, steadied itself through what was clearly long practice. "The weapon test wasn't aimed at us. That's the joke nobody laughs at anymore. We were the calibration target. A convenient mass. Something with enough structure to show whether the field generator could shear a gravity well cleanly." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, because it was genuine. "Cleanly. That was the word in the report, I'd bet my life on it. *Cleanly.*"

"They erased the station," Ulyana said quietly. "All four thousand. Every registry, every record. I've seen the depth of it. It goes down to—"

"To us." Marta's eyes moved to her. "You're the auditor. Reyes. We know about you too. We know your name is on the erasure order."

The room did not erupt. That was the frightening part. Sixty-three refugees, and none of them lunged, none of them shouted. They just... looked at her. The way you look at weather.

"I didn't know," Ulyana said. "My memories were edited. I've spent months proving that to myself, let alone anyone else."

"Mm," said Marta. "And if we believe you—and I'm saying *if*, child, don't make that face—what then? You testify? To whom? The courts the corporation owns? The customs service that disowned you?" She shook her head slowly. "You want to know what happened to the ones who tried to speak in the first weeks? There were forty-one of us who made it off-station. Forty-one. Do you see forty-one faces' worth of empty hooks on that wall? No. Because thirty-eight of them went quiet within a year. Accidents. Radiation leaks. One man simply stopped answering his comm, and when his neighbors checked, he'd been dead so long the med-scanner couldn't date it. As if time itself had agreed to forget him."

A man near the back spoke up—young, maybe thirty, with a burn scar crawling up his neck like ivy. "The nine seconds," he said. "That wasn't a test this time. That was a message. *We can reach anywhere. We can stop your hearts for nine seconds any time we feel like it.* They weren't calibrating the weapon. They were reminding us it exists."

"That's paranoia," Ulyana started.

"It's pattern recognition," Marta said gently. "I grew plants for a living, auditor. Pattern recognition is the whole job. You watch a leaf curl and you ask what changed. Everything changed after the nine seconds. Everyone who ever whispered the word *Meridian* got careful. Everyone who ever donated to us got poor suddenly, or dead politely. That's not paranoia. That's a leaf curling."

I sat with my cooling cup and felt something uncomfortable move in my chest. Because I knew what they were afraid of—I'd felt it myself, that night at the beacon, the universe blinking and only me noticing. But my fear had been abstract, a shiver in the dark. Theirs had addresses. Theirs had names on walls.

"Testify," Marta said at last, tasting the word like something spoiled. "You know what testimony requires? A witness willing to die. Not possibly die. Willingly. Because the moment one of us stands up and says *I was there*, the next nine seconds won't be a warning. It'll be housekeeping." She looked around the room, at the faces, at the children's drawings pinned among the photographs—crayon suns, crayon stations, a crayon family holding hands under a sky that had never existed for them. "I have sixty-three people. Three of them are seven years old. You can do that arithmetic better than I can pretend I can't."

"So you stay hidden forever," I said. "You stay a rumor wearing a rumor's coat until you all die of old age in a rock nobody named, and Meridian stays a lie."

"Yes," Marta said simply. "That is exactly what we will do. And I will sleep tonight, and feed my grandchildren tomorrow, and I will not apologize for it." She reached out and put her hand over mine—her hand small and rough and steady. "But listen to me, beacon man. I'm not sending you away empty. Cowardice and wisdom look identical from the outside. The difference is what you do with what you're given. So take this."

She pressed a data chip into my palm. Small. Warm from her hand.

"What is it?"

"The generator's serial provenance. Where the weapon was built, by whom, and—this is the part that matters—which platform it lives on now. We couldn't testify. But paper doesn't get edited, remember?" Her mouth twitched, almost humor, almost grief. "Neither does stubborn old women."

* * *

We walked back to the *Magpie* in silence, the tunnel lights bowing us out section by section, and I carried the chip in my fist like a coal.

At the airlock, Ulyana finally spoke. "They're right," she said. "About the risk. About the children. If I were them—"

"You'd hide too. Yeah. Me too." I looked back down the corridor, toward the warm dark full of printed faces. "That's what makes it worse, Ulyana. They're not wrong. They're just the proof that being right and surviving don't shake hands."

She nodded slowly. Then her comm chimed—an incoming transmission, encrypted, priority-flagged, routed through relays that made her frown before she even opened it.

She read it twice. When she looked up, her face had gone the color of the rock around us.

"The corporation knows we found them," she said. "And Theo—they're not offering to negotiate anymore. There's a fixer requesting a meeting. Tomorrow. Neutral ground." She swallowed. "He says he wants to talk about terms of surrender."

I closed my fist around the chip, and somewhere behind us, deep in the unnamed rock, sixty-three people turned down their lights and pretended, once again, not to exist.
