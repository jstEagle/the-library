## 3. Ten Years Invisible

The first safehouse to burn was the one she loved least.

It sat in the underbelly of Halcyon Ring, a rented locker behind a noodle stall that smelled of scorched ginger and old steam. Casper had kept it for six years—six years of a folded mattress, a water rationer with a cracked gauge, and a false panel above the ceiling tiles where she'd cached her original papers: the license issued to a woman named Casper Voss, birth-fleet Meridian, registered before the incident, before the registry was sealed and everyone aboard officially became dead.

She fed the papers into the stall's waste incinerator one page at a time, watching the heat curl them into black moths. The license went last. It had her face on it—younger, rounder, eyes that hadn't yet learned to count exits—and for a moment she held it over the flame without letting go, as if the fire might refuse it too.

"Careful," said the noodle vendor, an old woman named Besh who had never asked a single question in six years and therefore deserved every credit Casper had ever quietly slipped her. "That's how fires start."

"That's how they end," Casper said, and let go.

* * *

Ten years ago there had been no fire because there had been nothing left to burn.

She remembered the water first. Not the explosion—the fleet's official record said reactor cascade, hull breach, ninety-one souls—but the water. The Meridian's coolant lines had ruptured along Deck C before anything else failed, and Casper had been waist-deep in scalding fog, dragging a junior navigator by the collar toward a hatch that would not open, when the second breach took the corridor behind her. She remembered the sound of it: not a boom but a long exhalation, like the ship itself dying of relief.

She remembered waking three days later on a salvage tug called the Honest Doubt, wrapped in thermal foil, her lungs full of scar tissue, while men in unmarked armor asked her questions she couldn't answer because she'd been unconscious for all the interesting parts. Why were you on Deck C? Who ordered the evacuation? Did you see the cargo manifests?

She said no. She said no so many times that eventually they believed her, or tired of her, or decided a half-drowned navigator with burned lungs wasn't worth the airlock. They put her off at a waystation with a settlement stipend and a death certificate filed somewhere she'd never find, and told her, in the flat voice people use for things they've been ordered to say, that the Meridian never flew.

Never flew. Ninety-one souls on a ship that never existed, dead in an accident that never happened, on a route that appeared in no logbook. She had spent the first year after that trying to remember harder—as if memory were a muscle, as if she could flex her way back to the truth of what she'd seen on Deck C. She had spent the second year learning to forget instead. That was the harder discipline. That was the one that stuck.

Because forgetting, she discovered, was a skill you could build a life out of.

* * *

The second safehouse was harder. It was a berth on the freighter Kestrel's Lament, paid through a shell account under the name "C. Marrow," and it held not papers but people: a list, handwritten on oilcloth, of every contact who had ever helped her become nobody. The tug pilot who'd fished her out of the wreckage and never reported it. The registrar on Thessaly Station who'd sold her a clean identity for the price of a used engine. A customs officer on the Perimeter Line who looked the other way twice a year in exchange for nothing at all, which frightened Casper more than extortion would have.

She couldn't burn this one. Burning names felt like burning the people themselves, and some of them had risked real things for her. So she did the next best thing: she memorized the list, then cut it into strips and ate it.

Not literally. She soaked the oilcloth in bilge solvent until the ink ran grey, wrung it out, and flushed the pulp down the Kestrel's waste reclamation line, where it would be rendered into potable water for someone else's coffee. There was a poetry in that she chose not to examine.

Dree found her kneeling over the reclamation hatch at 0300 station time, sleeves rolled, hands stained grey to the wrist.

"You look like you're baptizing something," Dree said.

"I'm disposing of laundry."

"In the recycler."

"It's a long story."

Dree leaned against the bulkhead, arms crossed, and studied her with the particular patience of someone who has shared a bunk and a bank account and eleven years of haul routes with a person and still cannot read her. Dree Okafor was not a curious woman by trade—curiosity got freelancers audited—but she noticed things. She noticed that Casper slept facing the door even after nine years of trusting the lock. She noticed that Casper flinched at certain designations in fleet traffic chatter, syllables that meant nothing to anyone else. She noticed that once a year, on no anniversary anyone could name, Casper drank alone in their cabin and didn't come to bed.

"You've been strange since the manifest," Dree said now. "Stranger than your baseline strange, which is already a full standard deviation from human."

Casper wrung out the last of the cloth. Grey water swirled down into the dark.

"The manifest is a clerical error," she said.

"Clerical errors don't make you dissolve contact lists in the middle of the night."

"No," Casper admitted. "They don't."

She stood. Her knees ached—she was forty-one now, or whatever age a woman is when her paperwork says thirty-four and her body keeps its own ledger. In the dim utility light Dree looked older too, silver coming in at her temples, a woman who had chosen a small honest life running ore and machine parts through contested orbits and had never once asked why her partner insisted on routes that avoided every fleet checkpoint between here and the Rim.

"Dree. If I told you I used to be someone else—not a criminal, just... someone whose name is dangerous to have—would you want details?"

Dree considered this with the seriousness she gave to cargo weights and weather windows.

"I'd want to know if the danger follows you here," she said. "The rest is yours. You don't owe me your past. You owe me honesty about the present tense."

"The present tense is that someone, somewhere, wrote my name on a ship that officially never existed, and that name just surfaced in a customs database ten years after I buried it." Casper turned her stained hands over, palms up, as if the evidence might be written there. "Which means either the burial failed, or someone dug."

"And you're burning everything you buried it with."

"I'm making sure there's nothing left to find. If they come looking for Casper Voss, I want them to find C. Marrow, haul contractor, boring as ballast. Nothing connected. No trail."

Dree was quiet for a moment. Then: "Does it work? Erasing yourself?"

"No," Casper said. "But it buys time. And time is the only currency I've ever been rich in."

* * *

The third safehouse wasn't hers at all anymore, and that was the problem.

Maret Sool had been her fixer on Thessaly Station for most of a decade—a soft-spoken man with a gambler's memory and a smuggler's discretion, the kind of contact who could produce a new transponder code in four hours and never ask what the old one had been attached to. Casper hadn't spoken to him in fourteen months, not since she'd begun tightening her circle, but his name was on the dissolved list and his fingerprints were on half her infrastructure, and paranoia has a simple arithmetic: any node that knows you is a node that can sell you.

She commed him from a public relay booth on Halcyon Ring, voice masked, using a code phrase they'd agreed on years ago and never needed. He answered on the third ring, and something in the timing—too fast, too awake for the local hour—made the floor tilt under her.

"Marrow," he said. Using her alias. Good. But he said it quickly, like a man setting down something hot.

"Maret. I need a status check on the identity package. Full audit. I'll pay the rush rate."

A pause. In the background of his feed she heard sounds she shouldn't have: the shuffle of feet, the particular silence of a room where more than one person is breathing carefully.

"You should know," Maret said, and his voice had gone flat in a way she recognized from interrogators and debt collectors, "that someone came asking about you. Three days ago. Fleet-adjacent. Civilian clothes, military posture. They had your old designation, Marrow. Not your alias. The other one."

The booth seemed to shrink around her. Casper pressed her free hand flat against the metal wall, grounding herself against the vertigo.

"What did you tell them?"

"Nothing. I'm not stupid." Another pause, longer. "But I'm also not armored, and they made it clear they'd be back, and Marrow—I have a family on this station. I can't be the wall between you and whatever this is. I'm sorry. I'm getting out. By tomorrow this number won't exist, and neither will my ledger, and honestly, neither should yours."

"Maret—"

"Don't contact me again. Please. For both of us." And then, quieter, almost gentle, the way people speak to the dead: "Whatever you were, before—I hope you stay whoever you are now. Goodbye, Marrow."

The line died.

Casper stood in the booth for a long time, listening to the hum of the station around her—ten thousand lives circulating through corridors and conduits, none of them aware that somewhere in their midst a ghost had just lost her last friend from her first life. She thought about Maret's children, whom she'd never met but had once anonymously paid a medical bill for. She thought about the fleet-adjacent man in civilian clothes, sitting somewhere with a file that contained her old designation, patient as pressure.

Then she stopped thinking and started working.

By dawn she had liquidated two accounts, abandoned a third rather than trigger its withdrawal alarms, rerouted the Kestrel's Lament's next three contracts through a broker she'd never met face-to-face, and drafted—in her head, unwritten, unwritable—a map of everything that could still lead to her. The list was shorter than it had been yesterday. It was not short enough.

* * *

She went home to Dree smelling of solvent and burnt paper, and found her partner waiting up with two cups of tea and the expression of a woman who has done math she doesn't like.

"The Kestrel's contracts," Dree said, before Casper could sit. "You rerouted them. Through Voss Intermediary on the Perimeter Line."

"It was the cleanest option."

"It was the fastest option. There's a difference, and you know it, because you taught me the difference." Dree set down the tea. "Voss Intermediary is a shell inside another shell, and the inner shell was registered by Maret Sool, and Maret Sool just went dark—I checked, because when my partner starts moving money at 0400, I check. Which means our entire revenue stream now runs through a dead man's structure."

"I'll rebuild it."

"When? Before or after whoever scared him into the dark finds the same thread?" Dree stood, and for the first time in eleven years her voice cracked with something hotter than patience. "Casper. Look at me. I have watched you spend a decade being careful. Careful routes, careful contacts, careful silences. I married myself to that care. I accepted it the way you accept weather. But this—" she gestured at the whole of Casper, the grey-stained cuffs, the shadowed eyes, the coiled readiness that had crept back into her posture like a tenant returning to an old apartment, "—this isn't care. This is fear wearing care's clothes. And fear makes mistakes. Fear reroutes contracts through dead men at four in the morning."

Casper opened her mouth to argue and found nothing there. The argument was correct. That was the worst part—that Dree, who knew nothing of the Meridian, of Deck C, of the water and the questions and the ship that never flew, had diagnosed the disease more accurately than its carrier.

"You're right," Casper said.

Dree blinked. Eleven years of marriage to Casper Voss—or C. Marrow, or whichever woman she actually was—and Casper could count on one hand the times she'd surrendered an argument inside ten seconds.

"So tell me what to do," Dree said. "Not why. Why is yours. Tell me what to do."

And that—more than the manifest, more than Maret's frightened voice, more than the ash of her license—was what finally hardened the dread into something usable. Because Dree was in it now. Had always been in it; Casper had simply been too cowardly to draw the perimeter honestly. Every careful route had been a lie of omission told daily to the person who shared her bed. Every avoided checkpoint had been a decision made for two and disclosed to one.

If the past was surfacing—if someone had dug up the name and was following it down through shells and ledgers toward the living woman at the bottom—then hiding deeper was no longer protection. Hiding was just dying slowly, alone, in advance.

"They'll come to the dock," Casper said slowly, feeling her way into the shape of the future the way she felt her way through a debris field: instruments down, flying by instinct. "Whoever they are. They came to Maret because Maret was the easy node. But I'm the payload. They'll want the chip, or the name, or me—and they'll do it where I can't run, which means dockside, which means here."

"So we run first."

"No." Casper shook her head. "Running confirms value. Running tells them the name is worth chasing. We hold position, we finish the burn—every account, every artifact, every thread—and we make ourselves so boring, so clean, so thoroughly C. Marrow that when they arrive, they arrive at a dead end with a living woman standing in it who has nothing to give them."

"And if they don't believe the dead end?"

Casper looked at her hands. Under the grey stains, the scars from the burns were still faintly visible, ten years faded, a topography of the day her first life ended. Somewhere out in the dark between stations, a ship that never flew had a manifest with her name on it, and someone was reading that manifest the way she had once read star charts: looking for a vector home.

Or looking for a target.

"Then," she said, "I finally stop being invisible. And God help whoever's holding the light when I do."

Dree studied her for a long moment, then picked up both cups of tea, pressed one into Casper's hands, and touched her own cup to it—a toast with no occasion, which was somehow more binding than one with.

"To boring as ballast," Dree said.

"To boring as ballast," Casper echoed, and drank.

* * *

Two days later, the Kestrel's Lament finished her cycle and slid into her assigned berth at Halcyon Ring's commercial docks, right on schedule, exactly as boring as ballast. Casper stood at the airlock watching the docking clamps seat themselves, running her final inventory of erasures: accounts closed, artifacts destroyed, contacts cold, history compressed to ash and grey water. What remained was a woman, a partner, a ship, and a single anomalous line in a customs database that she could not delete because it lived in fleet archives she had no key to.

The berth lights flickered on, one bank after another, white and indifferent.

And there, at the foot of the ramp, where no one had any business standing at this hour, waited a man in civilian clothes with a military posture and a courier's case held loosely at his side, watching the airlock with the patience of someone who had all the time in the world and intended to spend it on her.

He smiled when he saw her. It was not an unkind smile. Somehow that was worse.

"Casper Voss," he called up the ramp, pleasantly, in a voice pitched to carry no farther than the two of them. "We should talk about the Meridian."
