## 6. Truce Clock

The announcement came in on the low-band fleet channel at 0400 station time, which was the hour Casper had learned to distrust most. Bad news always traveled in the dark, riding the quiet frequencies where no one was awake to argue with it.

She was awake because she hadn't really slept since the manifest. She sat in the cramped galley of the *Long Answer* with a cup of recaf gone cold between her hands and the customs chip lying on the table like something venomous she couldn't bring herself to touch again. She'd read it eleven times. *Casper Voss. Navigator, Meridian.* Eleven times the name had stared back at her with her own face behind it, and eleven times she had told herself there was an explanation—clerical error, recycled designation, someone else who'd chosen the same alias out of the same old registry of dead names that smugglers drew from like a communal well.

The announcement made liars of all those explanations.

*"By order of the Joint Admiralty Council,"* said a voice so flat it might have been generated rather than spoken, *"the contested-orbit ceasefire governing Sector Nine through Thirteen will lapse in thirty standard days. All licensed operators are advised to complete transit and cargo commitments before this date. The fleet assumes no responsibility for vessels or personnel remaining in contested space after cessation of hostilities protections."*

A pause, long enough to be deliberate.

*"This is your final notice."*

Casper set down the cup. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Ten years of careful living had taught her body to go still when everything else wanted to run.

Thirty days.

She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Thirty days until every faction that had spent years pretending to tolerate one another across the shipping lanes could stop pretending. Thirty days until the patrol corridors filled with armed hulls instead of bored inspectors. Thirty days until the first salvos of whatever came next—and everyone in the sector knew what would be targeted first, because everyone in the sector knew what was worth having.

The relay at the edge of dead space. The Archive.

She didn't know how she knew its name yet. That was the thing that frightened her most—not the announcement itself, but the way the word *Archive* had risen in her mind unbidden, fully formed, the way a navigator knows a heading before she can show you the chart. As if some part of her had been carrying the coordinates for a decade and had only now been given permission to look at them.

* * *

By 0700 the station was already different.

Casper saw it in the small things first, because small things were what she watched—that was the whole architecture of her survival. The fuel dock's queue had doubled overnight, and the ships in it weren't haulers. A corvette with its transponder scrubbed to a neutral charter code sat taking on reaction mass with the unhurried appetite of something preparing for a long, violent errand. Two men in matching gray jackets stood at the head of the gangway doing nothing at all, which was its own kind of message; men who were merely waiting looked like they were waiting, and these two looked like they were *posted*.

In the chandlery, the shelves near the register had been cleared of the expensive things—long-range comms boosters, hardened data cores, medical kits rated for vacuum trauma. Casper had seen that pattern once before, ten years ago, in the three weeks before the Meridian incident became a word people lowered their voices around. Panic didn't start with shouting. It started with shopping.

The station's public boards cycled the announcement every six minutes, sandwiched between freight rates and weather advisories for gas giants no one visited. Beneath it, the comment threads scrolled too fast to read: *good riddance*, *get out while you can*, *the Vellum Compact will hold the lanes*, *no they won't*, *anyone selling hull insurance, asking for a friend*. Someone had posted a grainy image of a ship breaking up in orbit, timestamped from the last war, captioned simply: *you remember how this goes.*

Casper bought what she needed without conversation—she'd cultivated a relationship with the chandlery's owner that consisted entirely of mutual disinterest—and carried the crates back down the ring corridor feeling the station's mood change around her like pressure dropping before a storm. People walked faster. People didn't make eye contact in the way that meant *I've decided not to see you*, which was different from ordinary anonymity and much worse. Ordinary anonymity was comfortable. This was the anonymity of crowds that expected to scatter.

Dree was aboard when she returned, running diagnostics on the portside thruster array with her sleeves rolled and a torque driver tucked behind her ear like a pencil. She glanced up as Casper came through the airlock, and Casper watched her do the same thing Casper did—read the other woman's face, measure the fear in it, decide how much to admit to knowing.

"You heard," Dree said. Not a question.

"Everyone heard."

"Thirty days." Dree went back to the diagnostic panel, but her hands had slowed. "That's not enough time to finish the refit properly. It's barely enough time to finish it badly."

"We'll finish it badly, then."

"That's not—" Dree stopped herself. Set the driver down. Turned around fully, and Casper braced, because she knew that turn, that particular squared-up stillness Dree got when she'd been holding a question long enough that it had grown teeth. "Cass. Four days ago you started pulling our route history off the nav core. Yesterday you burned the Kestrel safehouse—I know because Maro called me asking why his contact codes went dead. And now you're buying armor plating we don't need for a refit we won't finish. So I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to actually lie to me well or not at all."

Casper said nothing. The galley hummed around them. Somewhere below, the backup pods slept their dreamless sleep in the dark of the hold, and Casper felt their presence the way you feel a bruise—constant, dull, impossible to forget by deciding to.

"What happened?" Dree asked quietly. "Four days ago. What changed?"

And Casper, who had lied to customs clerks and fleet inspectors and mirror-faced archivists and, most expertly, to herself, found that the lie wouldn't come. Not because Dree deserved the truth—though she did—but because the truth had gotten too heavy to carry alone, and thirty days was suddenly all the time there was.

"There's a name on a manifest," Casper said. "Mine."

* * *

She told her almost all of it. The Meridian listing. The impossibility of the dates. The chip in its shielded case, which she showed Dree the way you'd show someone a wound. She kept back the Archive—kept back the word itself, the way it glowed in her mind, the way the hold's encrypted pings had been addressed to a designation she hadn't spoken aloud in ten years. Some truths she wasn't ready to hand over even while handing over others. She told herself she'd get there. She told herself there was time.

There were twenty-nine days left. She was already learning to count in the new currency.

Dree listened without interrupting, which was rare, and when Casper finished she was silent for a long moment, looking at the chip case rather than at Casper.

"The Meridian," Dree said finally. "My uncle served on a tender in that sector. Afterward. Recovery duty." His voice had a strange flatness. "He never talked about it except once, drunk, and what he said was that they never found the ship. Not wreckage. Not debris. Nothing. A capital vessel with four hundred souls just—absent. And the fleet closed the file anyway."

"I know."

"Do you? Because here's what I know, Cass. I know that when a fleet closes a file that fast, it isn't grief. It's housekeeping." Dree picked the torque driver back up, turned it over in her fingers, put it down again. "And I know that in twenty-nine days, every faction in this sector is going to be fighting over whatever's worth fighting over, and if there's anything left of the Meridian—if there's *anything*—it's going to be the first thing they fight over. You understand that, right? Whatever your name is doing on that manifest, you're not the only one who noticed. Manifests have copies. Copies have buyers."

The cold that went through Casper then had nothing to do with the station's climate controls. She had been thinking of the manifest as a threat to *her*—her name, her cover, her decade of careful invisibility. She had not yet made herself think of it as a map. But Dree was right, and the rightness of it landed like ballast shifting in a swell. A manifest was a pointer. A pointer to a ship that officially never flew, listed against a crew that officially never existed, filed in a customs system that someone had either hacked with extraordinary skill or opened with extraordinary authority. Either way, the file existed. Either way, other eyes could find it. Other eyes *had* found it—the clerk who flagged it, whoever the clerk reported to, and the smooth-voiced archivist named Sarn who had appeared at her dock within hours as if summoned by the mere disturbance of her name.

*Ilem Sarn,* she thought, and the thought had weight, direction, a vector. He had offered protection in exchange for the chip, and protection was a word with two faces, and she had sent him away, and he had smiled like a man whose schedule was longer than her patience.

If the Archive was real—if the thing her own cargo AI had been receiving pings about for years was real—then it wasn't just her past sleeping out there in dead space. It was leverage. It was the Meridian's missing four hundred, preserved somewhere, held by someone, and in twenty-nine days every gun in the sector would be pointed at it, because a weapon like that doesn't stay buried once the truce stops burying things.

"They'll destroy it," Casper said aloud, not meaning to speak. "Whoever gets there first. If they can't control it, they'll burn it. That's what you do with evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

"I don't know yet." And that was true, and it was also the most honest sentence she'd spoken in ten years, and it terrified her more than the announcement had. "But I think I'm going to find out."

* * *

That night she went down to the hold.

She told herself it was a systems check. The backup pods in her cargo—three sealed cradles, leased freight, contents undisclosed, the kind of contract that paid well precisely because it asked nothing—needed their environmental telemetry reviewed, and reviewing them was legitimate work, and legitimate work was what she did. She was Casper Voss, hauler, licensed, boring. She checked her cargo.

She knelt beside the nearest pod and pulled the diagnostic feed, and the numbers scrolled green and placid, and she watched them without seeing them, because what she was actually doing was listening. The *Long Answer*'s comm suite had been logging anomalies for years—encrypted bursts from a bearing no ship traveled, addressed to a callsign she had buried with everything else. She'd told herself it was noise. She'd told herself a lot of things.

Twenty-nine days. In twenty-nine days the truce would die, and the factions would arm, and the first missiles would fly toward the edge of dead space toward a relay full of the dead, and everything that the Meridian had been—everything *she* had been—would end as collateral damage in someone else's war. Unless she got there first. Unless she understood what it was before they turned it into ammunition.

It wasn't a choice anymore. That was the strange, clarifying horror of it. For ten years she had believed her freedom lived in staying still, staying small, staying unremarkable—a fixed point the universe's attention slid past. But the countdown had started whether she moved or not, and stillness now was just another way of being caught.

She rested her palm against the pod's cold shell, an unconscious gesture, almost tender, and hated herself a little for it.

"Dree," she said into the ship's intercom, later, standing in the dark of the hold with the nav charts already blooming across her retinal display, plotting a course she'd sworn never to plot. "Clear my morning. I need to talk to you about the routes we're going to stop flying."

Outside the viewport, beyond the station's lights, the contested orbits wheeled slow and silent, and somewhere far past them, at the edge of everything mapped, a beacon she had spent a decade refusing to hear pulsed patiently into the dark—like a clock, counting down with her.
