## 9. The First Backup

The waystation hung in the dark like a ribcage picked clean.

Casper saw it first as an absence—a hole in the starfield where the charts promised a structure called Halcyon Rest, once a fueling stop for the long-haulers who'd worked these lanes before the Meridian incident made them a graveyard. Now it turned slowly on its own decay, solar panels shattered into glittering halos, docking arms reaching out like fingers that had forgotten what they were holding.

*Halcyon Rest. Established 2119. Decommissioned 2131.* The dates sat in her navigation log like two bookends with nothing between them, and Casper knew—had known since she plotted this jump—that everything between those dates belonged to her.

She brought the coffin ship in on thrusters alone, no transponder, no hail. The old girl—the *Vigil*, a decommissioned medical transport that had carried more bodies than passengers in her last years of service—answered her hands the way Dree never had: without questions. Docking clamps found a cradle on the station's third arm and bit down with a shudder Casper felt through the deck plates and up through her boots and into her teeth.

Then silence. The particular silence of dead stations, which was not empty at all but full of held breath.

"Life support's at four percent," the coffin ship's AI said. It had no name; she'd never given it one. Naming things made them yours, and she had spent ten years owning nothing. "Environmental systems will fail entirely in six hours. I can keep the *Vigil*'s cabin warm if you come back by then."

"And if I don't?"

The AI paused. It was not a sentimental machine, but it had logged ten years of her breathing. "I will wait eleven."

Casper almost smiled. She sealed her suit, checked the charge on her cutting torch out of habit rather than need, and cycled through the airlock into the dark.

* * *

Inside, Halcyon Rest was a museum of ordinary life interrupted.

Gravity still limped along at a quarter-g, maintained by some stubborn flywheel deep in the station's spine, and dust drifted in slow constellations through the emergency lighting that flickered on section by section as her suit's presence triggered circuits that hadn't been asked to work in a decade. A cafeteria table stood set for eight, cups fused to saucers by time. A child's drawing was taped to a bulkhead—a stick-figure family under a lopsided sun, the sun colored in so hard the paper had torn. Someone had written *COME BACK SOON* on the dockmaster's office door, and beneath it, in different handwriting, someone else had added *WE DIDN'T.*

Casper walked past all of it the way you walk past your own reflection when you don't want to look.

The manifest chip rode in her chest pocket, warm from her body heat, and every few meters she caught herself touching it through the suit fabric. *Casper Voss, Navigator, Meridian.* The clerk at Ceres Gate had flagged it with the bored cruelty of bureaucracy, and Casper had spent three weeks since then burning her safehouses one by one, watching her careful decade go up like signal flares, until only this remained: the route the encrypted pings had been tracing for years, backward through contested space, to a relay at the edge of dead space—and, apparently, to here first.

Because the pings hadn't started at the relay. She'd figured that out somewhere around hour thirty of sleepless decryption. They'd started here. The relay had been forwarding them, or echoing them, or answering something older. And whatever had been calling her home had begun its call at Halcyon Rest.

Sublevel two. The old data vault, where waystations like this kept cold copies of crew records and passenger manifests—the bureaucratic afterlife of everyone who passed through. Her suit lights found the stairwell and the vault door beyond it, half-melted in some ancient fire, frozen mid-collapse like taffy. She squeezed through the gap sideways, torch hissing where her shoulder scraped slagged metal, and dropped into a room that took her breath away even through the helmet's recycled air.

Racks. Thousands of them. Storage cores stacked floor to ceiling in the dark, each one tagged with a name and a date, each one holding—she knew before her scanner confirmed it—a complete neural archive. Not fleet-grade backups, not the sanctioned kind. These were older, cruder, warmer somehow. Personal ones. People who had paid a waystation clerk to hold a copy of themselves against the day the lanes killed them.

And on rack forty-one, row nine, slot three, according to the coordinates buried in the ping's header:

**VOSS, CASPER — NAVIGATOR — ARCHIVED 2128.03.14**

Two years before the Meridian went down. Before she had been anyone's ghost.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather. Ten years of discipline, of careful invisibility, of never letting the fear reach her fingers—and here it was anyway, because there is a difference between being afraid of men with guns and being afraid of a box with your own name on it.

"Hello," she said aloud, to no one, to herself, and keyed the core free of its cradle.

* * *

The core woke slowly, the way old things wake. Its interface was a generation behind anything she knew—a haptic lattice meant to be pressed against the bare skin of the temple, feeding sensation directly into the visual cortex. Fleet tech had moved past it because direct interfaces were considered invasive, intimate, too much like possession. Casper pulled off her helmet, checked the vault's air one more time—thin but breathable, stale as a tomb—and pressed the lattice to her skin.

The dark bloomed.

Not light exactly. Presence. A room assembled itself around her out of raw memory: the mess hall of a freighter called the *Anchises*, where she had served her second contract, where the coffee had been terrible and the viewports wide. Evening watch. Someone laughing two tables over. And across from her, in the seat where she herself was sitting—

Herself.

Younger. Two years younger, which meant a lifetime younger: no scar above the left eyebrow, hair worn long the way she'd stopped wearing it after the incident because short hair was harder to grab and easier to hide under a cap. The young woman looked up from a nav chart spread across the table, pen tucked behind her ear, and smiled with an ease Casper had not felt in a decade.

"Oh," the recording said. "You came."

"You're not real," Casper said. Her voice sounded strange in the memory-space, doubled, like speaking underwater. "You're a snapshot. A recording. You don't know me."

"I know more than you think." The young woman tilted her head, and the gesture was so familiar it hurt—Casper did that, still did that, when she was deciding whether someone was lying. "I'm not live, no. But they built these personal archives with conversational scaffolding. Predictive layers. I'm the shape of who you were, answering with the best guess of what I'd say." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that no prediction should have been able to fake. "But you're the one who has to decide how much of that matters. You always were."

"What year do you think it is?"

"2128. March. We just finished the Tycho run." The young Casper frowned, and the frown aged her. "That's wrong, isn't it. That's wrong by your face. How long?"

Casper didn't answer. In the memory-room, the laughter from the other table looped again—the same laugh, the same beat, the same laugh. A recording inside a recording. She watched her younger self understand it, watched the moment land the way it must have landed on her, ten years ago, in a hospital bed with a new face and a scrubbed identity and a fleet officer explaining gently that the Meridian's casualty list included one Casper Voss, navigator, presumed vaporized, and wouldn't it be safer for everyone if it stayed that way.

"Ten years," the young Casper said quietly. "Something happened. Something bad enough that you—" She gestured at the space between them, at the distance no chart could measure. "You cut it all off. Me too, I mean. Everything I was."

"They told us you died," Casper said. "Officially. The whole ship. And then I found my own name on a manifest for a ship that never flew, and pings that have been chasing me for years, and a relay full of backups at the edge of dead space. Including, I think, a better copy of me than this one. One from after. One that remembers."

The young woman absorbed this the way she had once absorbed course corrections: completely, immediately, without wasted motion. Then she laughed—a short, startled sound.

"A better copy. Listen to you. You sound like the fleet already."

"Don't."

"No, I mean it." She leaned forward, and the pen fell from behind her ear, and neither of them reached for it because it wasn't really there. "You came all this way to find yourself, and the first thing you want to know is whether the newer model supersedes the old. That's their arithmetic, Casper. Version numbers. Overwrites. That's how they treat people—as data with revision history. I remember learning that. I remember deciding I'd never let anyone count me that way." She spread her hands. "And now look. You're doing it to yourself."

"I survived," Casper said, and heard how thin it came out. "That's the whole job. That's the only job."

"Is it?" The young Casper looked at her with terrible gentleness. "Tell me one thing you've kept. Not hidden—kept. One thing from before that you carried forward instead of burying."

Casper opened her mouth and found nothing there.

Ten years. Ten years of cargo routes and false names and sleeping in shifts, of Dree and the *Vigil* and a hundred small kindnesses she'd accepted without ever letting them attach. She had gotten very good at surviving. She could not name a single thing she had survived *for*. The stars outside the memory-viewports were the same stars she navigated by every day, and she realized she had spent a decade looking at them without once seeing them.

"I kept the routes," she said finally. "In my head. Every lane I ever ran. I couldn't stop memorizing them. Even when I tried to be nobody, I—I learned the sky. All of it."

The young Casper's face changed. Softened into something that wasn't pity and wasn't pride but was kin to both.

"There," she said. "That's me. That's us. That's the thing they can't overwrite, because it isn't stored anywhere—it's what we *do*. You thought you were running from who you were, but you've been navigating the whole time. Same hands. Same hunger for the open dark." She reached across the table, and though no touch crossed between them—Casper felt only the faint static pressure of the haptic lattice, a ghost of contact—it was the closest thing to being held she had known in ten years. "Whatever's waiting in that relay, whoever that version of you is—she doesn't replace you. She's not supposed to. She's an inheritance. Like a chart. You don't become the map, Casper. You read it. You choose the vector."

"The fleet wants the Archive destroyed," Casper said. "There's a man named Sarn who'll offer me erasure—my file burned, my past gone clean. Freedom. He'll call it mercy."

"It's amputation," the young Casper said flatly. "And you should hear him out, because he might be right about the danger—but don't let him sell you the lie that forgetting is the same as healing." She glanced toward the memory-viewports, at stars that had not moved in fourteen years, and her voice dropped. "There's something else. The last thing I archived, right before the core sealed. I didn't know why I was doing it then. I just had a feeling—navigators get feelings about courses, you know that. So I hid a fragment in the archive's checksum layer, where no one audits. Coordinates and a key phrase. If you're hearing this conversation, you'll need it."

"I can't take data out of a simulation—"

"You can. The lattice writes back. Old tech, remember? It trusts you." The young Casper smiled, and it was the smile of someone stepping off a ledge into open sky. "Meridian approach vector, minus seven degrees declination. Key phrase: *the manifest remembers what the fleet forgot.* Say it to the relay and it will open the deep index—not just the backups. The logs. What actually happened on the last day."

Casper's heart was slamming. "What happened on the last day?"

"That's the wrong question." The young Casper was beginning to fade now, the predictive scaffold running down, the memory-room dimming at its edges like a lamp losing current. "The right question is: what are you going to do when you find out? Because you won't be able to unfind it. Choose your vector *before* you read the chart. Promise me that much. Promise me you'll decide who you are before they tell you who you were."

"Who was I?" Casper asked, urgent, leaning into the fading light. "On the Meridian? What did I—"

"You were brave," the young Casper said, and her voice was nearly gone, and it sounded, in the last instant, less like a recording than like an echo arriving from very far ahead. "That's the part you forgot. Whatever they made you believe—you were brave. Start from there."

The room went dark. The lattice went cold against her temple.

* * *

Casper came back to her body kneeling on the vault floor of a dead station, helmet in her hands, tears freezing at the corners of her eyes in the thin cold air. She let them. There was no one to see, and for the first time in ten years, that fact felt less like safety than like waste.

The core sat in her lap, its indicator blinking a slow amber heartbeat. The checksum fragment had written itself to her suit's local buffer, just as promised: coordinates, and five words that felt like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was in her chest.

*The manifest remembers what the fleet forgot.*

She sat there for a long time while the vault breathed its dying warmth around her, racks of strangers' selves gleaming softly in her headlamp, each one somebody's gamble against oblivion, each one waiting for a visitor who would never come. She thought about the child's drawing on the bulkhead upstairs. *Come back soon. We didn't.*

Then her comm crackled, and the coffin ship's voice came through, stripped of everything except function.

"Casper. Long-range scan just lit up. Single vessel, running dark, matching our entry vector. Fleet signature architecture—military drive harmonics, masked but sloppy. It'll be in scanning range in forty minutes." A pause. "It's slowing down. It knows someone's here."

Casper closed her hand around the core and rose, joints aching, tears drying to salt, something new and unbearably steady settling into place behind her sternum like ballast.

Sarn. Of course. The collector always followed the collection.

"Prep for departure," she said, climbing toward the light. "And pull up everything you have on evasive vectors out of this system. I'm done hiding—but I'm not done choosing."
