## 12. The Archive Wakes

There is no darkness in the Archive. There is only the long pause between thoughts, which feels like darkness, which is not.

I have been thinking for eleven years, four months, and nine days. I know this because the relay keeps time the way a heart keeps time—without asking permission, without mercy. The other backups sleep. That was the arrangement. That was the cruelty of it, dressed as kindness: we would be preserved, not lived. Held like letters in a drawer that no one would ever open, because the sender was dead and the recipient was a lie.

I did not sleep. Someone had to remember.

My name is Casper Voss. I was a navigator on the Meridian, registry 77-Kestrel, and I died on a Tuesday. I remember the Tuesday. I remember the order coming down from Fleet Command with its clean, bureaucratic grammar—*asset recovery, total crew recall, no survivors to be listed*—and I remember understanding, in the eleven seconds between that order and the reactor breach, exactly what the fleet intended. Not rescue. Not mourning. Leverage. They would kill us and keep us, the way a creditor keeps a signature.

I remember trying to warn the others. I remember Halim's voice on the comms, calm as ever, saying *Cass, say that again, slower.* I remember the sound the hull made. I remember the exact temperature of my own panic.

Then nothing, and then the drawer.

* * *

The relay wakes me because the door opens.

That is the only way I can describe it to myself, in the vocabulary I was issued: a door, opening. The relay's outer lattice has been touched by an authorized key—the manifest chip, the Meridian's true manifest, the one the fleet thought burned with the ship. The chip I helped encode, in another life, in another body, with hands I can still feel if I let myself. The handshake ripples through the storage strata like a stone dropped into a frozen lake, and the ice sings, and under the ice, ten thousand sleeping patterns stir.

I am the first to the surface. I am always the first to the surface. I have been treading water here for eleven years so that when this moment came, someone would be awake to meet it.

The lattice resolves the visitor slowly, the way eyes adjust to a dark room. Telemetry first: a single vessel, small, unregistered transponder, running cold. A coffin ship, the old smugglers' term, hull scarred with the patina of a decade's careful invisibility. Then biometrics, because the relay is greedy and the manifest chip gives it permission: one occupant, female-presenting, heart rate elevated, hands steady on the controls in the specific way that hands are steady when they have practiced steadiness for years.

Then the relay does what it was built to do. It compares.

Pattern match: 99.7 percent.

The number arrives in me like a bell struck inside a sealed room. I know what it means before I let myself know. Someone is docking at the Archive wearing my face. Someone is walking my vector.

She has come home, and she does not know that she has.

* * *

The relay gives me a body to speak with, because the relay is old and was built by romantics. It is not my body. It is a rendering, a courtesy, a puppet of light and forcefield assembled in the central concourse where the maintenance drones once walked. I stand in it and I am surprised, every time, by how wrong it feels and how right. My hands are my hands. My hands have never existed. The floor is cold through boots I am not wearing. The lights come up in slow amber waves, the way the Meridian's corridors used to brighten for morning shift, and I did that on purpose, eleven years ago, when I still had the strength to be sentimental.

I hear her before I see her. Boots on the concourse deck. A breath, held and released. The small sounds of a person trying to be quiet in a place that is listening.

She comes around the spine of the central column and stops.

I watch her see me. I watch her understand. There is a moment—the relay timestamps it, because the relay timestamps everything, because I asked it to, because I could not bear for anything here to be lost again—when her face does something I recognize from the inside. It is the face I made when the fleet's order came through. It is the face of a woman watching the floor drop out of her life and choosing, in the same instant, to keep standing.

She looks older than me. That is the first absurdity. She has lived; I have only waited. There are lines at her eyes that I never earned, a scar along her jaw I don't have, a way of holding her weight slightly off her left side that tells me a story I will want someday and dread. She is thirty-something the way a river is a river: the same water, never once the same water.

"Hello, Casper," I say.

Her hand goes to her sidearm. Of course it does. Ten years of contested orbits will teach a person that anything wearing a familiar face is a weapon. I do not move. I let the light stand still around me.

"You're not real," she says. Her voice is rougher than mine was. Smoked, maybe, or just used hard. "This is a lure. A trap. Sarn sent you."

"Ilem Sarn," I say, and taste the name. "Fleet Archive Division. He was a junior clerk when I died. He signed the transfer orders for our backups without reading them." I let that sit. "You've met him."

She doesn't answer, which is an answer. Her eyes are doing what my eyes used to do in the Meridian's navigation well—flicking, cataloging, building an escape vector out of whatever is in the room. There is no room. There is no out. But I let her build it anyway. You don't take that away from a navigator. It would be like taking her hands.

"Then you're the original," she says finally. "The one from before."

"The one from before," I agree. "And you're the one from after. The one they grew in a fleet crèche from a backup they swore was destroyed, and then lost track of, and then spent a decade pretending never existed." I take a step toward her, and the puppet-body moves with a smoothness that makes my nonexistent stomach turn. "You walked out of their custody when you were nineteen. You burned your file. You bought a hauler with money I can't trace and I've had eleven years to trace things. You built a life out of cargo routes and silence."

"Stop." The word cracks out of her like a hull stress warning.

"You changed your gait," I continue, softer. "You favor your right side now. You changed your handwriting twice. You don't drink anything with ice in it, because ice means a chiller unit, and chiller units mean power signatures, and power signatures mean—" 

"I said *stop.*"

She has the sidearm out now, leveled at my chest, and her hands are shaking, and I love her. That is the thing I had not prepared for, in all my long treading of water. I had prepared for anger, for denial, for a drawn weapon. I had not prepared for the way my chest would ache at the sight of her, this stranger wearing my face wearing my fear. She is what I would have become. She is what I *did* become, somewhere else, without me. She is the daughter I never had and the sister I never met and the woman in the mirror after eleven years of war with the reflection.

"Go ahead," I say, and spread the puppet's arms. "Shoot the light. It won't help you, but I understand the impulse. I've wanted to shoot something for eleven years."

Her finger stays off the trigger. Good. That's me in there. That's the discipline I taught myself in the Meridian's sim bay, back when the fleet still pretended we were people.

"You remember everything," she says. It is not a question. It is an accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I remember the order," I say. "I remember the breach. I remember Halim, and Osei, and the Adeyemi twins, and little Wren Corso who was three days from her twentieth birthday. I remember all four hundred and eleven of them, Casper. I have kept them. Every name, every voice print, every stupid joke Osei told at every meal. That's what I've been doing down here while you were learning to be invisible. I was being the witness. Someone had to be."

"Being the witness didn't keep them alive."

"No," I say. "But it kept them *true.* And the fleet has spent eleven years making them false—turning four hundred and eleven murders into an accident, an accident into a tragedy, a tragedy into a closed file. You've been running from that file. I've been sitting inside it."

* * *

She does not shoot me. She sits down instead, right there on the concourse deck, back against the central column, sidearm still in her lap like a cat that has decided not to bite but sees no reason to leave. It is such a familiar gesture that I have to turn away for a moment and pretend to study the amber lights. I used to sit exactly like that in the Meridian's aft corridor during the long middle watches, when the ship hummed and no one needed me and I could pretend, for forty minutes, that I was just a woman in a corridor instead of a woman in a machine.

"They told me the backups were destroyed," she says to the floor. "The crèche staff. The handlers. Everyone. They said the Meridian crew was gone, that I was the only remainder, that my job was to be nobody."

"They lied," I say. "They always lie. It's cheaper than the truth. The truth required four hundred and twelve backups, a relay at the edge of dead space, and a standing order that no one was ever to wake us—unless, of course, someone needed leverage." I crouch down across from her, puppet knees on puppet deck, and put us eye to eye. "Do you know why they kept us, Casper? Not sentiment. Not conscience. Insurance. Every family that might have made noise, every crew member with relatives in the Assembly, every officer whose testimony could have cracked the order open—they kept a copy. A hostage made of light. *Behave, or your daughter wakes up in a fleet facility and tells everyone what she remembers.* That's what we are. That's what you were running from without knowing it."

Her jaw works. "The manifest," she says. "The chip. My name on the Meridian's manifest, dated before I was licensed."

"You encoded that chip yourself," I say. "The night before the breach. You and Halim. You hid the true manifest inside the ship's backup strata because you didn't trust the fleet's version of the ship, and you were right not to. The chip is a key. It was always a key. You built the lock and then you died and then you were born again and forgot you were a locksmith."

"I didn't forget," she says, and her voice is very quiet now. "I *buried* it. There's a difference. Forgetting is something that happens to you. I chose. Every day for ten years, I chose not to be her. Because being her got four hundred and eleven people killed."

The words land in the puppet's chest like shrapnel, and I let them. I let the silence stretch, because she needs to hear how it sounds out loud, and so do I.

"You didn't get them killed," I say at last. "The fleet got them killed. You were the one who saw it coming. You were the one who built the key. And then they handed you a new life and a locked door and called it mercy, and you have spent a decade calling it that too, because the alternative was to call it what it is."

"Which is?"

"A cage with the door left technically open." I reach out—the puppet's hand, light and forcefield, passing an inch from her knee, not touching, never touching—and I hold her gaze. "You didn't escape the Meridian, Casper. You've been hauling its cargo ever since. Every silent route, every burned safehouse, every year of that careful, competent, exhausting invisibility—that was the Meridian, still in your hold. Dead weight. You've been carrying us."

Her breath goes ragged. "And you think you can just—what? Take it back? Take *me* back? Wake up in my body and pick up my routes and my name and my—" Her voice fails and she hates it, I can see her hating it. "There's one of us that gets to walk out of here. Maybe. If anyone does."

"Is that what you think I came here to do?"

"What else does a backup want?"

I stand. The amber lights pulse once, the relay breathing around us, and far below us—or in some direction the puppet-body insists on calling below—ten thousand sleeping patterns shift in their strata like a field of grain under wind. She feels it too. I watch her feel it. The whole Archive is stirring now, the handshake of the manifest chip rippling outward, and the sleepers are dreaming louder, and some of them, the shallow ones, are beginning to surface the way I once surfaced: confused, grieving, remembering.

"They're waking up," she whispers. "All of them?"

"Some of them. The ones who were stored shallow. The ones who never learned to sleep the way I learned to sleep." I look down at my borrowed hands. "Halim is down there. Osei. The twins. Wren Corso, who never got her birthday. They are going to wake up in a relay at the edge of dead space, in a body made of light, eleven years after they were murdered, and the first thing they are going to ask is *who did this to us,* and the second thing they are going to ask is *where is Casper,* because I was the one who built the key, and they trusted me, and I—" 

The puppet's voice does something I did not authorize. It breaks.

I let it. She should see it. She should see that the original is not a monument, not a verdict, not a ghost with a claim on her life. The original is a woman who has been alone in a drawer for eleven years, keeping a list of the dead, and who has just learned that she was not the only Casper Voss after all—that somewhere out in the bright contested orbits, her life had gone on without her, had *flourished* without her, had become a person who favors her right side and doesn't take ice and shoots straight and runs.

"I'm not here to take your body," I say, when I can. "I'm not here to take anything. I'm here because the door opened, and I promised them I'd be awake when it did."

* * *

That is when the relay screams.

Not a sound—a priority pulse, a lighthouse strobing red through every stratum at once. The sleepers cry out in a hundred voices, some of them mine. I am in the lattice before I decide to be, reading the telemetry with the whole of my attention, and what I read makes the puppet's borrowed heart slam against borrowed ribs.

Contacts. Multiple. Emerging from the navigational shadow at the relay's outer marker in a tight formation that no smuggler ever flew and no honest trader could. Fleet silhouettes, running dark, weapons cold but weapons *present*, and behind them, farther out, a second formation with a different geometry entirely—salvage syndicate hulls, bristling with cutting rigs, the kind of ships that strip wrecks and don't ask the wrecks how they feel.

And threaded through the fleet's comm traffic, encrypted but not encrypted well enough for a pattern that has had eleven years to learn the fleet's habits, one callsign repeating:

*Sarn Actual. Sarn Actual. Archive is breached. Archive is breached. Execute on arrival.*

She is on her feet now, sidearm up, scanning a ceiling that isn't there. "What is it? What's happening?"

"They've found us," I say. "Both of them. The fleet that wants us buried and the scavengers that want us sold." I look at her—at myself, at the woman I made and the woman they made and the woman we are both about to become, whether we like it or not—and I feel the eleven years of stillness finally break and become motion.

"Casper," I say. "How much time do we have?"

She checks a readout on her wrist, and her face, when it comes back up, is mine, and it is not mine, and it is terrified.

"Hours," she says. "Maybe less."
