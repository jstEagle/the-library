## 8. The Drowned Deck Splits

The Drowned Deck had a rule older than anyone who enforced it: no cargo got opened aboard the Deck. Not because of superstition — though there was plenty of that, painted on the bulkheads in faded luck-sigils from a dozen homeworlds — but because opened cargo was evidence, and evidence was the one thing smugglers couldn't fence.

Ivo broke the rule at 0300 station time, in front of everyone.

They'd gathered in the galley because the galley was where hard things had always been said. It was a low-ceilinged room that had once been a tug's mess hall before the Deck grew around it like coral around a wreck: mismatched chairs, a table scarred with burn rings from twenty years of stolen coffee, and above the serving hatch a hand-lettered sign reading NO DEBTS BETWEEN FAMILY. Someone had added, in newer ink, *except favors*.

Ivo set the wafer on the table between them and let it sit there, small and silver and obscene, while they told them everything. The beacon. The nine seconds. The fragment of launch footage two centuries old, the Meridian rising off a green world on a pillar of fire, the timestamp burned into the corner of every frame. And then the part that made the room go quiet in a way rooms rarely went quiet on the Drowned Deck: the manifest fragments stitched through the wafer's deeper architecture, names and election records, the sleepers' own voices preserved in the margins of their own log.

"Every wafer I've ever carried," Ivo said. "Every run. They were all pieces of this."

Marrow spoke first, because Marrow always spoke first when there was money in the room. He was the Deck's oldest resident, a former long-haul rigger whose hands had been replaced so many times they no longer matched each other, one chrome-bright and one matte ceramic, and he drummed them both on the table now like a man counting coins only he could see.

"Do you understand what you're holding?" His voice was gravel poured slow. "The governing families have spent two hundred years building a charter that says the Meridian belongs to Halcyon. The syndicate says it belongs to labor. The Church says it belongs to God. And you're telling me the actual answer has been riding around in your jacket pocket for six years." He laughed, a short ugly sound. "That's not cargo, girl. That's the deed to half of heaven."

"I'm not a girl," Ivo said, out of habit more than heat.

"You're whatever the market decides you are tomorrow." Marrow leaned back, chrome fingers tapping. "We sell it. We sell it once, quietly, to whoever pays most, and we buy the Deck outright, we buy new hulls, we buy out of this life entirely. Every one of us retires rich enough to never touch a customs seal again. That's what family means. Family means nobody dies poor."

"That's not what family means," said Sefa.

Sefa ran the Deck's fuel lines and its funerals, which on the Drowned Deck were often the same job. She was broad and gray-tempered and she had pulled Ivo out of a depressurized hold when Ivo was nineteen, carried them forty meters through vacuum on her back with her suit screaming at her, and had never once mentioned it since. She looked at the wafer now the way she looked at unstable isotopes.

"You don't know what's in the rest of those fragments," she said. "You know what your grandmother wanted you to find. You said it yourself — she seeded these, she chose what went in them. A woman smart enough to hide a colony ship's true log inside contraband memory-wafers was smart enough to hide other things too. Things that could burn us. Names. Debts. Maybe crimes." She shook her head slowly. "Two hundred years ago people did something on that ship, or to it, or because of it, and the whole system agreed to forget. There's a reason records get erased, Ivo. Erasure is mercy. We should melt this down and pour it into the drive plasma and let the past stay dead."

"It's not the past staying dead," Ryn said. "It's four thousand people."

* * *

Ryn had been sitting apart from the table, the way Ryn always sat apart, one shoulder against the bulkhead as if the room might need bracing. They were the Deck's best pilot and its worst liar, and everyone knew both things, and everyone loved them anyway. Now they pushed off the wall and came to stand over the wafer, and Ivo watched their face do the thing it did when Ryn was trying to be cold and couldn't quite manage it.

"Four thousand sleepers," Ryn said. "Minus whatever the reactor's already eaten. Say three thousand left by the time anyone reaches them. Say the ship can wake half. Fifteen hundred slots." They held up one hand, fingers spread. "The families will want all fifteen hundred. The syndicate will want half. The Church will settle for a tithe and call it grace. Nobody at that table—" they nodded toward the direction of Halcyon's administrative spire, invisible through the hull but present anyway, like weather "—nobody is going to spend a single slot on a dock rat from the Drowned Deck. Unless we make ourselves impossible to ignore."

"So we sell," Marrow said.

"No." Ryn's voice didn't rise; it sharpened. "We don't sell. Selling means the buyers choose. We use it. We go to the Meridian ourselves, and we wake the engineers."

The room absorbed that in silence. Sefa's jaw tightened. Old Quill, who hadn't spoken yet, who mostly communicated in grunts and engine diagnostics, lifted his head from his hands.

"The ship is dying," Ryn said, quieter now, the way they talked when they'd already done the math and hated the answer. "Its reactor is failing. Its systems are two centuries stale. Whoever wakes up on that hulk wakes up into a coffin unless someone fixes it first. So we don't wake politicians. We don't wake priests. We don't wake whoever's name is first on some election record from before my grandmother's grandmother was born. We wake the people who can keep the rest alive — power techs, life-support specialists, reactor engineers, doctors. Fifteen hundred slots, and we fill them with the only people who matter, which are the people who can build a future. Our future. The Deck's future. We bring them home and suddenly the Drowned Deck isn't a smuggler den anymore, it's the shipyard of a new fleet, and every faction on Halcyon has to negotiate with *us*."

"And the other fifteen hundred?" Ivo asked.

It came out flat, and Ivo hated how flat it came out, because the question wasn't really a question. Everyone at the table heard what it actually was: *and the ones you'd leave asleep forever?*

Ryn flinched. Only slightly. Only in the hands. But Ivo had spent six years learning to read those hands.

"They're asleep," Ryn said. "Asleep isn't dead. If the engineers stabilize the ship, maybe we come back for the rest. Maybe we build reactors fast enough to save everyone. I'm not choosing who dies, Ivo. I'm choosing who lives long enough to save the others."

"You are choosing," Sefa said. "You just don't want to watch yourself do it."

"I'd rather watch myself do it than watch the Okafor family auction human beings by charter weight!" Ryn's voice cracked finally, and the room heard six years of dockside funerals in it. "You think the families will be gentler? You think the syndicate's seniority lists are kinder? Somebody is going to make this choice. I'd rather it be someone who's hauled water with the people who'll live under it."

* * *

Quill spoke then, which was itself an event. He was the oldest hand on the Deck after Marrow, a wiry ex-navy man who'd lost his hearing in one ear to a coolant flash and compensated by watching mouths with unsettling intensity.

"There's a fourth option nobody's said," he rasped. "We give it away. Free. Broadcast the whole thing, every fragment, unedited, to every band on the dial. Let the whole system have the truth at once and let them choke on it together."

"Nobody pays for free," Marrow snapped.

"That's the point," Quill said. "You can't sell what everyone owns. And you can't bury what everyone owns either. Sefa wants it destroyed because it's dangerous. Marrow wants it sold because it's valuable. Both of those are true, and both of them mean somebody controls it, and whoever controls it becomes the next thing the factions fight over. Make it worthless. Make it air. Then nobody owns the sleepers' choice except the sleepers."

Ivo felt something turn over inside them, slow and heavy, like ballast shifting in a hull. Because that was the shape of the thought they'd been carrying since the beacon sang — the shape they hadn't dared look at directly. The log belonged to the sleepers. Not to the Deck. Not to the families. Not even to the grandmother who'd hidden it, if hiding it had meant letting it sit in the dark for two generations while errands got run.

But saying it aloud would break something. Saying it aloud would put Ivo against Marrow's retirement and Sefa's caution and Ryn's fierce, desperate arithmetic — against the family, over a family none of them had ever met.

So instead Ivo asked the question that would break it later, gently, like setting a fuse:

"How much of it is even here? One wafer. One fragment. My grandmother scattered these across twenty years of runs. Some of them went through other couriers. Some of them are probably still circulating. Some of them—" and here Ivo's voice did something none of them expected, something small and cracked, "—some of them might have been opened by people who didn't know what they were holding, and copied, and resold, and are sitting in private collections right now wearing labels like *vintage pre-colonial cinema*. The manifest isn't in this room. It's everywhere. It's already loose. We stopped being able to destroy it or own it about six years ago, and none of us noticed."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough that the Deck's ventilation cycle completed twice, its familiar shudder rolling through the deck plates like breathing.

Marrow stood first. He gathered his mismatched hands off the table and squared his shoulders, and when he spoke, his voice had gone careful, which from Marrow was worse than shouting.

"I've buried eleven people on this Deck," he said. "Good runners. Kids younger than you, Ivo. I promised every one of their ghosts that the Deck would outlive me, that the life would get easier for whoever came after. This—" he tapped the wafer once, lightly, almost tenderly, "—this is the only chance any of us will ever see to actually keep that promise. I'm going to find a buyer. Quietly. I'd rather have you beside me than across from me, but I'll survive across."

He walked out. No one stopped him. On the Drowned Deck, walking out of the galley during a hard talk was a message, and the message was understood.

Sefa stayed. She reached across the table and covered the wafer with one broad hand, not taking it, just sheltering it, the way you'd cup a flame in wind.

"I won't help you sell it," she said. "And I won't help you use it. But I'm not going to melt it either. Not yet. I want to see the rest of the fragments first — all of them, assembled, before anyone decides anything. If your grandmother built a message, then read the whole message. That's not a side, Ivo. That's a condition."

"That's fair," Ivo said, and meant it, and saw in Sefa's eyes that Sefa knew it was also a delay, and accepted the delay anyway, because that was what family did when it could.

Then it was Ryn. Ryn hadn't moved from the bulkhead, arms crossed now, watching Ivo with an expression that had gone somewhere past anger into something rawer.

"You're going to broadcast it," Ryn said. "Aren't you. Free. To everyone. Quill's way."

"I don't know yet."

"You do. You've known since the beacon sang. You just haven't told me because you know what I'll say." Ryn pushed off the wall and crossed to the table, close enough now that Ivo could see the exhaustion underneath the fire — Ryn hadn't slept since the nine seconds either, none of them had, the whole Deck running on adrenaline and stolen coffee. "If you give it away, the factions fight anyway. Nothing changes except we lose our leverage. And if we lose our leverage, we lose our shot at the ship, and the engineers die in their pods waiting for politicians to finish suing each other. I'm trying to save people, Ivo. Actual people. Why does doing it the smart way make me the villain?"

"Because you picked who counts as people," Ivo said softly. "Engineers first. That's a principle. Principles are how we forgive ourselves."

Ryn stared at them for a long moment. Something moved behind their eyes — hurt, yes, but underneath the hurt, calculation, and underneath the calculation, love, ugly and stubborn and already rearranging itself around betrayal.

"Fine," Ryn said. "Fine. Keep your secret. Keep your grandmother's little scavenger hunt." They straightened, and their voice went level and terrible. "Just remember that while you're deciding what the truth deserves, the clock on that ship doesn't care. Every hour you spend being principled is an hour closer to the reactor dying and everybody losing the argument."

They left. The galley door sighed shut behind them, and the sign above the hatch — NO DEBTS BETWEEN FAMILY — seemed suddenly like an epitaph.

* * *

Ivo sat alone in the galley for a long time after, turning the wafer over and over, listening to the Deck settle around them: Marrow's door sealing two compartments away, Sefa's boots in the fuel gallery, Quill humming tunelessly in the machine shop, and beneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, the hum of the beacon relay feeding down from the mast topside — still singing, still repeating its impossible arithmetic of waking and dying, patient as only machines and the dead can be patient.

Family, Ivo thought, was a vector. It had magnitude and direction, and you didn't get to choose either — you inherited them, the way you inherited a beacon nobody ordered and a cargo nobody knew was cargo. The only thing you ever got to choose was whether to correct course.

On the table, the wafer caught the light and threw it back, and in its silver curve Ivo saw reflected a sliver of the galley, the empty chairs, the sign, the whole fragile architecture of the only home they'd ever had — and understood, with the calm of someone watching a hull breach in slow motion, that it was already splitting along seams older than any of them.

The comm panel chimed. Station-priority routing, encrypted, official formatting dressed in smuggler's clothing.

Ivo opened it.

One line, no signature, no origin stamp:

*Inspector Vess Oka requests a meeting. Bring the manifest. Come alone. Or don't come at all — but know that I already know about your grandmother.*
