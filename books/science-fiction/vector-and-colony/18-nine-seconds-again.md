## 18. Nine Seconds Again

The frost on the Meridian's observation deck had grown a new finger overnight.

Ivo noticed it first thing, the way you notice a change in someone's face — not dramatic, just wrong. The ice had been creeping along the viewport seams since they'd boarded, fed by the slow leak of atmosphere through a hull wound two centuries old, but this tendril reached further than any before it, curling across the glass like something reaching for them.

Behind the frost, the outer dark was no longer empty. Three fleets were burning toward them from Halcyon: the governing families' white-hulled charter ships, the syndicate's patched-together tugs with their cargo claws retracted like fists, and behind both, the Church of the Seeded Earth's vessels, their prows painted with the green shoot of the covenant sigil. Forty hours out, maybe less. Ivo had watched them grow from smudges to shapes to ships.

The reactor had thirty-one days left in it. Or so the AI said.

*It says,* Ivo corrected themselves, standing alone on the frozen deck with their breath ghosting silver. *Not "says." It estimates. It hopes.*

They had learned the caretaker's voice by now — not its words but its cadence, the way it delivered bad news in the same flat register as good, because for two hundred years there had been no one to perform either emotion for. Its name, if it had one, was a designation string too long and too old to be useful. Ivo had taken to calling it Keeper, and Keeper had not objected, which was the closest thing to warmth aboard the ship.

"Keeper," Ivo said. "Status."

The lights came up a fraction — the AI's version of turning toward them. "Reactor output has declined four percent since your last query. Cryo-bank integrity holds at sixty-two percent of original capacity. Fleet vessels are now within sensor resolution sufficient to identify individual hulls. Would you like the list?"

"No." Ivo pressed their palm against the frosted viewport. Cold bit through the glove. Somewhere below their feet, eleven thousand sleepers hung in their pods like fruit on a tree that was dying from the roots. "I want to know what happens when it fails. Not the reactor. The gravity."

A pause. For an AI that answered most questions in under a second, a pause meant something.

"The Meridian's drive mass is integrated with the station-keeping field," Keeper said. "When the reactor crosses critical decay thresholds, the field will destabilize. You have experienced this phenomenon before."

Ivo's stomach dropped before the words finished landing.

"You've experienced this phenomenon before," they repeated. "What do you mean, *before?*"

"Nineteen days ago," Keeper said, "the Meridian's reactor underwent a cascade event during which the drive mass fluctuated for nine point zero three seconds. The event propagated through spacetime at luminal velocity. Any gravity-dependent structure within approximately one light-day would have experienced a transient anomaly consistent with—"

"Nine seconds," Ivo whispered.

Nine seconds. Every ship in the system drifting like toys in a drained bath. Cargo shattering against bulkheads. Dockworkers thrown gently, horribly, off their feet. And the beacon — the beacon nobody ordered, the beacon with no paperwork and no owner, the beacon three generations of Iversens had kept alive out of something nobody could name —

— answering.

"It wasn't a malfunction," Ivo said. Their voice came out strange, thin, as if the cold had gotten inside their chest. "The first time. Nine seconds ago — nineteen days ago — that was *you.*"

"I transmitted a distress call," Keeper agreed. "As my instructions require. As they have required since launch."

"When else?"

Another pause. Longer.

"Keeper. When *else?*"

"One hundred ninety-seven years ago," the AI said. "During the initial reactor instability that preceded the loss of main communications. One hundred forty-three years ago. Ninety-eight years ago. Sixty-one years ago. Thirty years ago. Twelve years ago." A beat. "And every day since, at reduced amplitude, on frequencies your instruments were not built to hear."

Ivo stood very still on the frozen deck while two hundred years rearranged themselves around them like furniture in a dark room.

The beacon hadn't been silent. The beacon had never been silent. It had been screaming — patiently, methodically, at whatever volume the physics allowed — for help, for attention, for *anyone*, since before Halcyon Station had a name. And every Iversen who had ever climbed its ladder with a tool kit and a thermos of coffee, who had replaced its corroded couplings and logged its quiet hum and felt, without knowing why, that the thing mattered — every one of them had been keeping alive the throat of a scream.

Grandmother Sefa must have known. Grandmother Sefa, who escaped the ship before it went dark, who seeded the system with wafers full of the true log, who built or found or inherited the errand that became Ivo's whole life — she had known the beacon was a lighthouse for a drowning ship, and she had told no one, and she had made her descendants keep the light anyway.

"She couldn't fix it," Ivo said aloud, to the frost, to the dead air. "She couldn't answer it. So she kept it lit."

The tendril of frost on the viewport seemed, in the dim emergency lighting, almost like handwriting.

Below decks, Ryn was running numbers again. Ivo could feel it the way you feel weather coming.

* * *

Ryn had claimed the engineering bay as theirs by right of occupation — three days of sleeping in a maintenance hammock strung between coolant conduits, surrounded by printouts of power curves that the ship's own displays rendered more elegantly but less honestly. Paper didn't round up. Paper didn't flatter. Paper told you exactly how many people arithmetic could save, and the number sat on Ryn's chest at night like a sleeping animal that might wake hungry.

Forty days had become thirty-one. The curve steepened as it fell; that was the cruelty of exponential decay. Every day that passed took more than a day's worth of margin, because the cryo-banks drew harder on a weakening current, and the weakening current degraded the banks faster, and the whole system was a slope greased with its own meltwater.

Reviving a sleeper cost enough power to keep nine others frozen for a year. That was the exchange rate. Nine lives of suspension for one life of waking. And waking was itself a gamble — the older the pod, the rougher the revival, and these pods were older than nations.

Ryn had filled six pages with variations and every one of them ended in the same place: you could wake some, or keep all, but you could not do both, and doing nothing was also a choice, the worst one, dressed up as patience.

That was before the fleet. Now the pages had a second column.

Because here was what Ryn understood, lying in the hammock with the printouts fanned around them like tarot cards: the fleets weren't coming to help. The families wanted their charter share. The syndicate wanted labor's share. The Church wanted the covenant's share. None of them had come with spare reactors. None of them had come with anything except claims. And when they arrived — forty hours, thirty-six now — they would board a dying ship with eleven thousand sleepers and begin dividing a number that did not divide.

Unless someone changed the number.

Ryn sat up. The idea had been circling for two days, and it landed now with the weight of something inevitable: the manifest. The true crew manifest, the election records, the fragments scattered through two hundred years of smuggled wafers — Ivo had assembled nearly all of it, spread across the archivist's table in the great hall like a jigsaw finally confessing its picture. And buried in the technical annexes, in the boring plumbing of the mission plan, was a section Ryn had read four times:

Emergency Power Reallocation Protocols. Authored by the Meridian's chief engineer. Ratified by pre-launch referendum.

If the protocols could be authenticated — if the council named in the log could be woken under their own authority — then the reallocation would be legal, legitimate, *binding*. No faction could contest it without contesting the colonists' own signatures. The number would change. The arithmetic would belong to the sleepers.

Or the fleets would arrive first, and the manifest would become a bargaining chip, and Ryn already knew — had known since the Drowned Deck split, since the night they'd drafted the offer to the governing families in secret — which side of that line they stood on.

"I'm sorry," Ryn said quietly, to the empty bay, to the conduits humming their long decline. It was the same apology they'd been rehearsing for days. It didn't get easier. It got heavier, the way ballast does.

Then the floor moved.

Not shook — *moved*, the way a ship moves when the sea remembers it. A slow lateral drift, half a meter, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. The printouts slid. The hammock swung. And Ryn, who had spent nineteen days learning the Meridian's every groan and tick, felt the wrongness of it in their teeth before their mind caught up.

Gravity didn't fail. Gravity *stuttered.*

They knew that stutter. Everyone in the system knew that stutter. Nine seconds of it, nineteen days ago, recorded on a thousand instruments, argued over in a thousand bars.

Ryn grabbed the intercom. "Ivo! Get to the bridge — it's happening *again* —"

* * *

On Halcyon Station, Inspector Vess Oka was awake because she was always awake now, because sleep had become a country she could visit but not live in.

She stood in Customs Control with the night shift around her and the great tactical display painting the system in amber threat-lines, and when the alert came in she read it twice before her body believed it.

GRAVITATIONAL ANOMALY — SYSTEM-WIDE — IN PROGRESS

The station lurched. Coffee rose off desks in brown ropes. A junior officer floated past her, eyes wide, arms pinwheeling, and Oka caught his ankle and hauled him down as the nine seconds stretched — one, two, three — pens and data-slates and one unfortunate pastry orbiting the control room in a slow, absurd carousel — five, six — somewhere below, ten thousand dockworkers clinging to rails, and Oka thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *exactly nine. Count it. Count it, count it—*

Eight. Nine.

Gravity returned like a tide. Everything crashed down at once — cups, slates, people, dignity. Alarms bloomed across the display. And in the center of the board, where the beacon's icon had burned steady and unremarkable for years, a signal trace erupted: a transmission burst, high-amplitude, wide-spectrum, aimed outward at the dark ship and inward at Halcyon simultaneously.

Oka pulled the waveform before anyone else could. She laid it beside the archived record of the first anomaly — the one she'd spent nineteen days failing to explain, the one that had started all of this, the one that had led her to three generations of Iversens and a beacon with no paperwork.

The signatures matched. Not similar. *Matched.* Down to the harmonic structure, down to the modulation pattern, down to a tiny phase irregularity that no natural phenomenon produced twice.

"Same source," said her analyst, Ferro, pale. "Inspector, that's the same *event.* Same emitter. Same—" He stopped, because he'd seen it too.

"It's the ship," Oka said. "Both times. Both times it was the ship." Her voice sounded foreign to her. "Nineteen days ago the Meridian's reactor hiccuped, and the beacon relayed it, and we treated it like a crime scene. We locked down the station. We hunted a saboteur." She laughed — one dry note, no humor in it. "We investigated a drowning man for splashing."

Ferro was pulling the deep archive now, the old survey records, the ones predating Halcyon's founding charter. "Inspector — if the beacon's been tied to the Meridian's drive signature... and if the beacon's been transmitting on schedule..." His fingers stopped moving. He stared at the screen.

"How long?" Oka asked, though she already knew. She had traced the maintenance logs back three generations. She had held the oldest entry in her hands, in an Iversen's careful handwriting.

"Every survey that caught this frequency band shows it," Ferro said. "Going back. All the way back." He looked up. "Two hundred years, Inspector. It's been transmitting for two hundred years."

The control room was chaos around them — damage reports, injury counts, the families' fleet accelerating, the syndicate's, the Church's — but Oka heard none of it. She was standing in a memory: the beacon's maintenance shack, the decades of logs, the marginal notes in different hands across a century. *Coupling replaced. Antenna re-tuned. Still humming. Kept the light on.*

Three generations of smugglers, climbing that ladder, year after year, keeping alive a transmitter they could not have understood — because the woman who did understand it had died before she could explain, or chosen not to, or decided that an errand was kinder than a burden.

Sefa Iversen had escaped a dying ship carrying the truth in sealed wafers, and she had left behind a lighthouse, and she had trusted her blood to keep it burning until someone came who could read the light.

"They weren't maintaining a beacon," Oka said. "They were keeping a promise." She turned to Ferro, and the professional suspicion that had driven her for nineteen days — that had curdled into obsession, that had put Ivo Iversen in an interrogation room over tea — burned away all at once, leaving something rawer underneath. "Get me the fleet channels. All of them. And get me a line to the Meridian. If those ships reach her before the truth does, there won't be a station left to swear an oath to."

* * *

Ivo reached the bridge as the last tremor died, Ryn a step behind, and found Keeper's displays already rewritten — the AI had opened everything, every internal sensor feed, every diagnostic, arranged in a pattern Ivo recognized now as the AI's version of urgency.

"Explain," Ivo said.

"At 0417 ship-time, the reactor underwent a secondary cascade event. Drive mass fluctuated for nine point zero one seconds. The event was transmitted, per protocol, via the relay network terminating at your beacon." Keeper paused. "You have questions."

"One question." Ivo's hands were shaking. They let them. "The first event. Nineteen days ago. Was it a malfunction?"

"No."

"Was it random?"

"No."

"Then what was it?"

The bridge lights dimmed and brightened — the AI thinking, or composing, or gathering courage it had been manufactured without. When Keeper spoke, its flat voice carried something that in a human would have been called grief.

"My instructions require me to broadcast distress at maximum available amplitude whenever reactor decay crosses defined thresholds. For one hundred ninety-seven years, my transmitters operated at fractions of design capacity. The relay chain between myself and inhabited space was severed — deliberately, shortly after launch, by parties unknown. I could whisper. No one could hear." Another pause. "Nineteen days ago, my decay crossed a threshold that permitted a full-power pulse through residual coupling in the relay array. Your beacon received it. Your beacon amplified it. Your beacon — maintained, repaired, and kept operational by your family for three generations — was the only functioning node in a chain that everyone else abandoned."

Ivo closed their eyes.

There it was. The whole shape of it, terrible and clean: the beacon screaming for two hundred years into a colony that had struck it from the record, that had betrayed the ship it was built to welcome home, that had let the lighthouse go dark in every way that mattered except the one a family of smugglers refused to allow. Nine seconds of gravity-failure hadn't been a catastrophe. It had been a voice, finally loud enough, finally heard — and the world had responded by locking down customs and hunting the messenger.

"We thought it was an attack," Ryn said hoarsely. "Halcyon thought someone had done something *to* it."

"Something was done to it," Keeper said. "One hundred ninety-seven years ago. By your ancestors' government, according to the records you recovered. The beacon has been reporting the crime ever since." A beat. "No one investigated until you."

Ivo walked to the main display. Below them, the sleepers. Beyond them, the fleets, closing. Between, the void where a signal had traveled for two centuries, patient as frost.

"Keeper. If the reactor decays past the next threshold — what happens?"

"A third pulse. Stronger. And within seventy-two hours of that, cascade failure. Total loss of cryo-banks. Total loss of the drive mass." The AI let the silence sit for exactly one second. "The final distress call will be the loudest thing this system has ever heard. It will be heard by everyone. It will help no one."

Ivo looked at Ryn. Ryn looked back, and Ivo saw the guilt there, the bargain still folded away behind their partner's eyes like contraband — and beneath the guilt, something else: the same terrible understanding, arriving in Ryn at the same moment.

The beacon had been asking for help for two hundred years. The help had finally come, and it was three armed fleets and a handful of thieves, and every hour that passed brought the arithmetic closer to its cruelest term.

"Then we don't wait for the fleets," Ivo said. "We don't wait for anyone. Keeper — pull up the reallocation protocols. Pull up the election records. Pull up everything Grandmother smuggled." They set their palm flat on the console, on the ship's cold skin, as if taking a pulse. "The sleepers voted on this before they ever boarded. It's time somebody honored the ballot."

Ryn went white. "Ivo — wait — there's something you need to know first. About what I did. About the families—"

Above them, faint through the wounded hull, the stars wheeled slowly past the frost — and among them, growing brighter hour by hour, the running lights of everyone who had ever wanted a share of the dying.

"There's no time," Ivo said. "Tell me while we work."
