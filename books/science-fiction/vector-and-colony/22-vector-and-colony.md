## 22. Vector and Colony

The wafer press hummed like a sleeping animal, and I had learned to sleep beside it the way sailors sleep beside engines — not because the sound is pleasant, but because its silence would mean something worse.

Six months out from Halcyon. The Meridian's repaired drive burned a pale, stubborn violet in the observation blister behind me, and every hour of that burn was an argument the ship was winning against its own history. Two hundred years dark, forty days of dying arithmetic, one desperate week of waking — and now this: a hull older than the station I'd been born under, pushing itself toward a moon that didn't yet have a name anyone agreed on. The council had shortlisted eleven. They were arguing about it in the wardroom right now. I could hear them through three bulkheads, which told you everything about the state of the Meridian's soundproofing and nothing about the state of their deliberations, which had not changed since the day I woke them.

I turned back to the press. Forty fresh wafers, each the size of my thumbnail, each holding what my grandmother had once scattered across a system in the hope that someone like me would carry them without knowing why.

I pressed the seal. The machine exhaled. Wafer forty-one dropped into the tray with a sound like a coin landing on velvet.

* * *

You'd think writing a true log would be easy. The truth, after all, had already happened. All I had to do was arrange it.

It was nothing of the kind.

The complete mission log — all ninety-one wafers' worth, recovered, decrypted, cross-checked against the Meridian's own damaged archives — ran to eleven thousand hours of record. Launch footage. Council minutes from a world that no longer existed, a world called Vey that my grandmother had never once mentioned by name, as if the name itself were contraband. The sleepers' voices: arguments, jokes, a woman named Sefa Anand singing to her daughter's pod because the daughter was six and frightened of the dark and cryo-sleep was the longest dark of all. The contingency vote, taken eleven days before launch, when 4,112 people had agreed on exactly what should happen if the ship faltered and the colony they were leaving had forgotten them.

And the betrayal. That too. The first colonial government's decision, three years after landing, to strike the beacon from the record and let the Meridian stay lost — because a dead ship was a martyr, and a living one was a rival, and 4,112 sleepers in the outer dark were easier to mythologize than to feed.

I wrote all of it. I wrote it plainly, without adjectives where the record could carry the weight, and where the record couldn't — where there was nothing but frost on a face and a number in a ledger — I wrote the number and let the frost stand for itself.

Ryn found me at hour nine of the fourth day, when I was arguing with a sentence about the reactor failure and losing.

"You're writing it like a customs report," they said, dropping into the chair across from me. Their shoulder still moved stiffly. The bullet had gone through muscle and out the other side, and the Meridian's autodocs had done their best, but Ryn's best and the ship's best were two different things, and the gap between them lived in the way Ryn reached for things now, a half-second late, always with the left hand.

"It *is* a customs report," I said. "It's the report customs should have filed two hundred years ago."

"It's a eulogy. Let it breathe."

"It's a *log*. Grandmother would—"

"Your grandmother," Ryn said, gently, the way you'd set down something fragile, "seeded a system with contraband and let her own granddaughter carry it for six years without telling her. I loved the woman's work. I wouldn't take her editing advice."

I looked at the sentence I'd been strangling. *The reactor's containment lattice failed at 0300 ship-time due to a manufacturing defect identified and overruled during construction.* Overruled. Someone had known, before launch, and launched anyway, because the window to Vey's system was closing and the arithmetic of departure is its own kind of cruelty. I had been trying to make that sentence neutral. It refused to be neutral. It was a sentence about people who had decided that four thousand sleepers were an acceptable risk, and no amount of passive voice would make it otherwise.

I let it breathe. I wrote: *They knew, and they went.*

Ryn read it over my shoulder and said nothing, which from Ryn was applause.

* * *

The scattering was my idea, and I will admit it was a borrowed one.

Every week, the Meridian's forward mass driver launched a probe — navigation telemetry, hull status, a greeting in eleven languages for whatever might be listening ahead of us. Councilor Anand, Sefa's descendant, elected chair by the narrowest of margins and the widest of tempers, had approved the probes as a matter of protocol. *A ship that arrives unannounced*, she said, *arrives as a threat.*

I asked her for one more kilogram of payload per probe.

She looked at me for a long moment. She had her ancestor's face, or close enough that seeing her was sometimes like seeing the log move and speak. "Wafers," she said.

"Wafers."

"Full log? The betrayal too? The vote? The singing?"

"Full log. Unedited. That was the whole point of everything."

"The factions on Halcyon will—"

"The factions on Halcyon," I said, "can read what the sleepers decided, in the sleepers' own words, eleven days before they ever left. That's not a weapon. That's the opposite of a weapon. That's the thing nobody on Halcyon ever had." I thought of Vess Oka, and her tea, and the way she'd said *I've been reading your family for three years and I only ever found errands.* "They had a myth. Myths don't vote."

Anand signed the authorization. She had a bureaucrat's instinct for documents that outlast the people who sign them, and I think she understood, better than any of us, that the log was the ship's real cargo — that the Meridian had always been carrying itself, in pieces, ahead of its own hull.

So every week, a probe went ahead of us at higher velocity than the ship could ever manage, carrying forty wafers in a hardened shell, tumbling toward the moon, toward the system's outer comets, toward the dark between — seeded along our vector like a trail of bread crumbs made of truth. If we made it, the wafers would already be there, waiting, evidence of our arrival filed before our arrival. If we didn't, they would be all that was left, and they would be enough. I knew they would be enough, because I had spent six years being changed by wafers I didn't know I was carrying. That was the whole design. That had always been the whole design.

Grandmother, I thought, you magnificent, unforgivable woman. You didn't smuggle memories. You planted a forest and hired a courier who didn't know the route.

* * *

We passed the beacon on the 190th day.

It wasn't our beacon — ours was still back at Halcyon's margin, in Oka's hands now, its commissioning records restored by council decree and its maintenance logs, all three generations of Iversens, entered into the public archive under her name as witness. This was a new one, dropped by the Meridian's advance probes at the system's midpoint: a lighthouse for a world that had never been charted, broadcasting the approach corridor, the moon's ephemeris, and — at Anand's insistence — the first wafer of the log on open channel, repeating, patient, addressed to no one and therefore to everyone.

I took the watch alone. Some things you don't share.

The beacon hung against the black like a struck match, and I thought about the first time I'd flown out to the old one, six years ago, with a forged maintenance charter and a hold full of other people's stolen afternoons. I had told myself then — I had *known* — that I kept it out of love, though I couldn't have said love of what. A machine with no paperwork. A light with no owner. An errand with no end.

It had been love of the right thing all along. I just hadn't known whose.

"Beacon's answering," the comms officer said over the intercom, and I laughed — actually laughed, alone in the blister, because of course it was. Of course it answered. It had been built by the same hands, commissioned by the same planners, struck from the same record, and restored by the same stubborn inheritance. It answered the way my grandmother had answered, the way the wafers had answered, the way the Meridian had answered through nine seconds of failing gravity: *I am here. I was always here. You were the one who forgot.*

I keyed the channel. "Meridian actual to beacon. Confirm reception."

The beacon's reply came back in the AI's voice — the caretaker, the one who had kept faith with its dead instructions for two centuries and had asked me, in the frost and the dark, to choose. It had a new instruction now, written by a council of seven and ratified by a ship. It sounded, if a voice can sound, relieved.

"Reception confirmed. Vector locked. Colony, you are expected."

* * *

Ryn found me there at the end of the watch, and we didn't talk about the bargain, because we had talked about it enough — in the frozen corridor with the frost on the sleeping faces, in the med bay, in the long slow months after, when forgiveness and understanding had circled each other like ships at matching velocity, neither quite docking. What we had arrived at was not the old thing. The old thing was gone; the bullet had seen to that, and the bargain, and the truth. What we had was the new thing, which was smaller and stranger and, I had come to believe, load-bearing: two people who had each seen the other's worst arithmetic and stayed to check the figures.

"Anand wants you at the wardroom," Ryn said. "The moon vote."

"Eleven names?"

"Down to three. She wants the archivist's opinion on which one goes in the log first."

"Tell her the log takes all three. The log takes everyone. That's the rule now."

Ryn grinned. "You've become insufferable."

"I've become *my grandmother*," I said, and we both let that sit for a second, because it was true and because it no longer hurt the way it had, that first day in the Drowned Deck, when I'd opened a wafer of my own cargo and watched a launch two centuries old and understood that my whole life had been a delivery.

The moon — they'd settled on a provisional name, *Vey-Second*, which everyone hated and everyone used — hung ahead of us now, close enough that the probes we'd scattered were beginning to reach its orbit, close enough that its nightside showed a faint, impossible shimmer: ice, catching the drive-light of forty little messengers burning up gently in the upper atmosphere, delivering themselves.

I thought about the first line I'd ever written in the old beacon's maintenance log, back when I was a smuggler with a forged charter and no idea what I was carrying: *Beacon operational. No further action required.*

I'd been wrong. There was always further action. There was always another wafer, another errand, another light to keep for someone who hadn't been born yet. That was the inheritance, and this time I had chosen it, and choosing it had turned out to be the only difference that mattered — between an errand and a vocation, between a smuggler and an archivist, between a lie you maintain and a truth you tend.

I pressed the last wafer of the day. Wafer fifty-two. The true log, complete, unedited, singing to its daughter across the dark.

Then I sealed the tray, and keyed the mass driver, and sent it ahead of us into the black — another beacon, another reason nobody remembers yet.
