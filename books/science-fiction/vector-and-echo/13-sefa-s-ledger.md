## 13. Sefa's Ledger

The *Kestrel's Debt* smelled of engine grease and dried fish, and Sefa Okonkwo had not slept in thirty hours.

She stood in the narrow galley that served as her wardroom, watching the woman from the insurance company through the hatchway glass. Zephyrine Dunmore sat at the bolted table with her hands wrapped around a mug of recaf she hadn't touched, and beside her, handcuffed to the table leg by his own request — a precaution, he'd said, so your people don't shoot me on reflex — sat Toma Reyes, the Concord navigator who had once been a boy in a white uniform and was now something smaller and more honest.

Her privateers had taken the hauler forty minutes out of the debris shoal. Clean boarding, no shots fired, mostly because Dunmore had ordered her own pilot to stand down before Sefa's breaching team even had their cutters charged. That had told Sefa something. People with guilty consciences ran. This woman had opened the airlock herself.

"Captain." Her first mate, Yaw, leaned close, voice pitched under the recirculation hum. "The Concord patrol's transponder ping went out an hour ago. Fleet will know by now. We should be burning for Anchor space while we still have a lead."

"We're not running yet." Sefa pushed off the bulkhead. "Get me the ledger."

Yaw went still. "You sure?"

"I'm sure."

He hesitated a moment longer — long enough that she knew he understood what she meant to do with it — and then he left, boots ringing down the companionway.

Sefa entered the galley alone. The two prisoners looked up. The Concord man flinched; she'd expected that. Free Anchor colors did that to Concord officers, and vice versa. Eleven years of frozen lanes and cold hatred had trained both fleets to see each other as weather systems rather than people — fronts moving on a chart, inevitable, impersonal.

But it was the other one Sefa cared about. Zephyrine Dunmore. The name had gone through Sefa like a fishhook when Yaw read it off the hauler's manifest. Dunmore. She'd turned the name over during the whole approach burn, tasting it, and by the time her boarding team sealed the airlock she had already decided what this meeting was going to be.

She pulled out the chair across from Dunmore and sat, laying her sidearm on the table between them, deliberately casual, deliberately visible.

"You're the daughter," Sefa said.

Dunmore's face did something complicated. Not surprise — she'd been recognized before, clearly — but a kind of bracing, the way a hull braces against pressure. "I'm an investigator for Halcyon Mutual. I was tracking a manifest anomaly—"

"You're Ilsa Dunmore's daughter." Sefa let the silence sit. "Don't waste my air pretending otherwise. Your mother captained the ship that killed my brother."

The recaf mug trembled, just slightly, in Dunmore's grip. "Your brother was Meridian Vale crew?"

"My brother was *murdered* by Meridian Vale crew, if you believe the Concord version. Or he was a hero of the Free Anchor martyred by Concord treachery, if you believe ours." Sefa smiled without any warmth in it. "Funny thing about my brother. He never served either fleet when he was alive."

Toma Reyes made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

Sefa turned to look at him. "You know something, navigator?"

"I know—" He stopped. Started again. "I was a junior sensor officer the night it happened. I've spent eleven years knowing the official story was wrong. I didn't know how it was wrong." His eyes flicked to Dunmore. "Until recently."

"Then you'll both want to see this."

Yaw returned and set a slate on the table — an old one, its casing scarred and resealed, the kind of device that got handed down because no one could afford to replace what still worked. Sefa woke it with her thumbprint and turned it toward the others.

"This is the Free Anchor casualty ledger for the Meridian Vale. Every name we claim. Every family registered with the flotilla who lost someone aboard. It gets read aloud at the memorial every year. All four hundred and six names." She tapped the screen. "Read me the crew complement of the Vale as Concord recorded it."

Dunmore looked down. Sefa watched her lips move faintly, counting, the investigator's habit asserting itself over the grief. "Three hundred and eighty-one souls aboard, per the loss report. Concord registry."

"And yet our ledger carries four hundred and six names of the dead." Sefa leaned forward. "Twenty-five more than there were crew. For eleven years, the flotilla's historians have explained that as Concord falsifying the roster to hide foreign nationals aboard — smugglers, defectors, whoever suited the narrative of the week. And I imagine Concord has its own explanation for why our list doesn't match theirs."

"It does," Reyes said quietly. "They say Free Anchor inflated the count with fictional names to inflate the grievance."

"Fictional names." Sefa laughed, and it came out ugly. "Let me introduce you to a fictional name."

She scrolled the ledger with her thumb, past hundreds of entries, each with a portrait chip, a home station, a next-of-kin line. She stopped where she always stopped, though she hadn't looked at this page in three years, because looking at it was a wound you kept deciding to reopen or let close, and she'd never decided.

She turned the slate around.

The entry read: **OKONKWO, EMEKA J.** — Deckhand, Engineering Division, *Meridian Vale*. Born Ceres Anchorage, Free Anchor territory. Next of kin: Okonkwo, Sefa (sister).

"That's him," Sefa said. "That's my brother. Emeka. He was twenty-four. He fixed atmospheric scrubbers on fishing barges out of Ceres and he sang badly and he owed me money, which I forgave him the day they read his name at the memorial, because you forgive the dead everything." Her voice held level. She'd practiced holding it level for eleven years. "And according to every record Concord ever released, Emeka Okonkwo was never aboard that ship. No such crewman. So either my brother is alive somewhere, which would be a miracle I stopped praying for years ago, or he died on a ship that officially says he wasn't there."

Zephyrine Dunmore had gone very pale. She was staring at the entry — not at the name, Sefa realized, but at the division listing. *Engineering.*

"The maintenance logs," Dunmore murmured. "In the black-market archive. The service records for the Vale — they were signed off by engineering personnel. I assumed Brannoch worked alone, faking the entries. But the certification codes—" She looked up, and her eyes were wet and fierce at once. "These are real crew certifications. Free Anchor engineering guild marks. My mother's chief engineer couldn't have forged these. They're genuine."

"What does that mean?" Sefa demanded.

"It means—" Dunmore pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment, and when she lowered them she looked older, as if some load-bearing assumption inside her had given way. "It means the crew list was never just Concord personnel. It means my mother took aboard people from *both* fleets. Volunteers. Free Anchor engineers, Concord navigators, maybe independents. A mixed crew, on a ship that was supposed to be purely Concord registry."

"Why in God's name would anyone do that?"

Dunmore didn't answer immediately. Sefa watched her work it through — watched the same horror assemble itself behind the woman's eyes that Sefa could feel assembling behind her own.

"To make sure," Dunmore said finally, and her voice cracked on the second word. She steadied it. "To make sure that when the ship was lost, *everyone* mourned. Not just Concord. If the dead were only Concord dead, then Free Anchor grieves nothing and hates everything. But if the dead include twenty-five Free Anchor sons and daughters—" She gestured at the slate, at Emeka's face. "Then Free Anchor can't hate cleanly either. The grief crosses the lines. The war freezes, because neither fleet can turn its own dead into ammunition without desecrating them."

The galley was silent except for the recycler cycling.

Sefa stared at her brother's face on the slate. Twenty-four years old, caught mid-laugh in the portrait chip because he'd never once managed a serious photograph in his life. She thought of the memorial readings, year after year, the four hundred and six names rolling out over the anchorage crowds, and how the crowd always wept loudest at the unfamiliar names — the ones whose families were scattered, whose stories nobody knew. She'd assumed those were clerical ghosts. Fabrications. Padding for the grievance.

They'd been real. All of them. Real people who had volunteered — *volunteered* — to die anonymously aboard an enemy-flagged ship so that their deaths could hold two fleets apart like a hand flat on a table.

"He signed up," she said. Her voice sounded distant to her own ears. "Emeka signed up for this. He knew."

"You don't know that," Dunmore said gently. "There may have been deception—"

"There isn't." Sefa tapped the entry again, and the attached personal addendum unfolded — the last message Emeka had sent home, dated nine days before the loss, flagged by the flotilla censors for eleven years and released to next-of-kin only after a fight Sefa had personally led. *"Sef — taking a contract. Big money, big secret, can't say more. If anything happens, don't let them use me. Whatever they tell you I died doing, don't let them make a flag out of it. Promise me. — E."*

She'd thought it was smuggler paranoia. She'd thought he was embarrassed about the job, ashamed of it, running cargo for someone unsavory. She'd built eleven years of rage on top of that reading — rage at Concord, at the Vale, at the whole rotten architecture of the lanes — and her brother had stood in the middle of it all along, waving his arms, saying *don't, don't do this with my death,* and she hadn't heard him.

"Don't let them make a flag out of it," she repeated aloud. The words tasted like ash and like water at the same time.

Reyes had turned away, shoulders tight. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "Concord's casualty list has the mirror image. Twenty-five names that don't match the official complement. I saw the annex once, in the archives, when I was digging for the sensor data. Nobody ever explained it. The archivist told me it was a records error." He shook his head slowly. "It wasn't an error. It was the same design. Both fleets were given their own dead to bury. Both fleets were given a reason to freeze instead of fight."

"She planned it," Dunmore whispered. "Down to the mourning. Down to *this* — down to you, Captain Okonkwo, sitting here with that slate. She planned for a Free Anchor sister to one day sit across from her daughter and show her that face."

"Then she planned better than any admiral in either fleet," Sefa said, "and she killed my brother to do it."

The words came out harder than she intended, and she watched them land — watched Dunmore take the hit without flinching, which was somehow worse than if she'd flinched.

"Yes," Dunmore said quietly. "She did. And I don't know yet whether that makes her a monster or a saint, and I've spent the last month finding out that the answer might be both, and I am so tired, Captain, of everyone in this war treating those as different things."

Something in Sefa's chest shifted — not softened, exactly. Shifted. Like ballast settling.

She looked at the slate again. At Emeka laughing forever at twenty-four. At the line beneath his name: *Next of kin: Okonkwo, Sefa.* Eleven years she'd carried him as a weapon. Loaded him into every argument, every memorial, every bitter toast in every anchorage bar. *My brother died on that ship. Concord owes me. The universe owes me.*

And he had asked her — begged her — not to.

Outside the galley viewport, the slow stars of the smuggling circuit crawled past, indifferent. Somewhere behind them, a Concord patrol was reporting their position. Somewhere ahead, both fleets were beginning to move toward a wreck site that wasn't a wreck at all, and the truth in Sefa's hold was worth more than any cargo she'd ever carried, and more dangerous.

Yaw appeared at the hatchway, tablet in hand, and his expression told her before he spoke.

"Captain. Fleet broadcast on the open band. Both fleets." He swallowed. "Concord Third Squadron is mobilizing toward neutral space. Free Anchor response wing just cleared Ceres Anchorage. Everyone's converging on the shoal coordinates — and Command is asking us to confirm we have the vessel and its survivors in custody."

Sefa closed the slate over her brother's laughing face.

"They're not asking," she said. "They're claiming. Both of them. They want the ghost ship as evidence, and they want it before the other one gets there." She rose, tucking the slate into her jacket, against her heart, where she'd carried his memory for eleven years and would carry it a while longer. "Set course for the jump gate. Full burn."

"Direktor—" Yaw used her old rank, the way he did when he was scared. "Which fleet do we answer to?"

Sefa looked at Zephyrine Dunmore, who was watching her with something that wasn't hope exactly — hope required belief, and this woman had learned better — but was maybe its poor cousin. Recognition, perhaps. Two keepers of other people's dead.

"Neither," Sefa said. "We answer to the crew."

* * *

Later, in the quiet of the aft passage, Sefa found Zephyrine Dunmore standing at the viewport, watching the dark between the stars.

"My brother sent one message," Sefa said, coming up beside her. "Nine days before the end. Yours left recordings. Mine left a promise I broke for a decade." She exhaled slowly. "Tell me about the rest of them. The other twenty-four. Tell me who they were."

Dunmore was silent a moment. "Brannoch might know. He kept the ship; maybe he kept them too." Then, quieter: "There's something else. My mother left a second message. Sealed. Brannoch says it's marked *for when the lie starts killing.*"

Sefa felt the cold arrive ahead of the understanding, the way it always did before bad news. She looked out at the black, where two fleets were even now turning toward the same point in empty space, each carrying its ledger of the dead like a torch.

"Then it's started killing," she said. "Question is what it kills next."

Ahead, the nav display chimed: jump window closing in fifty-one hours.
