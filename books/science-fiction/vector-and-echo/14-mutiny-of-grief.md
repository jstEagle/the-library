## 14. Mutiny of Grief

The *Kestrel's Lament* smelled of engine grease and old grief.

Zephyrine felt it the moment she came aboard — not in any single detail but in the accumulation: the memorial strip bolted beside the galley hatch, twenty-three names etched into salvaged hull plating; the faded Free Anchor pennant hanging limp in recycled air; the way the crew looked at her when she crossed the mess, their eyes moving from her face to the data chip in her jacket pocket as if they could see through the fabric to the recording inside.

Sefa Okonkwo had given them quarters — a real gesture, Zephyrine understood, and a cage with soft walls. The privateer captain had lost a brother to the Meridian Vale, or so she'd said on the boarding deck, her voice level in the way that voices go level when the alternative is breaking. She'd let Zephyrine keep the chip. She'd let Brannoch keep his engines. But the *Lament* was running a course Sefa chose, toward coordinates Sefa kept, and every hour that passed tightened the circle another degree.

Now the whole crew stood in the mess hall, and the circle was closing.

* * *

It began quietly, the way mutinies do — not with drawn weapons but with a woman setting down her cup too carefully.

Her name was Idris Vane, and Zephyrine had catalogued her over two days of forced observation: gunner, senior by service if not by rank, a broad-shouldered woman whose hands were scarred in the pattern of someone who'd spent years handling charge casings. She set the cup down, aligned its handle with the table edge, and said, "Captain. We've been patient."

Sefa stood at the head of the table, arms folded. "Idris."

"Two days we've had that ship in our hold — our *tow*, at least. Two days we've known where the Meridian Vale is, and what it is, and who's been keeping it flying." Vane's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. "And we're still running dark along the drift like smugglers instead of broadcasting the find to every Anchor ship in range."

"Because broadcasting it hands Concord exactly the provocation they've wanted for eleven years," Sefa said.

"Or because you haven't decided yet whose side the evidence serves." Vane turned then, slowly, and her gaze found Zephyrine. "You. The adjuster. You've heard the recording. The old man played it for you in the wreck's belly while the rest of us stood watch outside like children sent from the room." A ripple of assent moved through the gathered crew — maybe thirty of them, filling the mess shoulder to shoulder. "So tell us. What does the black box say? Who killed our people?"

Zephyrine felt the weight of the chip against her sternum like a second heart.

She had rehearsed this, in the sleepless hours between watches — versions of the truth arranged and rearranged like cargo in an unbalanced hold. But rehearsal dissolved now, under thirty pairs of eyes, into something rawer. These were not abstract stakeholders. They were people who had named their dead, who wore those names on a strip of plating by the galley, who had built their lives inside a grievance the way sailors build lives inside a hull.

"The recording doesn't say what you think it says," she began.

"Then say what it does say."

"It says the Meridian Vale was destroyed deliberately. By her own captain." The murmur that went through the mess was low and ugly, like a hull taking stress. "Ilsa Dunmore. My mother. She scuttled her own ship, with her crew's consent, to stop a war — because both fleets were maneuvering toward open conflict over the Meridian corridor, and she believed only a shared tragedy could freeze them in place. Your brother didn't die to Concord treachery, Ms. Okonkwo. He died a volunteer in the only peace either fleet has ever managed."

Silence. Then Vane laughed — one short, disbelieving bark.

"A volunteer." She looked around the mess, inviting the others to share the absurdity. "Eleven years we've mourned them as victims. Eleven years the Anchor has built its spine on their graves. And this woman — this *stranger*, daughter of the woman who killed them — wants us to believe they lined up to die for a truce nobody asked for?"

"Ask the casualty list," Zephyrine said. "Your own records. Ask why Free Anchor names appear on a Concord-flagged crew roster. Ask why Concord names appear on ships your fleet chartered. My mother built that crew from both sides on purpose. So that everyone would mourn. So that no one could claim the dead belonged to them alone."

"That's not mercy," someone said from the back — a young man, engineering division, his jaw tight. "That's theft. She stole our grief and used it as a shield."

"Yes," Zephyrine said, because lying to them would be worse than cruelty. "She did. And it worked. There has been no war for eleven years. The jump lanes froze, trade starved, families stayed separated — but no war. Not until someone started servicing a ghost ship, and a claims adjuster started asking questions, and now both fleets are racing to a jump gate to decide what your brother died of." She pulled the chip from her pocket and held it up between finger and thumb, small and dull in the mess lighting. "This is the only copy of her confession. It exonerates both fleets completely. It also names her as the saboteur. Play it for Free Anchor command, and they'll have proof Concord didn't kill your people — and proof that the founding grievance of the last decade was staged. Do you honestly believe they'll file it away gently? Or will they broadcast it as proof that Concord's entire moral architecture is a lie, and use the outrage to justify everything they've wanted to do since the disaster?"

"You don't know that," Vane said.

"I'm a claims investigator. All I know is liability." Zephyrine's hand wasn't quite steady; she let them see it. "But I know what happens when grief gets handed a weapon. It stops being grief."

* * *

Toma Reyes spoke from the doorway.

Zephyrine hadn't heard him come in — he moved quietly for a big man, a habit she suspected predated his disgrace. He stood with his back to the bulkhead, and when he pushed off it and stepped into the light, half the mess stiffened. They knew what he was. Sefa had made sure of that within an hour of boarding him: *Concord navigator, drummed out, trust him as far as the airlock.*

"Listen to her," Toma said. "Not because she's right about everything. Because she's the only person in this room who's listened to the whole recording."

"And you'd vouch for her judgment, Concord?" Vane's lip curled. "Maybe you'd like to argue the other direction. Maybe the chip should go to Concord command, since you're so fond of—"

"I'll argue something better," Toma said. "I'll argue that neither of you should touch it." He walked the length of the mess slowly, and Zephyrine watched the crew watch him — suspicion warring with something else, something hungrier. He stopped across the table from Vane. "I was a junior sensor officer the night the Vale burned. I saw the jump signature after the explosion was logged. I reported it. It disappeared. I spent nine years believing Concord buried the truth because Concord was guilty." He let that sit. "I was wrong about the guilt. I wasn't wrong about the burial. Both fleets buried it, Ms. Vane. Both fleets needed the martyr more than they needed the truth. If you hand that chip to Anchor command tonight, do you know what happens? They verify it. They confirm it. And then they classify it — because a confirmed lie is worth more to a fleet than a confirmed truth. The grievance is their fuel. You'd be handing them a decade of fuel and asking them politely not to burn it."

"Then give it to the public. Broadcast it wide."

"To whom?" Toma spread his hands. "The colonies get their news through fleet channels. The independents get it through Anchor couriers. There is no public anymore — there are two audiences, and each one hears only what its fleet allows. Broadcast the truth and each side will edit it into ammunition before the echo fades. Concord will hear 'Anchor staged the disaster.' Anchor will hear 'Concord admits nothing.' I've seen how these machines grind, and I served inside one."

"So we do nothing?" The young engineer again, his voice cracking. "We just let my father stay a name on a plaque forever?"

"No," Zephyrine said. "We decide what he becomes."

Every face turned to her.

"He can stay a victim, and feed a war. He can become evidence, and feed a fleet. Or—" she closed her hand around the chip, "—he can become what my mother intended. A reason neither side can pick up a weapon without being ashamed. That's not nothing. That's the hardest thing there is. Shame is the only leash that's ever held on armed men."

Vane stared at her for a long moment. Then she turned to Sefa.

"Captain. You've heard enough. We all have. The question isn't what the recording says anymore. The question is who holds it." Her voice dropped, and the drop was worse than shouting. "And I say it shouldn't be the daughter of the woman who murdered our crew. No offense meant, adjuster. But you love her. Love bends testimony. Every court in human space knows it."

"She's not wrong," Toma said quietly, and Zephyrine shot him a look, and he met it without flinching. "About the bias. Not about the answer."

"Then what's your answer, navigator?"

"Neither fleet. Neither family. Neither of us." Toma looked around the mess. "A third party. Someone with no stake in either flag. Someone whose entire profession is impartial assessment of loss." He paused, and Zephyrine saw the trap close a full second before he finished the sentence. "Halcyon Mutual. Let the claim be adjudicated. Let the record be filed where both fleets' lawyers can tear at it equally. Neutral ground."

"Halcyon is Concord-chartered," Vane snapped.

"Halcyon pays Concord claims every quarter. It pays ours too. It's the only institution both fleets trust precisely because it belongs to neither." Toma turned to Zephyrine, and his eyes were apologetic and absolutely steady. "File it, Zephyrine. Through channels. Let the machine do what machines do best — make the truth boring. Boring truths don't start wars."

* * *

Later — much later, after the mess had emptied in clusters of muttered conversation, after Vane and three others had asked Sefa formally for a course change to the nearest Anchor relay and been refused, after the air in the *Lament* had gone taut as a mooring line in a storm — Zephyrine found Sefa alone on the aft observation deck.

The captain stood at the viewport, watching the debris shoal fall away behind them, the Meridian Vale still visible as a glint among the wreckage — Brannoch had insisted on towing her rather than abandoning her, and the *Lament*'s grapples groaned under the dead weight of a living ghost.

"They'll try to take it from me," Sefa said, without turning. "Vane and hers. Tonight or tomorrow. They won't hurt anyone — Vane's not that kind — but they'll lock the bridge, cut comms, and plot a course for Anchor space while the rest of us sleep."

"You could give it to them."

"I could." Sefa's reflection in the glass was unreadable. "My brother is on that plaque. Do you know what I thought when you told me the truth? I thought: eleven years I hated Concord, and the hate was the last thing he left me. And you want me to put it down." She turned. Her eyes were dry and very tired. "Here's what I've decided, adjuster. I've decided I don't trust myself. I don't trust Vane. I don't trust the navigator, and I surely don't trust Halcyon Mutual, which once denied my mother's death benefit on a technicality." She held out her open hand. "Give me the chip."

Zephyrine didn't move.

"If I give it to you, it becomes Free Anchor property. Property gets deployed."

"If you keep it, it becomes Dunmore property. Blood gets protected." Sefa's hand stayed open. "There's no clean pair of hands on this ship. Choose the least dirty."

For a long moment Zephyrine stood with the chip burning against her palm through her pocket, and she thought of her mother's voice in the dark of the wreck — *keep the lie, or end it cleanly, never half* — and she understood, finally, that her mother had never imagined this: a ship full of grieving people, each one certain that the truth belonged to them, each one honest, each one willing to break the galaxy a little to honor their dead.

"There's a third option," she said slowly. "One you haven't considered. One my mother left instructions for."

Sefa's hand lowered. "What kind of instructions?"

"Sealed ones. For Brannoch. 'For when the lie starts killing.'" Zephyrine held the older woman's gaze. "He hasn't opened them. I think it's time he did. Because the lie isn't killing anyone yet — but your crew is about to start, and my mother planned for exactly this kind of fracture. Whatever's in that message, she wrote it knowing we'd be standing exactly where we're standing."

Sefa studied her for a long moment, and something shifted behind her exhaustion — not hope, maybe, but the memory of it.

"And if her instructions are wrong?"

"Then we'll know we tried her way first," Zephyrine said. "And if her way fails, the chip goes to whoever's still standing. Fair warning, Captain — I intend to make sure that's a lot of people."

Behind them, down the corridor, a compartment door slammed, and footsteps began moving fast toward the bridge — and Sefa's hand went to her comm, and the *Kestrel's Lament* woke around them like a ship coming under fire.
