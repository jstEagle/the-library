## 20. Standdown

Sefa Okonkwo had spent eleven years learning how to hold a weapon steady while her hands shook.

She'd learned it the night the casualty list came through, learned it at her brother's empty funeral where they buried a data chip because there was no body, learned it through a decade of patrols along a frozen lane where every Concord hull on the scope was a reason and every Free Anchor order to hold position was an insult. Her hands were shaking now — she could feel the tremor in the grip of the rail pistol she still hadn't holstered — but the weapon itself was steady, aimed at nothing, because for the first time in eleven years there was nothing left to aim at.

The Meridian Vale hung between two fleets like a held breath.

From the bridge of the *Kite's Mercy*, Sefa watched it drift in the wash of the jump gate's field lights: a scarred freighter, hull pitted with eleven years of debris-shoal weathering, running lights burning amber and green as though it had somewhere ordinary to be. Behind it, forty Concord warships in wedge formation. Ahead, thirty-one Free Anchor hulls flying her own colors. Between them all, a dead woman's voice still echoing off every comm channel, replaying now on open broadcast because no one had ordered it stopped and no one dared.

*"...I am Captain Ilsa Dunmore, master of the vessel you believe you avenged. I am also the person who destroyed it."*

Sefa had heard it four times now. It did not get easier. The third time, she'd stopped hearing it as evidence and started hearing it as a eulogy, and that was worse, because eulogies required grief and she had been spending hers as fuel for so long she wasn't sure any was left over for mourning.

"Captain." Her first officer, Wren, stood at the tactical station with her hand hovering over the fire-control board like a woman afraid to touch it. "Concord flagship is hailing. Not us. All channels."

"Put it through."

Admiral Cassian Voss's face resolved above the plot table — silver-haired, granite-jawed, the face that had been printed on recruitment posters for a decade under the slogan *NEVER FORGOTTEN*. He looked, Sefa thought, like a man who had been forgotten by his own story and only just found out.

"This is Admiral Voss, commanding the Concord Third Fleet." His voice carried the flat care of someone reading words he had rehearsed and hated. "In light of... testimony received on this channel, I am ordering all Concord batteries to stand down. Weapons cold. I say again: weapons cold." A pause, long enough that Sefa saw him swallow. "We came here to bury our dead. It appears we have been standing on their grave for eleven years, arguing about who dug it."

The channel stayed open. On the Free Anchor side, Fleetmother Adaeze Kwan's voice came through a half-second later, rougher, less rehearsed: "Free Anchor flotillas, stand down. All weapons to safe. That is not a request."

And then silence — the specific, ringing silence of thirty thousand people across seventy ships all listening to the same dead woman at once, none of them sure what they were allowed to feel about it.

* * *

Sefa walked her ship alone.

It was what she always did after action stations, the ritual that kept her crew from seeing whether their captain was made of iron or just shaped like it. Deck by deck, compartment by compartment, past gun crews sitting on their ammo crates with their helmets off, past the med bay where nobody was hurt because nobody had fired, past the mess where someone had put a pot of tea on out of pure reflex and now forty sailors stood around it not drinking.

On the aft observation deck she stopped, because that was where she always stopped, and looked out through the armored glass at the Vale.

"You knew," she said to it. To her brother, maybe. Emeka Okonkwo, second engineer, listed among the lost, mourned among the martyred, and — she was understanding this now, layer by layer, like peeling back scorched plating — *willing*. He had volunteered. They all had. Concord spacers and Free Anchor spacers and neutral-born crew, mixed together on one manifest so carefully that every fleet would have to count its own children among the dead.

Her mother had called it madness when the news broke. *They put our boy on that ship and blew it up,* she'd screamed at the memorial broadcasts, and Sefa had built ten years of hatred on top of that scream like keel plates on a frame.

Emeka had signed on. Emeka had known. There was probably a recording somewhere of his voice saying why, and Sefa found she couldn't yet decide whether she wanted to hear it or would rather die first.

The door behind her opened. She didn't turn. Only three people aboard had the codes, and two of them were supposed to be prisoners.

"He flew well," Toma Reyes said. "Your brother. Brannoch showed me the maintenance logs — Emeka rewired the number three power trunk himself, back before. Said he wanted the ship to be able to run dark if she ever had to." A pause. "I didn't know him. I'm sorry. I know that's worth nothing."

"It's worth something," Sefa said, surprising herself. She turned. The disgraced navigator looked exactly like what he was: a man who had been carrying a smaller version of her own weight for eleven years and had finally set it down and didn't know how to stand up straight without it. "You pointed guns at my people an hour ago."

"Yes."

"And I pointed them at yours."

"Yes."

Sefa looked back at the Vale. "My brother is on that ship," she said. "Not a body. But his work is. His hands are in those decks. Eleven years I thought the Concord burned him up, and instead he—" Her voice did something she hadn't authorized. She let it. "Instead he helped build the lie that kept the lanes frozen. And the lanes being frozen meant the mining colonies starved, and the relief convoys stopped, and my cousin's station went dark for two winters. So tell me, Navigator. Was it worth it? Did the peace weigh enough?"

Toma was quiet for a long time. Through the glass, the gate's field lights rippled blue across the Vale's scarred prow.

"I've asked myself that since the night I saw the sensor ghost," he said finally. "Every year, on the anniversary, I'd run the numbers again. What the war would have cost against what the freeze cost. I never got an answer I liked." He shook his head. "But I don't think it was a math problem to her. I think Captain Dunmore looked at two fleets loading their guns and decided somebody had to be the tragedy, and she'd rather it be her name than forty million people. You can call that wrong. I've tried. I keep failing."

Sefa's jaw worked. "My mother will never forgive it."

"No. Mine either, probably." Toma almost smiled. "But we're not our mothers' verdicts. We're just the ones who have to live next door to each other after."

* * *

The negotiation, such as it was, took place on no one's flag bridge and lasted forty minutes, which Sefa would later understand was the fastest settlement in the history of the Drift.

It happened because Zephyrine Dunmore refused to leave it to the admirals.

Sefa watched it unfold on the open channel — the claims adjuster, the quiet woman with her mother's manifest and her mother's spine, broadcasting from the Vale's own comm array. She did not ask the fleets to agree. She informed them of what the dead captain's log specified: joint custody. A permanent memorial station at the debris shoal, staffed by rotating crews from both fleets, the ship herself preserved as she was, unclaimed by either flag. Every name on the manifest read aloud, once a year, by officers of both services standing side by side.

"The alternative," Zephyrine said, and her voice was tired in a way that Sefa recognized as the tiredness of someone who had been awake for three days making decisions that belonged to other people, "is that you fight over her corpse in front of everyone you command, having just heard her explain what fighting costs. I don't think you'll do it. But it's your choice. She gave you both the same gift: the truth, and the room to decide what to do with it."

Voss answered first. "Concord accepts joint custody." Then, quieter, almost to himself: "It's more than we deserved."

Fleetmother Kwan took longer. When she spoke, her voice had gravel in it. "Free Anchor accepts. And I want it minuted — in our records, in theirs, everywhere — that the grievance is dissolved. Not settled. Dissolved. There is no one left to blame, and we will not manufacture a villain out of a woman who bought our peace with her own." A breath. "Let the record show the Meridian Vale's crew died for both of us. Let it show we noticed."

Sefa sat down on her own bridge chair and put her face in her hands, and for the first time since the casualty list arrived, she cried like a person and not like a captain.

* * *

They stood the watch together because it seemed impossible and therefore correct.

The joint custody protocol called for a combined honor detail aboard the Vale until the memorial station's first rotation arrived — two officers from each fleet, walking the decks of the ship both sides had spent a decade using as a wound to lick. Sefa volunteered before she fully understood why. Toma Reyes was already going; Brannoch had asked for him by name, the old engineer apparently having decided that anyone who'd seen the sensor ghost and kept silent out of guilt had served his sentence.

So it was that Sefa Okonkwo, captain of the Free Anchor privateer *Kite's Mercy*, killer of two Concord corvettes in the skirmish at Halcyon Reach — she wondered, walking the Vale's corridor beside Toma Reyes, whether he knew, whether the names of those corvettes' crews were carved into him the way Emeka's name was carved into her — stood watch in the engine room of the most famous ghost in the Drift, holding a lamp instead of a pistol.

Brannoch had the engine humming. Not running — *humming*, a low tidal sound, life support and gravity and the amber-green running lights, everything a ship needed to keep breathing without going anywhere. The old engineer moved through the compartment touching things as he passed: a conduit housing, a gauge cover, the frame of the number three trunk that Emeka Okonkwo had rewired a lifetime ago. He touched them the way you touch a sleeping animal you've raised.

"She'll want to stay warm," he said, catching Sefa looking. "Ships notice. People think they don't, but the good ones do." He nodded at the trunk. "Your brother's work. Still holding. Best wiring job I ever inspected, and I told him so, and he said—" the old man's voice caught, smoothed itself with visible effort, "—he said, 'Make it last, Chief. Somebody should get some use out of me.'"

Sefa put her hand flat against the trunk housing. It was warm. Eleven years of secret maintenance, of a dead man's solder joints kept alive by a dead man's friend, and it was warm under her palm like something sleeping.

"I used to talk to him," she said, not meaning to say it aloud. "At the memorial wall. Every visit. Told him about the war like he'd want to hear it. Like I was keeping score for him." She laughed, and it came out broken. "He'd have hated it. He'd have hated all of it. The freezing, the hating, the — he wired a ship so it could run dark, and we turned the whole lane dark for a decade and called it honoring him."

"People honor dead men badly," Brannoch said gently. "It's not malice. It's just that grief is heavy and blame is easy to carry it in." He looked up at the humming trunk. "That's why she did it the way she did. Took the blame onto herself so nobody else would have to invent it."

The watch bell rang the hour. Sefa and Toma walked their rounds together, two enemies in borrowed authority, checking seals and hatches on a ship that belonged to everyone and no one. In the galley they found the crew's last meal still set out under preservation film — eleven years old, untouched, plates laid for twenty-six. Neither of them suggested clearing it away.

In the corridor outside the captain's cabin, they paused. The plaque was already mounted — Zephyrine had brought it aboard in a case, and Brannoch had fixed it to the bulkhead with the reverence of a man setting a keel stone:

**ILSA DUNMORE**
CAPTAIN
*SHE CHOSE THE STORM SO OTHERS COULD HAVE THE HARBOR*

Below it, in smaller letters, the full crew roster. Twenty-six names. Sefa read them all. Found Emeka's in the engineering section, third from the bottom, and pressed two fingers to the metal the way she'd once pressed them to the memorial wall.

"First watch report," Toma said softly, when they resumed walking. "All secure. No enemy contacts."

"There's never been an enemy contact on this ship," Sefa said. "That was the whole point."

* * *

The window opened at 0400, ship's reckoning, and everyone in the Drift who could see the gate saw it happen.

The jump field bloomed — that slow blue flower of distorted space, the gate's ancient ring drinking power for the first sustained charge in eleven years — and the lane beyond it, the Meridian Run, the artery that had fed half the colonies and been severed by grief, flickered from sealed to open like an eye remembering how.

Sefa watched it from the *Mercy*'s bridge, because the honor watch had ended and the ships were dispersing and someone had to take her people home. Around her, the fleet formations were dissolving — not retreating, dissolving, Concord wedges and Free Anchor wings interpenetrating for the first time in a decade as commerce captains and haulers and colony runners, waiting at the edge of the exclusion zone since before dawn, began to move toward the open gate in a slow, disbelieving river.

The first ship through was a grain hauler out of Perdita Station, old and ugly and overloaded, flying no colors at all. Its pilot keyed the all-channel band as it crossed the threshold, and its voice cracked halfway through the sentence: "Meridian Run, this is *Fat Agnes*, bound for Ceres Deep. Lane is — lane is clear. God, the lane is *clear*." And then, softer, almost private: "Thank you, whoever you were."

Behind it came another. And another. By the time Sefa gave the order to come about, the river had become a current, and the current was pulling everything toward the open dark.

Wren appeared at her shoulder. "Course, Captain?"

Sefa looked at the Vale one last time — small now against the gate's glow, amber and green lights burning, a monument with an engine — and then at the traffic streaming through the ring, and felt the thing in her chest that had been a weapon for eleven years slowly, painfully, becoming a compass again.

"Home," she said. "Then the memorial rotation. I put our name in for the first watch." She paused. "Emeka's name too. He'd want to see the traffic."

The *Kite's Mercy* turned for home with the fleet scattered around her like birds remembering flock, and Sefa Okonkwo stood on her own bridge with her hands steady at last, and wept once, briefly, cleanly, the way you cry at a funeral that has finally, finally become a funeral and not an accusation.

She was still watching the gate when the hail came through — a tight-beam, private-band, routed through the memorial station's new relay. The identifier resolved, and Sefa frowned, because she didn't recognize the code group, but the routing header said it originated from the Vale herself.

She opened the channel.

A voice she didn't know — young, female, tired past the far edge of tired — said: "Captain Okonkwo. This is Zephyrine Dunmore. I'm sorry to reach you like this, and I'm sorrier for what I'm about to ask. But I've been reviewing the black-box logs, and there's a fragment in them — a transmission fragment, timestamped ninety seconds before the detonation — that isn't my mother's voice. And I think, from the registry prefix, that it might be your brother's." A pause, and Sefa heard the woman choose her next words with great care. "Before I play it for the fleets, before it becomes anyone's evidence — I thought you should hear it first. May I send it?"

Sefa stood very still on the bridge of her ship, in the wash of the opening gate, with her whole crew pretending not to watch her.

"Send it," she said.
