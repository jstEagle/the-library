## 22. Echo

The lanes had been open for seven months, and the traffic still hadn't gotten used to them.

Zephyrine felt it every time they cleared a gate — the strange, giddy abundance of it. Freighters queued in orderly lines where for a decade there had been nothing but silence and sensor buoys and the frozen geometry of a war nobody wanted to name. Tugboats shepherding container barges. A Concord hospital ship running parallel with a Free Anchor grain hauler, close enough that their running lights bled together in the dark. Pilots who had learned to fly afraid now flew bored, which was its own kind of miracle.

She sat in the office that wasn't an office — a converted cargo bay on the *Kestrel's Wake*, bulkheads painted a soft industrial grey, a desk salvaged from a station that no longer existed, one porthole looking out onto whatever sky they happened to be parked under this week. The plaque beside the door read **DUNMORE & ASSOCIATES — IMPOSSIBLE CLAIMS INVESTIGATED** in letters Brannoch had routed by hand out of hull scrap, because he said a business should have roots even when its roots moved every six weeks.

*Associates* was generous. It was her, Toma, and an engine.

But the cases came. That was the part that still surprised her. Word traveled along the reopened lanes faster than the freight did — word of the woman who had stood between two fleets at the Gate of Ghosts and made them put their weapons down with nothing but a dead captain's voice. Half the people who hired her wanted that woman. The other half wanted the claims adjuster who had once flagged a manifest anomaly at Halcyon Mutual and refused to let it go. Zephyrine had decided, somewhere over the last few months, that both women could keep working. They were, after all, the same inheritance.

The chime at the cargo-bay airlock sounded at 0900 station time, which meant her appointment was early, which meant she was nervous. Nervous clients were usually honest clients. Zephyrine finished her tea, set down the tablet she'd been pretending to read, and opened the lock.

The man who came through was small and precise, the kind of person who ironed shirts on a ship where ironing was a moral eccentricity. He wore the livery of a mid-tier shipping concern — Calloway Lines, if she read the crest right — and he carried his data folio against his chest like a shield.

"Ms. Dunmore." His voice cracked on the first syllable and he started over. "Ms. Dunmore. My name is Emeric Voss. I'm a routing clerk. Junior. I know you probably have — I'm sure your docket is full, I can come back—"

"Sit down, Mr. Voss." She gestured at the chair across the desk. "You crossed four systems to get here. You're not going anywhere until I've heard why."

He sat. Perched, really, on the front third of the cushion, folio still clutched. Up close she could see the sleeplessness on him, the particular grey that settled under the eyes of people who had found something wrong and couldn't make anyone believe them.

"I flag anomalies," he said. "That's my job. Routing manifests, cross-checks, the boring arithmetic that keeps ten thousand ships from hitting each other. Three weeks ago a manifest came through my queue that shouldn't exist."

Zephyrine kept her face still, but something in her chest leaned forward.

"The *Aldanmere Rose*," Voss continued. "Container vessel, Calloway registry, lost with all hands fourteen years ago. Reactor cascade, officially. There was a settlement, memorials, the whole liturgy. Except last month a maintenance requisition crossed my desk from a yard in the Thessaly Drift — parts order, routine stuff, coolant couplings, a nav array. And the ship ID on the requisition was the *Rose*. Not a namesake. Not a re-registered hull. The *same* hull number. Fourteen years after she burned."

"And when you raised it?"

"With my supervisor?" He almost laughed. "Clerical error. Ghost entry. Archive contamination from the old merger. I've heard all of it. So I pulled the original loss file myself, off-hours, and Ms. Dunmore — it's sealed. Military order. Both militaries. Joint seal, which as far as I can tell has happened exactly once in recorded history, and we both know when the other time was."

The ventilation hummed. Somewhere aft, Brannoch's engine turned over at idle, that deep patient note Zephyrine had stopped hearing and started needing, the way other people needed rain.

"You looked me up," she said. "Before you came."

"I read everything about the *Meridian Vale*," Voss said simply. "I figured if anyone would believe a ship that's officially dead might not be, it would be you."

Zephyrine looked at him for a long moment. Then she extended her hand across the desk.

"Terms are on the wall placard. Retainer up front, expenses billed at cost, and one condition non-negotiable: you don't talk about this case to anyone until I tell you it's over. Not your supervisor, not your union rep, not whoever sealed that file. If someone searched your apartment three weeks from now, I'd like them to find nothing worth burning you for."

His handshake was damp and fierce. "You think it's real. The ship. You think it's actually out there."

"I think," Zephyrine said, "that the galaxy has a habit of keeping its ghosts alive for reasons, and the reasons are always worse than the accidents. Give me the requisition. I'll want the original loss notice too, unredacted portions only — don't go back into that archive, Mr. Voss. Whatever's in there already cost someone enough to seal it twice."

* * *

Toma found her an hour later in the cockpit, feet up on the console, watching the traffic stream past the viewport like luminous sediment.

"A client," he said, dropping into the navigator's seat. "An actual paying client, on day one of the new quarter. We're practically a firm."

"We're practically broke is what we are. But yes. A client." She handed him the requisition copy. "Tell me what you see."

He read it the way he read everything — fast, then slow, lips moving slightly on the second pass. His finger stopped on the hull number. "Thessaly Drift. That's Free Anchor patrol territory, or it was. And this yard code—" He frowned. "I know this yard code. Or I knew it. It was blacklisted when I was still wearing a uniform. Salvage front. They don't build ships there; they *unbuild* history."

"So the ghost has a mechanic."

"They always have a mechanic." Toma set the folio down carefully, as if it might bruise. "Zeph. The *Meridian Vale* nearly killed us. Not the finding of it — the *ending* of it. Sefa's crew wanting to hand it to Fleet, Concord wanting to display it, forty hours to a closing window, targeting locks on a ship flown by a seventy-year-old engineer with a death wish and a wrench. I aged nine years in nine days. And now you want to go find another one."

"I want to find out *why* this one exists," Zephyrine said. "There's a difference. The *Vale* existed because my mother built a lie big enough to stop a war. This—" she tapped the requisition, "—this is smaller. Colder. Somebody is maintaining a dead ship for profit or leverage, not peace. But somebody also went to the trouble of sealing the file jointly, which means two governments agreed on a story. Governments only agree on stories when the truth would cost them both something."

Toma was quiet for a while. Outside, a Free Anchor corvette slid past, close enough that its hull markings caught the light — the same fleet whose privateers had once boarded them with guns drawn, whose captain had shown Zephyrine a casualty ledger with her mother's forged name on it.

"Sefa sent a message last week," he said finally. "Did I show you? Joint memorial commission wants the *Vale* towed to Meridian Station for the anniversary. Brannoch's been asked to consult. He's insufferable about it. He's had the engine specs memorized since before either fleet existed and now admirals ask him questions."

"He's earned insufferable."

"He's earned worse than that." Toma smiled, but it faded quickly, the way his smiles did, guilt never far beneath. "You're really taking the case."

"I'm really taking the case."

"Then I'll plot us a route to the Thessaly Drift." He was already pulling up charts, hands moving with the old fluency, the disgraced navigator and the redeemed one occupying the same body without apparent friction. "Slow way. Smuggler circuit. We know how to travel quiet."

"We know how to travel quiet," she agreed. "It's the only way we know."

* * *

Brannoch heard them out in the engine room, standing with his arms folded under the great ribcage of the drive housing, listening the way he listened — completely, without interruption, eyes half-closed as though the words were sounds he was tuning.

When Zephyrine finished, he was silent long enough that she checked whether he'd fallen asleep standing up. He hadn't. He was looking at the reactor.

"Fourteen years," he said at last. "Your mother kept hers flying eleven. Whoever keeps this *Rose* has beaten her record by three."

"That's what worries me."

"It's what should interest you." He laid one scarred hand flat against the housing, and the engine answered him with a subtle shift in pitch, the conversation they'd been having for eleven years and counting. "Ships don't stay alive by accident, girl. Someone feeds them. Someone *chooses* them. Your mother chose martyrdom; her mechanic chose devotion. Now — what does a routing clerk's ghost ship choose?" He shook his head slowly. "You'll find out. And when you find out, you'll wish you could unfind it, and then you'll be glad you didn't, because that's the shape of this work. Ilsa knew. Why d'you think she left you the second message instead of deciding for you?"

Zephyrine rested her own hand against the housing, next to his. Warm metal. Steady pulse.

"Keep her humming till we're back," she said.

"She'll hum longer than I will," Brannoch said. "Which was always the plan."

* * *

They jumped for Thessaly on a Tuesday, through a lane so newly reopened that the nav beacons still carried both fleets' calibration codes stitched together like a diplomatic apology. Toma threaded the gates with a grace that made Zephyrine remember, uncomfortably, that the first time she'd met him he'd flinched at her surname — and that the flinch had been the truest thing anyone had ever told her about her mother.

On the fourth day out, in the long empty stretch between gates, Voss's folio open on her desk, Zephyrine did what she always did with impossible files: she read them like letters.

Because that was what they were. Every manifest was a letter someone wrote to the future. Every requisition, every seal, every clerical error was a sentence in a hand — digital signatures had handwriting too, if you knew how to look, pressure curves and timing intervals as individual as fingerprints. Her mother had taught her that, or rather her mother's ghost had, in the months after the recording, when Zephyrine had gone back through eleven years of forged documents and found, hidden in the authentication hashes of the fake disaster transponder codes, a pattern that resolved into nothing more than a date: her daughter's birthday, repeated seventeen times, invisible to every auditor in two fleets.

*I was here,* the pattern said. *I was always here.*

So she read the *Aldanmere Rose*'s requisition the way she'd learned to read grief, and halfway down the parts list she stopped breathing.

Coolant couplings. Nav array. Standard. Routine. And then, item twelve: **one (1) acoustic isolation suite, crew quarters, full refit.**

Acoustic isolation. For a ship with no crew. For a ship that had burned with all hands fourteen years ago.

Someone was soundproofing a grave.

Or — and the thought arrived cold and complete, the way the true ones always did — someone was keeping something *quiet* aboard a ship that was supposed to be empty. And the joint military seal on the loss file, the one both governments had agreed to, suddenly looked less like embarrassment and more like fear.

Zephyrine closed the folio. In the viewport, the Thessaly Drift was resolving out of dark — a vast slow tumble of ice and rock and old wreckage, glittering like a spilled jewelry box, beautiful the way all the places she'd learned to dread were beautiful.

Somewhere in there, a dead ship was being kept alive, and kept *silent*, and two governments had signed their names to the silence.

She touched the comm panel. "Toma. Change of plan. Before we go near that yard — I want to know what else the *Rose* ordered. Pull every requisition with that hull number, fourteen years' worth. I want to see the whole correspondence."

"Aye," Toma's voice came back, and then, after a pause, wry and fond and a little afraid: "You've got the look. The one you had in the Halcyon archives. Should I be worried?"

Zephyrine smiled, and found that it came easily — that the smile itself was an inheritance, passed down from a woman who had smiled at impossible odds and made them stand down.

"Worried later," she said. "Curious first. Always curious first."

The engine hummed beneath her, steady as a heartbeat, carrying the echo forward into the drift.
