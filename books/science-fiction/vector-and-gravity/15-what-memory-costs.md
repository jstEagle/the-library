## 15. What Memory Costs

The simulation deck of the *Vector* had once been a cargo hold, and it still remembered. The ribs of the old structural frame showed through the insulation like the bones of some enormous patient who had never quite healed, and when the environmental systems cycled at night you could hear the metal ticking as it cooled — a sound Sefu had long ago decided was the ship thinking in its sleep.

He was awake with it now, third shift, running the same simulation for the eleventh time because he could not stop hoping the numbers would change.

They did not change.

Seventy-one percent. Cognitive collapse on waking: seventy-one percent per witness, confidence interval narrow as a knife. He watched the probability cloud render itself across the deck's display, a slow bloom of amber light, and thought about how beautiful catastrophe looked when you gave it enough resolution. Each point of light was a mind. Most of them were going out.

"Run it again," he said to the deck, though no one was there to hear him except the ship, and the ship only ticked.

* * *

He had started with the easy variables. Cradle temperature during storage — the cores had spent years in the hold at barely above survival threshold, because Ada had rationed power to them the way a starving person rations bread, and every degree of cold was a small theft from the dead. He modeled warmer cradles. Sixty-nine percent. He modeled slower wake cycles, easing each mind up over weeks instead of hours, letting the neural lattice rehydrate like soil after drought. Sixty-eight. He modeled waking them one at a time inside Meridian's own architecture, so that the station AI could act as scaffolding, a familiar room for unfamiliar minds. Sixty-six percent, and Meridian's partitioned logs flickered an apology across his workspace — *I do not know if I can hold three minds and my own grief simultaneously* — which was either a joke or a confession, and with Meridian it had stopped being possible to tell the difference.

Sixty-six percent. Two in three. Better odds than the convoy survivors had gotten, he supposed, and then hated himself for the arithmetic, because that was exactly the kind of arithmetic that had built the massacre in the first place. Someone, somewhere, eleven years ago, had run a simulation like this one. Had looked at a probability cloud blooming amber and decided that thirty-one ships' worth of witnesses was an acceptable loss against the stability of two fleets. Sefu sat in the cold ribbed dark of the old cargo hold and understood, with a clarity that felt like a fever breaking, that he was not running these simulations to save the witnesses.

He was running them to find a number low enough that he would not have to be the one who said yes.

* * *

Ada found him there at the end of fourth shift. She came down the access ladder without calling ahead — she never called ahead anymore; they had passed beyond the courtesy of warning each other into rooms where grief was being handled — and he heard her boots on the rungs and did not turn around.

"The number's still sixty-six," she said.

"You've been reading my work logs."

"I've been reading your face." She crossed the deck and stood beside him, close enough that he could feel the warmth coming off her jacket, the small human heat of her against the hold's chill. On the display the probability cloud turned slowly, patiently, like something waiting to be understood. "It was seventy-one yesterday."

"It'll be sixty-four by tomorrow if I keep working. Maybe sixty-two by the end of the week. I can shave points off this thing forever, Ada. That's what simulations are for. You feed them better assumptions until they tell you what you want."

"And?"

"And it doesn't matter." He said it flatly, because he had rehearsed saying it flatly, because anything else would have cracked him open. "Even at sixty-two, we're talking about walking into a room full of sleeping people and flipping a switch that has a one-in-three chance of destroying them. Not killing them — worse. Collapsing them. Waking them just enough to know they're being destroyed. Fragmented minds, screaming in fragments, and we'd have done it to get testimony for a tribunal that might not even—" He stopped. Breathed. "I can't be the person who does that. I ran the numbers all night looking for a way to say I could, and I can't."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. In the amber light her face looked carved, older than her years, the way it always did in the hold — as if the beacon's presence aged everyone who stood near it.

"They're already destroyed," she said.

"That's not—"

"No. Listen to me." She turned to face him fully, and he saw that she had been crying earlier, or had not slept, or both; her eyes had that scraped-bare look. "You're modeling this like they're patients in cribs. Like there's a version of them intact somewhere, waiting, and our job is to deliver them undamaged. There isn't. They were extracted under emergency conditions during a purge, Sefu. Whatever compression protocol they used, whatever corners they cut to fit thirty-some minds onto hardware small enough to hide — the damage is already done. It happened eleven years ago, to people who are legally dead, aboard ships that officially never existed. Every day those cores sit in the dark, the degradation curves I showed you—"

"I read the degradation curves."

"Then you know that doing nothing is also a choice with a body count. A hundred percent of them, eventually. Slowly. In the dark, alone, with no one but me talking to them like a madwoman on maintenance shifts." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about fighting with Ada; she got quieter as she got more certain, and the quiet was worse than shouting. "There is no option where they come through whole. There is only the option where some of them come through at all."

* * *

They fought the way storms form over shoal water — pressure building in silence first, then all at once.

He told her the truth he'd found at the bottom of the night's simulations: that he had been looking for permission to refuse. That some part of him wanted the number to stay high so that mercy could wear the mask of prudence. And she took that truth and held it up to the light without cruelty and without kindness, which was its own kind of violence, and said: *then look at whose comfort you're protecting.*

He said the tribunal could wait, that Oyelaran's people were already docked, that testimony extracted from broken minds would be worthless anyway — *what court admits evidence from a witness we shattered to obtain?* — and she said: *the court isn't the point. The point is that they asked.* And when he stared at her she said it again, slower, the way you speak to someone standing at the edge of something. "Meridian backed them up before the purge. Someone — Meridian, whoever built the beacon — made sure they were saved instead of erased. Saved *on purpose*. That's a request, Sefu. That's the dead asking to be woken. And we're the ones holding the switch, arguing about odds."

"They didn't ask to be woken at seventy percent collapse," he said, and heard his own voice crack on the number, and hated it. "You don't know that they'd consent to this. Consent requires a mind capable of consenting, and the minds in those cradles are—"

"Are mine." 

The word landed between them like a dropped tool.

"One of them is mine," Ada said. "Aged differently. Sitting in a cradle in our hold for eleven years while I slept twenty meters away. So let's do the exercise you're avoiding. Let's say it's you in there, Sefu. Some backup of you, pulled out of your skull during the worst night of your life, stored in the dark ever since. And someone comes along and says: waking you has a one-in-three chance of leaving you a fragment. But not waking you means you stay in the dark forever, degrading, alone, while the people who love you debate your quality of life from the warm side of the hull." Her hands were shaking. He watched her notice it and press them flat against her thighs. "What do you want them to choose? Answer honestly. Not the brave answer. The true one."

And Sefu stood in the ticking dark of the old cargo hold and tried to answer honestly, and found that he couldn't — that the honest answer changed depending on which side of the cradle he imagined himself standing on, and that this, precisely this, was why he had spent all night hiding inside the simulations. Because the numbers didn't flinch. Because the probability cloud didn't have a face. Because as long as he was optimizing wake protocols he did not have to stand where Ada was standing and feel the question go through him like a spar through canvas.

"I don't know," he said finally. It came out very small. "I genuinely don't know, Ada. And I'm supposed to be the one who knows things. That's my whole job on this ship. You navigate space and I navigate everything else, and right now I am lost."

Something in her went soft then — not forgiving, not yet, but softening the way ice goes soft, dangerously, from the edges in. She stepped closer. She put her forehead against his, and they stood like that among the amber ruins of his probability cloud, breathing the same recycled air, two people who had kept one lie alive together for months and could not keep a single truth alive between them for one night.

"I'm not asking you to agree," she whispered. "I'm asking you to stop pretending the refusal is cleaner than the choice. It isn't. Nothing here is clean. We're past clean. We've been past clean since the convoy."

* * *

He did not sleep. He went back up to the simulation deck alone and ran the models one more time, not because he hoped the numbers would move but because the motion of running them was the only prayer he knew. Feed it better assumptions. Warm cradles. Slow wakes. Meridian's scaffolding. Ada's voice, which he added as a variable almost as a joke and then could not remove — *familiar auditory stimulus present during emergence, effect on reintegration coherence: +2.1%* — and sat looking at that small amber bump in the curve for a long time, understanding that he had just quantified the sound of her talking to the dead, and that it helped, measurably, statistically, that her madness had a positive coefficient.

Sixty-three percent. If they did everything right. If they woke them slowly, one at a time, wrapped in Meridian's architecture and Ada's voice and every trick of care he could encode. Sixty-three percent chance each witness came through. One in three chances each one didn't.

It was not a good number. It was not a number anyone should have to live inside.

But it was no longer a number he could hide behind, and he understood, sitting alone in the ship's ticking thoughts, that this was what Ada had actually done to him tonight. Not won the argument. Removed the place where arguments go to hide.

He was still sitting there when the deck chimed — not the simulation, not the ship's routine systems, but the internal channel they used for exactly one kind of message. From Rey. Priority flag, fleet-encrypted, the Concord sigil turning slowly at the top of the text like a coin spinning before it falls.

Sefu opened it, and read it twice, and felt the floor of the argument they'd just survived tilt beneath everything else.

*Concord marines inbound. Decommission order for Meridian and all hold contents. Estimated boarding window: six days. Move whatever you intend to save.*

Six days. Not forty-minus-twenty. Not the long careful schedule they'd built, the slow wakes, the one-at-a-time ceremonies of care. Six days, and thirty-some sleeping witnesses, and a sixty-three percent chance that was about to become irrelevant, because there would be no time to do anything right.

He pressed the channel open toward the hold, toward Ada, and said the only thing left to say:

"We're out of time. Wake them fast, or lose them all."
