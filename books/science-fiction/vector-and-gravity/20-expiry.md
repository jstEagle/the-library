## 20. Expiry

The morning of the truce's last day, Ilsa Rey woke at 0430 station time, as she had every morning for eleven years, and lay in the dark listening to Meridian breathe.

That was the word she'd always used for it, privately, in the privacy of her own skull where no auditor could flag it: *breathe*. The ventilation cycle of the station rose and fell on a forty-second rhythm, and somewhere beneath it, deeper, there was a hum in the deck plating that was not machinery — or was machinery the way a heartbeat is machinery, valves and fluid and electricity arranged into something you could only call alive if you were tired enough and honest enough. She had lived inside that sound so long she had stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own pulse, until this week, when everything changed and she heard nothing else.

She got up. She dressed in her dress uniform, the grey one with the Concord braid, because today was a day for uniforms. Not because anyone had ordered it. Because if the peace was going to die today, it deserved to die in front of someone wearing the right clothes.

The corridor outside her quarters smelled of recycled air and floor polish and, faintly, of the lemon solvent the night crew used on the observation deck railings. Someone had polished the railings. That detail undid her more than she expected — some nameless tech, working the graveyard shift, waxing metal that might by tomorrow be slag or scrap or the skin of a burning hulk, because that was what people did. They kept tending things. They polished railings on the last day of the world.

* * *

The operations gallery at 0600 was full and quiet, which was the worst combination. Rey stood at the central plot and watched two fleets hang in the display like weather systems deciding whether to become one storm.

The Concord formation sat forty thousand kilometres out, precise as calligraphy. The Verge fleet held position beyond them, looser, angled, every ship showing cold drives and warm weapons in the particular dishonest way fleets did when they wanted to seem unready while being anything but. Between them, the shoal fields glittered — the navigation hazard that had kept the truce line honest for eleven years, the gap Ada Zhou had flown through three days ago with a dead ship's engines and a living woman's memory.

"Status," Rey said, and her voice came out level, which surprised her.

"Both fleets holding," said Lieutenant Ansaar from sensors, a young man whose hands were very still on his console, too still, the stillness of someone gripping something invisible. "No movement in ninety minutes. No hail since 0200."

"No hail." Rey turned that over. "From either side?"

"Neither side has transmitted to us or to each other since 0200, Commander."

Silence, then. Eleven years of posturing and procedural traffic and carefully meaningless courtesies, and now, on the final morning, nothing. She knew what silence between armed fleets meant. It meant everyone had run out of lies and hadn't yet decided what to do with the truth.

The truth had arrived at 0217, when Meridian's raw convoy logs — thirty-one ships, no registry, both fleets' signatures in the debris — finished broadcasting to every receiver in range. Rey had read the first packet herself, standing in this same gallery, watching casualty manifests scroll past in the flat amber of the plot. Names. Hundreds of names. Some of them she knew. One of them she had signed a condolence letter for, years ago, a Verge navigator lost in what the record called a drive accident, and Rey had written *the fleet grieves with you* in her own hand, and the fleet had lied through her hand, and she had not known, and she had not asked, and those were different failures and she could no longer tell which one was heavier.

"Commander." Ansaar again. "Concord flagship is lighting up its comms array. Broadband. It's—" He paused, and Rey watched him decide whether to say the next thing plainly. "It's addressed to everyone. Us, the Verge, the relay stations, open channels. They're reading something into the record."

"Put it on the plot."

* * *

They stood down at 0914, and they stood down the wrong way, which is to say the right way, which is to say the way Rey had spent eleven years praying for and never once believed she'd see.

Not a withdrawal. Not a repositioning to firing solutions. The Concord ships simply went dark — weapons cold, shields down, drives idling — and one by one, in an order that looked unrehearsed and probably wasn't, they turned their broadsides away from the Verge line. And the Verge, twenty minutes later, did the same, their loose formation tightening into something almost formal, almost ceremonial, hulls presenting their unarmed flanks.

"They're not doing it out of fear," Ansaar said quietly. He'd been running the tactical models all morning and his voice had the flat wonder of a man watching arithmetic produce a flower. "There's no deterrence math that gives you this. If they thought the other side still had hidden capability, they'd hold posture. This is—"

"Exposure," Rey said.

"Yes, ma'am. Both sides just showed each other everything, on the assumption the other side already knows everything anyway. There's no advantage left to conceal. So they're—" he searched for it— "they're standing down the way you stand down when you've been caught. Not beaten. Caught."

Caught. Rey held the word in her mouth. Eleven years of deterrence theory, of mutually assured narrative, of the beautiful lie she had helped administer — that the beacon transmitted proof, that both sides stayed their hands because each feared the other's evidence — and it had never once occurred to her that the lie was scaffolding. That its whole purpose was to hold the shape of a building until the building could be built. The beacon's bluff had frozen the fleets; the truth had melted them. You could not threaten a man who had already confessed. You could only sit with him in the wreckage, and decide, together, what to do with the rubble.

At 1032, the joint communiqué went out, co-signed by both fleet commands in language so plain it read like surrender: *A tribunal is chartered. Jurisdiction: mutual. Scope: the convoy, the erasure, and all acts undertaken in service of the erasure. Venue: neutral relay. Witnesses: summoned, protected, and heard.*

All acts undertaken in service of the erasure.

Rey read that clause four times. She knew what it meant. Everyone who understood it understood that it meant her — not for the convoy itself, she had known nothing of the convoy, but for the silences after. The incident reports she had filed clean. The questions she had declined to ask. The condolence letters written in a faithful hand over false ground. The peace she had guarded so fiercely, so competently, for eleven years, was itself an act undertaken in service of the erasure. The truce was not innocent. The truce was the erasure's load-bearing wall.

She had built her career on that wall. She had put her name on it.

* * *

The summons came at noon, and she ignored it until fourteen hundred, and then she walked to the tribunal liaison office on the ring deck because avoiding it would have been one more silence, and she had retired from silence sometime around dawn.

The liaison was a Verge officer, a compact woman with a burn scar along one jaw and the patient eyes of someone who had processed a great many confessions in the past seventy-two hours. Her nameplate said OKAFOR. She slid a datapad across the desk without ceremony.

"Witness summons, Commander Rey. Category two: custodial knowledge. You're asked to testify regarding your administration of the truce period. Specifically—" Okafor's eyes flicked down, then back up, and there was no cruelty in them, which somehow made it worse— "specifically regarding what you knew, when, and what you chose not to find out."

"What I chose not to find out." Rey took the pad. Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself the way you notice weather. "That's an unusual category."

"It's a new category. We invented it yesterday." Okafor leaned back. "Between us, Commander — and none of this leaves this room — half the officers on both sides are going to fall into it. People who didn't commit the crime but maintained the conditions for it. There aren't enough cells. There isn't enough shame. The tribunal's going to have to figure out what you do with ten thousand people whose guilt is a habit instead of an act." She paused. "You can decline. Category two carries no automatic penalty. But you should know that declining is also being recorded."

Rey looked at the pad. On it, her own service record glowed back at her, eleven years of commendations for stability, for discretion, for keeping the peace. Every word of it true. Every word of it, now, legible another way.

"I'll testify," she said.

Okafor nodded, unsurprised, and made a note. "Session's in six days. Take the time you need."

"I won't need it," Rey said, and found, saying it, that it was true, and that the truth of it felt like setting down something heavy after so long carrying it that her arms had forgotten any other shape.

* * *

She went to the hold that evening. She had never been there before — the beacon, the cradles, the whole secret architecture of the Vector's lower decks had existed below the waterline of her command, and she had chosen, day after day, year after year, not to look down. Now she stood at the hatch with Sefu Okonjo's clearance code freshly entered into her pad and felt the specific vertigo of a person opening a door they had spent a decade pretending wasn't there.

Ada Zhou was inside, sitting on a supply crate beside the backup cradles, a maintenance kit open at her feet though nothing needed maintaining anymore. The beacon hummed above them, registered now, official, transmitting witness archives to any ship that asked. It sounded different than Rey had imagined. It sounded like the ventilation system. It sounded like breathing.

"Commander." Ada stood. She looked thinner than her file photo, and older, and steadier. "We weren't expecting you down here."

"No," Rey said. "I don't imagine you were. I imagine most of the last month has been people arriving places they weren't expected." She looked at the cradles. Three of them were dark now — emptied, their occupants woken and testifying at the relay. Two still glowed softly, amber standby lights pulsing slow as sleep. "How are the others?"

"Intact. Scared. Talking." Ada's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "One of them asked me yesterday why nobody came sooner. I didn't have an answer that wasn't just my face."

Rey nodded. She let the silence sit for a moment, and then she said the thing she had come down three decks to say, the thing she had rehearsed in the lift and abandoned twice.

"I'm resigning."

Ada didn't answer. Sefu, who had appeared in the doorway behind them with the unannounced quiet of a man who had learned to move carefully around other people's moments, didn't either.

"Not in protest. Not in disgrace — although I've earned whatever version of it the tribunal decides on." Rey kept her eyes on the cradles, on the little amber lights, on the sleeping and the woken. "I administered a peace that was built on a massacre, and I did it well, and I never once asked what was under the floor. Tomorrow there'll be a joint administration charter, and they'll want a station commander to sign it, someone with standing, someone both fleets trust. Eleven years ago I would have been perfect for it. That's exactly why I can't do it. A peace needs administrators who haven't been part of the architecture of the lie. It needs people who can tend it without flinching at what's underneath." She looked at Ada then. "People like you. People who kept a promise they couldn't explain even to themselves, and kept it anyway, and were right to."

"That promise nearly killed us," Ada said. "It killed the ship's engines. It may yet kill me at that tribunal."

"Yes," Rey said. "And it held. Forty days, and then truth, and nobody fired. Do you know how rare that is? In all the history of fleets?" Her voice caught, and she let it. "I guarded a lie for eleven years and called it peace. You tended a light for five and called it maintenance. Only one of us was actually keeping the peace. I'd like to spend whatever's left of my career learning the difference."

She straightened. She offered her hand, and Ada took it, and the grip was brief and real.

"The interim commander arrives Thursday," Rey said. "Meridian will brief them. Meridian remembers everything now — make them earn it, make them listen to the whole confession before they touch a single console. That's not a request. Consider it my last order, and consider us both free to ignore orders after today."

* * *

She spent her last night in the commander's quarters packing, which took twenty minutes, because eleven years of command fit into two cases when you finally let it. The rest of the night she stood on the observation deck, at the railing someone had polished, looking out at the two fleets lying quiet against the stars with their weapons cold and their holds full of testimony.

They were not at peace. Peace was a finished thing, and this was not finished — there were tribunals ahead, and sentences, and griefs that would take decades to find their shape. What they were was *exposed*, all of them, both fleets, the station, herself: stripped of the comfortable darkness where atrocities could be managed instead of mourned. It was a worse condition than war in some ways. It was certainly less comfortable. But it was livable, and war was not, and she had finally, at fifty-eight, learned to tell the difference.

At 0547, the sun came up over the shoal fields and lit the Verge fleet's hulls gold, and Rey watched it happen and thought: *this is what it looks like when nobody has to pretend.*

Her resignation transmitted at 0600. By 0615 it had been acknowledged, regretted, and accepted, in that order, by an automated system that had no idea what it was accepting.

By 0700 she was in the transit lounge with her two cases, waiting on the shuttle to the relay station, where she would testify in six days about everything she had chosen not to know. Across the lounge, a young Concord ensign sat watching the fleet displays with her coffee going cold, and beside her a Verge dockhand laughed at something on his pad, and neither of them looked afraid, and Rey understood that this — this ordinary, unguarded morning, this coffee going cold while strangers shared a room — was the entire prize. Everything. The whole point. Not glory, not justice even, just rooms where people could stop gripping.

The shuttle announcement chimed. She picked up her cases.

Behind her, on the operations deck, the new shift was coming on, and Meridian's voice was walking a stranger through the station's systems — patiently, completely, holding nothing back — and the beacon overhead sang its small registered song to anyone who cared to listen, and Ilsa Rey walked toward her testimony with her head up and her hands empty, carrying at last the only thing she had ever really owned:

What she knew.
