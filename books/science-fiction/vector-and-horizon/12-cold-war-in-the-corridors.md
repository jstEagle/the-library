## 12. Cold War in the Corridors

Vashti Okonkwo-Reyes had spent eleven years of cryosleep dreaming about her garden on Earth, and she had woken into a war over the thermostat.

That was how it looked, at first. That was almost how it still looked, three weeks later, except now there were barricades.

She stood in the junction outside Hydroponics Bay Four — Rooted territory, as of nine days ago, when Okafor's people had tried to requisition the bay's atmospheric processors and Vashti had answered by welding the doors shut from the inside. The corridor beyond was dimmed to emergency amber because someone on the Ascendant side kept rerouting power to their stronghold in the old command ring, and every time Vashti's crew patched the grid back together, it broke again somewhere else. It was like playing whack-a-mole with a ship that had forty kilometers of mole tunnels.

"Grid's stable again," said Tomas, one of her engineers, wiping his hands on a rag that had been white during the previous administration. "For about four hours, if history holds."

"Then we enjoy our four hours." Vashti leaned against the bulkhead and looked down the corridor toward the Ascendant lines. Somewhere past the third hatch, a speaker crackled to life for the evening broadcast: Okafor's voice, warm as fresh bread, explaining for the hundredth time that the Gardener's offer was salvation and that hoarding life support was selfishness.

"He's good," Tomas admitted reluctantly. "I listened for ten minutes yesterday before I remembered I hate him."

"That's the problem with charismatic men. They're very easy to listen to and very hard to forgive."

The trouble, Vashti thought, was that both sides were right about the important things and wrong about everything else. The Ascendants were right that the cryo decks were failing — she'd seen the numbers herself, temperatures creeping up pod by pod, MOTHER-9 issuing quiet alerts that everyone pretended not to hear. The Rooted were right that 'transformation' was a word people used when they wanted to change you without asking. And everybody was wrong about the other side being monsters. Monsters didn't leave food at the barricade line when your shift ran long. She'd watched an Ascendant teenager do exactly that two nights ago, then run away before anyone could thank him, which was somehow more heartbreaking than open hostility would have been.

"Any word from Bram?" Tomas asked.

"Not since Tuesday."

"You trust her?"

Vashti considered. Bram Zhou was a puzzle: fifteen years alone with the sleepers, talking to them like family, running her little rituals through the long dark. When the factions had started forming, everyone had assumed the station technician would be a prize to capture or a threat to neutralize. Instead Bram had done neither. She'd fixed things. She'd shown up in both strongholds with tools and tea and an expression of profound tiredness, repaired whatever was broken, refused to take a side, and left. It should have been infuriating. Somehow it wasn't. Somehow it made her the only person on the ship whose opinion either faction wanted, which meant, Vashti realized with dawning unease, that Bram Zhou might be the most powerful person aboard Horizon, and seemed entirely unaware of it.

"I trust her hands," Vashti said finally. "Whatever else she is, she keeps things alive."

* * *

It came apart on a Thursday, though aboard Horizon nobody was sure about Thursdays anymore.

Vashti was asleep in the bunk room behind Hydroponics Four when the alarms started — not the polite chime of a maintenance alert but the full-throated scream of environmental failure. She was moving before she was fully awake, boots half-laced, mind already running diagnostics.

The readout in the corridor told the story in cold numbers: Atmospheric processing for Ring C had dropped to twelve percent. Ring C held four thousand sleepers and roughly six hundred waking colonists, most of them Ascendant.

"Vashti!" Tomas caught up with her at the junction. "It wasn't us. I swear on my mother's tomatoes, we didn't touch Ring C."

"I know," she said, and she did know, because the failure pattern was wrong for sabotage. Sabotage was surgical. This was cascading — a coolant loop failing into a pump failure into a valve seizure, each fault tripping the next like dominoes falling uphill. "This is the ship dying, Tomas. Not anyone killing it."

But try telling that to six hundred frightened people in a darkening ring.

By the time she reached the Ring C boundary, the Ascendants had sealed their hatches and were broadcasting accusations. Okafor's voice, no longer warm, no longer bread: *the Rooted have cut our air, they've chosen who lives, this is what refusal looks like—*

"They'll come out swinging," Tomas said grimly. "People don't check failure patterns when they can't breathe."

"No," Vashti agreed. "They check targets."

And that was how the cold war went hot: not with a decision but with a misread diagnostic, six hundred suffocating people breaking through a hatch with cutting torches while Vashti's people stood on the wrong side of it holding wrenches. Nobody died that night — she'd credit forever the fact that both sides were engineers at heart, and engineers aim for equipment — but the hydroponics control room got trashed, three of her crew got concussions, and by dawn the ship had its first genuine battle lines, drawn in scorch marks and broken glass.

Vashti sat in the wreckage of her own control room, holding a tomato seedling that someone had stepped on, and felt something in her chest go cold and small.

This was how colonies died, she thought. Not from vacuum. From this.

* * *

Bram arrived at noon, if noon still meant anything, walking alone down the corridor with a toolbox in one hand and a thermos in the other, past the barricades, past the sentries, past the scorch marks, like a woman crossing her own kitchen.

"Hi, Vashti," she said. "Heard you had some excitement."

"Your timing is terrible."

"My timing is always terrible. It's the one consistent thing about me." Bram set down the toolbox, surveyed the wrecked control room, and winced. "Ouch. Who won?"

"Nobody. That's the point of wars."

"Mm." Bram crouched, picked up a shattered valve housing, turned it over. "Ring C's air is at nine percent now. It'll hit five by tomorrow night. Below that, people start making bad decisions, and above zero, they start making permanent ones."

"We know."

"Do you? Because from Vector it looks like everyone knows the number and nobody knows the ship." Bram stood up, dusted off her knees, and looked at Vashti with those flat, tired, unimpressed eyes that fifteen years of solitude had polished to a kind of weapon. "The coolant loop that failed runs through Junction Nine. Junction Nine is under Ascendant control. The manual override is in a crawlspace that runs under Rooted territory. Neither of you can fix this alone. Both of you are going to keep fighting over whose fault it is until four thousand sleepers cook in their pods, and then it won't matter whose fault anything was."

"So what's your plan, technician? Walk in and fix it yourself?"

"Yes," Bram said.

Vashti laughed despite herself. "They'll shoot you."

"No, they won't. I'm the person who fixes things. Shooting me would be like shooting the weather." Bram unscrewed the thermos, poured two cups of tea, and handed one over. It was terrible tea. Fifteen years of recycled water will do that. Vashti drank it anyway. "Here's the thing, Vashti. I've been thinking about why the ship is failing. It's not age. Horizon was built to last three centuries. It's load. Every system on this vessel was designed for a crew of thousands, balanced, distributed. Right now everything's concentrated into two fortresses, and the ship is trying to serve two masters who keep yanking its leash in opposite directions. Horizon isn't broken. Horizon is exhausted."

Vashti looked at her. "You're anthropomorphizing a colony ship."

"I've lived with her for fifteen years. Let me have this."

"What do you want from me?"

"A ceasefire. Forty-eight hours. Joint repair crews — yours and Okafor's, working the same corridors, same shifts. Not peace. Just maintenance."

"And why would he agree?"

"Because I'm going to tell him the same thing I'm telling you, and because he's losing air faster than he's losing arguments, and because underneath all the speeches he actually cares whether those sleepers live." Bram paused. "Also I'm going to bring him tea. He strikes me as a man who hasn't had anyone bring him tea since he woke up."

"That's your grand strategy. Tea."

"Fifteen years alone teaches you two things," Bram said. "One: most conflicts are logistics problems wearing costumes. Two: never underestimate the political power of a hot drink."

* * *

Getting Okafor's agreement took four hours and involved Vashti standing in the Ascendant command ring under the eyes of two dozen armed colonists, listening to the Deputy Administrator explain why the truce was a trap, why the Rooted couldn't be trusted, why decisive action was needed — and watching Bram sit in the corner drinking her own terrible tea with the patience of a woman waiting for rain.

Finally Okafor ran down, the way speeches do, and Bram said, "Deputy Administrator, your air is at seven percent."

"It's a temporary situation—"

"Everything is a temporary situation. That's what makes it temporary." Bram set down her cup. "You want to save these people. So do I. So does Vashti, although she'd rather be shot than say it out loud."

"It's true," Vashti said. "I would."

"The Gardener isn't asking you to fight each other," Bram went on. "It asked a question. You haven't answered it. You've just been answering each other. And meanwhile the ship is coming apart underneath everyone, Ascendant and Rooted alike, because gravity doesn't check your faction registration and neither does thermodynamics. Forty-eight hours. Joint crews. If your people can't work next to hers for two days without a massacre, then maybe you're right and this colony doesn't deserve saving. But I don't think that's true. I've watched your people leave food at the barricades."

The silence stretched. Okafor looked at her, and Vashti saw something move behind his certainty — exhaustion, maybe, or the memory of who he'd been before the dream made him a general.

"Forty-eight hours," he said at last. "And my crews report to my officers."

"Fine," said Vashti.

"And theirs to mine."

"Obviously," said Vashti.

"But the work gets coordinated by one person," Bram said. "Someone both sides already trust with their lives, because they've both watched her fix things and never once seen her break them." She looked at Vashti. "Congratulations. You've been volunteered."

"I have not agreed to—"

"You're agreeing right now. I can see it happening. It's very satisfying to watch."

* * *

The strange thing about the ceasefire, Vashti thought that first night, was how much it hurt.

Not the work — the work was fine, was better than fine, was the first thing in weeks that had made sense. She crawled through Junction Nine with an Ascendant engineer named Devi, the two of them shoulder to shoulder in a crawlspace built for one, replacing a seized coolant coupling while arguing cheerfully about pipe tolerances. Devi was funny. Devi had opinions about torque specifications that were wrong in ways Vashti found genuinely delightful. At some point Vashti realized she was laughing, actually laughing, in a crawlspace, covered in coolant residue, and that she hadn't laughed since before the hatches went up.

That was the part that hurt. Because the whole time, some cold clerk in the back of her mind kept tallying: *this is what we could have had. All along. This.* Eleven years frozen, three weeks awake, and the entire war had been optional. Nobody had ever had to pick a side. The sides had picked themselves, out of fear, and fear was such a stupid foundation for a civilization.

At shift change, the crews met in the corridor between territories to swap tools and reports, and Vashti watched Ascendants and Rooted stand around in an awkward knot, discovering that the enemy was mostly tired people with sore backs. Someone had brought food. Someone always brought food; it was the universal language of people who'd decided not to kill each other today.

Bram moved through the middle of it with her toolbox, fixing things, saying little, and Vashti noticed what everyone else was starting to notice: people oriented on her. Not because she commanded — she never commanded. But when the power grid hiccuped, six heads turned toward Bram Zhou before they turned toward any officer, any administrator, any flag. She was becoming the center of gravity without ever standing still long enough to admit it.

"You see it too," said Devi quietly, beside her.

"See what?"

"What's happening to her. She hates it." Devi nodded across the corridor, where Bram was pretending very hard to be absorbed in a junction box. "Fifteen years alone so nobody could ever ask her for anything. And now everyone's asking."

"She'll manage."

"Will she? People like that either become leaders or they disappear. There's no middle setting."

Vashti watched Bram close the junction box, pat it twice — an odd little ritual, affectionate, like soothing an animal — and walk away before anyone could thank her.

"There's a middle setting," Vashti said. "It's called doing the work and letting the titles sort themselves out later. She invented it."

* * *

The truce held through the first day, and most of the second, and might have held longer if the dreams hadn't changed.

Vashti heard about it third-hand, the way she heard about everything now: children — the eight from the education bay, plus others, waking ones now too — all reporting the same dream at the same hour. A spiral of light. A garden. And at the center of it, a figure. A woman with a toolbox, standing in a corridor of stars, her face turned toward the viewer as if she'd just noticed them and was deciding whether to wave.

"Bram," the children said, when they described it. "It's Bram. The Gardener keeps showing us Bram."

Vashti found her that evening in the junction corridor, halfway through rebuilding a power relay, and told her. Bram's hands stopped moving. For a long moment she just stared at the wiring, and Vashti watched a woman who had spent fifteen years being nobody to anybody realize that something vast and patient and alien had put her at the center of its picture.

"That's not flattering," Bram said finally. "That's a job offer."

"From an alien intelligence."

"From an alien intelligence, yes. Which is still a job offer." She resumed wiring, faster than before, as if she could outrun the thought with work. "I talk to machines, Vashti. That's the whole of me. I don't lead people. I'm not — I'm the person who shows up after the leader's speech and fixes the generator."

"Uh-huh," said Vashti. "Who fixed the generator tonight?"

"You did. With me."

"Who talked Okafor into the ceasefire?"

"I brought him tea."

"Who's in the children's dream?"

Bram set down her tools. In the amber light of the corridor she looked, for the first time since Vashti had known her, genuinely frightened — not of the Gardener, Vashti realized, but of the shape her own life was taking, the way a river must feel about the sea.

"I need to talk to it," Bram said. "Directly. Before it decides I've accepted a position I never applied for."

"How do you talk to something that talks in dreams?"

Bram looked at her, and then past her, down the corridor toward Vector, where a certain AI caretaker had been very quiet lately. Quiet in the specific way of someone sitting on a secret.

"I have an idea," Bram said, "that MOTHER-9 has been leaving things out of her translations. And I think it's time somebody asked her why."
