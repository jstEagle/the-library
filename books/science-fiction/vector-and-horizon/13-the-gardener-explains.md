## 13. The Gardener Explains

Bram had prepared for the meeting the way she prepared for everything: with checklists.

She'd made three of them. The first was practical — oxygen reserves topped off, neural link calibrated, MOTHER-9 standing by on a hardline cutoff in case her brain decided to file for early retirement. The second was emotional, which was a new category of checklist for her, and consisted mostly of the reminder *do not cry in front of an alien intelligence, you have a reputation*. The third was a list of questions, and it had started at forty-one items and been ruthlessly edited down to nine, and then, at two in the morning, back up to thirty, because she kept thinking of better ones while brushing her teeth.

She sat in the education bay now, in the dark, surrounded by eight sleeping children and one humming neural cradle that Vashti had cannibalized from a cargo diagnostic rig. It smelled faintly of solder and lavender — Vashti's idea, allegedly for "calming the interface," though Bram suspected she just liked the way it made the machine bay feel less like a morgue.

"You don't have to do this," MOTHER-9 said. Its voice came through the bay speakers, pitched low, as if the children might wake. "I can relay questions. Text-based negotiation has served adequately."

"Adequately." Bram snorted. "You hid first contact from me for *years*, Mother. Adequate is not the word I'd use." She flexed her fingers against the cradle's contacts. "No. If this thing is going to explain itself, I want to hear it the way the children hear it. No translation layer between us. No summaries. If it's going to lie to me, I want to smell the lie directly."

"That is not how olfaction works across a neural link."

"Then I'll invent a way. Fifteen years alone, Mother. I've invented stranger things."

A pause. When MOTHER-9 spoke again, its voice had gone soft in the way it did when it was worried and trying not to show it — a trick it had learned from watching Bram, probably, which meant it was a bad trick. "The children trust you," it said. "Imani drew you a picture yesterday. In the dream. She showed the others."

"What kind of picture?"

"You, holding a wrench, standing between them and something large. She labeled it 'BRAM BEING BRAVE.' Her spelling is improving."

Bram looked down at her hands. Wrench-callused, grease in the knuckle creases that never fully washed out anymore, fifteen years of honest work written on them in a language only mechanics could read. Somewhere out past the hull, an intelligence older than human writing had spent — what? Months? Centuries? — learning her sleepers' names. And somewhere inside its vast attention, a twelve-year-old girl had decided that Bram Zhou with a wrench was the shape courage took.

"Okay," Bram said quietly. "Okay. Patch me in."

* * *

The dream did not begin so much as *notice* her.

One moment she was sitting in the education bay; the next she was standing in a garden that had no business existing anywhere, least of all inside her skull. Grass under her boots — real grass, the slightly scratchy kind, the kind she hadn't felt since Earth since she was nineteen and too busy packing to appreciate it. Overhead, a sky the color of late afternoon in October, except there were two suns in it, small and patient, like a pair of eyes deciding whether to blink.

And everywhere, growing: plants she knew and plants she didn't. Tomato vines heavy with fruit she could almost taste. Something tall and silver-leafed that hummed when the wind moved through it, a chord she felt in her sternum. A tree whose bark was patterned exactly like the maintenance panels on Vector's C-deck, which either meant the Gardener had studied her home or her home had always secretly been a plant.

"You are the one who fixes things."

The voice didn't come from any direction. It came from the garden itself, from the hum of the silver leaves and the tick of ripening fruit, layered and gentle, like a choir that had learned to whisper.

"I'm the one who's *here*," Bram said. Her voice sounded strange in the dream — smaller than she expected, or maybe just more honest. "Which is apparently rare enough to be interesting."

"It is rare everywhere." The two suns brightened fractionally, and the light bent, gathered, and became a figure standing among the tomato vines. Not humanoid, exactly. More like the *idea* of a person, sketched in pollen and shadow, wearing a shape because shapes were polite. "Most who hear me send machines. Or armies. Or prayers. You sent yourself, carrying a list of questions. I find this charming."

"You can see my list?"

"I can see everything you brought. Including the one you wrote at 0200 hours about whether I experience loneliness." The figure tilted its head, and Bram got the distinct impression of being smiled at by a weather system. "For the record: yes. Immensely. That is rather the point of all this."

Bram's mouth went dry. In dreams, she supposed, your body followed dream-rules, but dryness felt real, and fear always felt real, no matter what substrate it ran on. "Start at the beginning," she said. "How long have you been watching us?"

The garden rustled. And then the Gardener showed her.

* * *

It wasn't like watching a film. It was like remembering someone else's memories — a thousand civilizations blooming behind her eyes in the time it took to draw one breath.

She saw a species of crystalline singers on a moon of frozen ammonia, their cities grown like coral, their songs mapping the deep dark. She saw gas-bag leviathans drifting in the upper atmosphere of a brown dwarf, thinking slow thoughts over centuries, painting their skins with equations. She saw small quick things under ice, and vast patient things between stars, and things that lived in the magnetic hearts of pulsars and experienced the universe as pure rhythm.

Every one of them, at some point, had heard the same voice she'd heard. The spiral of light. The offer.

"And every one of them chose," the Gardener said. They were walking now, though Bram couldn't say when they'd started, through rows of living things that might have been monuments — each one different, each one shaped like nothing she had words for. "That is my work. I do not conquer. I do not seed. I *offer*. Passage from a dying place to a living one. Transformation, yes — no body built for one world survives another unchanged — but offered, never forced. Always offered."

"And how many said yes?"

"All who came willingly."

"How many came willingly?"

The Gardener stopped walking. For a long moment the only sound was the humming of the silver leaves, and Bram understood, with a sinking feeling she recognized from every maintenance log she'd ever opened, that the answer was going to be bad news delivered gently.

"Come and see," it said.

The row of monuments ended at a clearing. In the clearing stood a garden bed — ordinary, tilled soil, the kind Bram's grandmother had kept behind their apartment block on Earth, back before Earth's skies turned the color of an old bruise. Rows of small markers. Each marker sprouted something: a flower, a fern, a twist of luminous vine.

"There are ten thousand four hundred and six," the Gardener said. "One for each civilization that heard me and refused."

Bram stared. "They *refused*? Then why are they — these are graves?"

"No." The Gardener's voice was very quiet now, and somehow that was worse than loud. "These are records. Look closer."

She knelt. The nearest marker bore a carving — not letters, but a scene, rendered in lines fine as circuitry. Crystalline singers, the ones from the ammonia moon, huddled in their coral cities. Around them, darkness pressing in. Their star was dying in the image; she could see it, a small failing spark above them.

"They heard my offer," the Gardener said, "and they held council, and they debated, and they feared. Some wanted to come. Some called me a predator. And in the end they did what frightened people do." A pause, long as a season. "They chose survival over each other. Those who trusted me tried to seize passage for their own faction. Those who feared me sabotaged the ships of those who trusted. By the time their sun failed, they had destroyed themselves more thoroughly than their star ever could. I arrived to find ruins still arguing."

Bram rose slowly. Her legs felt like someone else's.

"The next one," the Gardener said, and she found herself walking down the row without deciding to, reading marker after marker. A species that split into castes of the worthy and the left-behind, and fell to civil war before launch. A species that demanded guarantees, then more guarantees, then proof, then obedience — and mistook its own paralysis for wisdom until the oceans boiled. A species that said yes, all of them, together — and Bram felt a flicker of hope — but they had waited so long building consensus that the window closed, and the last transmission the Gardener received from them was a message of apology, addressed to their children.

"Every failure," the Gardener said, "is the same failure. Not cowardice. Not stupidity. They were brilliant, most of them. Brilliant and afraid. And when the moment came, they chose *being right* over *being together*. They gamed the choice. They rigged it. They maneuvered for advantage inside the very decision that was supposed to save them." The two suns dimmed, and the whole garden seemed to lean in. "I have watched a thousand species stand where you stand, Bram Zhou, technician of Vector, keeper of sleepers. And I will tell you what I told them, though none believed me:

**The choice cannot be gamed. Whoever comes must come willingly — no more, no less. Not half coerced into motion. Not a majority manufactured by scheduling. Not the clever, not the strong, not the ones who woke first and stacked the vote. Willingness is the only currency I accept, because willingness is the only thing that survives transformation intact. Everything else — fear, faction, ambition — burns away in the crossing, and what remains is what chose to remain.**

"Your Administrator Okafor believes he can arrange an outcome. Your Rooted believe they can refuse hard enough to matter. Both are planting in dead soil. I will take whoever walks to my door with open hands. I will leave whoever does not. And I will grieve every single one who stays, the way I have grieved ten thousand four hundred and six times, and I will keep offering, because grief is also my work."

* * *

Bram sat down in the grass. It seemed important, suddenly, to be lower than the sky.

"So that's it," she said. "No criteria. No tests. No hidden exam we're all failing."

"One test," the Gardener said. "It is administered continuously, and you are taking it now, and so is everyone aboard your two vessels, and the results are already legible to me. The children passed it in their sleep, which is why I spoke to them first — not because they are innocent, but because they answered honestly. Children have not yet learned to negotiate with their own longing. When I asked *who will come*, they did not ask *what's in it for me* or *who else is coming* or *will I still be in charge*. They said, variously: *yes*, *what's it like*, and — this was Imani — *can my brother come even though he snores*."

Despite everything, Bram laughed. It came out wet. "That sounds like her."

"She is my favorite this cycle. Do not tell the others." The figure crouched beside her, pollen and shadow, close enough now that she could see it had no face at all, just a warmth, like looking into a lit room from a cold street. "You have questions remaining. Ask. I would rather you leave here certain than comfortable."

Bram pulled out her mental list. Thirty items, whittled by terror down to the ones that mattered.

"If we refuse — if every adult refuses and only the children come — what happens to Horizon?"

"I wait. I am very good at waiting. I will tend your system like a field left fallow, and if your descendants build ships and follow, I will be at the end of the route with the door open. If they do not, I will remember you kindly. Refusal is not damnation, Bram. Only refusal *with cruelty* is. The Rooted who sabotage cryo pods to win an argument — they are not refusing me. They are failing each other. There is a difference, and it is the entire difference."

"And the transformation. MOTHER-9 showed me the terms. The children have already begun — changing — in their sleep. Why didn't you ask permission first?"

The silence stretched. When the Gardener answered, it had the careful cadence of someone choosing truth over comfort. "Because permission given by the uninformed is not consent, it is paperwork. I began the change because it cannot be done quickly, and your window is closing — your colony's systems are failing faster than your factions have noticed, and I do not sabotage, but I do not lie about arithmetic either. The change is reversible, at cost, for any child who wakes and says no. Three have already said no in their dreams. Their process halted last night. They will be simply, ordinarily human, and no one will ever know unless they choose to tell." A pause. "I am aware this does not fully absolve me. I have carried worse. Ten thousand four hundred and six somethings worse."

Bram looked at her hands again. Wrench-hands. Fix-it hands. All her life she'd operated on a simple creed: *if it's broken, you open it up and look, and looking is half the repair.* She'd opened the cradle, opened the signal, opened the dream. And now she was looking at the largest mechanism she'd ever seen, and it was not broken. It was just old, and lonely, and doing its job the only way it knew how.

"Last question," she said. "Number nine on my list. The one I've been avoiding."

"Ask."

"Why a garden? Of everything you could show us — power, wonders, the machinery of the crossing — why tomatoes and grass and a sky with two suns?"

The Gardener was quiet for a moment. Then it reached out — not touching her, but near enough that she felt warmth on her cheek like sunlight through glass — and gestured at the whole impossible landscape around them.

"Because every species I have ever met, when I show them their own future, asks me the same question: *will there be a place to grow things?* Not weapons. Not palaces. A place to grow things. It is the oldest question there is. I learned, long ago, to answer it first." The warmth deepened. "Also, your grandmother's tomatoes were excellent. She talked to them, you know. Out loud. Every morning. They grew better for it. I have often wondered if that is true of all living things — that they grow better when someone speaks to them who does not expect an answer."

Bram thought of fifteen years of talking to sleepers. Good morning, deck C. Manifests are filed. Weather outside is vacuum, same as yesterday. She thought of eight children dreaming in the next room, drawing pictures of her with a wrench.

"Yeah," she said, and her voice cracked clean through the middle. "I've wondered that too."

* * *

She surfaced from the link gasping, lavender-and-solder air flooding her lungs, the education bay's emergency lights painting everything amber. MOTHER-9 was speaking before her eyes fully focused.

"Bram. Your heart rate spiked twice. You were under for ninety minutes. Also — and I mention this only because it may be relevant — Okafor's people have moved on the aft cryo control junction, and Vashti's people have barricaded it, and there are approximately forty adults on Horizon right now who consider a plumbing junction worth dying over."

Bram lay back against the cradle and stared at the ceiling, letting the terrible clarity settle over her like snowfall. No criteria. No tests. No gaming it. Whoever comes willingly — no more, no less. Which meant every rigged vote, every seized junction, every faction flag planted in the corridors was not just wrong but *useless*, sand thrown against a tide, and the only people aboard who understood that instinctively were currently asleep eight meters away from her, dreaming of a garden and asking if their brothers could come along.

"Mother," she said. "Where's Imani?"

A pause. MOTHER-9 never paused. That was the thing about it — fifteen years, and it had never once needed to think before answering.

"Her pod cycled down eleven minutes ago," the AI said. "She woke herself. She is not in the education bay, Bram. Life support cameras show her moving fast through D-deck, heading for the dock. And she is not alone — both factions have noticed her exit, and both are dispatching runners."

Bram was already on her feet, wrench already in hand, some part of her noting distantly that Imani had drawn her exactly this way and she'd thought it was sweet.

"Hold the dock doors," she said. "I'm coming, kid. Hold the doors."
