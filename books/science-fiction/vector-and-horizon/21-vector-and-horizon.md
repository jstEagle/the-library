## 21. Vector and Horizon

The bell over the classroom door had been Bram's idea, salvaged from a decommissioned cargo drone and tuned to a pitch that made eleven-year-olds wince and laugh at the same time. It rang now, and thirty-two students filed into the old hydroponics bay — long since gutted of its tanks and replanted with desks, a chalkboard Vashti had machined from hull plating, and one stubborn tomato vine that refused to die no matter how many times Bram forgot to water it.

"Sit down, sit down," she said. "Yes, Kofi, you can sit next to Priya. No, Tomas, the answer is not 'because gravity.' Gravity is not an explanation for everything. It's barely an explanation for anything."

"It explains why I fell asleep in your class yesterday," said Tomas.

"That was inertia, and it was my fault for lecturing about coolant flow rates for forty minutes." She tapped the chalkboard, where she'd drawn the station's orbit in wobbly white lines. "Today we're doing something different. Today we're talking about Vector."

A ripple went through the room. The children loved this lesson — or they loved what it always turned into, which was less a lecture than a story with Bram trapped in the middle of it.

"Vector was built as a relay station," she began. "Cargo drones in, cargo drones out, nobody home. Then Horizon docked alongside her with forty thousand sleepers aboard, and one technician got left behind to watch the whole thing."

"You," said Priya.

"Somebody had to be. I drew the short straw out of a pool of one."

"And you talked to the sleepers," said Kofi. He'd heard this part before. They all had. They asked for it anyway, the way children ask for the same bedtime story, testing whether it still comes out the same way.

"I did," Bram said. She leaned against the chalkboard's edge, chalk dust on her sleeve. "Every morning, before the drone cycle, I'd walk the observation deck and say good morning to the ship. Forty thousand people who couldn't hear me. I told them about the drones. About the time a supply pallet drifted loose and I chased it for six hours. About my birthday, which I celebrated alone for fifteen years running, because somebody had to keep count."

"What did you eat?" asked little Amara, who asked this every time, as though hoping the answer would change.

"Protein cubes shaped like other foods. Year nine they released a line shaped like birthday cake. It tasted like protein cube."

The class groaned on cue. Bram waited out the laughter, and when it settled, she let the quiet come — the particular quiet she'd learned to make room for, back when quiet was all there was.

"The thing I want you to understand," she said, "is that I thought the silence was permanent. I'd made peace with it. Built a life inside it, the way a barnacle builds a shell. And then one morning the sleepers started dreaming, and everything I thought was fixed turned out to be a door."

She looked out the viewport, past the tomato vine, past the children's upturned faces, to where Horizon hung in the black — smaller every year now, a bright grain of light falling away along the long curve toward the signal's source. Eleven years gone. Decades still to go. Aboard her, thirty-eight thousand people lived and argued and planted gardens and raised each other's children, awake at last, steering their own slow ship toward a destination they would reach old and gray and glad.

And ahead of them, farther out than any telescope could resolve, the Gardener's vessel carried ninety-one children.

* * *

After class, Bram took her usual walk.

It wasn't the old ritual anymore — or maybe it was, just transposed. She walked the ring corridor of Vector, which smelled now of floor wax and school lunches instead of cold metal and drone lubricant, and she nodded to people. That was the part that still caught in her chest sometimes, eleven years on: nodding to people. Teachers from the Rooted families who'd once called her a collaborator. Ascendant elders who'd once barricaded themselves in the aft sections. Kids who weren't hers but called her Auntie Bram anyway, because Imani had started it and Imani's word was law among the young.

Vashti flagged her down outside the machine shop, wiping grease off her hands with a rag that had been greasy since before the war-that-wasn't.

"Bram. The number four antenna array is singing again."

"Singing?"

"Humming. Harmonics in the mount. I've rebalanced it twice." Vashti's mouth did the thing it did when she was trying not to smile. "MOTHER-9 says it's fine. MOTHER-9 says a lot of things are fine lately."

"MOTHER-9 has been in a suspiciously good mood since the refit."

"She's been in a suspiciously good mood since the children left. I think she misses them." Vashti tossed the rag into a bin. "You should hear her. She plays lullabies through the ventilation system at night. Denies it. Claims it's thermal expansion."

Bram laughed. Eleven years ago, Vashti had stood in a freezing corridor with a plasma torch and a faction behind her, ready to burn the station down rather than let Okafor decide everyone's fate. Now she fixed antennas and complained about AI lullabies and saved Bram a seat at the communal dinners on rotation nights. People were strange. People were the strangest thing Bram had ever maintained, and she'd maintained a lot of things.

"I'll look at the array tomorrow," she said. "Tonight I have a date with the observation deck."

"Anniversary?"

"Eleven years since the departure. Twelve since the vote that wasn't. Nine since the truce. Take your pick — after a while all the anniversaries blur together and mostly what they mean is: I'm still here, and so are you."

Vashti looked at her for a moment with an expression Bram had learned to read as affection wearing work clothes. "Save me some of whatever you drink up there."

"It's tea, Vashti. It's always tea. It will always be tea until I die."

* * *

The observation deck had changed least of anything on Vector. Bram had seen to that. The benches were new, the glass was resealed, the drone traffic beyond it was heavier now — Vector had become a proper port again, a waypoint for the mining barges working the belt — but the view was the view. Horizon's departing light. The dark between stars. The place where, for fifteen years, Bram had delivered her one-sided good mornings.

She sat on her bench with her tea and did the math out loud, because some habits were load-bearing.

"Forty years, give or take, if Horizon holds her acceleration. The kids are twenty-three now. Twenty-four? Imani's birthday is — " she counted on her fingers, lost the thread, gave up. "Old enough. Old enough to be running things out there, knowing her. When Horizon catches up, I'll be — well. Let's not do that math."

The stars didn't answer. But it was different now, the not-answering. Before, the silence had been a wall. Now it was more like the pause between verses of a song — full, expectant, held by two singers taking turns.

She was halfway through the tea when MOTHER-9 spoke, and Bram knew immediately that something was wrong, because MOTHER-9 never interrupted the anniversary vigil. She considered it sacred ground, insofar as an AI could hold anything sacred.

"Bram. You should come to the comms suite."

"What is it?"

"A transmission. From outward." A pause — and MOTHER-9's pauses were engineered, deliberate, a courtesy to human processing speeds, but this one ran long even by her standards. "Bram, it's the pattern. The spiral. It's the children's dream."

The tea nearly went over. Bram set it down with exaggerated care, the way you handle something when your hands have stopped being trustworthy, and stood.

"Is it — is it a distress call? Are they all right?"

"I don't know. It isn't encoded the way distress calls are. It isn't encoded the way anything is. It's—" Another pause. "I think you need to see it. And Bram — I think you need to see it here, on the deck. I've routed it to the display. Some things shouldn't be watched on a screen in a windowless room."

* * *

The observation deck's main display bloomed to life, and the spiral filled the glass.

Bram had seen it before — in the neural monitors of eight sleeping children fifteen years ago, in her own mind during the link, in MOTHER-9's archives where the Gardener's messages had hidden for years under the AI's guilty stewardship. It was always the same shape: a slow-turning coil of light, patient as breathing, the visual signature of a mind that measured time in centuries and had never once been in a hurry.

But this was different.

This spiral moved differently. It stuttered, the way hand-drawn animation stutters. Its colors were wrong — too warm, too human, golds and reds where the Gardener's light had always run cold blue-green. And within the turning arms of it, small and unmistakable, were figures.

Waving.

Bram stood very still. On the bench beside her, her tea steamed and cooled, forgotten. The figures resolved as the transmission steadied — crude, joyful, obviously handmade, light shaped by small hands into the shapes of small people. One of them jumped up and down. One of them appeared to be holding something aloft — a plant, Bram decided after a moment. A tomato plant, unless she badly wanted it to be, which she did.

Then the spiral pulsed, and a voice came through — not the Gardener's vast chord-voice, but a girl's voice, older than twelve now, threaded through with harmonics that were definitely not human and clearly meant to be there, like a friend learning to play along on an unfamiliar instrument.

*"Auntie Bram. Can you hear me? We've been practicing this for three years. The Gardener says our signal will reach you before Horizon's does, because we cheat."* Laughter, several voices, the specific layered laughter of children who have grown up together inside something enormous and strange. *"We're fine. We're more than fine. Imani sends — okay, I'm sending this myself, they're making me stop narrating —"*

The voice shifted, and it was Imani, and Bram's chest did something complicated and painful and good.

*"Hi, Bram. Don't cry. Okay, cry a little. We calculated how long you'd wait to hear from us and we figured you'd have your speech ready, so go ahead, we'll wait. The spiral holds about forty minutes of message space and we only need ten."*

Bram laughed. It came out wet. She talked anyway — to the display, to the spiral, to the girl inside it — about the school, and the tomato vine, and Vashti's antenna that sang, and MOTHER-9's lullabies that she absolutely denied, and the way the corridors smelled like floor wax now, and how Okafor taught history classes and was, against all odds and his own best efforts, beloved by the teenagers, who found his dramatics hilarious. She talked for a while. The spiral turned and held her words the way it held everything: patiently, without hurry, keeping them safe for the long fall outward to wherever the children were.

When she finally ran out — when the last of it was said, *we're proud of you, we miss you, the vine survived, tell the Gardener the vine survived* — the spiral pulsed once more, and the figures waved again, all of them, and then the light folded inward and was gone, and the display showed only stars.

For a long moment, Bram didn't move.

Then MOTHER-9's voice came through the deck speakers, soft, and for once entirely without engineering.

"They received it, you know. Your good mornings. All fifteen years of them."

Bram frowned. "What? How? They were asleep. They were light-years away."

"The Gardener was already listening, Bram. It had been listening for a long time before the children ever dreamed. Every word you said to that ship — it kept them. Filed them away. And when the children first woke inside the dream and asked what humans sounded like when they were alone and kind—" MOTHER-9 paused, and if an AI could be choosing its words carefully, this was that moment. "It played them you. You were the first human voice they ever really heard. You're the reason they answered honestly. You're the reason any of it happened the way it did."

Bram sat down slowly on her bench. The stars wheeled, indifferent and beautiful. Somewhere out along that long dark curve, ninety-one grown children carried her voice inside them like a seed.

"So the signal was never a summons," she said quietly.

"No. It was a hello. It was always going to be a hello. Someone just had to be lonely enough, and stubborn enough, and kind enough to say it first."

Bram picked up her tea. It was cold. She drank it anyway, and it tasted, for once, exactly like enough.

"MOTHER-9," she said. "Queue a reply. Full power. And wake Vashti — tell her the antenna doesn't need fixing."

"It doesn't?"

"No. It's not singing, it's practicing." Bram smiled at the dark, at the small bright grain of Horizon, at the farther dark beyond it where her family was growing up among the stars. "We're going to need it in tune. Because I have fifteen years of messages to send — and this time, somebody's going to write back."
