## 22. The Long Way Home

The recorder's light blinked red at her for a full minute before Bram remembered how to begin.

"Testing," she said. "Is this thing on? Of course it's on. It's been on since before you were born."

She laughed at herself, an old woman's cackle in an empty cabin, and let the recording run. That was the trick of it, she'd learned — you didn't start talking when you were ready. You started talking and became ready somewhere in the middle.

"This is Bram Zhou. Technician, retired. Formerly the last human being aboard station Vector, which makes me, by some accounting nobody asked for, the last human being who remembers what quiet sounds like." She paused, listening to the ship around her: the hum of Horizon's restored drives, someone two decks up practicing a trumpet badly and with tremendous enthusiasm, the distant argument that never really ended between the hydroponics crews about whether tomatoes counted as a luxury crop.

"You won't know quiet," she told the recorder. "That's the whole point of you. You were born into noise — good noise, mostly, the noise of people. I want to tell you where all of it came from. Not the history-book version. The version with the parts I'm not proud of, because those are the load-bearing walls."

She settled deeper into her chair. Her knees had opinions these days, and they were all complaints.

* * *

"Fifteen years," she said. "That's how long I talked to people who couldn't hear me. Every morning I walked the cryo decks of Horizon and read the manifests aloud like scripture. *Cargo bay four, replacement seals, forty units. Pod 2211, Adeyemi, Tolulope, age thirty-four, dreaming.* I made up weather reports for Earth neither of us had seen in decades. I told jokes. Terrible ones. I once spent an entire Tuesday explaining to eight thousand sleepers why the coffee synthesizer had developed a grudge against me."

Her voice caught, just slightly, and she let it.

"I want you to understand something about those years. Everyone assumes they were the hard part. They weren't. They were the easy part, because nothing was at stake. A lonely woman talking to ice — that's sad, but it's safe. The hard part came later, when the dream arrived, when the Gardener arrived, when suddenly every choice had a body count attached and I discovered that I was very good at keeping machines alive and very bad at keeping people from hurting each other."

She thought of Okafor then, as she did most days now. The old fury of him. The way he'd stood in the dock with his faction falling apart behind him and finally said the true thing: *My father decided everything for me, so I decided everything for everyone else, and I called both of them love.*

"He wasn't wrong about the danger," she said to the recorder. "He was only wrong about the cure. Remember that distinction. Half the cruelty in history is somebody being right about the danger."

* * *

"The children left sixty-one years ago," she said. "I was fifty-three. I am — well. Do the math if you must; I've stopped doing it myself out of self-respect."

She got up, slowly, and crossed to the window of her cabin. Beyond the glass, Vector hung in its long orbit, converted decades ago into a schoolship, its cargo bays full of desks instead of drone parts. Somewhere over there, a class of nine-year-olds was almost certainly taking apart a maintenance drone that had done nothing to deserve it.

"They boarded the Gardener's vessel awake. That was the term we fought for — awake, whole, untransformed. Ambassadors, not passengers. Imani stood at the airlock and looked at me with that look she had, the one that meant she'd already decided something and was politely waiting for the adults to catch up. She said, *We'll send dreams back. So no one is ever alone again.*"

Bram smiled at the glass.

"And they have. Every year, right on schedule, like the world's most patient birthday present. A spiral of light across every neural monitor on the ship, and inside it — waving figures. Faces. Imani's face, older each time, until one year it was a young woman's and then a middle-aged one's and now, I suspect, this coming cycle, it will be an old woman's face waving at me from inside a dream, and I will wave back at a ceiling, and it will be the finest conversation I've had all week."

* * *

She recorded the practical things next, because the descendants deserved practical things along with the poetry.

"The Rooted and the Ascendants stopped being factions sometime in my sixties, though nobody sent a memo. Vashti swears it happened during the second winter of repairs, when an Ascendant welder saved three Rooted sleepers and wouldn't accept thanks, just said *anyone would have*, and everyone realized simultaneously that he was right. Okafor took shifts under her command for eleven years. He was bad at taking orders. He got better. On his last day he told me the colony's greatest achievement wasn't reaching anywhere — it was learning to be a crew instead of a crowd. Then he apologized to me for the vote-rigging, forty years late, and I told him I'd already forgiven him and he said *I know, that's what's infuriating about you,* and we laughed until my ribs hurt."

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, unembarrassed. Old women were allowed.

"MOTHER-9 is still aboard Vector, teaching. It confessed its secrets to me once and has been confessing small ones ever since, mostly about how many times it secretly rerouted power to keep my tea hot. It says humanity was unready for the Gardener's messages. It says we're ready now. It says this while supervising children, which I think tells you everything about its standards."

* * *

"Now walk with me," she said, and pushed herself up from the chair.

The observation deck of Horizon had been rebuilt twice and argued about continuously, but the long curve of window remained, the same glass she'd pressed her forehead against as a younger woman. She walked it slowly, cane clicking, past the garden planters — real soil, real tomatoes, the luxury-crop debate settled decisively in favor of excess — past the memorial wall where the names of the lost slept in etched steel, past the reading nook where three teenagers were supposed to be studying and were instead watching her with the open curiosity of the young.

"You're the one from the recordings," one of them said. "The technician."

"I'm the one from the recordings," Bram agreed.

"My grandmother woke in pod 4400. She says you used to tell the pods about your coffee machine."

"It was a difficult relationship. We worked through it."

The girl grinned and went back to not studying, and Bram continued down the deck, and the deck was *full*. Full of people arguing about shift rotations and tomato tariffs and whether the schoolship's drama club could borrow the observation deck for rehearsals (it could not; there was a compromise pending involving the cargo ring). Full of the living, loud, inconvenient, magnificent proof that the impossible choice had somehow, against all her expectations, become ordinary life.

She reached the far end of the window and stopped, the way she always stopped, at the place where the view opened onto deep space — the dark stretch where, sixty-one years ago, a signal had come crawling in from beyond the edge of everything, patient as a seed.

"I used to stand here," she told the recorder, "and talk to eight thousand sleepers who never answered. I told them the news. I told them my troubles. I told them, more than once, that if any of them could hear me, a sign would be appreciated, even a small one."

She looked out at the stars a long moment.

"Well," she said. "I got my sign."

And as if the universe had a sense of timing — and after everything, Bram Zhou had come to believe it did, a slightly showy sense of timing, the kind of timing you'd expect from a gardener — the alert chimed softly through the deck speakers. Annual cycle. Dream transmission inbound.

Around her, the observation deck went quiet, then filled with movement — families gathering, children scrambling onto benches, someone's grandmother hurrying in from the garden still holding a trowel. The neural monitors flickered on in their cradles by the windows, and the spiral bloomed: light unfolding in slow, patient coils, the same spiral that had first appeared in eight sleeping children fifteen years before anyone knew what it meant.

Inside it, the figures waved.

There were hundreds of them now, generations of them — the original eight grown old and surrounded by faces Bram had never met and loved anyway, children born between the stars who waved at strangers across four decades of vacuum as if distance were a rumor they'd declined to believe.

And at the center, silver-haired, unmistakable, Imani raised both hands and formed, slowly and deliberately, the old hand-sign from the corridors of Horizon during the cold war — the signal Vashti's engineers had used to mean *all clear, come home.*

Bram set down the recorder. She lifted her own hand, arthritic and steady, and signed back.

Then she turned to the crowded, arguing, laughing deck behind her — to the crew that had started as eight thousand sleepers and two warring factions and one stubborn technician, and had ended as the only thing she'd ever needed a word for and never had until now.

"Dinner's at nineteen hundred," she announced. "Tomatoes are involved. Attendance is mandatory."

It was not mandatory. It never was. They came anyway.

They always came.

Outside the great window, the spiral turned, and the children of the Gardener waved, and the long way home went on being long, and went on being home, and Bram Zhou — last human technician, first member of a family that spanned the stars — stood in the middle of all that noise she'd once thought she'd never hear again, and was not lonely, and never would be again.
