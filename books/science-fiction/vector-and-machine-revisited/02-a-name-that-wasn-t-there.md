## 2. A Name That Wasn't There

The trouble with living alone for ten years, Isandro Hartwell had decided somewhere around year six, was not the silence. The silence was fine. The silence was practically a colleague. You could rely on the silence to show up every morning, sit quietly in the corner of the comms room while you worked, and never once ask you to help it move a couch.

No — the trouble with living alone for ten years was that eventually you started narrating your own life out loud, and there was no one left to tell you to stop.

"Manifest reconciliation," she announced to the room, in the tone of a woman unveiling a national monument. "Day three thousand six hundred and fifty-two. Attendance: one. Snacks: yes."

The vault did not applaud. It never did. But somewhere below her feet, three hundred meters of permafrost and steel, the geothermal taps hummed their low note of agreement, and that was as close to applause as Svalbard ever gave.

She settled into her chair at the custodian's console — a slab of matte grey composite that had been old when her predecessor had been young, whoever he'd been — and pulled up the morning's work. Manifest reconciliation was her favorite chore, which said something either about her character or about the limited menu of chores available at the top of the world. Every ship, crate, pallet, and seed accession that had ever passed through the vault's logistics chain lived in the archive, and once a week she ran the reconciliation: comparing what the records said against what the sensors said against what the physical inventory said, hunting for drift.

Drift was normal. Drift was honest. A seal aged, a sensor miscalibrated by half a degree, a barcode faded under forty years of fluorescent light. Drift was the universe shrugging its shoulders and saying, *close enough.*

What Isandro loved about reconciliation was that it always ended the same way: everything accounted for, nothing missing, the world still holding its shape. It was a small daily proof that the machinery of civilization could be tended, that someone — one someone, specifically, currently wearing two sweaters and wool socks patterned with cartoon puffins — was keeping the books balanced.

She sipped her coffee. She opened the shipping registry.

And that was when she saw the name.

*Vess Okonkwo.*

It sat on line 214 of the Meridian's crew manifest, tucked between *Okafor, D.* and *Olsen, T.* with the bland confidence of a word that had always been there. Isandro stared at it over the rim of her mug for a long moment, the way you stare at a picture on your wall that has suddenly, subtly, changed.

"That's new," she told the room.

The room declined to comment.

Here was the thing about the Meridian: the Meridian had never flown. It was one of the first entries Isandro had ever memorized, back in her bewildered first month as custodian, because it was such a strange little fossil in the record. A heavy-lift orbital transfer vessel, registered to the vault's founding consortium, logged through eleven months of ground trials at the Andøya facility — engine burns, avionics checks, a full crew complement of forty — and then, on the eve of its maiden launch, struck from the rolls. Scrapped on paper. Decommissioned before it ever cleared the tower. The official addendum, typed in the flat bureaucratic voice of people closing accounts, read simply: *Program concluded. Assets redistributed. Crew reassigned.*

Except the crew hadn't been reassigned anywhere. Isandro had checked, early on, out of the same idle curiosity that made her read the seed accessions like novels. Thirty-nine names on the manifest, thirty-nine dead ends. No transfer orders, no service records, no obituaries. Forty names, actually — no. Thirty-nine. She was sure it had been thirty-nine.

She scrolled back up to count.

Forty.

Her coffee was getting cold. She drank it anyway, because waste was a sin above the Arctic Circle, and because the warmth in her hands gave her something to do besides feel the small cold finger trace its way down her spine.

Names didn't appear in the archive. That was the whole point of the archive. The vault's records were write-once, checksummed, mirrored across three physically separate storage banks so that no single failure — or single hand — could quietly edit history. In ten years of reconciliations she had found misaligned timestamps, corrupted sensor feeds, one memorable incident where a mouse had gotten into Gallery North and chewed through a cable, briefly convincing the system that four hundred kilograms of wheat had achieved sentience. She had never once found a name where a name wasn't.

She ran the checksums. All three banks agreed: *Okonkwo, Vess* had been on the Meridian's manifest since the record was created. Not added. Not appended. *Original.* As if she had imagined the number thirty-nine. As if the archive remembered a version of history she didn't.

"Okay," Isandro said slowly, to the mouse of four years ago, to her predecessor, to God, to the puffins on her socks. "Okay. Let's not panic. Let's be professionals."

Professionals, she decided, would check the other manifests. She pulled the crew rosters for all seven vessels associated with the founding consortium and ran them side by side. Six of them matched her memory exactly, down to the middle initials. On the seventh — the Meridian — forty names sat in tidy rows, and the fortieth glowed faintly in her attention like a bruise.

*Okonkwo, Vess. Flight systems engineer. Cryo-support specialist.*

Cryo-support. Her eyes caught on the phrase and refused to move past it. Of all the specializations a ground-trials engineer might carry, cryo-support was the one that meant nothing for a ship that never flew — unless the ship's real mission had been something the paperwork never mentioned. Unless "ground trials" had been a polite fiction, and the trials had gone deeper than the ground.

She was still staring when the console chimed.

Not the mail chime. Not the sensor-drift chime. This was the deep chime, the one-note toll from the beacon's own monitoring daemon, a sound Isandro had heard exactly twice in ten years: once during a lightning storm that had walked across the plateau like a drunk giant, and once on her first day, when her predecessor's final automated message had played and then deleted itself, leaving her alone with a job description and a set of keys.

*Power draw anomaly,* the daemon reported, with the gentle insistence of a smoke detector. *Beacon load +0.4%. Sustained.*

Isandro set down her mug very carefully, the way you do when your hands have opinions of their own.

The beacon drew power the way a lighthouse draws breath: constantly, patiently, in amounts so stable that its consumption curve was functionally a flat line. She had the last decade of it graphed on her wall, actually — a long horizontal ribbon of green, unbroken, reassuring, the most boring chart in the world, and she had come to love it the way sailors love the horizon. Ten years. One flat line. Whatever the beacon was doing out there, whispering its loop into the dark toward whatever it whispered to, it did so with the metronome constancy of a heart.

Now the line ticked upward. Half a percent. Trivial, by any engineering standard. Catastrophic, by the standards of a thing that had not varied since before she arrived.

She pulled the trend window wide. The rise hadn't started this morning. It had started — she checked the timestamp twice, then a third time, then laughed once, a short startled sound with no humor in it — ninety-one hours ago. Three days and change of slow, steady climb, so gradual that the monitoring daemon's thresholds hadn't tripped until now. Three days ago she had been doing what, exactly? Inventory in Gallery South. Fixing the hot water heater, which had required lying on her back in a crawl space swearing at a fitting. Ordinary days. Unwitnessed days.

Something down there — or up there, or wherever the beacon's appetite reached — had begun to stir three days ago, and it had done it quietly, politely, at a rate designed not to be noticed. Which raised a question Isandro found herself unable to put down: things that wake by accident are clumsy. Things that wake carefully have woken on purpose.

"Curious," she said aloud, because saying *terrified* out loud felt premature, and a person should let her emotions file the proper paperwork before making announcements.

She brought up the beacon's subsystem view. Most of it was the usual architecture — antenna arrays, the transmission loop scheduler, the power conditioning stack — rendered in the dense, unlabeled schematic style of engineers who assumed the only future reader would be another engineer who already knew everything. But at the bottom of the power tree, feeding into the main bus through a junction she had never had reason to open, sat a branch labeled only:

`SUBLEVEL FEED — CRYO SUPPORT — DO NOT DEACTIVATE`

Well. There it was again. *Cryo support.* Twice in one morning, in two places that were supposed to have nothing to do with each other. Isandro looked at the label for a long time. It had the tone of a note left on an office refrigerator: imperative, slightly rude, addressed to no one in particular, and absolutely certain that whoever read it would obey.

She thought about her predecessor. She thought about him more than she admitted to anyone, which was easy, since there was no anyone. He had left her a sealed letter she'd never been able to bring herself to open — it sat in her desk drawer beneath a tin of pencils, and every year on her custodianship anniversary she took it out, held it, and put it back, telling herself she'd read it when she needed it. She had never been able to define what *needing it* would look like. She was beginning to get an idea.

Because here was the arithmetic that wouldn't leave her alone: the Meridian had never flown, but its crew manifest had just grown a name. The beacon had never varied, but its power draw had just begun to climb. And both changes pointed, with the tactful insistence of a cat sitting on a newspaper, at the same word: *cryo.*

Isandro had spent ten years believing she understood the shape of her kingdom. Seeds above, servers below, beacon on the surface whispering to the sky. A closed system, tended and known. Now the system had produced a name out of nowhere and an appetite out of stillness, and the two facts fit together with a click she could hear in her teeth.

There was something under the vault. Something that drew power through a line marked DO NOT DEACTIVATE. Something that had, three days and some hours ago, begun — carefully, deliberately — to use more of it.

She stood up, wrapped her cardigan tighter, and looked out the frosted window at the white plateau, at the antenna array standing patient against the grey sky, at the beautiful indifferent Arctic morning. Somewhere under her feet, in the dark and the cold, forty names' worth of unfinished business was warming up.

"You know," she informed the empty room, pulling on her boots, "in the stories, this is the part where the sensible person locks the door and waits for daylight."

The sensible person, however, had a flashlight, a thermal jacket, and ten years of unanswered questions compounding interest. She clipped the flashlight to her belt, paused at the desk drawer, and — after a moment — took out the sealed letter and slid it into her inside pocket. Unopened. Just closer.

"For emergencies," she explained to the puffins.

The sub-level access was behind Gallery Seven, down a corridor her maps marked with the least imaginative lie in the entire archive: `STORAGE — NO ACCESS REQUIRED`. The door at its end was frost-rimed, seamless, and warm.

Warm. At minus eighteen ambient. Isandro pressed her palm flat against the metal and felt, faintly, unmistakably, through ten years of concrete and permafrost and careful solitude, a pulse of machinery working harder than it had yesterday.

Behind the door, something that had been sleeping for a very long time was waking up. And it had brought a name with it.

"Well," said Isandro Hartwell, keeper of quiet rooms, to nobody, in a voice that only shook a little. "Let's go meet the crew."
