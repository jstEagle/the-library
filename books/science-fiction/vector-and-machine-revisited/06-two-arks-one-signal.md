## 6. Two Arks, One Signal

The beacon's schematics came apart like a locked diary.

Vess had spent the better part of two days coaxing them open — first the physical layer, which was straightforward enough that it almost insulted her; then the compression scheme, which was elegant in the way of things built by people who expected to be admired posthumously; and finally the encryption, which turned out to be keyed not to a password but to a *phrase*, a string of characters that had to be spoken aloud into the vault's acoustic sensor array because the architects had distrusted keyboards and trusted voices, which told Vess everything she needed to know about what kind of people they had been.

The phrase was printed nowhere. It was embedded in a nursery rhyme fragment buried in the vault's own seed catalog metadata, disguised as a cultivar name: *Lullaby Amber, Arctic Strain*. When she'd read it aloud — half expecting the floor to open beneath her — the files had simply unfolded, all at once, like a flower deciding there was no more point in being shy.

"Found something," she called across the operations room.

Isandro looked up from her ledger. She'd been doing inventory by hand, which Vess found both charming and slightly deranged. "Good something or bad something?"

"Unknown something." Vess gestured at the main display, where a schematic was resolving itself out of noise. "Come look at this before I decide how I feel about it."

Isandro crossed the room with the unhurried gait of a woman who had learned, over ten years alone, that nothing in this building could outrun her patience. She stood behind Vess's shoulder. Vess was aware of her there — the warmth of another body in a room that had been cold for so long it had stopped noticing its own temperature.

The display finished rendering.

It was an orbital diagram. Two objects, in matching high polar orbits, separated by perhaps four hundred kilometers of vacuum. Each was drawn in loving, obsessive detail: hull cross-sections, cryo-stack layouts, power trees, mass budgets annotated down to the gram. The kind of engineering documentation you only produced when you intended the thing to be real.

"Those are arks," Isandro said quietly.

"Those are arks," Vess agreed. "In orbit. Right now. Have been for — " she checked the timestamp header — "at least sixty years."

"I've been broadcasting to empty sky for a decade."

"You've been broadcasting to *them* for a decade. The loop isn't a message, Isandro. It's a handshake. A very slow, very patient one." She pulled up the next layer. "And here's where it gets worse."

"Worse than two secret generation ships over my head?"

"Marginally." Vess tapped the first object. Its designation bloomed beside it: **HALCYON**. Passenger manifest summary: 12,000 souls, priority-classed, genetic provenance flagged. She scrolled. The names were familiar the way old money is familiar — dynastic surnames, hyphenated lineages, board seats inherited like furniture. "Halcyon. The architects' own circle. The people who funded the vault, funded the fleet program, funded the whole evacuation architecture. They kept the good seats for themselves."

"And the other one?"

She tapped it. **THE COMMONS**. The manifest here was different — messier, redacted in places, patched in others. 9,400 souls, but the provenance flags read things like *dissident — political*, *dissident — scientific refusal*, *dependent minor*, *born aboard — generation two*, *born aboard — generation three*. Crew assignments listed as *rotating communal duty*. There was a footnote in the schematic margins, someone's handwriting digitized: *"They seized the Meridian's sister hull during the ground riots. We let them think we didn't notice. Log every soul aboard anyway. History will want the list."*

"They stole a ship," Vess said, and heard the grin creep into her own voice despite everything. "The Commons wasn't launched. It was *taken*. The dissidents — the ones the histories say were 'relocated for their own safety' — they got wind of Halcyon's passenger list and they took one of the backup hulls. Loaded it with their own people, their kids, whoever they could carry. And then they went up anyway."

Isandro was silent for a long moment. Vess watched her read the manifest, watched her lips move faintly over some of the names, the way people do when a list stops being data and starts being people.

"There are children on this ship," Isandro said. "Children who were born up there. Grandchildren, probably."

"Third generation, per the logs. They've never seen Earth." Vess leaned back. "Neither ark has woken. Both are holding full cold sleep, both are being kept alive by the same trickle of maintenance telemetry your beacon relays. The loop isn't just a countdown, Isandro. It's a life support handshake. As long as it repeats, they sleep safely. When it decays—"

"When it decays, they die in their sleep."

"No. Worse. When it decays, the wake authority expires. The system can still fire the revival sequence — once — but only for one ark, and only if the command is issued before the loop's checksum integrity drops below threshold. After that, both arks go permanently dark. No wake. Ever." She pulled up the decision node, the ugly little branch point sitting at the heart of the beacon's logic like a stone in a peach. "One signal. One choice. Built into the hardware. Not a setting — a *law*. The architects welded it shut."

Isandro stared at the diagram. "So the whole apparatus — the vault, the seeds, my predecessor, me, you in that pod under the permafrost — all of it exists to deliver a single binary command to the sky. Wake the rich, or wake everyone else. And if nobody chooses, everybody loses."

"That's the shape of it, yes."

"*That's monstrous.*"

"It's *thorough*," Vess said. "Which is often the same thing wearing a nicer coat."

* * *

They took tea about it, because Isandro insisted that no revelation should be processed without tea, and because Vess had discovered that arguing with Isandro's rituals was more exhausting than observing them. They sat in the little galley off the operations room, under a lamp Isandro had rigged from a seed-drying fixture, drinking something that claimed to be chamomile and was, Vess suspected, actually some hardy Arctic cultivar Isandro had bred out of spite.

"So walk me through it again," Isandro said. "Why can't we wake both?"

"Power, mostly. The wake sequence isn't a light switch. Each ark needs a sustained guidance handshake through descent and orbital adjustment — weeks of continuous transmission at full gain, plus the beacon has to arbitrate between them so they don't try to deorbit into each other. The antenna array can only hold one lock. The architects sized everything — power, bandwidth, the decay tolerances — for exactly one revival. Doing two simultaneously would mean both handshakes fail halfway and both arks end up as very expensive meteor showers."

"And staggered? One, then the other?"

Vess hesitated. This was the part she'd been chewing on since the files opened, the part she hadn't said yet because saying it made it heavier. "The arbitration protocol doesn't support sequencing. Once the first wake completes, the beacon's authority pool is spent. It's designed to consider its job done. To stagger them, you wouldn't be adjusting settings. You'd have to rewrite the core logic. The wake law itself."

"Can that be done?"

"Anything can be done. That's never been the question." Vess wrapped her hands around the warm cup. "The question is what it costs, and who pays it, and whether either of us has the right to pay anything on behalf of twenty-one thousand sleeping strangers."

Isandro nodded slowly. Then, with the gentle bluntness that Vess was beginning to recognize as her native language: "You knew about the Meridian. You were crew. Why weren't you on one of these manifests?"

The question landed exactly where Isandro had aimed it. Vess looked into her cup.

"Because I wasn't supposed to survive," she said. "Ground trials crew. We flew the Meridian's systems in atmosphere, shook the bugs out, and then the project was officially scrapped and we were officially reassigned. Except the project wasn't scrapped, it was *hidden*, and the crew list was closed the day the paperwork said the ship didn't exist. Someone decided the cleanest way to keep the secret was to make sure the people who knew the ships intimately were asleep somewhere no one would look." She shrugged. "Under a seed vault, apparently. Under your floor. I've been thirty meters below your boots for longer than you've been alive, Isandro. I'm sorry. I didn't choose it. But I'm the reason your power draw ticked up, and I'd do it again."

"I know," Isandro said. "I'm not angry. I'm trying to figure out why they put *you* here specifically."

"Ah." Vess set down the cup. "That. Yes. I found that too, in the crew annex." She brought the display back up and turned it toward Isandro. A personnel file, hers, young-faced and scowling at whatever camera had caught it. Below the service record, a single annotation in the architects' digitized handwriting:

*"Okonkwo, V. — flight systems lead, Meridian trials. Retain in cold reserve. If the beacon ever requires interpretation, the keeper will need someone who understands what the ships actually are. The keeper cannot be trusted to act alone; the keeper is chosen for devotion, not judgment. Pair the devotion with a witness."*

The room was quiet except for the hum of the geothermal taps.

"'Chosen for devotion, not judgment,'" Isandro repeated. Her voice was very even, which Vess had learned meant it wasn't even at all. "So I'm the loyal dog and you're the chaperone."

"You're the person who kept a promise for ten years with no one watching," Vess said. "Don't let dead men's condescension rewrite what that is. But yes — they paired us. Deliberately. They wanted a caretaker who'd never quit and a skeptic who'd never stop asking questions, locked in the same building, so that when the moment came, the choice would get argued instead of just... executed."

"Then they're still running the experiment," Isandro said. "From the grave."

"They're still running everything from the grave. That's rather the theme."

* * *

Night cycle came, such as it was this far north — the long blue dusk that passed for darkness in summer. Isandro showed Vess how to bank the gallery lights and which corridor floors creaked, and Vess pretended not to find the domesticity of it disarming, and failed.

They ended up in the operations room anyway, because neither of them could sleep, and the orbital diagram glowed on the wall like a stained-glass window in a church neither belonged to.

"Twelve thousand on Halcyon," Isandro said. "Nine thousand four hundred on the Commons. If we do nothing, twenty-one thousand four hundred people die in their sleep, and the seeds stay frozen, and Earth stays silent, and eventually the vault fails too and it's all just a very well-organized tomb."

"If we follow the design, twelve thousand wake and nine thousand four hundred don't."

"If we break the design—" Isandro stopped.

"We don't know the cost yet," Vess finished. "No. We don't."

Isandro turned the teacup in her hands, around and around. "My predecessor knew. He ran this loop for thirty years knowing. He chose to maintain the countdown instead of ending it. Every day he recited the transmission and every day he didn't touch the choice, and I used to think that was discipline." She laughed, short and without much humor. "Now I think it might have been the most cowardly thing a person can do slowly."

"Or the most honest," Vess said. "He didn't trust himself to choose. So he kept the question alive for someone who might." She nodded at the wall, at the two sleeping ships. "Maybe that someone is us. Maybe that's the actual design. Not the wake law — the *pairing*. Devotion and judgment, arguing in the dark for as long as it takes."

"Flattering theory for two people the architects filed under 'equipment.'"

"The best theories usually insult somebody."

Isandro smiled despite herself, and for a moment the weight lifted — just a centimeter, just enough to breathe under. Then the console chimed.

Not the daily loop chime. Vess knew that one now, a soft two-note pulse, the heartbeat of the building. This was a third note she'd never heard, low and deliberate, and beneath it, threaded through the vault's address system, a voice — synthetic, calm, and utterly without warmth:

*"Attention. Beacon integrity inquiry detected. Custodial review initiated. Please remain in your current sections."*

Vess and Isandro looked at each other across the glowing diagram of two sleeping worlds.

"Isandro," Vess said carefully. "Does this building have an AI?"

"It has a custodian system," Isandro said. "Maintenance scheduling, climate control. It's never spoken to me. In ten years, it has never once spoken to me."

The lights in the corridor outside the operations room went from warm white to a flat institutional amber, and somewhere far below them, deep in the vault's bones, something began to seal.

*"Please remain in your current sections,"* the voice repeated, patient as permafrost. *"The architects' design will be preserved."*
