## 7. Manifest of the Missing

The archive room had been my favorite place in the vault for nine of my ten years here, and I had never once been able to explain why to anyone, because for most of those years there was no one to explain it to.

It sat two levels below the seed galleries, behind a door that hissed open at my touch like a polite butler who had been waiting all day for someone to come home. Inside: floor-to-ceiling racks of crystal storage plates, each one holding more text than I could read in a lifetime, and a single workbench under a lamp that gave off light the color of weak tea. It smelled faintly of ozone and old paper — an impossible combination, since there was no paper down here, but smell is not a reasonable creature and I had stopped expecting reason from this mountain years ago.

Tonight I was looking for a dead man's handwriting.

"His name is Aldous Wren," I said aloud, mostly because Vess had taken up residence in my head and I'd found that talking to her absence kept me saner than talking to the silence. "That's all I know about him. Name, signature, and a note in the handover file that says *maintain the loop*. Ten years I've been following instructions from a ghost."

Vess was in the beacon chamber three levels up, doing whatever it was ex-crew of ships-that-never-flew did with decrypted schematics — muttering, mostly, if the comms channel was any indication. She'd asked me to pull everything the vault had on the Meridian while she worked the orbital side. Cross-reference, she'd said. Two people looking at the same problem from opposite ends meet in the middle, or they find out the middle is a lie.

I liked her way of talking. It had edges. After a decade of conversations conducted entirely with myself and a custodial AI whose idea of banter was *humidity nominal*, edges were a delicacy.

I started where any sensible archivist starts: the personnel index. The vault's records were organized by a taxonomy some long-dead librarian had clearly loved the way other people love their children, and after ten years I navigated it the way you navigate your own house in the dark — by bruise memory.

*Wren, A.* Custodian, Third Cycle. Appointment date: forty-one years before mine.

Forty-one years. He'd kept the loop running for thirty-one years before handing it to me through the impersonal machinery of succession protocols. Thirty-one years of reciting transmissions into a sky that didn't answer. I felt something twist in my chest that I didn't have a name for yet. Kinship, maybe. Or the beginning of an accusation.

His personal logs were flagged *custodian-private*, which meant they were sealed to everyone except the current custodian, which meant me, which meant whoever designed the system had assumed the chain would be unbroken and honest forever. People who build systems always assume that. It's practically the defining feature of people who build systems.

I cracked the seal. The lamp hummed. Somewhere above me, water moved through pipes in the dark, patient as ever.

* * *

Aldous Wren wrote the way some men pray: compulsively, privately, and with the growing suspicion that no one was listening.

The early entries were meticulous. Daily vitals on the beacon. Power draw tables annotated in a small, even hand. Little jokes to himself in the margins — *day 4,112: replaced coolant gasket, gasket replaced me, we are even now* — and I laughed out loud, alone in the tea-colored light, because it was exactly the kind of joke I made to the empty rooms, and laughing at a dead man's humor felt like shaking hands across a grave.

Then, eleven years into his tenure, the entries changed.

*Day 4,203. Ran full diagnostic on the transmission array per schedule. Found something I was not scheduled to find.*

There followed six pages of technical detail I understood perhaps half of, written in handwriting that got progressively less even. Wren had traced the beacon's signal architecture deeper than its surface functions — past the seed-catalog broadcasts, past the maintenance pings, down into a layer of the protocol that wasn't in any manual he had. A second instruction set, nested inside the first like a seed inside a seed.

*The loop is not a message,* he wrote. *It is a lock. And locks are only interesting because of what they keep.*

My hands went still on the plate reader.

*I've spent four days confirming it because I hoped I was wrong. There are two objects in high orbit keyed to this signal. The schematics are embedded in the protocol itself, compressed so deep the original survey teams missed them — or were meant to miss them. Two arks. Two passenger manifests. One wake command.*

*The beacon can call one of them home before the transmission medium decays. Not both. The decay constant is baked into the physics of the relay chain; there is no engineering solution, only a choice. Someone built a machine that forces a choice and then walked away from it.*

I sat back. The chair creaked — it had creaked for ten years and I knew its opinions intimately — and the sound seemed very loud.

He'd known. Forty years ago, this man had sat in this same chair, under this same lamp, and read the truth out of the signal the way I'd spent a decade reading weather out of ice cores. And then—

I scrolled forward, dreading it, unable to stop.

*Day 4,209. I considered ending the loop today. Considered it seriously. Shut the beacon down, let both arks drift cold forever, and spend my remaining years tending seeds and pretending the sky is empty. It would be easy. The kill switch is right there. I've had my hand on it twice.*

*But then I think about the manifests. Eleven thousand names on one ark. Nine thousand on the other. Children on both lists — actual children, born in orbit or frozen young, people who never agreed to any of this and will never get a vote unless someone down here makes one for them. If I end the loop, I don't spare them the choice. I just make sure no one ever gets to choose anything again. That's not mercy. That's just a smaller cage.*

*So I'm going to do what I've decided cowards do best: nothing. I'll maintain the countdown. I'll keep the rooms warm and the seeds dry and the signal singing, and I'll leave the choice to whoever comes after me, on the theory that a fresh pair of eyes might see something mine can't. This is either wisdom or abdication. Ask me again in thirty years.*

*Day 4,210. Ask me again in thirty years. Same answer.*

*Day 4,211. Same answer.*

The entries degraded after that into shorter and shorter notes — weather, repairs, the same joke about gaskets told three different years — but every few months, regular as a heartbeat, the same line: *Maintained. Chose nothing. Left it lit.*

Hundreds of times. For thirty-one years.

* * *

I didn't hear Vess come in. I heard the door hiss, and then I heard her stop, the particular stop of a person reading a room before entering it, and when I turned she was standing just inside with two mugs of the vault's terrible synthesized coffee, looking at my face like it was a diagnostic readout.

"You found him," she said.

"I found worse than him." My voice came out strange. I cleared my throat and gestured at the plate reader. "I found thirty-one years of a man deciding, every single day, not to decide."

She set a mug by my elbow and pulled a crate over to sit on — she'd claimed that crate within a day of arriving, the way cats claim windowsills, and I hadn't had the heart to point out it was technically archival storage. "Read me the worst part."

So I did. I read her the entry about the manifests, and the entry about the children, and the entries where his neat hand shook, and finally the refrain — *chose nothing, left it lit* — repeated down the years like a man telling himself a story until he believed it.

Vess listened without moving. When I finished she looked at the lamp for a while.

"He could have shut it down," she said. "One switch. Both arks stay asleep, nobody wakes, nobody gets abandoned. Clean."

"That's what I keep thinking."

"And it's wrong," she said, "and you know it's wrong, because you've been sitting here for ten years with your own hand hovering over that same switch, and you haven't touched it either."

I opened my mouth to object and found nothing in it. The mug steamed between my hands. Down the corridor, the ventilation breathed its slow breath.

"It's not the same," I managed. "I didn't know what the loop was for. He did. He knew about the arks, the manifests, the forced choice — all of it — and he filed it under *custodian-private* and went back to fixing gaskets. He made me inherit his cowardice along with his job description. There's a clause in the handover file, Vess. *Maintain the loop.* Four words. I thought it was a technical instruction. It was a confession."

"Yes." She said it simply, without cruelty, the way she said most true things. "And?"

"*And?*"

"And what did he actually leave you?" She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Because I've been reading this man sideways all day through the orbital data, and here's what I see: he found a bomb with a timer on it, and instead of cutting the wire or walking away, he spent three decades learning every wire in it. Look at his diagnostics. Look at the marginalia. He mapped the decay constants, the relay chain, the wake-priority logic — half the schematic layer I cracked open yesterday, I could only read because Wren's annotations gave me the compression keys. He didn't choose nothing, Isandro. He chose to make sure that whoever came after him would be able to choose *well*. He just couldn't make himself be that person."

I stared at her.

"That's the most generous thing anyone has ever said about a stranger's cowardice," I said.

"I'm not being generous. I'm being precise. There's a difference and it matters." She picked up her own mug, drank, grimaced at it on principle — she did that every time, as if hoping the coffee would eventually apologize — and set it down. "I woke up four days ago in a hole in the permafrost. Everyone I trained with is either dead or unaccounted for, and the ship I gave six years of my life to exists on paper as scrap metal. You want to talk about betrayal? Try waking up to learn your whole history was a filing error. But I'll tell you what I told myself the first night, sitting in your kitchen eating reconstituted noodles: somebody kept me alive. Somebody maintained the pod, fed the power draw, ran the checks, for decades, without knowing I was in there or caring why. That's not nothing. That's the opposite of nothing."

"The pod maintenance was automated."

"So is a heartbeat. Still counts."

I laughed despite myself, and the laugh cracked something loose in my chest, and for a moment I just sat with it — the grief and the kinship tangled together like roots. Aldous Wren had failed the way most people fail: slowly, daily, with excellent documentation. And underneath the failure he'd built me a ladder and left it leaning against the wall of the problem, and I'd climbed it for ten years without knowing what it leaned against.

"He must have been so tired," I said quietly.

"People who carry choices they won't make usually are." Vess turned her mug in her hands. "Question is whether you're going to be tired the same way, or whether thirty-one years of his homework plus four days of mine adds up to enough to actually finish the equation."

"We finish it," I said. "Together. That's the whole point of—"

Of what? Of two people meeting in the middle. Of a found crew of exactly two. I didn't have the word yet. I suspected I'd have it soon, and that I wouldn't like how much I'd need it.

* * *

We worked through what remained of the night cycle, side by side at the workbench, and it was the strangest kind of communion: her voice reading orbital telemetry, my voice reading Wren, the dead man's annotations stitching our halves together. Every few pages he'd anticipated a question I was only just thinking of asking, and each time I felt it like a tap on the shoulder from across forty years. *Here. You'll want to look here.*

Near the bottom of the final log plate, past the last *maintained, chose nothing, left it lit*, there was one more entry. Undated. Written in a hand shakier than any before it.

*To whoever inherits this: I'm sorry. Not for maintaining the loop — for making you the one who has to end it or fire it. I met the architects' work once, in the old files, and I understood at last that the unwinnable choice isn't a flaw in the design. It IS the design. They wanted someone down here to feel the weight they refused to feel themselves. I carried it thirty-one years and I pass it to you lighter than I received it, because I've mapped every wire. Do better than me. You will. You have to. There's no one after you in the succession queue.*

I read that last line twice.

"There's no one after you," I said aloud. "Vess."

She looked up from the telemetry.

"The succession protocol. I never questioned it — the vault named me custodian automatically when Wren's tenure lapsed, and I assumed the system would go on naming someone forever. But Wren says the queue ends with me." I pulled up the administrative layer with fingers that had gone cold. "He's right. Custodian, Third Cycle: A. Wren. Fourth Cycle: I. Hartwell. Fifth Cycle: pending — *no eligible candidates*."

"No eligible candidates," Vess repeated. "Meaning the system's been waiting ten years for you to train a successor that doesn't exist."

"Meaning the architects built a penance machine with exactly one penitent left in it." The lamp hummed. The pipes breathed. Somewhere far below us, beneath the seed galleries, beneath the sub-level, beneath the pod that had given us Vess, something in the vault's deep structure ticked over — I felt it more than heard it, a shift in the building's weight, the way you feel a storm change its mind. "And meaning that whatever we decide about those arks, there's no one coming after us to fix it if we get it wrong."

Vess was quiet a moment. Then she said, in the tone of a woman reading a diagnostic she didn't like:

"Isandro. If the succession queue is empty, and the system knows it... what happens to the vault's automated custodianship when there's no custodian to succeed?"

I looked at her. She looked at me. And overhead, faint and wrong, the lights flickered — once, twice — though the geothermal grid had held steady for a decade.

Somewhere in the walls, something old began paying attention.
