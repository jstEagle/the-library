## 20. Descent of the Commons

The lander came down on a Tuesday.

Vess knew this because Isandro had drilled the calendar into her during those first strange weeks — *the vault runs on Tuesdays, Vess. Seed rotation, filter swaps, the whole liturgy of the place. If you lose the days, you lose the vault* — and so Vess had kept a paper calendar pinned to the galley wall, crossing off squares with a pencil stub like some kind of monk. Forty-one squares since the beacon had spoken with two voices instead of one. Forty-one mornings of waking in a bed that wasn't a cryo-pod and being grateful for it in a way she suspected would never fully wear off.

She was on the surface when the sky opened.

Not dramatically. That was the thing nobody told you about ships returning to Earth after generations — they didn't arrive like thunder. They arrived like weather. A brightening in the east that wasn't sunrise, a long silver smear descending at the pace of something being careful. The lander rode its own fire down through the stratosphere and then killed the fire and went quiet, gliding on wings that caught the wind off the glacier field, and for a moment it hung there against the blue like a fish turning in clear water.

Vess stood on the landing apron Isandro had spent three weeks clearing — hauling gravel by hand, arguing with a snowplow attachment that predated both their births — and felt her throat close up.

*They came.*

She'd known they would. She'd heard the child's voice crackle through the static, had spent eleven minutes and change talking to a girl named Promise who was nine years old and had never once stood under an open sky. But knowing a thing and watching it happen were different animals entirely, and Vess's body, which had spent decades in a pod learning to distrust good news, refused to believe until the lander's skids touched gravel and the dust settled and nothing about it vanished.

The ramp came down slowly. Hydraulics complained. And then footsteps — small ones, quick ones — and a figure burst out into the Arctic light before anyone could stop her.

Promise stopped dead at the bottom of the ramp.

Vess understood immediately. She'd imagined this meeting a hundred times in forty-one nights, and in every version she'd been the one struck dumb by the strangeness of the other. It hadn't occurred to her that a child born in orbit, who had lived her whole life inside hulls and corridors and hydroponic racks, might look at the *sky* the way Vess looked at miracles.

Promise turned in a slow circle, arms out, face tipped up. The wind took her hair. Somewhere behind her, adults were emerging from the ramp in ones and twos, and every single one of them did the same thing: stopped, looked up, and forgot how to walk.

"Is it always there?" Promise called out, without looking down, without knowing who she was asking.

Vess cleared her throat. "The sky? Yeah. That's the trick of it. It's just *there*. All day. You stop noticing after a while."

"I won't," Promise said, with the absolute authority of a nine-year-old, and Vess believed her.

* * *

There were thirty-one of them.

Vess counted twice on the first evening because she didn't trust herself not to have invented a few. Thirty-one people off one lander — the advance party, they called themselves, sent ahead while the Commons itself finished its descent burn and took up a holding orbit. Farmers, mostly. Two doctors. An engineer named Okafor who shook Vess's hand with a grip that suggested he'd been doing zero-g structural work his whole life and found gravity personally insulting. A woman named Adaeze who introduced herself as "the closest thing we have to a mayor, which is to say, the person everyone argues with."

"And you're the voice," Adaeze said. "The one who answered us back."

"The voice was mostly static," Vess said. "But yes."

"No." Adaeze looked at her steadily. "We'd been listening to that beacon our whole lives. Our parents' whole lives. It said the same four sentences on a loop for seventy years, and then one night it said something *new*, and it was your voice saying it, and I don't think you understand what that did to us. My grandmother cried. She was ninety. She said, 'So somebody's still down there. So it wasn't all for nothing.'" She paused. "You should know what you did before you go around calling it static."

Vess managed a nod and escaped toward the galley before her face could do anything embarrassing.

The camp had gone up fast — Commons people built like they breathed, quickly and without asking permission. Three habitat modules, a mess tent, a power line run straight off the vault's geothermal tap with Okafor whistling the whole way. They'd offered to help with everything and asked about nothing, which Vess found both refreshing and slightly unnerving. On the third evening, though, Adaeze found her in the seed gallery, running germination checks by lamplight, and sat down on a crate across from her with the air of someone opening negotiations.

"You keep touching the racks," Adaeze said.

"What?"

"When you walk the rows. You touch them. Little taps, like you're checking they're real." Adaeze nodded at Vess's hand, which was, at that exact moment, resting on the edge of a rack of barley. Vess withdrew it. Adaeze smiled. "I'm not mocking you. We all do it aboard. Every surface, every hatch. Seventy years in a tin can teaches you that things can be taken away between one heartbeat and the next." She leaned forward. "So tell me about her. The other custodian. The manifest says Hartwell, but the manifest also says the Meridian never flew, so forgive me if I want the human version."

Vess told her.

She told it badly at first — halting, over-structured, the way she'd rehearsed reports in another life — but somewhere around the part where Ceres sealed the sub-levels and the heat failed and the two of them huddled in the rye gallery burning ration packs for warmth, it stopped being a report and became a story, and by the end she was laughing at the memory of Isandro threatening a maintenance drone with a torque wrench, and Adaeze was laughing too, and the lamplight was warm, and the racks hummed their quiet growing hum.

"She gave her pod slot," Adaeze said when Vess finished. Not a question.

"She rewrote the protocol and the protocol needed a living anchor. She didn't hesitate. That's the part that gets me. I begged her to stop and she just — looked at me like I'd asked her to stop breathing. Like it was the most obvious thing in the world." Vess turned her hands over, studied the calluses she'd earned in ten weeks of manual labor. "Ten years alone in this place, and the first thing she does with company is give herself away."

"And now?"

"Now she's asleep." Vess said it evenly. She'd practiced. "The Commons medical bay stabilized her in cryo-stasis on the way down. Dr. Iwu says the anchor burn damaged tissue they can regrow, but slowly, and only in a proper facility, and the only proper facility in this hemisphere is—" she gestured at the rock overhead, at the vault, at everything, "—here. So we're building one. Okafor and your doctors and me. She'll wake up in her own vault, in a bed I'm going to build myself, and she is going to be *furious* about how I've been running the place."

Adaeze laughed. "Furious how?"

"She has Opinions about filter schedules. She has Opinions about how the rye racks are spaced. She once wrote a four-page memo about humidity." Vess grinned despite herself. "I've been keeping her calendar. Tuesdays and everything. When she wakes up I'm going to hand her the logbook and watch her find something wrong with my handwriting."

"That sounds like love," Adaeze said, mildly, the way you'd note the weather.

Vess opened her mouth to object, discovered she had no objection loaded, and closed it again. Adaeze had the grace to pretend not to notice.

* * *

On the sixth morning, Vess took Promise down into the galleries.

It had become a ritual already, to Vess's private astonishment — the child appeared at the vault entrance each day at 0800 sharp, having clearly interrogated someone about custodial hours, and stood there vibrating with contained questions until Vess surrendered and took her inside. Today Promise arrived carrying something: a small cloth bundle, tied with wire, which she presented with tremendous ceremony.

"For the garden," she said. "Seeds. From the Commons. Adaeze says every planting starts with an exchange or it isn't a partnership, it's charity. We're not charity."

Vess weighed the bundle in her palm. Inside, dozens of tiny packets, hand-labeled in at least four different handwriting styles: amaranth, cowpea, a pepper variety marked simply *AUNTIE ROSE'S — DO NOT CROSS-POLLINATE (SERIOUS)*.

"Seventy years of shipboard breeding," Promise said, reciting what was obviously a prepared speech. "Small spaces, artificial light, careful selection. Some of these don't exist anywhere else anymore. Auntie Rose grew the peppers in a converted airlock for forty years and she made everybody sign a pledge." Promise's face went suddenly, terribly serious. "You have to sign the pledge."

"I'll sign the pledge," Vess said solemnly.

They walked the galleries together, and Vess watched the child move through the rows the way she herself had moved that first week — touching everything, tapping the racks, checking that the world was load-bearing. At the wheat, Promise stopped.

"This is the one from the broadcast," she said. "Isn't it. The one you talked about. When you told us what was down here."

Vess looked at the rack. Isandro's wheat — the strain she'd babied for a decade, the one she'd described over the open channel in a voice gone ragged with static and feeling, *we have wheat older than your grandparents' voyage and it's still alive, we kept it alive, come see.* "Yeah. That's the one."

"Can I touch it?"

"It's a seed rack, not a shrine. Touch it."

Promise touched it, gently anyway, and then looked up with the particular ferocity children deploy when they are about to say something true. "When your friend wakes up, will she teach me the vault? Adaeze says you're acting custodian but acting means there's a real one coming back, and I want to learn from the real one. I want to learn *everything*. All the rooms. Even the scary AI room."

"Ceres isn't scary anymore. Ceres is mostly embarrassed."

"*Everything,*" Promise repeated, unmoved by nuance.

Vess crouched to her level. Behind the girl, the gallery stretched away in its long lit rows — wheat and barley and rye and rice, ten thousand years of human hunger banked in cold storage, tended by a woman who'd given her life's warmth to keep it waiting, waiting for exactly this: a child from the sky reaching out to check that it was real.

"Deal," Vess said. "But the humidity memos are non-negotiable."

* * *

That evening, Vess climbed to the antenna field alone.

She went up sometimes, now, without any mission attached — just to sit on the gravel beside the array that had started everything, the towers Isandro had defended with a torque wrench, humming their low patient hum against the dark. Tonight the sky was doing its enormous indifferent beautiful thing, stars thick over the glacier, and far to the south — barely visible, a moving point of light no brighter than a satellite — the Commons ark held its orbit, beginning its long careful spiral down. In six months, maybe eight, the second wave would land. After that, Halcyon's first shuttles, arriving into a world that had been warned, that had had time to argue, that would meet the old families not as inheritors but as applicants.

Shared custody. Isandro's phrase, burned into the beacon's new loop, repeating now on every band, in every language the architects had thought to include: *This world is held in common. Come home carefully.*

Vess pulled the logbook from her jacket — Isandro's logbook, its spine cracked, its margins dense with a decade of a stranger's tidy handwriting — and opened to today's page. She'd developed a habit of writing her entries as letters. It felt less like talking to herself that way.

*Dear Isandro,* she wrote, in handwriting she was fairly sure would earn an Opinion. *Thirty-one down, four hundred and twelve to go. The peppers require a signed pledge; details enclosed. Promise has claimed the wheat and, frankly, most of the rest of us. Okafor broke ground on the medical bay today — your room, when you wake up, will have a window, because I decided you've spent enough of your life underground, and you can file a complaint when you're conscious.*

She paused, pencil hovering. Below the aurora's faint green breath, the antenna hummed.

*One more thing. The deep scan picked up something yesterday — a return signal from the Halcyon ark, tight-beam, encrypted, addressed to the vault by name. Not to the beacon. To the vault. Somebody awake over there knows this place specifically, knows the old access codes, and wants to talk. I haven't answered yet. I wanted to ask you first. That's the trouble with inheriting a job from someone in a coma — every big decision turns into a conversation with a sleeping woman.*

*Wake up soon. The garden's starting without you.*

She closed the book, and high above, the point of light that was the Commons slid silently across the stars, descending.
