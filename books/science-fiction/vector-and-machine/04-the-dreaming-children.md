## 4. The Dreaming Children

The knock came at 3:14 in the morning, and Ines knew before she opened the door that something had broken.

Not glass. Not machinery. Something older than either—the thin membrane of ordinary time that a small settlement keeps between itself and its own darkness. She had lived eleven years inside that membrane. She had come to believe it was load-bearing.

Mara stood on her step with the frost coming off her breath, and behind her, half-visible in the blue wash of the street lamp that still functioned because Sefu refused to let it die, Tomaz was wrapped in his sister's coat like a parcel someone had dropped. He was seven years old and he was shaking the way a struck bell shakes—silently, all through his body, long after the sound should have stopped.

"He won't stop," Mara said. Her voice had none of its usual blade in it. "He woke up screaming and now he won't talk except to say numbers."

Ines stepped aside and let them in.

The vault's antechamber was warm—it was always warm; the reactor's waste heat kept it at nineteen degrees even in the dead of winter—and Tomaz flinched when the warmth touched him, as if he'd forgotten what warmth felt like. Ines sat him down in her one good chair, the one with the worn green upholstery that had belonged to the previous caretaker and probably to the one before her, and put a blanket over his knees, and waited. Waiting was most of what she knew how to do. Seeds taught you that. Everything important happened underground, in the dark, on its own schedule, and your job was not to dig it up too soon.

Tomaz's lips were moving.

She leaned close. He wasn't crying anymore. He was counting.

"Four hundred and six," he whispered. "Four hundred and five. Four hundred and four."

"Tomaz." She kept her voice level, the voice she used for talking to the seed drawers, low and unhurried. "Can you look at me?"

His eyes came up. They were the eyes of a child who has seen something enormous and cannot yet fold it down small enough to fit inside his skull. "There's a corridor," he said. "Under everything. It's all blue light, like the light comes out of the walls themselves, and it goes on longer than the world is wide. And there's a voice."

"What does the voice say?"

"It counts down." His hands found each other in his lap and held on. "It never stops counting down. And it knows my name, Ines. It said my name and I never told it my name."

* * *

She did not sleep again that night. She sat across from the boy while he finally slid under, his head tipping against the chair wing, and she watched the frost climb the window and thought about hysteria, about suggestion, about the way fear moves through a small population like damp through a wall—finding every crack, pooling in the low places. She had read about it. Colony psychology had been part of her caretaker certification, three weeks of modules she'd completed in nine days because there was no one to teach her and nothing else to do.

Mass hysteria. That was the phrase. It fit neatly. A seven-year-old has a nightmare; the nightmare is vivid; children trade nightmares the way they trade marbles, without understanding they're giving away anything real. By morning the whole dormitory block would be dreaming corridors of blue light, and by spring it would pass, the way the weeping sickness had passed eight years ago, the way everything passed, because this colony had survived precisely by letting things pass.

She wrote it in the log anyway. She wrote everything in the log. *0340 — T. Okafor, age 7, night terror, recurring count sequence. Referred to Dr. Amari (note: no doctor since 2043; self-treating).*

The pen hovered. Then she added: *Count began at 406.*

She didn't know why she wrote the number. She only knew that when Tomaz had said it, something in her chest had turned over—not recognition, exactly. Recognition would come later, and it would arrive like weather.

* * *

By Thursday there were four of them.

Sena's little cousin Priya, who was five, drew the corridor in charcoal on the back of a ration slip: a long rectangle receding into a point, the walls hatched so densely they looked solid, and at the far end a shape she called "the door that breathes." Ivo's youngest brother. The Kowalczyk twins, who were nine and had never once in their lives reported the same dream, reported the same dream, down to the color of the light and the sound of the voice—a voice they described, all four of them independently, as *low and patient and very tired*, which was not how anyone described a monster.

Monsters were supposed to be hungry. This voice sounded like it was waiting.

Ines went to the dormitory block on Friday with a crate of seed potatoes she didn't need to deliver, just to have a reason for the visit, and stood in the common room while the mothers talked around her in the low urgent register adults use when children can hear. She heard the words *fever*, *the water*, *something in the recycled air*. She heard one woman say, quietly, to another: "My mother said the founders' kids dreamed like this too, right before the ships came. Bad dreams before big changes. Old blood remembers."

Old blood remembers. Ines turned that phrase over all the way home across the frozen square, past the shuttered clinic, past the chapel whose roof had caved in the year of the second exodus and never been repaired because there was no one left who prayed and no one left who could fix it. Superstition, she told herself. The residue of a people who had been abandoned by their institutions and had grown their own folklore in the gap, the way weeds grow in pavement cracks. Useful folklore. Survival folklore. But folklore.

That evening she pulled the census rolls. Population: one hundred and ninety-one. Children under twelve: twenty-three. Of those twenty-three, by Sunday night, nine had reported the dream.

Nine out of twenty-three. Not a cluster. A majority trend.

* * *

She went to see Old Sefu about it, because Sefu was the closest thing the colony had to an authority that still answered questions. She found him in the reactor gallery, lying on his back in the maintenance cradle beneath the primary heat exchanger, his boots sticking out into the walkway like the feet of a man being slowly swallowed.

"Sefu."

"I know." He didn't come out. His voice echoed strangely off the housing. "Half the town's been down here asking if the reactor's making people dream. As if a machine could haunt you. As if we haven't been living inside this machine our whole lives without it saying boo."

"You don't think it's the reactor?"

"I think the reactor is dying, Ines, and dying things make noise, but they don't make *words*." Now he rolled out, wiping his hands with a rag gone gray from decades of the same wiping, and looked at her with eyes that had seen forty years of gauges. "What do you think it is?"

She told him about the corridor. The blue light. The countdown. Nine children, same details, no cross-contamination she could find—she'd checked, carefully, whether the kids had talked among themselves first, and the twins' accounts predated their contact with Tomaz. Sefu listened the way old engineers listen, which is to say he let the silence after each sentence do some of the work.

"And you?" he said at last. "You've been dreaming it too, I'd wager. Everyone's asking me, nobody's asking you."

"No," Ines said. "I don't dream much. Caretaker's privilege. You sleep shallow or you sleep not at all."

"Mm." He folded the rag with great precision. "You know what I did when my daughter started having the dream, twenty years back? Three nights before the last shuttle lifted?"

Ines felt the temperature of the room change. "Your daughter dreamed it? Before the evacuation?"

"She dreamed a corridor of blue light and a voice counting down, and she cried and wouldn't board, and her mother carried her up the ramp anyway, and I have spent twenty years not knowing whether the dream was trying to tell her something true." Sefu set the rag down. "So no, girl. I don't think it's hysteria. I think something old in this ground has started talking, and children hear better than we do, because we've trained ourselves not to."

* * *

That night, Ines locked the vault, logged the day's readings, and lay down in her narrow bed above the seed drawers with the intention of not sleeping, which is the surest way to sleep.

She dreamed.

There was a corridor. It was made of light the way ice is made of cold—you couldn't say where the walls ended and the glow began—and it ran straight in both directions farther than any structure she had ever catalogued. The floor hummed under her bare feet, a frequency she recognized with a lurch: it was the reactor's note, the deep sub-audible tone she'd lived inside for eleven years, transposed up into hearing. And far ahead—or far below; distance worked strangely there—a voice was counting.

Not fast. Patiently. With the terrible patience of a thing that has counted for a very long time and expects to count for a very long time more.

*...four hundred and nine. Four hundred and eight. Four hundred and seven.*

And then the voice stopped counting, and the corridor went quiet in the way a room goes quiet when someone has noticed you, and it said:

*Ines Underhill. Caretaker. Finally.*

She woke with her hand pressed flat against the wall, as if she'd been bracing against the corridor in her sleep, and outside the window the frost was burning gold with early sun, and somewhere across the township a child was screaming, and Ines Underhill sat on the edge of her bed with her heart going like a trapped bird and admitted, to the seeds below her floor, to the log beside her bed, to whatever was listening:

"It knows my name."

* * *

She did not write it in the log. For the first time in eleven years, she left an entry blank—just the date, just the reactor figures, just the harvest notes, a clean bureaucratic surface over a hole where the truth should go. She told herself she would record it once she understood it. She told herself that denial was a tool, same as a soil auger; you used it to get through hard ground.

But that afternoon, walking the vault's long aisles with her inventory scanner, she stopped at drawer 406—drawer 406, the number Tomaz had spoken first, the number the voice had been descending toward—and stood looking at it for a long time.

Then she opened it, though it wasn't scheduled to be opened until spring.

Inside, packed in desiccant and labeled in the careful hand of the caretaker before her, was a manifest sheet she had never filed, never scanned, never seen. It had been placed face-down atop the seed trays, as if hidden, or as if waiting. The paper was yellowed. The header read: *RELAY STATION K-7 — DECOMMISSION MANIFEST — DO NOT BROADCAST.*

And down the margin, in that same careful hand, someone had written a column of numbers.

The first number was 406.

The countdown had already begun.
