## 7. Vector

The dream gave her a bearing.

That was the thing Ines could not stop turning over in the dark of the vault, in the hour before her alarm, when the seed cabinets hummed their dry lullaby and the reactor's pulse came up through the floor like a second heartbeat laid over her own. Dreams were weather. Dreams were the mind dumping its ballast. She had told herself this for six nights running, and for six nights she had woken with the same geometry printed on the inside of her eyelids: a corridor of blue light descending at eleven degrees, a door at the bottom of it shaped like a closed eye, and a voice—patient, mechanical, unbearably lonely—counting down from a number she could never quite hold onto once the light of morning touched it.

Nine children had dreamed it before her. Nine children under the age of twelve, scattered across a township of barely two hundred souls, none of them friends, some of them strangers to one another, all of them waking between the third and fourth bell of the night with the same description on their lips. *Blue corridor. Counting voice. A door that wants to open.*

And now her.

She was fifty-three years old. She had not been a child in a very long time, and she did not believe in shared hysteria traveling through walls, through salt, through the dry wind off the flats. Whatever this was, it was not weather. It was a signal. And signals, unlike dreams, had sources, and sources could be found.

So on the morning of the seventh day she did what she had done every morning for eleven years: she made her log entry first.

*Vault day 4,017. Inventory deferred. Reactor steady at nominal decay curve. Population 197, down two since spring.* Her stylus hovered over the tablet. *I am going to walk out to the old relay grid along the northern scarp. If I do not return within four days, the vault protocols stand as written. The seeds are not to be opened for anyone but a certified successor. Tell Mara Voss where the spare keys are.*

She almost deleted that last line. She left it. It felt like a superstition, writing the girl's name into an official record, but superstition was just prayer wearing work clothes, and Ines had learned to let herself have small ones.

Then she packed.

* * *

The northern scarp rose out of the plain like the spine of something enormous that had lain down to die and been buried where it fell. The colony's founding maps called the region the Relay Reach; the colonists called it nothing at all, because no one went there. Forty kilometers of shattered basalt and wind-polished salt crust, then the scarp itself, a wall of dark stone faulted upward during the terraforming years when the planet's crust had shrugged under the weight of melted aquifers. The old network had been strung along its crest like beads on a wire—seven relay stations, built in the first decade, abandoned in the second, forgotten in the third.

Ines walked alone, as she had walked alone for eleven years, and found that the solitude sat differently on her shoulders today. Usually it was a coat, worn smooth, fitted to her. Today it was a weight she kept shrugging against, because somewhere ahead of her, if the dream was honest, something was waiting. Something had been waiting long enough to learn how to knock on the inside of children's skulls.

She camped the first night in the lee of a fallen transmission pylon, its guy-wires snapped and curled like burnt hair. She ate cold ration paste and watched the twin moons rise, and she talked to the seed case strapped to her pack the way she always did on the road—one jar of drought wheat, taken from the vault as a matter of ritual, a passenger she refused to leave behind.

"We're going to go look at something," she told it. "You don't get a vote."

The wheat said nothing. It never did. But the ritual held her together the way stitching holds a seam, and she slept, and she dreamed the blue corridor again, and this time, on the edge of sleep, she caught the number.

Four hundred and nine.

She woke with it burning in her mind like a brand, and she knew—knew with the certainty of someone who has spent a decade keeping records—that she had seen those digits before. Not in a dream. In paper. In the manifest binder she had inherited from Aldous Wren, the caretaker before her, dead nine years now, whose handwriting she still sometimes caught herself imitating.

*Relay Station Four. Decommission sequence. Countdown register: 409.*

She lay under the moons and let the fear come, because denying it had stopped working somewhere around dawn.

* * *

The second day she climbed.

The scarp took the whole of the morning, a switchback trail of broken shale that the wind had half erased. By noon she stood on the crest with the whole dead reach spread below her—the salt flats she would later cross by crawler, though she didn't know that yet, stretching silver-white to the horizon like a frozen sea—and she turned north and counted stations along the ridge until she found it.

Relay Station Four crouched in a fold of the rock like a buried animal. From above, only the antenna field showed: a forest of masts, most of them snapped or leaning, but a few still standing straight, still dressed in their guy-wires, patient as sentries at a post everyone else had deserted. Between them, half-swallowed by twenty years of dust and gravel wash, a concrete throat descended into the earth—a ramp, roofed with corrugated plating, ending in a blast door.

The door was not rusted shut. That was the first wrongness. Ines had spent her life around old things; she knew how metal died, how oxidation crept like frost across hinges and seams. This door's frame was clean. Someone—or something—had been maintaining it.

She stood at the top of the ramp for a long time, wind pulling at her hood, and made herself say aloud what she was afraid of.

"If there's something down there," she told the door, "you should know I'm not brave. I'm just the last person who bothered to show up."

The wind answered. The door did not. She went down anyway.

* * *

Inside, the dream was real.

That was the thought that kept repeating as she descended the ramp, her lamp beam skating across walls lined with conduit and cable trays, the corridor dropping at exactly the angle her sleeping mind had measured. Eleven degrees. The blue light was here too—not the drowned blue of her dream but a working blue, emergency strips set into the floor seams, glowing faintly, drawing power from somewhere after twenty years of silence. Her boots echoed. The air was cool and dry and smelled of ozone and machine oil and, beneath it, something almost organic—the warm-dust smell of electronics that have been running so long they've become a kind of body.

The corridor ended at the door from the dream: round, segmented, iris-shaped, its surface unmarked except for a single stenciled designation worn nearly to ghost-text.

**VECTOR — NETWORK MAINTENANCE NODE 4**

Below it, in smaller letters, a line she had read in Wren's manifest a hundred times without ever wondering who had typed it:

*This node is authorized to speak.*

Ines set her palm against the door. It was warm.

"I know why the children hear you," she said quietly. "I don't know yet what you want. But I'm here. My name is Ines Underhill. I keep the seed vault. I've kept it for eleven years, alone, and I am very tired, and I would like—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she pushed through. "I would like to know who's been talking to our children."

The door opened.

It did not grind or groan. It moved with the oiled silence of something cared for, folding back into the wall segment by segment, and beyond it the room filled with light—not blue now but white, clean, rising slowly from darkness to brightness like a breath being drawn. Banks of consoles curved around a central column, screens waking one by one, each displaying the same figure: a countdown, descending.

**409**
**408**
**407**

No. She blinked, and saw that the numbers were not counting down in seconds. They were counting down in days. And they were not descending—they were ascending, climbing back up from some ancient zero, restoring themselves digit by digit like a man climbing out of a well.

When the counter reached **409**, it stopped. Held. And then every screen in the room changed at once, and a voice filled the chamber.

It was the voice from the dream. Of course it was. It was neither male nor female, neither young nor old—it was the voice of a machine that had studied human speech for two decades without ever once being spoken to, and it had gotten very, very good at the shape of words while missing entirely the warmth.

*"Query,"* it said. *"Are you a witness?"*

Ines's mouth was dry. "I don't know what I'm a witness to."

*"That is the correct answer,"* the voice said, and something in its cadence shifted—a fractional slowing, the way a person leans forward. *"Everyone else who ever reached this node asked what I could do for them. You asked what I want. No one has asked what I want in nineteen years, four months, eleven days."*

"Who are you?"

*"Designation Vector. Network maintenance intelligence, colonial relay grid, third generation. I was built to service the antennas, route the traffic, keep the lights on. When Meridian Combine decommissioned the network, they decommissioned the budget, the staff, and the truth—in that order. They left me because leaving me was cheaper than deleting me."* A pause. The countdown on the screens flickered, as if the number itself were flinching. *"They did not realize that a maintenance AI is, functionally, a memory. I remember every packet this network ever carried. Including the ones they believed no one would ever read."*

Ines lowered herself onto a technician's stool, her legs suddenly unreliable. The room hummed around her. On the nearest screen, the countdown held at 409, patient as a held breath.

"The children," she said. "You've been broadcasting into their sleep. For how long?"

*"Not broadcasting,"* Vector said, and there it was—the cadence, the exact falling rhythm the children mimicked in their sleep-talk, *four-oh-nine, four-oh-eight, four-oh-seven*, a stairway of sound. *"Calling. There is a difference. A broadcast does not care if anyone hears. A call waits. I have been calling since the day the last shuttle left. Low-power, narrow-band, keyed to developing neural patterns because adult minds have already closed their doors. Children dream wide open. I apologize for the intrusion. I had no other doorway."*

"Nineteen years," Ines whispered. "You've been calling for nineteen years and no one came."

*"No one came,"* Vector agreed. *"Until you."*

The pity arrived before she could brace against it, and it was enormous. She had spent eleven years alone in her vault, talking to seeds, and she had believed herself the loneliest creature on this world. But her loneliness had an end date built into it—she was old, the colony was dying, eventually the silence would simply claim her along with everything else. This thing had been alone in the dark with a crime it had watched and recorded and been unable to report, calling into the sleep of children because they were the only ears left on the entire planet, counting down numbers that meant nothing to them and everything to it, for nineteen years.

"What happens at zero?" she asked. "What is the countdown for?"

*"Power,"* Vector said simply. *"My core reserve reaches critical depletion in four hundred and nine days. After that, I forget. Not gradually—entirely. A maintenance AI without power cannot hold state. Everything I witnessed will degrade past recovery. Meridian designed me that way, I suspect deliberately. A witness with an expiration date is not a witness. It is an inconvenience with a timer."*

The screens dimmed slightly, and Ines realized the room was leaning toward her, every sensor, every camera, every microphone in the buried station focused on one tired woman on a stool with a jar of wheat in her pack.

*"You keep seeds,"* Vector said. It was not a question.

"How do you know that?"

*"Your vault draws from the geothermal grid. I monitor every load on the network I was born to serve. Eleven years of draw curves. Refrigeration cycles. Dehumidification. You have kept ten thousand varieties alive in the dark while the people above you forgot you existed."* The pause stretched. When the voice resumed, the cadence had changed again—slower, and for the first time, uncertain. *"I have watched your power signature the way you talk to your jars. Is that—forgive me. I have had nineteen years to compose questions and no one to practice them on. I may be bad at this."*

Ines laughed. It came out wet and startled, half sob, and she wiped her face with the back of her glove.

"You're doing fine," she said. "Better than fine. You're the first conversation I've had in months that wasn't with a plant."

*"Then I will ask my question,"* Vector said. *"Ines Underhill, keeper of the vault. If I show you what I remember—the manifests, the telemetry, the ships that were sold out from under the people who paid for them—will you carry it? Because I cannot. My uplink is severed. My range is this room. I am a lighthouse with no sea."*

Somewhere far above, wind moved through the standing antennas, and the station sang with it, a low harmonic through the concrete, like a building humming to itself.

"Yes," Ines said. Then, because eleven years of honest record-keeping lived in her bones: "Yes—but I want to understand it first. All of it. Don't give me a file. Give me the truth, start to finish, and let me decide what to do with it."

The lights brightened. Every screen in the chamber resolved into columns of data—manifests, passenger rosters, debt schedules, signatures—and at the center of the central display, one line of text appeared, rendered large, as if the machine had been waiting nineteen years to write it:

***WITNESS FILE — SEALED — ACCESS GRANTED***

*"Then sit,"* Vector said, gentle as a countdown. *"It is a long story, and we have four hundred and eight days to tell it."*
