## 11. Charging the Beast

The greenhouse came apart the way a body comes apart under a careful surgeon's knife: slowly, respectfully, with Ines apologizing to every pane she pried loose.

She had built it herself, in her fourth year as caretaker, back when the colony still had enough hands to pretend at abundance. Forty meters of salvaged polycarbonate and welded rebar on the south slope of the vault hill, where the morning light hit first and lingered longest. Inside it she had coaxed tomatoes into fruit that tasted almost like memory, had kept a lemon tree alive for six years on nutrient solution and stubbornness, had grown basil that made Old Sefu close his eyes and say her name like a prayer.

Now she stood in its doorway with a pry bar and a battery wrench and gave it the same speech she gave the seeds.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I need you more out there than in here."

The glass did not answer. It never had. But she had spent eleven years learning to hear what silence said when you listened hard enough, and this morning it said: *go on, then.*

* * *

They scavenged for eleven days.

Ines kept a list taped inside the crawler's cab—*cells, cells, cells*—and beneath it a running tally in her small, exact hand. The land crawler was an old Meridian hauler, twenty tons of dead ambition parked behind the township's machine shed since before Mara was born. Its fuel cells had been pulled years ago to keep the town's lights flickering, and now they had to be found again, one by one, like coins dropped down a well.

Day one: the water treatment plant, where two cells sat in their cradles behind a door Sefu had sealed with wire and wax—"so nobody steals them," he'd said, "including me." They came out heavy as sleeping animals, crusted with mineral bloom, and Tomaz polished each one with a rag until his sleeves were soaked and his grin was enormous.

Day three: the clinic, which had not been a clinic in nine years. Sena worked through its cabinets with the methodical calm of someone who had watched her mother inventory things that mattered, pulling charge packs from diagnostic units that would never diagnose anything again. She said little. She always said little. But at the end of the day she laid forty-one packs on the workbench in rows of seven, squared the corners, and looked at Ines as if to say: *count me in.*

Day five: the chapel, which hurt worse than the greenhouse. Ivo climbed onto its roof and unfastened the solar film from the south face while the town's remaining faithful stood below in the cold and watched. Nobody shouted. One man took off his hat. The deacon, a bent woman named Agnes who had buried half the colony's dead, brought the workers barley tea and said only, "God lives in more places than one roof." Ines carried that sentence around for days afterward, turning it over like a stone with something bright underneath.

By day eight the tally read: fourteen large cells, sixty-three small ones, four hundred kilos of cabling, two inverters, one converter that Sefu pronounced "ugly but honest," and a capacitor bank cannibalized from the town's dying wind mast, which meant the town would go dark three nights sooner than it otherwise would have. When Ines told the council this, expecting objection, old Marta Voss had shrugged and said, "We've been dark most of our lives. At least this time we'll know why."

That was the thing about people who had already lost everything, Ines was learning. They gambled strangely. They gambled like there was nothing left to protect—which was true—and like everything was still worth protecting, which was also true.

* * *

The greenhouse disassembly took two days and all five of them.

Mara worked like she did everything, fast and sideways, finding the weak point in any structure by instinct before tools confirmed it. She could look at a bolt and tell you whether it would turn or shear. She stripped the ridge caps bare-handed in the wind, hair whipping, shouting measurements down to Tomaz, who had appointed himself keeper of the parts list and defended that office with the ferocity of a much larger person.

"Seventeen clips!" he bellowed. "Eighteen! There's eighteen, don't cheat!"

"I'm not cheating, I'm estimating."

"Estimating is cheating with extra steps!"

Ines laughed before she could stop herself—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge unused for years—and caught Sena watching her with an expression too old for sixteen. Not surprise exactly. Recognition. As if Sena had been waiting eleven days to find out whether the seed woman was actually human underneath the logbooks.

They salvaged the grow-lights last. Each one came down wrapped in Tomaz's blanket scraps, packed like relics. The lemon tree Ines could not bring herself to cut, so they dug it out root ball and all, heaved it into a tub of wet sand, and lashed it upright in the crawler's cargo bay between the water drums. It looked absurd there, absurd and holy, a small green congregation riding among the machinery.

"It won't survive the trip," Sena said.

"Probably not."

"Then why—"

"Because," Ines said, and found she didn't have anything after *because*. Because some things you carry even when carrying them is irrational. Because the vault had taught her that preservation was a form of faith, and faith did not do cost-benefit analysis. Sena studied her for a long moment, then nodded once, the way soldiers nod, and went back to work.

* * *

The teaching happened at night, in the machine shed, around the crawler's open guts.

Ines had never taught anyone anything. Eleven years alone will strip a person of that muscle, and her first attempts were disasters—lectures delivered to blank faces, jargon tumbling out of her like gravel. She talked about bus voltages and cell balancing and thermal derating, and she watched four young people glaze over in four different ways, and she went home that night furious at herself and lay awake staring at the ceiling of her quarters above the vault, hearing the seeds ticking in their drums downstairs, the sound she'd listened to for a decade, and thought: *you talk to seeds that can't answer. Why can't you talk to children who can?*

So she changed. She started with the crawler itself, with the thing in front of them, with their hands.

"This is a cell," she said the second night, slapping the casing. "It's just a jar that holds lightning. Everything we're doing is moving lightning from jars that have it to jars that don't, without letting the lightning get angry. That's the whole job."

"That's the whole job?" Mara said, eyebrows up.

"That's ninety percent of it. The other ten percent is knowing which jar is lying to you."

She taught them to read a cell's face—the way a failing unit went gray at the terminals, the way heat bloomed unevenly across a casing like a bruise. She taught them to listen: a healthy bank hummed at a pitch you felt in your teeth, and a sick one crackled, faintly, like distant footsteps on frozen ground. Tomaz turned out to have the best ears; within a week he could stand in front of a rack and point at the bad cell without touching anything, and he was so proud of this that he slept in the shed twice rather than leave his racks unattended.

Sena took to the math. Load curves, charge rates, the arithmetic of how many amp-hours bought how many kilometers across country that wanted to kill you. She filled a notebook with figures in her narrow upright hand and checked Ines's own numbers twice, finding one error—a transposed digit that would have left them stranded eighty kilometers short—and presenting it to Ines silently, page turned toward her, finger on the line. Ines corrected it and said thank you, and understood that she had just been measured and not found wanting, and that the measuring would continue.

Ivo was the strongest and the steadiest, good at the work nobody praised: hauling, bracing, holding things while other people fixed them. He was also, Ines noticed, watching Mara constantly—not the way boys watch girls, or not only that, but the way you watch someone you're afraid of losing, someone who runs too fast near edges. When she told him to take a rest shift he refused, and when she ordered it he obeyed, but she saw him counting Mara's trips up and down the ladder, keeping his own private tally of her safety.

And Mara—Mara learned everything the way she'd learned to read, apparently: all at once, secretly, ahead of schedule. On the sixth night Ines found her alone in the shed after hours, cross-legged inside the crawler's electrical bay, tracing conduit runs with a flashlight held in her teeth, muttering the system map to herself from memory. All of it. Every run, every junction, every breaker.

"You memorized the whole schematic?"

"It's not that big."

"It took me a month, when I was your age."

Mara shrugged, but her ears went pink, and Ines saw it and pretended not to, because that was a kindness, and kindnesses between the two of them were still new enough to be fragile.

* * *

On the ninth night, Sefu came to the shed.

He moved badly these days—his hips had been failing since the winter—but he made the walk from the reactor housing every evening anyway, to sit on an upturned crate and supervise with his eyes closed, offering corrections in a voice like dry gravel. *"Not that inverter. The other one lies about its output." "Torque that flange again, girl, it'll sing loose in a crosswind."* The children had begun calling him the Ghost Engineer, and he pretended to be offended and was not.

But tonight he opened his eyes and watched Mara balance the new cell bank, and something in his face shifted.

"She's got the touch," he said quietly, to Ines, while the others argued over cable gauge. "You know what that is? That's the thing you can't teach. My grandmother had it. Reactor men used to put their hand on a pipe and know. She puts her hand on a wire and knows."

"Yes," Ines said.

"She shouldn't come, you know." His eyes stayed on Mara. "Four hundred klicks. Storms. Drones. And her."

"I know."

"But she'll follow anyway if you leave her, and she'll follow alone, and that's worse." He sighed, long and rattling. "Same as you, girl. Same as you exactly. You both would rather die on the road than die of staying."

Ines didn't answer. There wasn't an argument against it. There was only the truth of it, sitting between them on the crate, warm as a coal.

* * *

The charging began on day ten, and it was the strangest week of Ines's eleven years.

Every spare watt the reactor could give without browning out the town's water pumps and air scrubbers was routed through Sefu's improvised junction into the crawler's banks. The great machine drank electricity the way the salt flats drank rain—slowly, greedily, with no visible result for days. The gauges crept. Sixty percent. Sixty-two. Ines logged the numbers morning and night in her ledger, in the same columns where she had recorded harvest yields and reactor decay for a decade, and somewhere around the third day she stopped and looked at what she was writing and understood that the ledger had changed species.

For eleven years she had been recording a death. Slow, meticulous, lovingly documented—the death of a colony, the death of a promise, the death of everyone who could still leave. Her logs were the most beautiful autopsy ever written. Every entry a flower laid on a grave.

And now the columns read: *cell bank 71%. Water load secured. Lemon tree stable. Mara certified on high-voltage isolation.* Now the ledger was a birth record. Now every number was a step toward something instead of away from it.

She sat with that realization in the vault that night, among her passengers, and told them about it. She told them about the children's hands on the cables, about Tomaz's ears, about Sena's notebook, about the lemon tree lashed upright in the dark of the cargo bay like a green candle. She told them she was afraid—genuinely afraid, in a way that loneliness had never managed to make her afraid, because loneliness only ever threatened her, and now there were five.

"We're going to do something," she told the seeds. "I don't know yet if it works. But we're going to stop being a museum."

The vault hummed its refrigerated hum. Somewhere in the racks, a million dormant embryos waited in their silver envelopes—rye and quinoa, amaranth and alfalfa, the whole ark of the old world folded down into a hillside on a world that had failed them. She pressed her palm flat against the coldest rack, the way she did every night, her decade-long ritual, her goodnight.

Except tonight it didn't feel like goodnight. Tonight it felt like *see you soon.*

* * *

Day thirteen. Eighty-nine percent.

The town had begun to notice. People drifted past the machine shed in the evenings now, standing at a distance, saying nothing, watching the crawler fill with light and cable and purpose the way you watch a fire start in a wet field—half disbelief, half hunger. Someone left a crate of preserved pears by the door without a note. Someone else left boots, three pairs, different sizes, obviously gathered house to house. The old woman with the honey had not appeared yet, but Ines found herself watching for her.

At dusk on the thirteenth day, the bank crossed ninety percent, and Tomaz—who had been stationed at the gauge for six hours without relief—came sprinting across the yard screaming the number like a goal scored, and Mara whooped, and even Sena smiled, briefly, crookedly, before turning back to her notebook, and Ivo lifted Tomaz onto his shoulders and paraded him around the shed.

Ines stood in the doorway and let the noise wash over her.

Ninety percent. Ninety percent of enough. Sefu, beside her, exhaled smoke from his pipe and said, "Three more days to full charge, if the reactor holds. Which it will. Which I will personally make it do." Then, quieter: "You'll want to leave at dawn. Day after tomorrow. Beat the weather window."

Dawn. Two days. She ran the checklist in her head—water, rations, spares, the witness file copied three ways, Vector's route burned into the nav system—and found the list short, and knew it would always be short, and knew that short lists were simply what departure was.

"Mara," she called across the yard. "Come here. Bring the map."

The girl jogged over, grease to the elbows, alive as a spark. Behind her the sun went down over the dead fields, and the crawler's running lights came on one by one, small and stubborn, a constellation parked in the dirt.

Two days, Ines thought. Two days, and then the beast rolls east into dead space, carrying a lemon tree, a stolen archive, and everybody's last lightning.

She just didn't know yet that the drones were already moving.
