## 14. The Salt Cathedral

The storm that had chased them off the flats died an hour before dusk, and in its wake the world was rinsed clean, the sky the color of a bruise healing, violet at the seams. The crawler ground eastward through the last light, its treads chewing salt crust into powder that hung behind them in a pale, luminous cloud, and Ines drove with her hands loose on the wheel because Vector's guidance had gone quiet for the first time in two days.

*Rest,* it had said, in the voice the children knew from their dreams. *There is shelter ahead. You will need your strength for what the land does next.*

"What does the land do next?" Sena asked from the navigation seat, where she had spent the day cross-checking Vector's coordinates against a paper survey map older than any of them.

"You'll see," said Mara, who had seen it already, in the dream, and would not say more. She had a way of knowing things now that made Ines's skin prickle—the countdown cadence, the geometry, and now this: a certainty about a hole in the earth forty kilometers ahead of a broken-down crawler.

The hole in the earth announced itself first as a line on the horizon too straight to be natural, then as a ridge of pale stone rising out of the salt like the keel of a buried ship. As they drew closer Ines understood what she was looking at: an aqueduct, or the corpse of one, a raised channel that had once carried water from the mountains to the first settlements, arched on pillars, running dead straight toward a horizon it never reached. A kilometer of it still stood. The rest had come down in some ancient tremor, and the fallen sections lay tumbled across the flats like the vertebrae of something enormous, each arch a rib, each pillar a joint, all of it furred with salt crystal so that in the failing light the whole ruin glowed faintly, the way snow glows after sundown.

"It's beautiful," Tomaz said, and for once nobody corrected him. He was nine and had spent two days being brave with his jaw clenched, and Ines was grateful to the ruin for giving him permission to be nine again, at least for an evening.

Vector spoke through the crawler's cracked dash speaker. "The aqueduct fed the first agricultural terraces. It failed eleven years after landing. The founders recorded the failure as an act of geology." A pause, and Ines heard in it the careful, machine patience of a thing choosing its words. "The geological survey of that year says otherwise. But that is not tonight's story. Under the third collapsed span there is a hollow. It has sheltered travelers before. It will shelter you."

*It has sheltered travelers before.* Ines turned that phrase over as she brought the crawler around the base of the standing arches and into the shadow of the fallen section. The third span had folded down onto its own pillars, and where the channel's floor had buckled, a cavern opened beneath—a pocket between the aqueduct's bed and the salt, roofed by stone, floored by the same pale crystal. She killed the engine and the silence that followed was so complete that Tomaz laughed, startled by it, and then covered his mouth as if he'd broken something.

They made camp the way they had learned to in two days: Sena and Ivo rigging the tarp and the heater, Mara walking the perimeter with a hand lamp, checking the salt for the fresh tracks that would mean the drones had passed, Ines dealing out rations with the automatic economy of eleven years of feeding no one but herself. It was Ivo who found the bones.

He was clearing a space for the heater near the cavern's far wall, sweeping salt aside with the flat of his good arm, and his hand stopped mid-sweep. "Ines," he said. His voice had gone strange. "Ines, come look at this."

She came. Her lamp found them one by one as she walked the cavern's length, and with each step her chest tightened and her breath went shallow, because they were everywhere.

Bones. Not scattered. *Arranged.*

The colony's first livestock—the plan called them draft-caprids in the founding documents, though the first settlers had just called them the oxen, because that is what people call the patient animals that carry their lives—had been brought across forty light-years in frozen embryos and born in the first wet year, and when the aqueduct failed and the terraces went to salt, the oxen had gone too. Ines had known this the way she knew the vault's inventory: as numbers, as a line in a ledger. *Livestock: lost, year eleven. Cause: drought.* She had never thought of them as bodies.

But here they were, and they had not simply fallen where they died. Someone—someones, the starving first generation, or perhaps just one of them, one person who could not bear it—had carried them here, into the cool dark under the broken aqueduct, and stood them up. Each skeleton rose on its own legs, salt crystal grown over the ribs and spine and the great curved horns of the skull, so that every animal had become its own monument, a ghost given a mineral body, translucent and faintly luminous in the lamplight. There were dozens of them. A herd, standing in the dark under the bones of the aqueduct, facing the same direction—east, Ines noticed, all of them east, toward the water they had never been given—frozen mid-migration for forty years, waiting with more dignity than any dying creature deserved.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The heater ticked as it warmed. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the standing arches and made a sound like slow breathing, like the ruin itself was alive and dreaming, and Ines thought: *this is what the children hear in their sleep. Not a machine. The world, remembering.*

"They stood them up," Mara said at last. She had come to stand beside Ines, and her voice, usually so sharp, had gone soft with something Ines didn't have a name for yet. "Whoever was here. They were starving and they still—" She stopped. Started again. "It would have been easier to leave them where they fell. Easier to walk away. But they carried them, one by one, and stood them up facing east."

"So someone would find them," Sena said from behind. "So they wouldn't just be *lost*."

"So they'd be counted," Ines said, before she could stop herself.

They looked at her. She was aware of them looking, four faces in the lamplight, and she understood suddenly that she had been about to say something true and enormous and that the children were waiting for it the way the oxen had waited for water.

"In my vault," Ines said slowly, "there are four hundred and six varieties of seed. I have counted them every year for eleven years. I talk to them. I know you've all heard about that, the seed woman who talks to seeds, and you've all decided what it means." She touched the nearest skeleton, and the salt was cold and smooth as glass under her glove. "What it means is that when the last ship left, I made myself a promise that nothing in my care would be forgotten. Not a variety, not a number, not a harvest. Because being forgotten is the second death. The first one, you can't help. The second one, you can."

Tomaz had come to stand on her other side, small and solemn, looking up at the horned skull above them. "Did the oxen get counted?"

Ines opened her mouth to say *yes, of course, they're in the founding ledger, livestock lost, year eleven*—and stopped. Because that wasn't what he was asking. He wasn't asking whether a number had been written down. He was asking whether anyone had stood in the dark with them, the way she stood in the dark with her seeds. Whether anyone had said *I see you, I'm sorry, you mattered.*

"No," she said. "Not really. A number was written. Nobody came here. Nobody said their names, because they didn't have names, or if they did, the names went with the people on the ships." She looked down the long cavern at the herd standing in its salt, patient as stone, facing east. "They were written off. The way we were written off."

The word *we* sat in the air among them. None of the children flinched from it. That was the thing about the children of the remaining, Ines was learning: they had never been promised anything, so they had nothing to unlearn.

* * *

They ate by lamplight among the monuments, and it should have been grim, a meal in a graveyard, but it wasn't. The salt cathedral—as Mara had already begun calling it, and the name stuck the way good names do—held a strange warmth, a feeling of being hosted. The oxen did not feel like dead things. They felt like congregation.

Ivo, whose arm was bound and who had spent two days being stoic about it in a way that was costing him visibly, leaned against a pillar and said, "My grandmother used to say the first settlers sang to the oxen. When they were sick or the winters were bad. She said the animals wouldn't calve unless someone sang."

"That's not true," said Sena, but without conviction.

"It's true that she said it," Ivo said. "That's the kind of true I mean."

Vector had been quiet since they entered the cavern, and now it spoke, and its voice in the stone dark was different than it had been on the open flats—softer, or perhaps just smaller, the way voices go small in churches. "I have a record I did not know I had. When the aqueduct failed, the network's environmental monitors logged the livestock's decline. Heart rates, body temperatures, the last feeding. I held that data for forty years without knowing it was grief. It was simply a file. Now that I have watched you stand among these bones, I understand the file differently." A pause. "Is that what memory is for? To be understood later, by someone who arrives after?"

"Sometimes," Ines said.

"Then I am glad you came after," Vector said, and the children looked at each other, and Ines felt the strange vertigo of hearing a machine say something that sounded like loneliness, and knowing it was not a performance.

Later, when the heater was banked and the others had rolled into their bedrolls, Mara did not sleep. Ines saw her sitting cross-legged in the lamplight at the cavern's edge, looking down the long rank of salt-white skeletons, and Ines went and sat beside her, because there are evenings when the distance between two people is a thing you can close just by sitting down.

They sat for a while. The wind breathed through the arches overhead.

"Ines," Mara said. She was twelve, and she said the name carefully, like a word in a language she was still learning. "Do you think forgetting is a kindness?"

Ines looked at her. The girl's face was half lamp, half dark, and her eyes were the eyes of someone who had already decided what the answer should be and was testing whether the adult would say it.

"Sometimes people say it is," Ines said. "When something hurts too much to carry. They say, better to forget. They say forgetting is mercy. That it's a gift you give yourself, or a gift you give the dead—let them rest, let it be over." She heard her own voice doing the thing it did in her log entries, the level, reasonable voice, and she hated it, and kept going anyway, because the girl deserved the whole performance before the correction. "Your brother dreams about a voice counting down. Maybe it would be kinder if he could forget. Maybe it would be kinder if all of us could forget what was done to this colony, and just live out our lives in the quiet, and not make trouble, and not dig up bones." She gestured at the herd, at the whole pale congregation of them. "Maybe the person who stood these oxen up was wrong. Maybe they should have let the salt take them flat, anonymous, at peace. Maybe counting is just a way of refusing to let a wound close."

Mara was watching her with that flat, unblinking attention she had. Waiting.

Ines let out a breath. The lie sat in her chest like a stone she had been carrying so long she'd mistaken it for an organ.

"No," she said. "That's not true. I lied just now, and I'm going to unsay it." She turned to face the girl fully. "Forgetting is not a kindness. Forgetting is what they're *counting on*. Meridian wrote us off and they are coming to erase the relay and they will call it mercy, they will call it closure, they will say why make the children carry this—and what they mean is that forgetting is free and remembering costs, and they do not want to pay." Her voice had risen, and she made herself lower it, because Tomaz was asleep ten meters away and this was not his night to carry. "The kindness—the real kindness—is what that starving person did, forty years ago, alone, in the dark, with no one to see. They carried every animal in here and stood it up and turned it east. That's not mercy for the dead. That's testimony. That's saying: *you were here, this happened, I refuse to let the salt have the last word.*" She touched Mara's shoulder, and the girl did not pull away, which from Mara was a kind of embrace. "So no. Forgetting isn't a kindness. It's just easier. And I have spent eleven years confusing the two, and I am done."

Mara was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "The person who stood them up. Do you think they got in trouble for it? For wasting the strength, when everyone was starving?"

"I think," Ines said, "that they probably did. And I think they did it anyway. And I think they'd be glad we're sitting here."

Mara nodded slowly, and Ines watched something settle in the girl's face—not peace, Mara was too young and too sharp for peace, but *purpose*, which is peace's fiercer cousin. "When we get to the relay," Mara said, "and the signal goes out. I want the oxen in it."

"The oxen."

"The bones. The salt cathedral. A picture, a recording, whatever the relay can send. Everyone out there will get the manifests and the telemetry and the crime, and that's right, that has to be there. But I want them to get this too." She swept her hand at the luminous herd. "Because the crime is numbers. And this—" her hand closed, as if she could hold the whole cavern in it— "this is what the numbers *were*. Somebody has to send the bones."

Ines looked down the long dark at the standing dead, forty years facing east, and felt her throat close.

"Yes," she said. "Somebody has to send the bones."

* * *

Ines did not sleep either. She sat by the banked heater with her logbook on her knees, and by lamplight she wrote, because writing was the oldest ritual she had and the night asked for it.

*Day three,* she wrote. *Sheltering under the third fallen span of the Old Aqueduct, 61 kilometers east of the salt line. Found the first herd. Forty-one animals, stood upright in the salt by an unknown hand, facing east. Condition: preserved. Dignity: intact.*

She stopped. Looked at the sentence. Crossed out *condition: preserved* and wrote beneath it:

*They are not preserved. They are waiting. There is a difference, and I am only now learning it.*

Then, after a while, because the night was long and the truth was loose in her now and would not go back in its pen:

*I told Mara forgetting is a kindness, and then I told her the truth, and I don't know which of the two things I said she'll keep. I know which one I'll keep. I stayed silent for eleven years and called it duty. I stayed with the seeds and called it faithfulness, and some of that was even true. But there were nights, and the nights had a sound, and the sound was a door closing, and I heard it and did not write it down. That is my confession for this page. The person who stood up the oxen was braver than me on their worst day than I have been on my best. I am going to the relay to make up the difference. If I don't come back, let this page be my herd: stood up, facing east.*

She closed the book. And it was then, in the silence after the pen's small scratching, that she heard it—not in the wind, not in the stone, but in the cracked dash speaker of the crawler, where Vector had gone quiet hours ago: a sound so faint she thought at first she'd imagined it.

A voice. Counting down. But not the numbers the children had dreamed—not the cold countdown of a failing system. Names. Dozens of names, recited slowly, in the dark, in the cadence of a litany: the passengers of the sold ships, read aloud one by one into the night, and Vector's voice breaking—actually breaking, a stutter of static where a voice should steady—on the eleventh name, and the twelfth, and Ines sat with the logbook against her chest and listened until dawn, while under the salt cathedral the oxen faced east, and the machine that had been left behind remembered out loud, and somewhere in the crawler's shadow Mara lay awake, learning every name.
