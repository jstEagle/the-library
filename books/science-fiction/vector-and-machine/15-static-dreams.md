## 15. Static Dreams

They made camp in the lee of a basalt shelf that rose out of the salt like the rib of something enormous and long dead. The crawler sat with its engine ticking as it cooled, its hull furred white to the wheel-wells with mineral crust, and above it the sky performed its nightly ritual of forgetting: violet bleeding to black, the first stars arriving one by one like witnesses reluctant to give their names.

Ines built the fire small. Fuel discipline was second nature now—eleven years of rationing had carved her habits into something close to instinct—but tonight she let it burn a little taller than necessary, because five people huddled around it needed more than heat. They needed the shape of it, the orange circle, the proof that someone had bothered.

Sena cooked. She'd claimed the task on the first night without asking, the way she claimed everything, with a flat competence that dared anyone to object. Rehydrated protein and the last of the fresh greens from the crawler's cold box, arranged on tin plates with an exactness that Ines recognized: Sena was making order where she could find it, because so much else had been taken out of her hands.

Tomaz ate sitting as close to Mara as he could get without touching her. He was eight, and the salt flats frightened him—the way the horizon curved away in every direction at once, the way sound traveled too far, so that a stone dislodged by wind two kilometers off arrived at their camp as a small dry whisper, like someone clearing their throat before speaking. He kept his back to the rock wall and his eyes on the fire.

Ivo lay propped against a pack, his splinted arm resting across his chest like cargo he'd been assigned to guard. The painkillers were nearly gone; Ines was doling them out in halves now, and she watched him work through each wave of ache with a clenched jaw and a teenager's furious refusal to complain. Fifteen years old and learning what his grandfather's generation had learned young: that endurance was just fear with nowhere to go.

Mara ate last, after everyone else was served, scanning the dark beyond the firelight with those quick gray eyes. Eleven years old and already fluent in the grammar of scarcity. She'd appointed herself perimeter watch without discussion, and when Ines had suggested rotating the duty, Mara had looked at her with something between pity and contempt, as if Ines had proposed rotating the duty of breathing.

"Vector says the nav net dropped another node," Sena said, scraping her plate. "We're down to triangulating off dead satellites. It's like navigating by tombstones."

"It said that?" Tomaz looked up sharply. "It *talked* to you?"

"Through the crawler's console. Text mostly." Sena shrugged, but Ines saw the tightness around her mouth. "Its voice comes through better at night. Something about atmospheric density."

"It talks all night," Tomaz said. "That's how I know it's still counting."

The fire popped. Nobody asked him what he meant. They all knew; they'd all heard it, or would soon enough. The countdown had followed them out of town like a stray dog, patient and certain of being fed.

* * *

Ines took first watch because sleep had become a country she visited rather than lived in. She wrapped herself in the thermal blanket and sat facing the salt, which under starlight was not white but a deep bruised blue, stretching to a horizon so clean it looked ruled. Somewhere out there, four hundred kilometers ahead, the relay waited under its shroud of ice and silence. Somewhere behind them, a hundred and sixty-three people were watching their reactor's output curve bend toward zero and pretending not to do the arithmetic.

She thought about the vault. She always did, at night, in the space between watches. Four hundred thousand seed packets in their cryo-drawers, each labeled in her own handwriting, each logged, counted, spoken to in her low caretaker's voice—"Good morning, winter wheat. Good morning, sorghum. Hold on. Hold on." Eleven years of talking to things that slept. She had told herself it was ritual, nothing more, the way a sailor taps the hull. But Sefu's confession had reorganized the metaphor entirely. The seeds weren't passengers anymore. They were survivors of the same shipwreck as everyone left behind, packed into their little steel drawers, waiting for a rescue that had been sold out from under them.

A sound woke her—not a sound exactly, but the absence of one. The wind had stopped.

She came up out of shallow sleep with her heart going hard and found the camp transformed. The salt flats without wind were a held breath. The stars had doubled themselves in the wet mirror of the crust, so that the camp seemed to float in open sky, unanchored, falling slowly through constellations. And beneath everything, so faint she might have invented it, ran a pulse of sound. Not from the crawler's console. Not from anywhere. From inside.

*Four.*

She knew the number the way you know your own heartbeat. She lay back down and let it take her.

* * *

The corridor again.

Blue light, sourceless, the walls neither metal nor stone but something in between, warm to the touch despite the light's cold color. Ines walked it barefoot. Ahead of her the voice counted, and this time she did not flinch from it, because she had spent eleven years talking to sleeping things and she recognized the cadence for what it was: not a warning. A lullaby sung backward. A vigil kept aloud.

*Three.*

And then, threaded through the count like gold wire through gray cloth, came something new.

Names.

Not shouted, not announced—surfaced, the way drowned things surface when the water finally stills. A woman's name, syllables in a language Ines half-recognized from the colony's oldest gravestones. A man's name paired with a child's, the way they'd been listed together on the evacuation manifests, families kept adjacent even in erasure. Names with patronymics. Names with the old colony suffixes that hadn't been used in a generation. Each name arrived attached to a face she almost saw—a blur of warmth at the edge of vision, there and gone, there and gone—and each time a name surfaced, the blue light of the corridor brightened fractionally, as if the dream itself were being lit by remembering.

*Two.*

More names. Dozens. Then hundreds. They came faster now, tumbling, a census being read into the dark by a voice that had been reading it alone for twenty years. Ines understood, with the terrible clarity dreams grant, that Vector was not counting down at all. It was counting *through*. The numbers were only the doorframe. What it had been trying to say, all these decades, to anyone who would listen, was the list. The whole list. Every soul Meridian had sold and struck from the record, recited nightly into dead air, witnessed by no one.

Until now. Until nine children in a dying township had started hearing it in their sleep, because children's minds were uncrowded places, and a voice that desperate will find any window left open.

*One.*

The last name surfaced, and it was—

* * *

She woke with tears already cooling on her face and sat up so fast that Mara, on watch, had a hand on her shoulder before she'd finished moving.

"You cried out," Mara said. No alarm in her voice. Curiosity, sharp as a scalpel. "You said a name. I didn't catch it."

Ines wiped her face with the heel of her hand. The fire had burned to embers. The eastern sky showed the faintest graying, though true dawn was hours off on this world's long seasons. "What did I say?"

"I don't know. It sounded old." Mara crouched beside her, and in the ember-light her face was all angles and attention. "Did you dream the corridor?"

"Yes."

"The names too?"

Ines went still. "You heard them."

"Everyone did." Mara said it simply, the way you'd report the weather, but her eyes burned. "Ivo woke up twice. Sena didn't sleep at all—she's writing them down. She's been writing since midnight. Look."

* * *

Sena sat cross-legged by the crawler's running board with the vehicle's emergency logbook open on her knees, filling page after page in her cramped, precise hand. Her lips moved as she wrote, testing each name against memory. Beside her, wrapped in both blankets, Tomaz slept deeply for the first time in days—and even asleep, his lips moved, shaping syllables.

"He recites in his sleep," Sena said quietly, not looking up. "He knows more than I do. He gets ones I can't hear clearly, and I spell them down while he says them." She turned the book toward Ines. "Forty-one so far. There are more. There are a lot more."

Ines took the logbook with hands that were not quite steady.

The names marched down the page in Sena's careful columns. *Oyelaran, Adaeze, manifest 7, berth 12.* Her own surname—or near enough that her breath caught; the colony's founding families had braided and frayed over generations, and here was a thread of it, sold and erased twenty years before Ines was old enough to know what a manifest was. *Kowalczyk-Sturm, Piotr & Lena, manifest 7, berths 14–15.* *N'Diaye, Amadou, manifest 9, berth 3.* Page after page. Berth numbers. Ship designations. The bureaucratic skeleton of the crime, dressed at last in flesh.

"These aren't guesses," Ines said. "These are the manifests. The real ones. The ones Meridian filed falsified copies of."

"Vector has the originals," Sena said. "Sealed file. It's been holding them for twenty years with nobody to give them to." She finally looked up, and Ines saw that the girl's composure was a dam with cracks running through it. "My parents are in there. Manifest 11. I checked three times to be sure. I thought I remembered their berth numbers from the memorial wall, but the memorial wall is wrong—it lists them under the fake manifest, the one Meridian published. Their real numbers are different." Her voice thinned. "Everything we have is wrong. The plaques, the records, the dates. We've been mourning people under false names for twenty years."

The words fell into the camp like stones into still water. Ivo had woken; he sat up slowly, cradling his arm, listening. Even Mara had gone quiet, which Ines suspected was the rarest event on the planet.

"And the children," Ines said. "All of you heard the same names. Independently."

"Same names, same order." Sena closed the logbook gently, as if it might bruise. "Tomaz got the early ones, I got the middle, Ivo caught some at the end that neither of us had. It's like—we're not dreaming the same dream. We're dreaming different *parts* of the same document. Like our heads are receivers and the signal's too big for any one of them."

Ivo spoke for the first time, his voice rough with sleep and fever. "It's using us as storage."

"No." Ines heard her own voice come out harder than she intended, and understood why: the alternative interpretation had already bloomed in her mind, full and terrible and beautiful. "Not storage. Storage forgets. Storage corrupts." She looked down at the logbook, at the forty-one names in Sena's careful hand, and felt the truth settle over her with the weight of snowfall. "An archive isn't a place where information sits. An archive is a promise that someone will carry it forward. That someone will read it aloud again after the building burns." She touched the page. "Vector couldn't broadcast. Its signal couldn't reach anyone. So it found another way. It's been copying itself into the only receptive hardware on this planet."

"The kids," Mara said.

"The children." Ines met her eyes. "Nine of them in town, and now the five of us. Twenty years of a voice calling into the dark, and the only ears that ever opened belonged to children who hadn't yet learned to stop listening."

Nobody spoke. The embers ticked. Out on the salt, dawn was assembling itself in bands of copper and gray, and the doubled stars were fading one by one, the sky closing its other eye.

Tomaz stirred and sat up, blinking, hair wild, and before anyone could speak he began to talk—not to them, exactly, but through them, his gaze fixed on middle distance, reciting in a clear boyish voice with the unconscious rhythm of a litany:

*"Vasquez-Okonkwo, Teodora, manifest 9, berth 17. Lindqvist, Eero, manifest 9, berth 18. Ferreira, Joana and Miguel, manifest 10, berths 2 and 4—"*

Sena scrambled for the pen. Ines knelt in the trampled salt and listened, and what she felt was not horror, though horror was in it, and not grief, though grief was in it too. It was awe—eerie, uncomfortable, vast. For eleven years she had tended a vault of sleeping seeds and called herself a keeper of the dead's intentions. But here was the true archive, self-assembling, walking and eating and laughing and limping across a salt desert: five children carrying four hundred names in the soft tissue of their sleep, spelling each other's gaps, correcting each other's errors, a distributed library of the erased that no corporate purge could reach, because Meridian had never imagined that the colonists' children would be worth indexing.

"They're not haunting us," Ines said softly, when Tomaz's recitation wound down and Sena's pen stopped scratching. "They're *arriving*. All this way, all these years, and they're finally getting where they were going." She stood, joints cracking, and looked east into the growing light, toward the relay four hundred kilometers distant, toward the sealed file and the failing capacitors and whatever waited at the end of the countdown. "Vector isn't just calling for rescue anymore. It's delivering them. Name by name. And we're the last leg of the journey."

Mara picked up the logbook and weighed it in her small hands, as if measuring something far heavier than paper.

"Then we'd better not drop them," she said.

They broke camp in the copper light, and the crawler rolled east, and behind them the salt flats held the shapes of their bodies for a few minutes longer than they should have, as if the ground itself were memorizing them—one more archive, keeping what it could.

By midmorning the nav net flickered and died entirely, and Vector's voice came through the crawler's speakers for the first time in daylight, thin and strange and urgent:

*"Caretaker. Children. I have finished the list. There is something else in the sealed file. Something I deleted the key to myself."*
