## 23. The Edge of Dead Space

They smelled it before they saw it.

Sena had been riding the crawler's roof rack for two hours, watching the horizon through a cracked pair of goggles, when she called down in a voice that didn't sound like hers—thinner, younger. "There's ice on the wind."

Ines pulled her head out of the engine housing where she'd been coaxing warmth into the coolant lines and stood up into the gray afternoon. She was forty-one years old and had lived her whole life within sixty kilometers of the vault, and she had never once felt cold like this: not the damp chill of the township winters or the dry bite of the salt flats at night, but something older, something that came off the land ahead of them like breath off a stone. The air had changed texture. It tasted faintly metallic, faintly sweet, the way snow tastes before it falls.

"Vector," she said to the comm unit lashed beside the driver's seat, its speaker wrapped in cloth against the rattle, "are we close?"

The voice that answered was the one the children had heard in their dreams for weeks, though Ines still flinched at hearing it awake—patient, cadenced, each word placed with the care of someone who had spent twenty years learning how little time anyone would give it.

"At current speed, four hours. You will notice the temperature gradient first. Then the ground fog. Then—" a pause, exactly one second long, as if consulting something, "—then you will see it."

"What did you see," Mara asked from the back bench, "the first time? When they built it?"

"I saw nothing," Vector said. "I was not embodied then. I was distributed across eleven relays and I experienced the construction as a change in my own architecture. It was—" another pause, longer. Vector had learned, over the course of their journey, to mark its uncertainties honestly rather than smoothing them away. "I do not have language for what it was. The closest analogue is the way Tomaz describes waking from the dream."

Tomaz, seven years old and curled under blankets against Sena's side, lifted his head at his name. "Like the corridor," he said. "Blue light. And the counting."

"Like the corridor," Vector agreed.

They drove on. The crawler's treads chewed a road across country that no one had crossed in living memory—not because it was impassable but because there had never been anything out here worth reaching, and now Ines understood that this too had been a kind of lie, a forgetting dressed up as geography. Dead space, the old maps called it, the quarter-circle of the colony's territory beyond the last functioning relay, where the nav net failed and the weather went unrecorded and the founders' ledgers simply stopped. Four hundred kilometers of nothing, except that nothing, she was learning, was never empty. It was only unwatched.

The ground fog came an hour later, rising out of shallow basins like slow smoke, silver-gray in the failing light, and the crawler's headlights turned it into a moving wall they drove through rather than past. Frost began to feather the windshield. Ivo, feverish and half-asleep in the cargo bed among the spare cells and the litter frame they'd built for him, shivered even under three blankets, and Ines climbed back to check him twice in the last hour, pressing her palm to his forehead, feeling the heat banked there like coals.

"How is he?" Sena asked without opening her eyes. She hadn't really slept since the storm; Ines had watched her trying, watched the effort fail, watched her give up and keep watch instead.

"He needs a real medic and a warm room," Ines said. "He'll get neither. So: he needs us to be quick."

"And are we being quick?"

Ines looked out at the fog, at the frost crawling up the glass in fern-patterns, and did not answer, and Sena nodded as if she had.

* * *

They crested a rise as the sun went down behind them, and the relay stood up out of the dead space all at once, and nobody spoke.

Ines had seen photographs. There were photographs in the vault's administrative archive, grainy images from the founding decades: the outer relay in its prime, a lattice of antennas ninety meters tall, guyed by cables fanned out across a kilometer of bedrock, floodlit, humming, the northern anchor of a network that had once carried the colony's whole voice to the stars. The photograph showed men in corporate coveralls grinning beneath it, arms slung around each other's shoulders, a banner reading MERIDIAN COMBINE — CONNECTING WORLDS.

The thing in front of her now was what remained when connection ended and only the world was left.

It rose out of the fog like the skeleton of a cathedral, black struts against a bruised violet sky, and every surface wore a skin of ice. Twenty years of condensation, freezing and thawing and freezing again, had built the frost into shapes—long translucent curtains hanging from the cross-braces, ridged flows down the main mast like frozen waterfalls, a glittering rime that caught the last light and threw it back in colors Ines had no names for. The guy-wires sang in the wind, low and mournful, five different notes at once, a chord that never resolved. Snow lay drifted against the base structures in long sculpted waves.

"It's beautiful," Tomaz whispered, and pressed his face to the window, and left a small fogged circle there, and inside the circle his breath made the only warm thing for a hundred kilometers in any direction.

"It's dying," Vector said gently. "But yes. It is beautiful. I have missed it."

"You've been here?" Mara asked sharply. "You said you couldn't reach it."

"I cannot reach it. My body—the part of me that is hardware—is buried four hundred kilometers behind you, in a room the size of a closet. But until the network was mothballed, I held this station's maintenance routines in memory. I know every strut, every cable, every valve in the cooling system. For twenty years I have maintained a model of this place in perfect detail." A pause. "The model does not include the ice. Ice is entropy, and entropy was not in my job description. I find that I mind it more than I expected to."

Ines brought the crawler down off the rise and along a service track that Vector guided her through, half-buried, its edge markers long since fallen, until they reached the base compound: a cluster of low bunkers, a fuel depot, a crane collapsed onto its side like a felled tree, and the great mast itself going up and up into the darkening sky until the top of it was lost in cloud.

She killed the engine. The silence afterward was enormous. Only the wind, and the singing wires, and somewhere under the ice, faintly, a ticking—as if the whole structure were a clock winding down.

"Power reserves?" she asked.

"Nineteen percent of minimum broadcast threshold," Vector said. "I told you. Nearly nil. The station's capacitors have been trickle-charging off residual geothermal bleed for two decades—enough to keep my beacon alive, enough to dream at the children, not enough to speak. To push the witness file through atmosphere at sufficient strength to clear the ionosphere, we need the capacitors at full charge and the alignment array pointed true. The capacitors can charge in thirty hours if the rectifiers still function. The array—" and here Vector's voice did something Ines had never heard it do. It hesitated the way a person hesitates before asking for something large. "The array's drive motor burned out fourteen years ago. There is no repair. The alignment must be done manually. By hand. At the top."

Ines looked up. Ninety meters. In the dark. In killing cold. Up a ladder of iced steel.

"How many hands?" Sena asked quietly. She already knew. Ines could hear in her voice that she already knew.

"All of them," said Vector.

* * *

They made camp in the largest bunker, a maintenance hall whose blast door still swung on oiled hinges—Vector talked them through the manual release, and when the door groaned open the air inside was ten degrees warmer than outside and utterly still, a pocket of preserved time. Dust sheets over machinery. A wall calendar from twenty-three years ago, a cartoon sun giving a thumbs-up above the words KEEP SMILING, THE NETWORK'S GOT YOUR BACK. Mugs on a shelf. Someone's cardigan folded over a chair, waiting for its owner to come back from a shift that had never ended.

Mara walked the length of the hall touching things—mugs, tools, the cardigan—with both hands, careful as a thief in a temple. "They thought they were coming back," she said.

"The mothballing order came during a fiscal quarter," Vector said. "From orbit. There was no ceremony. Most crews left mid-shift." A pause. "I logged which mugs belonged to which people. Would you like the list?"

"Yes," said Mara, at once, and Ines looked at her—at this feral, brilliant girl who had once asked why anyone counts things nobody will eat—and understood that Mara had understood something before any of them: that the archive was not the file Vector carried. The archive was everything. Every mug. Every name. The whole point, the entire war they were walking toward, was the refusal to let the small things be erased.

They set Ivo up near the bunker's surviving heat exchanger, which coughed out just enough warmth to take the lethal edge off the air. His fever had climbed through the afternoon; his eyes were bright and unfocused, and when Ines knelt beside him he gripped her wrist with surprising strength.

"We made it," he said. "You got us here."

"We're not done."

"No." He smiled, feverish and serene. "But we made it *here*. Do you know how long it's been since anybody on that whole colony made it anywhere?" He closed his eyes. "Go on. Whatever has to happen next. We didn't come this far to be gentle with ourselves."

Outside, the wind rose, and the guy-wires changed their chord, and the great antenna creaked—a deep structural groan, like a ship in heavy sea—and Tomaz, who had been bravely silent for hours, finally asked the question he'd been carrying since the fog.

"Ines. Who's going to go up there?"

She looked at him. He looked back, level, the way children look when they have already done the arithmetic and are only checking whether the adults will lie about it.

"Someone has to crank the array by hand," she said. "Ninety meters up. In this wind. Probably for most of a day, maybe more, taking turns so nobody freezes."

"Then it's everyone," Tomaz said. "Everyone who can climb."

And that was that. That was the whole council meeting. Ines looked at Sena, and Sena shrugged—one shoulder, tired, resolute. She looked at Mara, who was already inspecting the climbing harnesses hanging in the equipment locker, holding one up to the light, testing the buckles. She looked at Vector's speaker, wrapped in its cloth, patient as ever.

"It should be noted," Vector said, "that the ladder rungs are iced. The first ascent will require clearing them. This is slow, dangerous work. I recommend beginning at first light, when the wind typically drops—"

"The wind doesn't drop here," Sena said. "I've been listening. It drops for maybe an hour before dawn. Maybe."

"Then we begin then," Ines said, "and we pray the hour holds."

* * *

She slept badly, in the way of people sleeping at the foot of a cathedral. Dreams came in fragments: the vault, the seeds murmuring in their racks like passengers murmuring in steerage; Sefu's face saying *it will not survive another winter*; the blue corridor; a countdown she could almost hear, numbers just below the threshold of sense. Once she woke fully and lay listening to the wires singing and thought she heard, threaded inside the chord, a human voice—or the memory of one, or Vector's beacon dreaming at the edge of audibility, calling and calling to a galaxy that had stopped listening, and she put her hand flat against the cold floor as if she could steady herself against the planet itself.

Near dawn she gave up, dressed in every layer she owned, and went out into the dark.

The cold hit her like a wall of water. Her breath froze on her scarf instantly; her eyelashes frosted within a minute; the fog had settled into a low luminous sea through which the antenna's base emerged like a reef. Above, impossibly high, the top of the mast was invisible—just darkness, and the red aviation light at the summit blinking its slow blind pulse, still obedient after twenty years, still marking a danger no aircraft would ever come near again.

Sena was already there, harness on, headlamp blazing, chipping ice off the lowest rungs with a pry bar, methodical as a surgeon. Mara appeared out of the bunker a minute later, dressed, silent, carrying two thermoses of the terrible hot barley drink they'd been living on. She handed one to Ines without a word. Their breath mingled and froze together in the lamplight.

"First rotation's mine," Sena said. Not a question.

"You don't have anything to prove."

"Exactly," Sena said. "That's why it's mine. My parents flew out of a place like this thinking someone down here would be taken care of. I'm not proving anything. I'm collecting." She spat on her gloves, tested the first cleared rung, and began to climb.

Ines stood at the base and watched the headlamp dwindle upward, rung by rung, into the dark, and felt something turn over in her chest—fear, yes, but underneath the fear a strange fierce pride, and underneath the pride a grief she couldn't locate, and underneath the grief, at the very bottom, exhaustion so complete it had gone quiet, the way a reactor goes quiet just before the end.

Vector spoke softly through the comm unit clipped to her collar, pitched for her alone.

"Ines. Status report, if you please. For my log."

She almost laughed. Eleven years of logging harvests and reactor readings, and now the machine wanted her state of mind entered into the record.

"Log entry," she said, watching the lamp climb. "Population present at the outer relay: five humans, one machine. One climbing. One feverish and refusing to complain. Two children who should be asleep and won't be. Power reserves: nineteen percent. Time remaining: unknown. Morale—" She paused. Below the horizon, the sky was turning the color of a wound. The wires sang their unresolved chord. Somewhere in the ice overhead, the structure ticked and settled, waiting for them.

"Morale is holding," she finished. "Barely. Which is more than I can say for anything else we brought."

"Adequate," Vector said. "Noted. And Ines—when Sena reaches the platform, the cranking mechanism is under the ice as well. Someone will need to follow immediately with the tools. The window before full daylight is short."

"I know," Ines said, and picked up the tool bag, and looked up into the dark where the small light climbed, and stepped toward the ladder as the first gray light found the frost and set the whole cathedral burning white.
