## 24. Hand-Crank Heaven

The wind at the edge of dead space did not howl so much as *insist*—a long, flat exhalation off the salt that had been traveling, Ines thought, since before any of them were born, gathering nothing on the way but cold. It came up the antenna field in sheets, found the gaps in their thermal wraps, and pressed its fingers there without apology.

The outer relay rose out of the white like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down to die and changed its mind halfway. A hundred masts. A forest of guy-wires singing their own thin notes. And above them all, the alignment array: a dish the size of a chapel roof, seized at forty degrees off true, crusted with a decade of ice and mineral dust, its drive motors long since stripped for parts by whoever had mothballed this place and walked away.

Vector's voice came through the handheld unit Sena wore on a cord around her neck, calm as ever, but Ines had learned by now to hear the seams in that calm.

"The manual crank assembly is intact," Vector said. "Bearing friction will be significant. I have no way to estimate how many rotations are required. The last alignment log in my memory ends with the number four thousand one hundred and six."

"Four thousand," Tomaz repeated. He was nine, and he said it the way other children said *a million*, which was to say: without any real understanding of what it meant, only that it was enormous.

"It may be fewer," Vector offered. "It may be more. The ice adds resistance I cannot model."

Ines looked up at the dish, then at the three children looking up at it with her—Mara with her jaw set the way it always set when the world asked her something unfair; Sena already doing arithmetic with her eyes, her lips moving; Tomaz simply staring, his breath pluming, his small hands flexing open and shut as if rehearsing.

They had left Ivo two days ago at the substation, feverish, insisting, armed with Vector's protocols and a promise. Ines carried that promise now like a stone sewn into her coat lining. Every hour spent here was an hour Ivo sat alone in the dark rationing water. Every rotation of this crank was a debt being paid toward him.

"All right," she said, and heard her own voice come out flatter than she intended, so she tried again. "All right. We take turns. Short shifts. Nobody's a hero."

Mara snorted. "Nobody here qualifies anyway."

"Speak for yourself," said Tomaz.

* * *

The crank was bolted to a platform at the base of the array's support pylon, reachable by a ladder of rungs that the frost had made treacherous. The handle itself was a steel arm longer than Tomaz was tall, its grip wrapped in what had once been rubber and was now a fossil of rubber, cracked and crumbling under Ines's gloves.

She went first, because she would not ask them to do anything she hadn't done.

The first turn was impossible. She put her whole body into it—boots braced against the platform grating, back curved, breath held—and the handle moved perhaps ten degrees with a groan that traveled up through the pylon and into the dish overhead, shaking loose a fine snow of ice crystals that the wind took and scattered like salt from a spilled cellar.

"That's one," she gasped.

"You counted wrong," Mara called up. "It didn't go all the way around."

"I'm establishing precedent. Precedent doesn't have to be good."

She got the second turn halfway before her arms quit. She stood there a moment, forehead against the frozen steel, feeling the wind try to peel her off the platform, and understood with sudden clarity why the people who built this place had installed a hand crank at all. Not as a backup. As a *ritual*. Someone, decades ago, had stood exactly here, in exactly this wind, and turned this exact handle, believing the voice they sent out would matter to someone. Ines put her hands back on the grip.

"Sena. Take over."

They worked out the rhythm by trial: thirty seconds each, or until failure, whichever came first. Tomaz lasted eleven seconds on his first shift and was furious about it, and furious about being furious, and climbed down red-faced while Sena swung up past him without comment, which was its own kindness.

And then Vector began to sing.

It was not, strictly speaking, singing. It was a cadence—a work chant assembled, Vector later admitted, from fragments of loading-dock recordings, mining-haul rhythms, and one corrupted file of an old Earth sea shanty whose lyrics had degraded into beautiful nonsense. But pitched against the wind, timed to the strain of the crank, it landed in the chest like a drum.

*Pull on the count and hold on the hold,*
*the wire remembers what the world forgot—*

"What does that even mean?" Mara shouted, hauling on the second half-turn.

"It means nothing," Vector said. "That is what makes it a shanty. Meaning is optional. Rhythm is not."

*Turn it, turn it, the sky is asleep,*
*wake it with iron and knuckle and breath—*

Something happened in the next hour that none of them planned and none of them could afterward fully explain. The turns stopped being turns. They became measures. Sena pulled on the downbeat. Tomaz, who had figured out that he couldn't out-muscle the bearing but could out-*time* it, learned to start his push just as Vector's cadence dipped, riding the harmonic of the pylon itself, adding his small weight to the structure's own oscillation. Mara called it cheating. Vector called it resonance engineering. Tomaz called it nothing, because he was laughing too hard.

Ines stood at the bottom of the ladder, rotating them through, chapping her lips shouting counts over the wind, and felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight for eleven years.

* * *

By the fourth hour the light had gone the color of old pewter and the temperature had dropped into the range where exposed skin stopped hurting and started simply leaving. They wrapped Tomaz's face in a scarf until only his eyes showed. They taped Sena's split glove. Mara refused the scarf—"I can't hear the count with wool in my ears"—and Ines let her, because some battles were not worth the breath.

They had completed, by Vector's tally, nine hundred and twelve rotations. The dish had moved eleven degrees.

"Do the math," Mara said, hanging off the ladder, arms dead, watching Sena climb. "Don't do the math, Sena. I can see you doing the math."

"At this rate we finish in fifty-one hours," Sena said. "The convoy arrives in eight."

Silence, except the wind and the wires.

"So we don't go at this rate," Ines said.

She looked at their faces—Sena's pale and stubborn, Tomaz's eyes enormous above the scarf, Mara's narrowed against the grit—and saw the moment the arithmetic landed in each of them differently. Sena recalculated. Tomaz went still. Mara smiled, and it was not a nice smile, and Ines loved it completely.

"Double shifts," Mara said. "Two on the crank at once. It's a two-person handle if you're stupid enough."

"We're stupid enough," said Tomaz.

* * *

Paired cranking changed everything and cost everything. Two bodies on the steel arm, backs together or facing, pushing in counterpoint, doubled the torque and halved the endurance. Shifts dropped to fifteen seconds. The rotations per hour nearly tripled, and so did the damage: torn blisters that froze before they could bleed properly, a wrenched shoulder for Sena that she hid for two full shifts until Ines saw her favoring it and ordered her down, the first order Ines had ever given that someone obeyed without argument.

Ines climbed up herself, paired with Mara, because Mara would accept no partner who hadn't taken the same punishment.

They pushed together, cheek to cheek almost, both of them grunting on the strain, and Vector's cadence rolled underneath:

*Four for the founders who sold the ride,*
*one for the ledger where the names were filed,*
*turn it over, turn it over, the page is blank—*
*write it again, child, write it again—*

"Who's it talking about?" Tomaz called from below, where he'd been assigned the critical job of pouring warm water over the bearing housing to keep the ice from welding it solid.

"The erased passengers," Sena said quietly. "There were four hundred and eleven."

"Then why does it say four?"

"Because the song only holds what fits in the song." Sena looked up at the dish, at the degrees still to go. "The rest of them are in us now. That's what Tomaz's dreams are. That's the archive."

Tomaz considered this, nine years old with a kettle of near-boiling water in mittens, standing in a killing wind at the edge of the settled universe. "Okay," he said finally. "But the song should say four hundred and eleven. When we fix the dish, I'm telling it to fix the song."

Above him, Mara laughed.

It came out of her sideways, mid-strain, punched loose by the absurdity of it—a small boy issuing editorial corrections to an artificial intelligence at the end of the world—and it rang off the pylon and the wires and the ice, bright and unguarded and utterly unlike any sound Ines had heard from her in all the weeks since the township. Mara laughed and kept pushing, laughed and pushed, and Ines pushed with her, matching her rhythm, and for three or four rotations neither of them felt the weight at all.

"There it is," Ines said.

"Don't," Mara warned, still laughing. "Don't make it a thing."

"It's a thing."

"*Don't.*"

"It's the best thing I've heard in eleven years, and I keep a very detailed log."

Mara laughed harder, which ruined her leverage, which made the crank stall, which made Vector patiently resume the count from the top of the measure, and somehow that was funnier still.

* * *

Night fell at what should have been midafternoon, the sun drowning early behind a wall of cloud, and they kept working by headlamp beams that the wind kept trying to steal. Vector adjusted the cadence to match their failing tempo, slowing it, stretching it, the way you shorten your stride to walk beside someone smaller. Ines noticed. She suspected the children noticed. No one said anything, because acknowledging it might make it stop.

At rotation two thousand seven hundred, Tomaz fell asleep standing up against the ladder and had to be carried to the crawler and zipped into a sleeping bag between the battery cells, protesting to the last that he was fine, he was *fine*, he had a job—

"You'll have a job tomorrow," Ines told him. "The bearing needs guarding overnight. It's a position of great responsibility."

"Liar," he said, already gone.

They worked on without him. Three sets of hands now, and the math bent the wrong way again, and they bent with it. Ines's palms split along the blister lines and she wrapped them with tape from the med kit and kept going, and somewhere past midnight—though *midnight* was a fiction this far from any clock that mattered—the dish crossed the halfway mark and the pylon's groan changed pitch, higher, easier, the bearing finding some forgotten polish beneath the corrosion.

"It knows us now," Sena murmured, half-delirious, hauling on the downbeat. "It's remembering how to move."

"Everything remembers," Vector said. "Given sufficient pressure."

* * *

Near dawn—or near what passed for it—the wind died.

Not gradually. It simply stopped, all at once, the way a held breath is released, and the silence that followed was so total and so vast that Mara actually staggered on the platform, robbed of the force she'd been leaning into for fourteen hours. The guy-wires stopped singing. The salt lay white and endless and perfectly still under a sky going gray-rose at the rim of the world.

In the quiet they could hear the crank turning. Just the crank: steel, bearing, breath. Turn after turn after turn, three exhausted figures taking their fifteen seconds, and no wind to fight, and the dish sweeping upward degree by degree like a slow eyelid opening.

"Final segment," Vector said softly. "One hundred and twelve rotations to lock."

They didn't cheer. Something in the quality of the silence forbade it. They simply worked, faster now, Sena and Mara paired and Ines solo-filling the gaps, and the numbers came down—ninety, seventy, fifty—and the eastern sky caught fire along its edge, and at rotation forty-one Tomaz came stumbling out of the crawler in his sleeping bag like a molting bird, having slept four hours and decided it was plenty.

"My job," he croaked.

"Your job," Ines agreed, and lifted him onto the platform to pour the last warm water over the bearing.

Twenty. Ten. Five.

On the final rotation the mechanism changed character entirely—the grinding resistance vanished, replaced by a smooth, gliding ease, as though the array had been waiting all these years for exactly this much force and no more—and the dish settled into its lock with a deep, resonant *clunk* that they felt through their boots before they heard it.

Alignment achieved. The dish faced the narrow window of sky through which, if anyone was still listening, a signal could pass.

Nobody spoke. The sun cleared the horizon and struck the dish full-on, and it blazed, a perfect circle of firelight, throwing their shadows long and thin across the salt behind them—four shadows, five counting the crawler, six counting the ghost they'd left at the substation with a beacon and a promise.

"Capacitors are beginning to charge," Vector said. Its cadence-voice was gone now; this was its other voice, the careful one, the one it used when something mattered. "Reserve power is low. Charge time to minimum broadcast threshold: approximately ninety minutes."

Mara slid down the ladder and stood in the snow at the base of the pylon, craning her neck, her ruined hands hanging at her sides, her breath steaming in the still air. Then she turned to Ines, and her eyes were wet, and she was smiling, and she pointed up at the blazing dish with a blistered finger.

"Tell it to sing the four hundred and eleven first," she said.

Behind them, out on the white plain to the south, something glinted—brief, hard, moving. Sena saw it too. Her hand closed on Ines's sleeve.

"Ines," she said. "How fast can ninety minutes be?"
