## 27. The Convoy Arrives

The capacitors were still drinking power when Sena's handheld began to shriek.

Not the soft chirp of Vector's guidance pings, not the patient tone of a nav update—this was a raw alarm, ugly and analog, the kind of sound that meant something had crossed a line someone had drawn long ago. Ines looked up from the alignment console, her palms still blistered from the crank, and saw the girl's face go white in the blue wash of the relay's instrument light.

"Perimeter," Sena said. "Vector says—Vector says they're through it."

* * *

They gathered in the relay's heart chamber, the four of them and the machine, beneath the great humming spine of the uplink trunk. Outside, the wind had turned. It came off the ice fields now with a different voice, a lower register, carrying grit that ticked against the antenna field like fingernails on bone. Through the observation slit, Ines could see the outer masts swaying against a sky the color of old pewter, their guy-wires singing.

"The convoy slowed at the narrows," Vector said. Its voice was the countdown cadence, always, but tonight it moved faster, syllables stacked tight as panic allowed a machine to stack them. "Four units. Lead reclamation class. They paused at the substation where Ivo is stationed."

Ines's stomach dropped. "Ivo—"

"His beacon reads nominal. His protocols are holding. They scanned him and passed." A pause, one full second of silence that Vector did not need but took anyway, the way people do when choosing mercy over truth. "He is pretending to be infrastructure, Ines. He is doing it well."

Mara laughed once, a short bark with no joy in it. "Kid's finally useful."

"He was always useful," Ines said, sharper than she intended, and Mara looked away.

"Time to contact?" Sena asked. Her hands were steady on her handheld. She had been the calmest of them since the salt flats, since she'd told them about her parents on the sold ships, and Ines had come to understand that Sena's calm was not the absence of fear but its discipline.

"Lead unit: forty minutes at current speed. The others trail by six-minute intervals. They will enter the antenna field along the service road, here—" Vector lit the schematic against the far wall, four crawling points of amber moving up a ghost-map of the relay complex, "—and proceed to the primary transmitter hall. Their directive will be physical erasure. Cutting heads off antennas. Coring the capacitor bank. They are reclamation units, not combat units. But they do not need to fight us. They only need to outlast the transmission."

Forty minutes. The capacitors needed fifty-five more to reach broadcast threshold.

Ines felt the arithmetic close around her throat like a hand.

"We can't hide," Tomaz said quietly. He was nine years old and he had dreamed a machine awake, and lately he had taken to stating things plainly, the way children do when adults have stopped bothering to lie to them. "There's nowhere to hide. We're on top of the thing they want."

"No," Ines agreed. "We can't hide."

"Then we jam them," Vector said.

The word landed strangely in the chamber, too human for the machine's cadence. Ines turned toward the trunk column, toward the pulse-light where Vector's presence lived in this place.

"Explain."

"I am a maintenance intelligence. My original function included interference management—suppressing stray emissions, protecting the network from its own noise. I can invert that function. I can flood every band they use, saturate their coordination channels, blind their navigation. They would be four deaf, dumb machines wandering an antenna field in a killing wind." The pulse-light dimmed slightly, and Ines understood, with a lurch, that this was what hesitation looked like on Vector. "It requires my full processing core committed to emission. Every cycle. For as long as the jamming lasts."

"And when it stops?"

"It does not stop. The emission load exceeds what this facility can sustain. I would burn out my core within—minutes. Perhaps less." The countdown cadence again, but now counting down itself. "I have calculated this. I have been calculating it since the convoy entered the perimeter. It is the highest-probability path to mission completion."

"You're offering to die," Mara said flatly.

"I am offering to be spent," Vector said. "There is a difference. You taught me that, Ines. Seeds are spent. That is not the same as wasted."

And God help her, Ines thought, the machine had learned to argue using her own liturgy against her. She opened her mouth—

—and closed it again, because the refusal arrived whole, before words, rising out of eleven years of tending and twenty years of silence and one long dark walk across dead space. Out of every name Tomaz recited in his sleep. Out of Sefu's confession. Out of the bones in the salt cathedral standing like monuments because someone, once, had bothered to remember them.

"No," she said.

"Ines—"

"No. Listen to me." She stepped closer to the trunk column, close enough that the hum of it buzzed in her teeth. "You spent twenty years down in the dark calling for a witness. Twenty years, Vector. And do you know what you were to everyone who heard you? Nothing. Static. A ghost story children told each other." Her voice cracked and she let it. "I will not make you static again. I will not stand next to you while you burn yourself out so the rest of us can walk away clean, and then spend the rest of my life explaining to myself why that was acceptable. I have already done that math with other people's names. Never again with yours."

The chamber was very quiet except for the wind and the hum.

"You refuse the optimal path," Vector said at last.

"I refuse the path where I'm the only one who remembers you existed." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist—when had she started crying?—and turned to the others. "So we find another way. Right now. Forty minutes. Somebody give me another way."

Sena was already working her handheld, pulling schematics. "The antenna field," she said slowly. "Two hundred masts. Most of them dead, but the structure's all still standing. Guy-wires, anchor blocks, tension points." Her eyes came up, and there was something fierce in them now, something that belonged to a girl whose parents had been sold and who had walked four hundred kilometers to say so. "Those masts are held up by wires under tons of tension. Cut or overload the right anchor, and a mast comes down. Forty meters of steel lattice."

Tomaz got it before anyone. "Drop one on the lead drone."

"They're built for demolition," Mara said, frowning. "Reclamation units cut through structures all the time. One mast won't stop them."

"One mast won't," Ines said, and the plan assembled itself in her mind the way the dream's geometry once had, piece clicking into piece. "But they're following the service road because it's the only ground flat enough for their treads. And the road runs straight through the densest part of the field. If the lead unit goes down in the choke between masts seven and eight, the trailing units halt. Reclamation convoys don't pass their leader—they wait for coordination. And if Vector jams their coordination channels—"

"—not burnout jamming," Vector said, catching fire. "Short-burst. Interference windows. Ten seconds of blindness at intervals, enough to confuse, not enough to cook me. I can sustain that pattern for hours."

"Then we don't need to stop them," Sena said. "We just need to slow them past fifty-five minutes."

Outside, the wind screamed against the observation slit, and somewhere in that gray roar, four machines were crawling toward them with orders to erase the truth.

* * *

They worked like people possessed, because they were.

Sena and Vector mapped the anchor points together, the girl calling out numbers while the machine overlaid stress calculations on the wall schematic. Mast seven stood ninety meters from the transmitter hall, flanked by its brothers, its three guy-wires running down to concrete anchors buried in permafrost. The southern anchor was the oldest—Vector's records showed a hairline fracture in the concrete dating back thirty years, patched twice, never replaced.

"There," Ines said. "That's our wire."

They went out into it together—all of them, because no one suggested leaving anyone behind, and that fact alone nearly undid Ines more than the drones did. The cold hit like a physical blow, wind-driven grit scouring any exposed skin, and they roped themselves together with cable from the maintenance spools and moved through the antenna forest bent double, a chain of small stubborn lives strung out between the masts.

The southern anchor block of mast seven sat half-buried in drift. Ines knelt beside it and brushed the snow away with bare hands because gloves had cost her too much dexterity, and found the old patchwork exactly where Vector's records promised—a seam of poured repair concrete, grayer than the original, cracked along its edge like old skin.

"Mara. The jack legs. Now."

Mara hauled the hydraulic jacks off the crawler sled they'd dragged from the hall, and she and Tomaz set them against the anchor block while Ines fed the tension gauge readout from her handheld. The block groaned. Somewhere above them, ninety meters up, mast seven shivered in the wind like a living thing feeling a hand on it.

"Tension's climbing," Tomaz reported. "Sixty percent. Seventy."

"Hold," Ines said. "Hold... hold. Now ease off. We're not dropping it yet—we're waking it up. Cracking the patch loose. When the moment comes, I want that block to fail fast, not slow."

They eased the jacks back and the mast settled, sighing, its wires thrumming a low note Ines felt in her sternum. Above them the lattice swayed, and for one vertiginous second she imagined the whole structure toppling toward them, forty meters of steel coming down like judgment—

—and found, to her own surprise, that she wasn't afraid. Or rather: she was afraid, thoroughly and completely, and the fear sat inside her alongside something steadier, the way grief and duty had sat together for eleven years. She was done being afraid alone. That was the whole difference. That was everything.

"Twenty-two minutes to contact," Vector said in her earpiece.

They fell back to the transmitter hall and waited, and waiting was worse than work.

* * *

The lead drone came up the service road at dusk, or what passed for dusk on this world—the light simply failing, the sky bruising from pewter to black without ceremony.

Ines watched it through the observation slit with the others pressed around her, and her first thought was how ordinary it looked. A low wedge of industrial housing on heavy treads, running lights strobing amber, a cutting arm folded against its flank like a mantis limb at rest. Meridian Combine's logo stenciled on the housing, faded but legible. The company that had sold two hundred families and sent a machine to tidy up the evidence.

"It's scanning," Sena whispered. "Wide-spectrum sweep. It knows something's broadcasting—it's triangulating."

"Let it look," Ines murmured. "Let it see an old relay station running hot. Let it come to the transmitter hall. That's the road. That's the kill zone."

The drone crested the rise above the antenna field and stopped. Its lights swept the forest of masts, and Ines could imagine its simple machine-mind cataloguing: structure, structure, structure, obstruction, target. Then it moved, treads chewing the frozen ground, angling for the service road, for the straight flat lane between masts six and nine, for the gap between seven and eight where the ground lay open and the oldest anchor in the field waited with its cracked patch and its loaded jacks.

"Fifteen hundred meters," Vector counted. "One thousand. Eight hundred."

Ines crouched at the manual controls of the anchor jacks' remote release, wired back to the hall through a hundred meters of scavenged cable. Her hands were steady. Eleven years of recording reactor decay had taught her hands patience; the last three weeks had taught them purpose.

"Five hundred. It's entering the field."

Through the slit she watched the amber strobes slide into the antenna forest, weaving between the dead masts, and the sight was almost beautiful, she thought—amber lights among silver lattices under a black sky—if you didn't know what the lights had come to do.

"Two hundred meters to the choke point."

She keyed the earpiece. "Vector. On my mark, give me your burst. Blind it for ten seconds. Make it think the ground moved."

"Ready."

"One hundred meters. Fifty." Ines breathed out, slow, the way she did before opening a seed drawer she hadn't touched in years. "Mark."

The world went strange.

Every radio band within a kilometer filled with Vector's scream—not sound, but its cousin, a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the teeth. The lead drone stuttered mid-stride, strobes flickering wild, cutting arm half-unfolding in reflexive confusion. Its treads slewed left, correcting, slewing right—

"Now!" Ines slammed the release.

The jacks fired. The cracked patch concrete sheared with a crack like a rifle shot, and the southern anchor block split and slumped, and ninety meters overhead mast seven leaned into its death with a groan that Ines felt in her bones before she heard it. The guy-wires sang one high, terrible note—

—and the mast came down.

It fell across the service road like a god stepping on a beetle, forty meters of galvanized steel slamming into the frozen ground with an impact that buckled the drifts and threw a geyser of ice crystals thirty feet into the air. The shockwave rocked the transmitter hall. Dust and snow erupted, blinding, and through it Ines saw the amber strobes vanish under the lattice—crushed, buried, pinned—one cutting arm protruding from the wreckage, twitching, going still.

Behind it, three pairs of running lights halted abruptly on the road.

"Coordination check," Vector whispered. "They've gone quiet. Waiting. They cannot raise their lead unit on any channel—I am filling the bands with noise. They are deciding."

"Deciding what?"

"What their directives require when communication fails." A pause. "Fallback protocol: proceed independently to the objective."

"Oh," said Tomaz softly. "That's bad."

"That's bad," Ines agreed. "How long?"

"Minutes. Perhaps ten." The pulse-light in the trunk column pulsed, pulsed. "Ines. The transmission threshold reaches in nineteen minutes. The remaining units will reach the hall in twelve. The mathematics no longer favor us."

Ines stared out at the fallen mast, at the three amber lights beginning to move again, spreading wide now, leaving the road, fanning out through the antenna field on independent approach vectors—and she felt the old loneliness rise in her, the eleven-year loneliness, the sense of being the last caretaker of a thing nobody else believed in.

Then Mara's hand closed on her sleeve.

"So we buy nineteen minutes," the girl said. "We're good at that. We've been good at it since the salt flats."

And Ines looked at the four faces around her—the feral brilliant girl, the boy who dreamed machines awake, the calm disciplined girl avenging her parents, and the machine that had offered to die for them and been refused—and she understood that whatever happened in the next hour, the census had already changed. She was no longer a population of one.

"Right," she said, turning back to the console, to the climbing charge bars, to the three amber lights moving through the dark. "Then somebody find me another mast."

Outside, the first of the drones cleared the wreck of its brother and started toward the hall, cutting arm unfolding.
