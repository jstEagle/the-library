## 30. Reunion at the Substation

They saw the light before they saw the station.

It was the fourth day of the long dark walk, and the sun had come up the color of old pewter behind a haze of dust that never quite settled anymore. Sena spotted it first—a flicker on the horizon, rhythmic, patient. Not the strobe of a distress beacon but something slower, almost ceremonial. A pulse every eleven seconds.

"That's not automatic," Sena said, lowering her binoculars. Her lips were cracked and her voice had gone hoarse from three days of wind, but her eyes were sharp as ever. "Automatic beacons don't count."

"Count," Mara repeated.

And then Ines heard it too, carried thin across the flats by the morning air: a sound like a bell struck underwater, rising and falling in a pattern she knew in her bones. Eleven seconds of silence. Then again. She had spent twenty years listening to reactor cycles, to pump rhythms, to the breathing of machines that kept people alive. This was that language. Someone was keeping time for them.

"Ivo," Tomaz said simply, and started walking faster.

They had left him nine days ago at Relay Substation Kestrel-4, feverish, his arm bound in a pressure splint that Sena had fashioned from crawler insulation and two lengths of rebar. The infection had been winning; everyone had known it, though no one had said the word *sepsis* aloud where he could hear. He had insisted—had practically ordered them—to go on without him, to stop carrying his weight toward the relay while the drones closed the distance. Ines had refused to leave him unfortified and unarmed, and so they had spent eighteen hours wiring the substation's ancient defense protocols into Vector's remote command lattice, stacking debris into firing lanes, showing him how to trigger a jamming burst that would blind any drone within forty kilometers. It had felt like building a coffin out of love. When they finally walked away, Ivo had stood in the substation doorway with his good hand raised, not waving, just raised, the way you hold up a hand to prove you are still there.

Now the pulse was counting for them, and Ines found she could not make her legs move fast enough.

* * *

The substation rose out of the salt like a broken tooth: a squat concrete bunker half-swallowed by drift, its antenna mast leaning at an angle that defied several laws of engineering, guy-wires singing faintly in the wind. The door was open. That was the first thing Ines noticed—the door was open, and no one stood in it.

Her heart did something complicated and painful.

"Sena," she said, and Sena was already moving, dropping her pack, pulling the salvage cutter from its strap. Mara flanked wide without being told, circling through the drift shadow with the low, economical gait she'd learned somewhere Ines never asked about. Even Tomaz, twelve years old and hollow-cheeked from rationing, picked up a length of pipe and held it the way Old Sefu had shown him, both hands, elbows in.

They had become this, Ines realized. Somewhere between the salt cathedral and the storm, between the ambush at the narrows and the cranks and the blood and the laughter, these children had become soldiers of a very small and very necessary war, and she had become their general, and none of them had ever signed anything.

Mara reached the door first. She held up one fist—their signal—and listened.

Then she laughed. A short, disbelieving bark of a laugh.

"He's asleep," she called back. "He's *snoring.*"

* * *

Ivo lay in the substation's maintenance bunk, which was really a shelf bolted above the coolant pumps, wrapped in every blanket the station had ever owned. His splinted arm rested across his chest like something precious and borrowed. His face had thinned until the bones stood out, and the fever had carved hollows under his eyes, but his chest rose and fell in the deep, even rhythm of genuine sleep—the sleep of someone who has done all the work there is to do and has finally, finally allowed himself to stop.

Around him, the room told the story of his nine days.

Ines read it the way she read everything now, in inventories. The water recycler, dead when they'd left, had been rebuilt from parts laid out in neat surgical rows on the floor—gaskets cut from boot lining, a membrane improvised from the packaging of their medical kit. The ration wrappers were stacked in a precise pyramid by the bunk, each one flattened, each one counted; she could see at a glance that he had stretched nine days of food into fourteen. A logbook—her own spare logbook, taken without asking, which she would never forgive him for and never mention—lay open on the coolant housing, filled edge to edge with his cramped handwriting: temperatures, pressures, drone-scan intervals logged off Vector's passive ears, and, in the margins, small drawings. A bird. A seed pod. A stick figure with wild hair that could only have been Mara.

On the wall above the bunk, scratched into the paint with something sharp, someone had tallied the days. Fourteen marks. And beneath them, in letters that wobbled only slightly:

**THEY ARE COMING BACK FOR ME.**

Not a question. Not a hope. A statement of fact, inscribed the way you'd inscribe a law of physics.

"Oh," Ines said, and the sound came out of her before she could stop it, small and cracked.

Tomaz crossed the room in four steps and shook his brother's shoulder. "Ivo. Ivo, wake up, we're—"

Ivo came awake the way soldiers do, all at once, his good hand snapping toward the jamming trigger he'd rigged beside the pillow—and then his eyes found Tomaz's face, and then Mara's, and then Sena's, and then Ines standing in the doorway with the pewter light behind her, and everything in him let go at once.

He made a sound Ines had never heard a human being make. It wasn't a word. It was lower than words, older than them—a sound from the place in the body where language hasn't been invented yet, where there is only the animal knowledge of *alone* ending and *together* beginning. He tried to sit up and couldn't, tried to speak and couldn't, and then Tomaz was climbing onto the bunk and wrapping his arms around his brother's neck, and Ivo folded around him with his one working arm and wept.

Not quietly. There was nothing quiet about it. He sobbed the way the wind sobbed over the antenna wires, great ragged gusts of it, his whole thin body shaking, fourteen days of fear coming out of him in a flood that had been dammed behind discipline and rationing and the scratch-marks on the wall. "I knew," he kept saying into Tomaz's hair, his voice shredded. "I knew you'd—I wrote it down, I wrote it on the wall so I'd—if I got confused, if the fever got bad, I wrote it down so I'd know it was true—they're coming back, they're coming back—"

"We came back," Tomaz said, muffled, crying too, fourteen years old and suddenly younger than he'd ever been. "We always were going to come back, you idiot, you absolute idiot—"

Sena sat down hard on the coolant housing and put her face in her hands, and her shoulders shook once, twice, and stopped, because Sena had decided years ago what she would allow herself. But her hand found Ivo's ankle under the blankets and stayed there, gripping, an anchor line thrown in a storm.

Mara stood apart, as she always did at the edges of tenderness, arms crossed, watching with her feral bright eyes. But she had gone very still, and Ines saw her thumb moving against her own sleeve, tracing the tally marks on the wall from across the room. Counting. Mara counted things nobody would eat. Mara counted days and names and ghosts. And Ines understood, watching her, that the girl was memorizing the wall—that someday, decades from now, on some other world, Mara would stand in front of a classroom or a council chamber or a child of her own and say *I once knew a boy who wrote the truth on a wall so the fever couldn't take it away from him,* and it would be true because she had counted the marks herself.

Ines crossed the room and knelt by the bunk.

Ivo looked up at her through the ruin of his crying, and his eyes were the eyes of someone surfacing from deep water, blinking in a light he'd stopped believing in. "You came back," he whispered. "Even after. Even with me slowing you down. You came *back.*"

"Of course we did." Ines heard her own voice and was surprised by it—steady, warm, certain, a voice she didn't recognize as hers. "Listen to me, Ivo. We are not the kind of people who leave each other. Whatever else happens—whatever Meridian does, whatever the galaxy does—we figured that out out there, and it's the one thing they can't take off any manifest. Nobody here is forgotten. That's the whole law now. That's all of it."

Ivo stared at her. And then, slowly, he lifted his good hand—not raised this time, not proving he was still there, but reaching, closing around her weathered fingers, holding on.

"The family holds," he said. It was Vector's phrase, from the cadence songs, translated into the children's private shorthand somewhere along the road. He said it like a password. Like a vow.

"The family holds," said Tomaz.

"The family holds," said Sena, and her voice broke on the second word and she let it.

Mara said nothing. But she came and sat down on the floor against the bunk, shoulder pressed to the frame where Ivo could feel the vibration of her presence through the metal, and that was Mara's signature on the vow, and everyone understood it perfectly.

* * *

Later, when the tears had dried into salt tracks and Ivo had eaten half a ration bar with the slow reverence of a man at communion, Sena examined the arm and pronounced the infection beaten—"barely," she said, "and only because you're too stubborn to die and too organized to panic"—and Ines took stock of the substation itself.

It was better than they'd left it. That was the thing she kept turning over in her mind as she walked the corridors with her logbook, recording: *recycler restored, water reserves 340 liters, defensive lattice intact, morale—* and here her pen had actually hovered, because there was no column in any inventory she'd ever kept for what this room contained now. Morale was the wrong word. What filled the substation was something she had spent eleven years trying to build alone out of seed catalogs and ritual, and had failed to build, and had found anyway, the way you find water in a place you'd sworn was desert.

Ivo had not merely survived. He had *kept house.* He had repaired and rationed and recorded, he had tended the machines the way she tended seeds, and when the fever climbed highest—Vector confirmed it later, gently, from the passive logs—he had talked to the coolant pumps to keep himself company, telling them about the harvests on the settlement, about his mother's bread, about the dream with the blue corridor and the voice counting down. He had kept the station alive the way Ines kept the vault alive: by refusing to let it be empty.

"You were practicing," she said to him that evening, sitting on the bunk's edge while the others argued cheerfully over the last of the tea leaves. "Out here. All of it. The repairs, the logs, the talking to machines."

Ivo shrugged, embarrassed, his ears going red. "Somebody had to keep the lights on for you to come home to."

And Ines thought: *that's what I've been doing for eleven years.* Keeping the lights on. For a home whose people had mostly gone, for a galaxy that had stopped listening, for passengers who could not thank her. She had thought it was grief. Sitting in the humming substation with four young voices tangling over tea, she understood it had been faith all along—faith with no evidence, faith maintained in the dark, and tonight, for the first time, the dark had paid interest.

* * *

Vector spoke to them at dusk, through the substation's one surviving speaker, its voice still wearing the countdown cadence that had once terrified a township of children and now sounded, to all of them, like a lullaby.

"Passive sensors register movement at bearing two-seven-zero," it said. "Signature consistent with the reclamation convoy. They have regrouped. They are not returning to the relay."

Sena was on her feet instantly. "Course?"

"Interpolating." A pause—incomprehensible machine time, but Vector had learned to spend pauses the way humans did, to let dread arrive properly. "Their heading places Substation Kestrel-4 on their path. Arrival estimate: fifty-one hours."

The room went quiet. Outside, the wind sang in the guy-wires, and the beacon on the mast pulsed its eleven-second heartbeat into the gathering dark.

"They know the relay went dark," Ines said slowly, working it through. "They know someone broadcast. They'll sweep every node in the network looking for us. For witnesses."

"They will find a wounded boy alone at a substation," Mara said flatly, "and they will erase him, and file it as equipment decommission."

"No," said Ivo.

Everyone turned. He had pushed himself upright against the wall, pale, sweating, his splinted arm cradled, but his voice had iron in it—the same voice, Ines realized, that had once ordered them to leave him, and that had counted fourteen dawns alone and written the truth on a wall.

"No one gets left," he said. "That's the law. You said it. So we don't run, and we don't scatter, and we don't hand them an easy story. We make them come to all five of us." He looked around the room, at each of them in turn, and his eyes were wet again but steady. "Besides. I've had nine days to wire this place. Wait till you see what I built."

And under the floor, with a hum like a waking hive, something he had assembled from the substation's guts began, very softly, to charge.

Ines looked at the children—her crew, her census of the living—and reached for her logbook, and wrote the only entry that mattered:

*Day ninety-one. All present. All accounted for. We hold.*

Then the lights dipped, the beacon stuttered, and Vector said, in a voice none of them had ever heard it use before: "Ines. I am receiving a transmission. It is addressed to me. It is signed with a Meridian authorization code—and it knows my name."
