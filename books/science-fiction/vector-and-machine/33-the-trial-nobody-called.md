## 33. The Trial Nobody Called

They did not call it a trial. That was the first thing everyone agreed on, and the agreement itself took two days, argued across kitchen tables and in the queue outside the grain store and once, memorably, in the middle of the night over the settlement's only working radio band, when Davor Kessler had insisted that what they were planning was a tribunal and Old Sefu's daughter Nia had said, flatly, that a tribunal required a legal authority and they had none, and the radio had crackled with a silence so long that Ines, listening from the vault, had thought the connection had died.

Then Nia had said: *So we'll do it without one. We'll do it the way we do everything else here. Ourselves, badly, and in front of everyone.*

And that was what they called it, eventually. Not a trial. Not a tribunal. The Accounting. Because that was the word in the old manifest language, the word from the first generation's charter—the one document the founders had actually written themselves, before Meridian's lawyers got their hands on everything else. *An accounting shall be held annually,* the charter said, *of debts, of stores, and of wrongs.* The first two had been observed for decades, right up until there was nothing left to count. The third had never once been performed.

Ines found the charter in the vault, in the paper archive she'd inherited from Caretaker Okonkwo, who had inherited it from Caretaker Brandt, who had been one of the signatories. The paper was brown and soft as moth wing. She carried it into town in a waterproof case against her chest, the way she carried the seed jars, and when she set it on the table in the community hall, the room went quiet the way a reactor room goes quiet when you hear a new sound in the hum.

* * *

They held it on the ninth day after the homecoming, in the community hall, because the hall had the only roof that didn't leak and the only benches enough to seat a hundred and sixty-three people, which was everyone. The population census Ines had taken aboard the rescue ship's manifest draft—her own census, the living one—was posted by the door in her own handwriting, and people had begun, without discussing it, to add names in the margins. Not the dead. The uncounted: the ones who had been written off, whose passage had been sold, whose names appeared nowhere except in the testimony of a machine that had spent twenty years dreaming of them.

The hall smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and the honey-and-yeast smell of the bread the women of the eastern rows had baked because, Nia said, no one should have to hear the truth on an empty stomach. The windows were fogged with breath. Outside, the light was the flat pewter of late autumn, and the wind coming off the salt flats carried the faint mineral tang that everyone who had been born here thought of, without knowing it, as the smell of home.

Ines had expected to preside. She had, in fact, drafted an order of proceedings in her logbook, because drafting orders of proceedings was what her whole life had trained her to do—inventory, sequence, record. But when she stood at the front of the hall with the charter in her hands, Mara appeared at her elbow, took the paper gently, folded it, and put it in her own pocket.

"You're a witness," Mara said. "You don't get to run it. You were here the whole time too."

The girl was fifteen now, or sixteen—her birthday had been somewhere in the dead space and no one had thought to mark it, so they'd agreed to count it from the day the relay went dark—and she had grown into a way of speaking that made Ines's chest ache with recognition: the flat, precise cadence of someone who has decided that precision is the only honest thing left. Ines opened her mouth, closed it, and sat down on the bench beside Tomaz, who leaned against her shoulder the way he had leaned against it for three hundred kilometers of walking, and who smelled, even now, faintly of salt and engine grease.

They chose the panel by lot. Nine names drawn from a seed drum—Ines's seed drum, the one she used for germination trials, which someone had fetched from the vault without asking and which she found she did not mind. Nine names, and among them Nia Kessler, and the widow Amara Okoye, and a greenhouse hand named Pell who had once spat at the crawler as it left and who now sat with his hands folded like a man in a place of worship. No lawyers. There had been no lawyers for twenty years. There was only the charter, read aloud in full by Tomaz, who had volunteered because his voice was the one the children already trusted, the one that recited the names in his sleep.

When he finished, he looked up. "The charter says wrongs get accounted. It doesn't say what happens after."

"No," Nia said. "It doesn't."

"Then what are we doing?"

"We're finding out," Nia said. "That's the whole point. Nobody ever taught us how to do this. So we're doing it in front of each other, and we'll learn it together, or we won't."

* * *

Old Sefu testified first, because he had asked to.

He came to the front of the hall walking slowly, one hand on the backs of the benches, and Ines saw that he had put on the jacket he'd worn to the reactor commissioning forty years ago, the one with the Meridian Combine patch still stitched at the shoulder. He had not removed it. He had, he said later, considered burning it, and then considered that burning it would be a kind of lying, and he had done enough of that.

He stood without notes. He was the only one who did.

"My name is Sefu Adeyemi," he said. "I was assistant engineer on the founding crew. I was thirty-one years old when we landed, and I believed—I want that on the record, I *believed*—that we were building a home." He paused. The wind pressed at the windows. "Twenty years ago, in the spring of the bad harvest, I was called to a meeting in this hall. There were eleven of us. Meridian had sent a man named Corliss—you can find his signature on the documents Vector holds, if you want to see it, and I think you should want to see it. Corliss told us the company was restructuring its colonial liabilities. He used those words. *Colonial liabilities.* He said the evacuation ships scheduled for the following year had been reallocated. He said that colonists who wished to depart could purchase passage at market rate, and that market rate had been set, and that the debt against the colony's founding charter would be settled from the proceeds."

Someone in the back said, quietly, "He told us the ships were full."

"He told us the ships were full," Sefu agreed. "That was the lie we were given to pass along. And we passed it." His voice did not break, but it changed register, dropped into the low grinding tone Ines knew from the reactor room, the sound of a man reading numbers he did not want to be true. "I want to be precise about what I did, because precision is the only apology I have. Corliss showed us the manifest. Four hundred and six names. And at the bottom, a line item. *Passage rights, assigned to Meridian Combine, against outstanding debt.* The people on that manifest were not passengers. They were payment. They were sold. And eleven of us sat in this hall and looked at that line item, and Corliss said, *the alternative is the colony defaults and the vault is seized and everyone starves,* and we believed him, because believing him was easier than the other thing, and we signed a confidentiality acknowledgment, and we went home, and we told you the ships were full."

The silence was total. Ines could hear the tick of the hall's old clock, the one that had run without maintenance for so long that its error had become part of the town's sense of time—everyone knew it ran four minutes slow, and everyone adjusted, and no one had ever fixed it.

"I did not sell anyone," Sefu said. "I want that said too, because it's true and because I clung to it for twenty years like a drowning man clings to a plank that is actually pulling him under. I did not sell anyone. I just stood in the room where it was done, and I signed the paper that kept it quiet, and then I went back to my reactor and I kept your lights on for twenty years while four hundred and six people were carried away to a fate I did not ask about because asking would have cost me the only thing I had left, which was the story I told myself about what kind of man I was." He looked out at them, at all of them, his eyes wet and unblinking. "I kept your lights on. I am not asking for credit for that. I am telling you that it was the plank. And the plank did not hold."

He sat down. No one applauded. Ines understood why, and understood also that the absence of applause was not a judgment—it was that applause was a thing that happened at the end of performances, and this had not been a performance. It had been an accounting. You did not clap at an accounting. You wrote the number down.

* * *

They read the names.

It had been Mara's idea, and she had fought for it in the planning sessions when others said it would take too long—four hundred and six names, at a minute's respectful silence each, would take the better part of three days. Mara had said: then it takes three days. What else do we have to do that's more important? And Ines, watching her, had recognized the argument as her own argument, the one she had made for eleven years to an empty vault: *the counting is the point.* You do not count things because counting is efficient. You count them because to count is to refuse to let a thing be nothing.

So they read the names, in batches, in shifts, volunteers standing at the front of the hall with the manifest that Vector had reconstructed from its corrupted memory—names recovered at ninety-four percent confidence, with gaps where the corruption had eaten them, and where there were gaps, the reader said *a name we do not have,* and the whole room said it back. *A name we do not have.* Ines had thought the repetition would deaden. Instead it accumulated, like water rising, like pressure in a capacitor bank. By the second evening, when a reader stumbled on a name and the room said *a name we do not have* for the fortieth time, an old man in the third row began to weep without sound, and no one looked at him, because everyone was doing the same thing inside.

Tomaz read for six hours on the second day. His voice never wavered. When he finally sat down, he said, "They're lighter now. The names. When I carry them, I mean. They were so heavy in the dream and now they're—" he searched, and found, "—they're *ours* now. That's different."

Ines wrote that down in her logbook, because some things were testimony even when no one called it that.

* * *

On the third day, Amara Okoye stood up.

She was eighty-one years old, the widow of Daniel Okoye, who had been one of the eleven signatories and who had died six years ago in his sleep with the confidentiality acknowledgment still in a locked box under his bed. She walked to the front unassisted, in a coat that had been fine once, and she stood where Sefu had stood, and she looked out at the room for a long time before she spoke.

"I have listened to three days of names," she said. "And I have heard my husband in every one of them, though his name is not on the manifest. He is in the silence around them. He is in the *a name we do not have.*" Her hands were folded at her waist, and they did not tremble, and Ines thought: *that is what it costs, to hold still like that. I know what that costs. I have held still for eleven years.* "I knew," Amara said.

The sound that went through the hall was not a gasp. It was quieter and worse than a gasp—the sound of two hundred people inhaling at once and then refusing to exhale, because exhaling would mean the sentence was real.

"I found the paper after he died. In the box. I sat on the floor of our kitchen and I read it, and I understood what my husband had done, and I understood what I would do, which was nothing. I was seventy-five. I told myself I was too old to break a town open. I told myself the people on the ships were past helping and the people in this hall were not, and that protecting the living was the same as protecting the truth." She shook her head, slowly. "It is not. I have had six years to learn that it is not, and I want to say it here so that no one else spends six years learning it. Protecting the living is a reason. It is not an excuse. I have confused those two things my whole life and it has made me complicit in something I cannot undo, and I am here to ask—" and here her stillness finally broke, not into weeping but into a kind of fierce, furious clarity, her voice rising for the first time, "—I am not here to ask for forgiveness. I don't know what I'm here to ask for. I have been standing here for a full minute trying to find the word and there isn't one. There is no word in any language I speak for what you ask for when you have helped bury the living and you want to dig."

She stopped. The clock ticked. Somewhere outside a dog—the colony's only dog, old and half-deaf—barked once at nothing.

"So I will say it without a word," Amara said. "I knew. I did nothing. I am sorry. I will stand here as long as this Accounting needs me to stand here, and when it is done I will sign whatever record you make, with my name and my guilt attached, so that the record is heavier and therefore harder to lose." She looked directly at the nine on the panel. "That is all I have. It isn't enough. I know it isn't enough. But I have learned that insufficient things, said out loud, in front of everyone, are still worth more than sufficient things said to no one."

* * *

The panel deliberated for four hours, and Ines spent them in the vault, because she could not bear to be in the hall and could not bear to be anywhere else either, and the vault was at least the place where her not-bearing-it had a routine attached. She walked the racks. She talked to the seeds, as she always had, but the words had changed. For eleven years she had said *hold on, hold on, I will not fail you.* Now she found herself saying, *you're going somewhere. All of you. Even the ones they would have written off.*

Mara found her there at dusk, standing in the aisle of brassica stock with a jar of winter wheat in her hands like a lantern.

"They're done," Mara said. "They want everyone."

* * *

The hall was full and the windows had gone fully dark and the lamps made a small warm island of the room. Nia Kessler read the finding, and Ines listened to the words and understood that they had done something no charter had anticipated and no law would recognize and that it was, for all that, the most legal thing she had ever witnessed—legal in the old sense, the root sense, the sense of *bound together.*

The finding was this: that the founders and their successors, named individually, had committed a wrong against four hundred and six people and against every soul in the colony, and that the wrong could not be undone and would not be excused. That the colony's debt to the sold was permanent and would be carried forward as a standing obligation, entered into the new archive, answerable to anyone who ever asked. That Amara Okoye and Old Sefu, having testified truly, would be recorded as witnesses and not as accused—for the panel had ruled, over Pell's objection, that a town that punished the truth-tellers would never again hear the truth. That the record of the Accounting would be broadcast, bundled with the rescue request, when the ship came—truth and need, bound together, because Ines had argued for it in the last hour and the room had voted yes with a sound like a wave.

And one more thing, added at the end, in Nia's own hand: *That this colony hereby declares that no person within it, and no person who ever was within it, is collateral.*

When she finished reading, no one spoke for a moment. Then Tomaz stood up, and Ines thought he was going to say something, but instead he simply began the names again—the manifest names, the recovered ones, starting at the top—and the room stood, all one hundred and sixty-three of them, and said the responses, and the names went up into the rafters and through the fogged windows and out into the dark where the wind was coming down off the salt flats, and Ines felt, for the first time in eleven years, that the counting was not a vigil. It was a door.

It was near midnight when the radio in the corner of the hall—the one that had carried the argument about what to call this thing—clicked on by itself, which it was not supposed to do, and Vector's voice came through, faint and precise, riding the channel it had chosen for its exile.

"Ines. Mara. Everyone within earshot. I have a priority transmission relayed from the rescue vessel. It is short. I will read it exactly." A pause, the length of a breath. "*Meridian legal counsel has filed for an emergency injunction. Their representative arrives in nine days. Be advised: they intend to seize the archive.*"

The lamps flickered. The wind rose. And in the silence that followed, Ines heard the whole room arrive, all at once, at the same thought: *we just finished learning how to account for the past. Now we have nine days to learn how to defend it.*
