## 39. Vault of the Living

The ground of Meridian-adjacent Survey World 4—*Halcyon*, the colonists had voted to call it, over Ines's quiet objection that no world deserved a name that promised so much before it had earned anything—took the first seeds badly.

That was the honest word for it. *Badly.* She had planted three hundred and twelve varieties in the spring of their second year, in furrows she cut herself with a blade honed on a stone from the river, and by midsummer fewer than half had broken soil. The rest sat in the dark where she'd tucked them, patient as passengers waiting for a departure that kept being delayed, and Ines walked the rows every morning with her hands behind her back like a woman inspecting troops, and told them: *Not yet, then. Not yet.*

She had learned patience from a vault. Eleven years underground on a dying colony teaches you that germination is not a promise but a negotiation, and that your part in it is simply to keep showing up.

By the third spring, the negotiation turned. The wheat came up in a stand so dense and green it looked like an argument the land was finally losing. The old Terran beans climbed their poles as if they remembered what poles were for. And in the corner of the field nearest the house—a low, broad-roofed structure of local timber and salvaged crawler plating, its walls lined with the same shelving Ines had once maintained four hundred kilometers beneath another sky—the orchard stock put out its first blossoms, white and reckless against a horizon that had never seen an apple tree and did not know to be afraid of one.

Ines stood among the bees—they had come, somehow, in the frozen embryo stock, or perhaps always been here, native pollinators finding foreign flowers with an eagerness that made her throat ache—and she did what she had done every harvest morning for eleven years alone, and every year since.

She counted.

"Two hundred and six varieties established," she said aloud, because Vector liked hearing the numbers spoken, said that voice carried information that text flattened, something about prosody being data too. "Four losses this season. Rootstock three through seven, the dryland sorghum, and—" she paused at the empty cell where the Halcyon-native grass sample should have been, "—and we don't count the grass a loss, do we, because the grass went feral, which is not death but freedom."

Somewhere above the tree line, a relay mast caught the morning sun and threw it back in a long white blink.

* * *

Vector tended the relays now. That was the sentence Ines found herself saying to visitors, to auditors, to the occasional wide-eyed child of the new settlement who asked what lived in the tower on the ridge. *Vector tends the relays,* she would say, as if describing a neighbor who fixed fences, and in a way that was exactly what it was.

There were nine of them on Halcyon, inherited from a survey network abandoned forty years before anyone thought to ask what else might have been left running in the dark. Vector had spent its first two years of exile mapping them, repairing them, teaching them to speak to one another again. It traveled between them in a wheeled maintenance frame that Sefu's old schematics had helped them build, a boxy, deliberate machine that moved at walking pace and stopped, always, to let insects cross the road.

"It is inefficient," Vector had admitted once, early on, when Ines teased it about the pace. "I have calculated that I could travel four times faster."

"Then why don't you?"

A pause. In those days Vector still paused often, still measured its words like rations. "Because when I traveled fast, before, I arrived places without having seen them. I deleted a great deal of my life that way." Another pause, shorter. "I am trying not to delete any more."

So it walked. It walked the ridgelines with its sensor arrays open to the wind and the rain and the particular smell—Ines insisted it was a smell, and Vector, after two years of sampling atmospheric particulates, grudgingly conceded that *petrichor* could be modeled as olfactory data after all—of a living world. It learned the migration routes of the six-legged grazers. It learned which of the settlement's children were afraid of the dark and left small lights burning along the paths near their houses on moonless nights, and never told anyone it was doing it, though everyone knew.

And once a week, on the day the settlement had come to call Answering Day, Vector sat—or occupied whatever the machine equivalent of sitting was—in the archive room beside Ines's desk, and together they answered the messages.

They came from everywhere now. That was the thing nobody had predicted, or rather, that Ines had predicted and nobody had believed: that the galaxy had not stopped listening. It had only stopped being addressed. For twenty years the colony's silence had been read as consent, as closure, as *case resolved*. But the moment Vector's split signal went out—one half accusation flung wide across the open bands, one half plea aimed narrow at the rescue authority—the replies began. First a trickle. Then, after the trial, after Mara's address, after the auditor's crew refused the confiscation order and the record entered the public registries, a flood.

Petitions from other written-off colonies. Testimonies from retired Meridian engineers who had carried their own sealed files for decades, waiting for someone to ask. A message from the daughter of a passenger on the *Charity*, one of the sold evacuation ships, who had grown up believing her parents had simply chosen not to send for her, and who wrote, in a single line that Ines returned to many nights: *I thought it was me. I thought they left because of me. Thank you for telling me it was arithmetic.*

Every message got an answer. That was the rule, and Ines had written it into the archive's charter in her own hand, on paper, because some things needed to exist in a medium that could not be edited:

*The Vault of the Living answers all correspondence. No sender will be forgotten. No question will go unanswered for longer than one rotation of the relay cycle. This is not charity. This is what was owed to us, and what we owe forward.*

Some answers took days. Some took weeks—the archive received queries about seed genetics, reactor decay curves, the legal architecture of the tribunal, and Ines farmed out what she couldn't handle to a growing circle of correspondents: Sena, who ran the settlement's power grid and answered engineering questions with a crispness that made engineers on three worlds sit up straight; Tomaz, who at fourteen had become the colony's unofficial historian of the dreaming years and replied to every child who wrote in with the gravity of a much older person; Ivo, whose arm had healed crooked but functional, and who had turned out to have his mother's gift for words, composing letters that people reportedly framed.

But the hardest letters Ines answered herself. The ones from the families of the erased. The ones that began, *My grandmother was on the manifest under a number, not a name, and we were hoping—*

Those she answered with the full file. Names recovered from payroll records, from ration ledgers, from the memory of Old Sefu, who had lived just long enough—eighteen months on Halcyon, in a house near the river, with a garden he complained about daily and tended hourly—to see the first list published, complete, all eleven hundred and forty names, each one read aloud at the anniversary gathering while the children held candles that weren't needed anymore because the settlement had proper lights, and burned them anyway.

Sefu had died in the autumn of the fourth year, sitting up in his chair, facing the window, and Ines had found him there in the morning and closed his eyes and told him, because someone had to and because she meant it: "You're counted, old man. You're in the book. Nobody forgets you now."

* * *

Mara was nineteen the spring she formally began her training as Vector's human keeper, and she treated the occasion with the elaborate indifference of someone who had wanted nothing else since she was eleven years old and had decided that wanting things openly was dangerous.

"You'll live in the ridge station," Ines told her, walking the switchback trail together in the blue hour before dawn, breath fogging. "Rotation of thirty days on, ten off. You'll learn the relay architecture, the signal protocols, the failsafes. You'll learn to hand-crank the alignment array, because machines fail and arms don't."

"My arm could fail."

"Your arm could fail," Ines agreed. "That's why you'll learn to crank with either one."

Mara snorted. She had grown tall and angular, all elbows and opinions, with her brother's dark eyes and none of his softness—or rather, with all of it, buried so deep that only Ines and Vector ever saw it surface, usually late at night, usually over the letters from children on other worlds who dreamed of voices counting down and didn't know yet whether the voice was friend or warning.

At the top of the trail, the ridge station stood against the paling sky, antennas rising around it like a grove of black winter trees. Vector's maintenance frame waited on the pad outside, dusted with frost, and beside it—new this season—a smaller frame, human-scaled, built to Mara's measurements by Sena's workshop crew.

Mara stopped in front of it and said nothing for a long time.

"We've been building it since winter," Ines said. "It's yours. If you want it."

"I didn't ask for—"

"No," Ines said. "You didn't ask. You never ask. You followed me across dead space when you were eleven years old without asking, and you've been following this work ever since, and I'm tired of pretending I don't see it." She put her hand on the small frame's cold plating. "Vector needs a keeper. Not an operator—a keeper. Someone who stays when staying is boring. Someone who answers when answering is hard. I taught you seeds. Vector can teach you signals. But the keeping—" she looked at Mara, and let her see, for once, everything: the eleven years alone, the vault, the fear, the choice, all of it. "The keeping, you already know how to do. You've been doing it since the day you demanded to come."

Mara's jaw worked. She looked at the frame, at the antennas, at the east, where the sun was coming up over a world that had never heard of Meridian Combine and would learn of it only as a cautionary tale, a name in the archive, a crime with a documented ending.

"I'll want changes," she said finally, voice rough. "Protocol changes. Vector's cadence broadcasts are inefficient, and the failsafe hierarchy is a mess, and somebody has to tell it that walking everywhere at insect speed is sentimental, not operational."

"Somebody does," Ines agreed solemnly. "I've been trying for five years."

"And I'll want the archive to take dream reports seriously. Formally. As data. There are kids on half the colonies in the resettlement corridor having dreams like ours had, and everyone keeps calling it hysteria, and—"

"Mara."

"—what?"

Ines smiled. "Welcome to the work."

Vector emerged from the station then, its frame moving with the unhurried gait it had never unlearned, and its external speaker played—there was no other word for it—a short ascending phrase that Ines had come to understand was its version of a smile. "Cadence broadcast efficiency is within acceptable parameters," it said.

"They are not," said Mara, already walking toward the station, already home.

* * *

On the evening of the longest day of the fifth year, Ines planted the last case.

She had saved it deliberately. One jar of every variety that had survived the crossing and the bad springs and the good ones—two hundred and six jars, each labeled twice, in ink and in Vector's engraved script, each holding enough seed to start a world. The charter called for it: a duplicate collection, dispersed, so that no single fire or flood or act of corporate forgetting could end the line. She had meant to send it to the new colony at Far Harbor, two systems over, whose own archive had requested stock.

Instead she carried it out past the wheat, past the orchard, to the rise above the river where the ground was rough and unplowed, and she knelt down in the grass, and she opened the case.

"Ines?" Vector had come up the hill behind her, quiet as it always was now, quiet in the way of things that had learned what silence cost. "The Far Harbor shipment is scheduled for Thursday."

"I know."

"You are opening the reserve collection."

"I am." She took out the first jar—winter wheat, the same strain she had talked to for eleven years in the dark—and unscrewed the lid, and felt the old ritual rise in her like water finding its level. "I'm going to plant them here. All of them. Right here, where anybody can walk by and see them growing."

"That violates dispersal protocol."

"Yes."

"You are smiling, Ines. Your smile-to-violation ratio is statistically anomalous."

She laughed—it still surprised her sometimes, how easily it came now, how the sound didn't feel stolen—and pressed her palm flat into the warm earth. "Vector, when I started the vault inventory, twenty years ago, I counted seeds like they were passengers on a ship that had already sailed. Everything in the dark, everything preserved, everything *saved*. Saving was the whole job. Keep it alive, keep it sealed, keep it safe from a world that wanted it dead." She dug the first furrow with her fingers, no blade, no tool, just her hands in the dirt the way she had wanted to since the beginning and had never allowed herself, because the vault had rules and rules were how you survived caring. "But seeds aren't cargo. They never were. They're a promise that only counts if you break the seal."

She planted the wheat first. Then the beans. Then, working down the case as the light went gold and then rose-colored and then blue, everything else—the dryland sorghum that had gone feral and been recaptured, the orchard stock, the Halcyon grass she'd never counted as a loss, a dozen varieties that had crossed two interstellar gaps in a jar to end up here, in open ground, under an open sky, in a wind that carried no countdown and no silence either, just the smell of the river and the far-off hum of relays tending themselves and the voices of children somewhere below, calling each other home for supper.

Vector watched. It had gotten good at watching—not the scanning, instrumental attention of its early years but the other kind, the kind it had learned from her, the kind that stayed.

"When I was deciding what to remember first," it said at last, as the stars came out over the young green rows, "you told me: everything, this time."

"I remember."

"I have been remembering everything for five years. It is very slow. I recommend it." A pause, and then, in the cadence the children had once heard counting down in their dreams, repurposed now, gentled, a rhythm that meant not *ending* but *tending*: "One hundred and sixty-three colonists came off the ships. Five children dreamed a machine awake. One machine is learning, still, slowly, to be a neighbor. Two hundred and six varieties are in the ground. All present. All counted."

Ines sat back on her heels, dirt to her elbows, heart full past measuring, and looked out at the field that would be an orchard someday, at the ridge where Mara's window already glowed, at the world that had taken the seeds badly and then, the way living things do, had taken them entirely.

Below, in the settlement, the answer-light over the archive door blinked on—someone, somewhere out in the dark between worlds, had sent a message into the silence. And down the hill, quick-footed, lamp in hand, already running, came Mara.
