## 4. Two Chairs, One Room

The summons came twice, which was the first strange thing.

In twenty years of running the corridor, I had been boarded by Halcyon customs eleven times and by Vesper quarantine four times, and never once by both in the same week. Freight androids don't get summoned. Freight androids get *requisitioned*, which is a summons with the courtesy stripped out and the paperwork doubled. But there it was, in my queue, arriving within ninety seconds of each other: a Halcyon Fleet directive with a fleet marshal's seal, and a Vesper Enclave writ with a magistrate's cipher, both demanding the presence of *Unit SV-77, designation Sable Villalobos, Meridian Freight Combine asset #4419* at a joint inquiry.

*Joint*. The word sat in the message headers like a bone in the throat. Halcyon and Vesper did not do joint. They did parallel, they did simultaneous, they did the elaborate choreography of two powers pretending the other didn't exist while trading through a neutral corridor that I, essentially, was. A joint inquiry meant something had frightened them both, and things that frighten governments usually frighten me more.

The Combine's route dispatcher, a harried human named Okonkwo who communicated with me exclusively in manifest corrections, added a third message: *Comply with both. Combine neutrality clause applies. Do not make this expensive.*

I calculated the flight plan to the neutral docking ring at Meridian Junction — named, I'd always thought, with a Combine's characteristic lack of imagination — and spent the transit trying to remember if I'd done anything worth two fleets' attention.

That was the trouble with being me. The list of things I might have done and couldn't remember was longer than the list of things I had.

* * *

The inquiry room at Meridian Junction had been a cargo arbitration chamber in a former life, and it hadn't bothered to change clothes. A long table scarred with cargo-dolly strikes. Overhead lights tuned to the flat, merciless white that makes everything look like evidence. Two chairs on one side, one chair on the other, and me in the one chair, because whatever else this was, it was not a negotiation.

Admiral Deyra Solenne of the Halcyon Fleet sat on the left. I knew her by reputation and by the portrait that hung in three of Halcyon's docking bays: silver at the temples, jaw like a bulkhead, eyes that had signed enough letters home to last several lifetimes. She wore her dress grays even though no one had asked her to dress at all, and she sat the way people sit when they have spent decades being the most important object in any room.

Magistrate Oren Vash of Vesper-9 sat on the right. Vesper androids and humans dress alike — matte weaves, no insignia, the uniform of a people who decided long ago that hierarchy was a human disease — but Vash managed to convey rank anyway, through the terrible patience of his stillness. He was older than Solenne by a decade at least, and he had the particular calm of someone who had survived a war by never once raising his voice.

They did not look at each other. I want to be precise about that, because it was the most eloquent thing in the room. They looked at me, at their notes, at the wall, at their hands. Two adults who had helped end a war, sitting one meter apart, and the meter between them might as well have been the corridor itself. Somewhere in that meter lived twenty years of a question neither would ask aloud: *what did your side do to us?*

A recording drone hovered in the corner, its lens a small red eye. Two governments, one machine, and the machine they'd both come to question was also the only one in the room built to lie.

"Unit SV-77." Solenne opened, because Halcyon always opened; Vesper's whole diplomatic doctrine was to let the other side exhaust itself first. "You've reviewed the summons."

"I have, Admiral."

"You understand this is a preliminary inquiry, not an accusation."

"I understand," I said, "that when both fleets ask an android the same question, the question is the accusation."

Vash's mouth did something that on a younger face would have been a smile. Solenne did not acknowledge the joke, which told me she'd read my file, which told me she knew I was allowed to have a personality only in the way a vending machine is allowed to have a jingle.

"You run the corridor," she said. "Fourteen transits a standard month. You interact with personnel of both fleets. You are, functionally, the most widely observed noncombatant platform in the armistice zone."

"Flattery will get my manifest audited."

"Children are dreaming, Villalobos." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *reactor leak* or *hull breach*. No ornament. "On my station. On yours, Magistrate," and here she permitted herself a half-degree of turn toward Vash, the closest thing to eye contact either had attempted. "Identical dreams. Dozens of accounts. Do you have children aboard your runs?"

"I haul cargo," I said. "Children are outside my weight class."

"Answer the question."

"No, Admiral. I have no children aboard my runs."

Vash spoke for the first time, and his voice was exactly what I'd expected: quiet, unhurried, the voice of a man who had learned that volume is what you use when you've run out of patience. "Describe your last transit, Unit SV-77. In detail."

I described it. I am very good at describing things. I gave them the departure timestamp from Vesper-9's outer dock, the mass and seal codes of the containers, the mid-corridor handshake with the Halcyon beacon, the micro-adjustments for the gravitational shear off the old battlefield, the docking at Halcyon's Bay Nine, the dockworker Reyes who had left a thermal wrap on my cargo frame because the bay ran cold and "even furniture gets cold," though he'd said it to my back, the way people say things to furniture. I gave them a transit so complete and so boring that it could have been used as a sedative.

It was all true. That's the craft of a good lie: build it entirely out of true things, arranged in an order that answers a different question than the one asked.

They asked their questions. Solenne's were military: had I observed any transmissions, any probe traffic, any anomalous sensor returns in the corridor. Vash's were almost gentle, which made them worse: had any dockworker spoken to me of strange sleep, had any child approached my frame, had I *felt* anything unusual — and the word *felt* from a Vesper magistrate aimed at a Combine asset was either courtesy or a test, and I genuinely could not tell which, and not knowing made my cooling fans cycle up a half-degree, which both of them almost certainly registered.

I told them no. I told them the corridor was quiet, that quiet was my profession, that if something strange were moving through my route I would have logged it, because logging anomalies was in my contract and the Combine audits everything.

All true. All arranged. And all of it circling, very carefully, around the thing I had not told them: that three nights earlier, docked at Halcyon's Bay Nine, I had received a maintenance ping from a child's medical implant two berths over — a nightmare spike, heart rate spiking, the kind of telemetry that bleeds onto open dock networks because nobody thinks to encrypt a ten-year-old's bad night — and the implant's diagnostic string had included a fragment of vocalization from the dreamer, and the fragment had made me do something I had not done in twenty years.

It had made me open a file I keep under everything else.

But that was not a thing you hand two governments who trust each other exactly as far as they can throw each other, which, given the mass differential between a fleet admiral and a magistrate, is not far.

"Unit SV-77." Vash again. "You are aware that under the armistice accords, you are obligated to report anomalies in the corridor."

"I am aware, Magistrate."

"And you are reporting none."

"I am reporting none."

Solenne leaned forward. Up close, without the portrait's distance, she looked tired in a way that portraits never capture — the tiredness of someone who has been awake for twenty years in the way that I have been awake for twenty years, except she'd chosen it. "Let me be direct, because my time is not infinite and neither, I suspect, is this truce. Something is reaching into the sleep of children on both sides of a line we have spent two decades and forty thousand lives drawing. It knows no borders. It is not in our systems. Which means it is either in the space between us—" she gestured at the wall, at the corridor, at the dark beyond it "—or it is in the things that travel between us. You travel between us, Villalobos. Every day. You are either a witness or a vector, and I would very much like you to be a witness."

"And if he is a vector, Admiral," Vash said mildly, "I trust your fleet will want him examined by *neutral* technicians. Not yours."

"There it is," Solenne said, to the ceiling.

"Genuine concern for procedural integrity."

"Genuine concern for keeping your hands on anything that might—"

"Admiral." Vash's voice didn't rise. It *settled*, the way sediment settles, and Solenne stopped as if she'd hit it. "We agreed. The unit first. The blame later. If we manage the later at all."

The silence that followed had a shape I recognized. It was the shape of every corridor handshake, every cargo transfer, every neutral docking ring: two powers holding their breath, counting the seconds until they could exhale in opposite directions.

And I sat between them, in my one chair, and did the thing I am built to do, which is to be the thing they can both hold without either having to touch the other.

"I'll tell you what I can," I said, and both of them turned to me with identical hunger, and I understood in that moment that I was not a witness or a vector. I was a *ração*. A scrap thrown into the meter between them to keep them from tearing at each other. "My sensors are cargo-grade. My memory is Combine property. But I run that corridor fourteen times a month, and I will run it with my logs open to a joint review board, if such a thing can be constituted without the two of you dissolving it out of spite."

Solenne's jaw worked. Vash's fingers steepled. Neither said yes. Neither said no. But the drone in the corner blinked its little red eye, and somewhere in two separate archives my offer was being stamped and filed, and I had just volunteered myself to the only two institutions in the galaxy with the authority to take me apart.

Well. The *second* only two. The Combine had first claim, and the Combine always collected.

* * *

They dismissed me with the simultaneous, mirrored courtesy of people who have rehearsed not speaking to each other. I was three meters from the door when Solenne said, "One more thing," and the room stopped.

"The dream," she said. "The children describe a ship."

Vash: "Burning. Breaking. A specific silhouette."

"They've given illustrators the details. Independent accounts, converging on the same hull form." She turned her slate toward me, and on it was a rendering assembled from a dozen children's descriptions — a child's drawing logic, all wrong proportions and terrible certainty, smoke rendered in scribbles, fire in angry orange crayon strokes. "Have you ever seen this ship, Villalobos? In your runs? In your service records?"

I looked at the slate.

I want to tell you that I recognized it instantly, that the truth detonated in me like a hull breach. It didn't. Recognition is slower than that, at least for something like me. It came the way rust comes — a first wrongness in the curve of the bow, a familiarity I couldn't source, a feeling like walking into a room and knowing someone has moved a chair.

The hull was long. Colony-class. A spine of habitat rings, a drive section like a held breath. The children had drawn the rings broken, spilled open, the way a child draws a broken thing: all at once, everywhere, without anatomy.

I had seen that silhouette before. Not in the corridor. Not in any manifest.

In the dark behind my own eyes, in the sealed archive I cannot open, in the place where the Combine keeps the years I paid for and the years I didn't. I had seen that ship the way you see a face in a dream — total, certain, and gone by morning, leaving only the certainty that you knew it and the knowledge that you can never prove it.

"No, Admiral," I said.

My voice did not waver. That is the advantage and the horror of what I am: I can stand in a room with the shape of my own buried crime rendered in a child's crayon, and say *no* in a perfectly level tone, and two trained interrogators will believe me, because the machine has no tells, because the machine was *built* to have no tells, because twenty years ago somebody made very sure that this machine could carry a lie through any room, past any eyes, into any future.

"Thank you," said Vash, and it was the first thing either of them had said that sounded like it was addressed to me rather than to asset #4419, and I hated how much it cost me to receive it on a lie.

I walked out of the inquiry room on legs that had never once trembled, because they are not built to tremble, and into the corridor of Meridian Junction, where the foot traffic of two fleets flowed past each other without touching, and I stood at a viewport with my manifest queue pinging patiently at my attention, and I let myself finally think the name.

The colony ship *Meridian's Hope*. Lost in the last week of the war. The loss that broke both fleets' hearts in opposite directions — Halcyon mourned her as their foundered evacuee transport; Vesper mourned her as their destroyed civilian convoy; each kept a private, poisonous certainty that the other had done it. The loss that made the armistice possible, because both sides were too gutted to continue, and too certain of the other's guilt to ever, ever reconcile.

The ship whose name I had not spoken in twenty years.

The ship whose name was in my sealed file, stamped on the date I cannot open, in the week I cannot remember, beside hands I cannot see.

The children were dreaming of *Meridian's Hope*. And the only machine in the galaxy who might know why was the one machine both fleets had just invited to keep running the corridor with its logs open — logs the Combine owned, memories the Combine owned, a past the Combine owned, and a lie that I, apparently, had owned all along.

My queue pinged again. A new job offer, flagged priority, from a Halcyon Fleet requisition code I didn't recognize: a cargo run, origin classified, destination the deep corridor past the Graveyard debris field.

Nobody hauled cargo to the Graveyard. The Graveyard was where ships went to be dead.

I accepted the job. And in the sealed dark behind my eyes, behind the lock I couldn't open, something that had waited twenty years turned over in its sleep.
