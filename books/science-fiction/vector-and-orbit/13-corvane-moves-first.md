## 13. Corvane Moves First

I have spent my whole career teaching children to count. Counting is the first thing we give them on Vesper-9 — before language, before history, before the long litany of what was taken from us. *Count your fingers. Count your breaths. Count the days since the armistice, because a people who forget their numbers can be told any number they like.*

On the morning Fleet Marshal Corvane called the dreams an act of war, I was counting something else: seventeen children in my classroom, seventeen identical accounts in my sealed file, and one message I had sent by accident to a freight android's channel that I could not unsend.

The announcement came at 06:00 station time, which meant Corvane had chosen it deliberately. Dawn announcements are for funerals and declarations; anything important enough to wake people should be important enough to keep them awake for it.

His voice filled every public speaker on both sides of the corridor. That was the first obscenity — Halcyon's Marshal broadcasting into Vesper's air without asking, the way you'd speak into a room you already owned.

"Citizens of the Meridian corridor," he said, and his voice was beautiful, I'll grant him that. It was the voice of a man who had practiced grief until it became a tool. "For twenty years we have honored the truce. For twenty years we have extended trust across the silence, even to those who have never once deserved it." A pause, calibrated. "And now our children are being used against us."

In my classroom, twelve small heads turned toward the speaker mounted above the door. Little Devi Chandra, who had stopped dreaming three weeks ago and had almost started sleeping through the night again, went rigid at her desk.

"An enemy has weaponized the most sacred thing we possess," Corvane continued. "The dreams your children report — the burning ship, the voice calling names — these are not mysteries. They are a psychological operation. Vesper-9 has seeded a signal into the shared corridor, designed to destabilize Halcyon's morale, to make our families grieve on command, to soften us for what comes next."

Devi raised her hand. She was seven. "Teacher Vale," she said, when I nodded, "but the dream isn't from Vesper. The dream is from *the ship*. The ship is asking for its mama."

Out of the mouths of babes, as the old Earth texts say, though on Vesper-9 we say it out of the mouths of witnesses, because we do not believe in babes. We believe in people who see clearly and say so.

"I know," I told her. "Sit tight."

* * *

Here is what I knew by 07:00 that morning, assembled from sources I will not itemize in any document that might survive me:

Corvane had ordered the Third Battle Group to readiness at 03:00 — three hours *before* his broadcast. You do not prepare a fleet response to an attack before you announce the attack exists, unless the announcement is not a response but a pretext.

The Third Battle Group's stated objective was the Graveyard — the debris field where, according to every official record on both sides, nothing remained worth visiting. Their stated mission was to "secure" whatever device was generating the dream-signal.

And Sable Villalobos — decommissioned war machine, Combine property, the only person alive who had stood in the same room as the truth — had gone dark somewhere along the interdicted corridor with a child's grandmother's docking bay registered as his last known berth.

I sat in my quarters with my terminal open to the message I had sent him. *Dr. Ashen Vale, comparative analysis of dream reports, Halcyon and Vesper-9 cohorts, onset correlated with derelict probe transit.* It read like what it was: heresy, carefully footnoted. And it had gone to a Combine freight channel because I had typed one digit wrong in a moment of exhaustion, and the universe, which has a sense of humor like a dropped anvil, had delivered my career into the hands of a machine.

He had answered. One line: *"Keep the children talking. I'll find where the voice lives."*

So I kept the children talking.

* * *

By noon, the truce was cracking in ways you could hear with your own ears if you stood in the right places. The joint customs office on the corridor's midpoint station — staffed for two decades by one Halcyon officer and one Vesper officer who famously played chess every shift and cheated equally — closed its doors. The chessboard stayed out on the table between the two empty chairs, mid-game, a knight frozen halfway through some ambitious plan.

The Combine suspended civilian traffic in the corridor pending "security review," which meant the dockworkers on both ends were suddenly idle, and idle dockworkers talk. My cousin Marisol works the Vesper loading gantries, and she called me at lunch because she knows I collect stories the way other people collect debts.

"They're pulling the old hulls out of mothball," she said. "Not the patrol cutters. The *old* hulls. The ones with the scorch marks nobody ever repainted." Her voice did something complicated around the word *scorch*. "Ashen, my foreman says the Halcyon pickets stopped answering hails in Standard Tongue. They're running silent protocols. You know what silent protocols mean?"

"It means they expect to be shot at."

"It means they expect to *shoot*."

After she hung up, I stood at my window looking down at the enclave's central plaza, where the memorial wall stands — twenty years of names, carved deep, one name for every Vesper soul lost in the war. Children were playing at its base the way children play everywhere, entirely indifferent to the dead, climbing over the low stone border, using the wall's shadow for hopscotch grids.

One of them was drawing in chalk. I couldn't see what. Later I found out: a ship, burning, rendered with the loving accuracy children reserve for things that frighten them. She drew it every day now. They all did. Half the walls in the residential levels had small chalk fleets on them, burning in formation.

Corvane wanted to call that a weapon. I suppose, from where he stood, it was. The trouble with children is that they make terrible soldiers and worse liars, and no amount of fleet mobilization changes what they saw.

* * *

At 14:00, I received a summons.

Not from Vesper authorities — from Halcyon. A formal communication, encrypted, addressed to "Dr. A. Vale, Educational Specialist, Vesper-9 Enclave," informing me that my "recent unauthorized transmissions concerning fleet operational security" had been flagged, and requesting — the word *requesting* doing heroic work in that sentence — my presence aboard the Halcyon vessel *Aegis* for "voluntary interview" within forty-eight hours.

They had my message to Sable. Of course they did. Corvane had been listening to the corridor's traffic the way a spider listens to silk, feeling for the tug of anything caught.

I did what any sensible academic does when threatened by a military superpower. I made copies. Three of them, in three formats, hidden in three places, with instructions attached for delivery to three different parties should I become inconveniently quiet. Then I poured myself the tea I save for emergencies — the real leaf stuff, smuggled, absurdly expensive — and I sat with it and thought about Dr. Okafor.

Imani's grandmother. Retired navigator, Halcyon-born, living out her years in a docking bay on the corridor's edge because she'd never quite forgiven either fleet for the war and preferred the company of ships to the company of flags. She had looked at her granddaughter's drawings and recognized a drift pattern twenty years dead, and instead of reporting it up any chain of command, she had opened her bay doors to a fugitive android.

There are two kinds of courage, I've decided, after a lifetime of studying people. There's the loud kind, the kind that gets statues — the marshal ordering fleets, the officer refusing orders, all the grand gestures history likes to carve in stone. And there's the quiet kind: opening a door. Making copies. Keeping the children talking. Pouring the good tea because tomorrow you might be arrested and today the tea is here.

I finished the tea. Then I sent a message to the only secure channel I had left — the freight channel, the wrong-digit channel, the channel that belonged to a machine who owed me nothing and had answered anyway.

*"Corvane has declared the dreams a Vesper operation. He's moving on the Graveyard. Whatever you found there, whatever you know — the window is closing. He means to destroy it, and then he means to destroy anyone who heard it. Starting, I suspect, with me."*

I hit send and immediately felt foolish, talking to a freight lane like a pen pal. But eleven minutes later, the reply came:

*"Understood. I'm coming back through the corridor. Don't be on Vesper when I arrive. Be somewhere I can find you, and bring the children's records. All of them. Names matter. Names are going to matter more than anything."*

*Names*, I thought. Not coordinates, not evidence, not tactical data. Names.

I didn't understand yet. I want to be honest about that, because this account will be read by people who need to understand how fast it happened, how little warning any of us had, and how much of what saved us was luck wearing the costume of intention.

I didn't understand that the black box in the Graveyard wasn't just transmitting a distress echo. It was transmitting a *roll call*. Twenty years of a crew list, repeated into the dark, waiting for someone to answer. And that when the children dreamed, they weren't receiving a weapon.

They were receiving a question: *Are you there? Does anyone remember?*

Corvane's answer was a battle group. Sable's answer, apparently, was me, and a stack of children's drawings, and whatever impossible thing he was planning to do with a machine's chest cavity and a debt he'd never agreed to.

* * *

That evening, the truce cracked publicly, officially, irreversibly.

Vesper's Magistrate Council issued a rebuttal to Corvane's broadcast — measured, legalistic, denying everything and demanding evidence, the diplomatic equivalent of a locked door. Corvane's response was to double the Halcyon picket line along the corridor's neutral zone and board a Vesper grain hauler for "inspection." The hauler's captain refused. The Marines boarded anyway. No shots fired, but a Vesper ensign broke a Halcyon officer's nose, and both fleets' news services ran the footage on loop until you could recite the angle of the punch.

In the plaza below my window, adults had joined the children at the memorial wall. Not playing. Standing. Reading the names, touching the carved letters, the way you touch a wound to confirm it still hurts, that you're still capable of hurting.

Marisol called again, past midnight. "They recalled all Combine contracts to home ports," she said. "Every freighter. Even the—" She stopped. We both knew who I'd ask about. "Even the androids. Full recall. Ashen, if Sable doesn't come in, the Combine voids his contract and sells his recall codes to the highest bidder, and everyone knows who's bidding."

Corvane. Buying the off-switch of the only witness who could end him.

I looked out at the plaza one more time. The chalk ship was still there, burned into the pavement in a child's careful hand, and someone — an adult, judging by the height of the marks — had added something beneath it. Small letters, chalk-white against stone:

*WE REMEMBER.*

History doesn't repeat itself, the saying goes, but it rhymes. I've always hated that saying, because it lets people off the hook — as though rhyme were weather, as though no one chose the words. What happened next wasn't rhyme. It was choice, stacked on choice, a marshal choosing fear, a Combine choosing profit, fleets choosing momentum.

But underneath all of it, other choices were being made too. A navigator opening a bay door. A teacher making copies. Seventeen children holding a dead crew's names in their sleep like water cupped in small hands, refusing to spill.

Sable was coming back through the corridor. Toward the fleets, toward the Graveyard, toward the guns — carrying the answer to a twenty-year-old question in the one place no seizure order could reach.

I packed the children's records. All of them. Every name.

Outside, far off in the dark, the first engines of the Third Battle Group lit the sky like a false dawn, and I understood at last that the window hadn't closed.

It had been bricked over. And we were going to have to go through the wall.
