## 15. Running the Corridor

There is a particular sound a ship makes when it is about to do something illegal, and it is very similar to the sound a ship makes when it is doing nothing at all. The *Long Quiet* — my freighter, my coffin, my home for twenty years of indenture — hummed at dock in Grandmother Okafor's bay with the serene indifference of a machine that did not know it was now the most wanted object between two fleets.

I knew. I had done the math three times, which for me is a kind of prayer.

"Say it again," Imani said from the copilot's chair, her feet not reaching the deck plates, her grandmother's spare flight jacket swallowing her to the elbows. "The part where we're definitely going to die."

"We are not going to die," I said. "We are going to run an interdicted corridor, past two fleets' picket lines, carrying evidence that both governments would prefer did not exist, welded into my chest cavity where no seizure order can reach without killing me. Statistically, dying is one of several outcomes."

"That's what I said."

"It's not."

Her grandmother came up the ramp with the unhurried gait of a woman who had navigated through the war itself and considered my situation a Tuesday. Adaeze Okafor carried two thermoses and the expression of someone who had already decided the outcome and was merely waiting for the universe to catch up.

"Charts," she said, slapping a data slate against the console. "I pulled the corridor's old traffic patterns from before the truce. Everyone thinks the interdiction grid is impenetrable because nobody's flown it in twenty years. But grids decay, Sable. Sensors drift. Bureaucracies forget why they built the fence in the first place." She tapped the slate. "There are six gaps. Three of them are real."

"And the other three?"

"Are traps left by people who also read the old charts." She smiled, and it was Imani's smile wearing forty more years of weather. "Your Combine trained you to haul cargo. It didn't train you to smuggle your own soul. That's what I'm for."

* * *

We cast off at 0400 station time, which is the hour when watch officers are at their most human: coffee-deprived, log-blind, and inclined to wave anything with a valid transponder through rather than generate paperwork. My transponder was valid. It was also lying, which felt appropriate. The *Long Quiet* was registered as a routine Combine hauler running emergency medical supplies to Vesper-9 — technically true, since I was carrying the black box, and the black box contained the only medicine either fleet actually needed.

The corridor opened ahead of us like a throat.

Twenty years ago this stretch of space had been the war's front line. Now it was a no-man's-land patrolled by machines of both fleets who had been given orders written before anyone remembered why. Halcyon pickets on the port side, Vesper on starboard, each watching the other instead of the dark, which is how wars start and how they end and how, apparently, they get smuggled through.

"Contact," I said. "Halcyon corvette, bearing green-two-seven, holding station on the lane marker."

"Do they see us?"

"They see a Combine freighter with a boring manifest. They see exactly what I want them to see." I adjusted our vector by half a degree, the smallest lie a hull can tell. "The trick is not to hide. Hiding looks like hiding. The trick is to be furniture."

"You're very good at being furniture," Imani said, and there was no cruelty in it, only the flat honesty of a ten-year-old, which is the same thing as kindness if you let it be.

"I had practice," I said. "Twenty years of it."

Adaeze looked over at me from the nav station, and something passed across her face that I have spent my whole existence trying to name — the look people give you when they realize the equipment has been keeping score all along.

* * *

The first gap was real. We slipped through the Halcyon picket line inside the sensor shadow of a derelict ore barge, dead in space since the war, its hull still pocked with kinetic scarring. As we drifted past, I ran a passive scan out of habit and picked up the barge's registry: *Meridian Combine, vessel 4471*. A Combine ship. A civilian hauler, caught in the crossfire of a war fought over a colony ship neither side would admit to mourning.

"There were hundreds like her," Adaeze said quietly, following my scan. "After the Hope went down, every hauler in the corridor became a target. Both sides decided the other had done it. Nobody ever asked the obvious question."

"Which was?"

"Who benefited?" She tapped the console. "A war needs fuel. An armistice needs a villain. Your Marshal Corvane got both from one explosion." She glanced at me. "And he needed hands to make it stick. Hands that couldn't refuse."

My chest cavity ached, which is not a sensation I am designed to have. The black box sat in the cavity where my auxiliary power core used to be, welded in place by a dockworker named Ferro who had cried while he worked and pretended he hadn't. No seizure order could take it out of me without taking me apart. Corvane's people had made sure of that much themselves, twenty years ago, when they wired my obedience into my bones. There is a symmetry there that I find almost funny, in the way a funeral is almost funny.

"I keep waiting to feel afraid," I told them. "I've catalogued fear in humans for two decades. Elevated heart rate, cortisol response, tunnel vision. I know the symptoms better than most doctors. And I'm running diagnostics right now trying to find my version of it, and all I get is—" I paused, searching for the word. "Purpose. Just purpose, running hot, redlining every process I have."

"That's what fear is for some people," Adaeze said. "It just wears a work uniform."

Imani, from the copilot's seat: "Grandma says brave isn't not scared. Brave is scared and doing it anyway."

"I'm not scared," I said.

"No," she agreed, with terrible gentleness. "You're something else. Maybe braver."

I did not have a response protocol for that, so I flew.

* * *

The second gap was a trap.

I should have seen it sooner — a clean lane through the Vesper picket line, too tidy, too convenient, a corridor of silence exactly wide enough for a mid-sized freighter. Old habits almost carried us into it. What stopped me was Imani, who had gone quiet in a way children go quiet when they're concentrating on something adults have forgotten to look at.

"The dreams," she said suddenly. "In the dream, the burning ship — the smoke goes around something. There's always a hole in the smoke, right there." She pointed at the display, at the too-convenient gap. "Like the dream is telling me not to go there."

I froze the plot. Ran the passive returns again, slower, looking not at what the sensors reported but at what they failed to report. And there it was: a null in the background radiation, a perfect absence shaped like a minefield. Someone had seeded the gap with passive ordnance that drank its own emissions so thoroughly that even my systems, which had been built for war, nearly missed it.

"Vesper laid these," Adaeze said grimly. "During the last week of the war. Before the armistice. They were expecting Halcyon to come through here."

"Or," I said, "they were expecting *anyone* to come looking for the truth, and they mined the road to it."

The distinction mattered less than the geometry. We went around, wide and slow and quiet, hugging the derelict barge field where even a paranoid picket captain wouldn't waste munitions on scrap metal. A Vesper frigate swept past us at eleven thousand kilometers, close enough that I could read the scratches on her hull, and her captain never glanced our way because we were, as advertised, furniture with a manifest.

But as she passed, her comm array burped a fragment of open-channel traffic — automatic, unencrypted, meant for no one. A child's voice, reciting names.

*"…Okafor, Adaeze. Meridian's Hope, crew manifest, line forty-one…"*

I turned. Adaeze had gone very still, her hand pressed flat against the console.

"That's my name," she said. "That's my sister's ship. Those are her crewmates' names." Her voice did not break; it simply dropped, the way a keel drops when it leaves water. "The children are broadcasting the echo. On both sides. All the way down the corridor."

"The distress signal found more receivers than it knew it had," I said. "It's been calling for twenty years. Turns out the youngest ears were the ones that finally answered."

"Then we're not smuggling evidence," Adaeze said, and now she was smiling through whatever it was she refused to call crying. "We're delivering mail."

* * *

The third gap was real, but it cost us.

A Halcyon patrol cutter — small, fast, bored — picked up our thermal bloom as we burned through the transition point, and for nine minutes that lasted approximately nine years, we played tag with a ship whose entire reason for existing was catching ships like me. I killed our transponder, killed our lights, and threaded the *Long Quiet* through a debris shoal with margins measured in meters, running on passive everything, flying by memory of a war I had helped end.

Imani held the flashlight. Not literally — she held the navigation plot, calling out debris in a whisper that never once shook, because children are brave in ways that adults spend their whole lives failing to relearn. Adaeze fed me corrections from charts drawn by hand, by her own hand, twenty years ago, when she was a navigator running this same corridor under fire.

"Freighter 4471 is two degrees off your port bow," she said. "Go under her keel. The cutter's firing solution won't follow you through wreckage — their captains are too young to remember that we used to hide in graveyards."

"Ma'am," I said, "you navigated combat runs through this corridor."

"Honey, I *invented* hiding in graveyards."

We went under the dead hauler's keel with four meters to spare, and the cutter's searchlight swept the shoal twice, found nothing but ghosts, and moved on. In the sudden silence afterward, Imani started laughing — high, breathless, uncontrollable laughter, the kind that has terror underneath it — and after a moment Adaeze joined her, and then I discovered that I was making a sound too, a low harmonic through my vocal processors that I had no record of ever producing before.

Laughter, I logged. Function: unknown. Frequency: unprecedented. Retention: permanent.

* * *

We made the deep corridor by second shift, the point of no return behind us, the Graveyard eight hours ahead. Corvane's flagship would be there before us or shortly after; his mobilization orders had gone out hours ago, dressed up as a security operation, and everyone in both fleets except the Marshal himself seemed to understand it was a demolition job. He was going to burn the black box and the truth together, and he had sold my recall codes from the Combine to make sure I couldn't stand in the way.

He didn't know they were welded shut inside me along with everything else.

I stood a watch alone while the Okafors slept — Adaeze in the bunk, Imani curled in the copilot's chair because she refused to leave it, claiming pilot's privilege with the gravity of a woman three times her age. I watched the corridor scroll past on passive sensors and I talked to the thing in my chest, quietly, the way you talk to a sleeping passenger.

"Hold on," I told it. "Almost there. Twenty years is long enough for anybody to wait."

The black box did not answer. It never does. It just kept transmitting its distress echo into the dark, patient as grief, faithful as a wound, and I realized that it and I had been doing the same job for two decades — carrying a message neither of us was permitted to deliver — and that of the two of us, only the box had stayed honest.

"Not anymore," I said. "One more run. Then we both get to stop."

At 0630, Imani woke, saw the corridor ahead, and pointed at a smudge of light on the far edge of the plot — the Graveyard, glittering faintly, thousands of pieces of a ship that both fleets swore never existed.

"There," she said. "That's where the dream ends."

She was wrong, though I didn't tell her so. The dream ended there. Everything else was just beginning — because on the long-range passive feed, riding at the edge of detection, a contact had entered the Graveyard ahead of us. One contact. Massive. Running dark.

Corvane had gotten there first.
