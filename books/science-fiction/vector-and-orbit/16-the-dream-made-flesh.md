## 16. The Dream Made Flesh

The first thing you should know about the Graveyard is that it doesn't look like a graveyard.

I'd imagined something like the memorial garden on Halcyon Station — neat rows of names on a wall, little lights, grown-ups standing quiet with their hands folded. What I saw out the viewport was nothing like that. It was junk. Beautiful, terrible junk. A whole field of broken ships drifting in the dark, turning slow, catching the light from our running lamps and throwing it back in pieces. Hull plates. A cargo pod with its doors open like a mouth. Something that might have been an engine block once, big as my whole apartment block, just sitting there being dead.

"That's her," Grandma said. Her voice had gone thin. "The Meridian's Hope. Third cluster in, off the old drift line."

"You can tell that from here?"

"I navigated her sister ship for six years, baby. You don't forget how a hull sits in space." She adjusted her glasses even though she wasn't looking through them. She does that when she's scared. She thinks nobody notices. Everybody notices.

Sable didn't say anything at all. He never says much when it matters most, which is a thing about him I've decided to forgive, because when he does talk it counts double. But his hands were doing something strange on the controls — moving through checks he'd already done three times, like a kid re-reading the same page of homework hoping it'll change.

"Corvane's flagship will be here within the hour," he said finally. "Maybe less."

"Then we'd better hurry," Grandma said.

"Or," I said, because somebody had to be honest around here, "we should maybe think about the part where a warship is coming to shoot us."

Sable turned his head toward me. He has a way of looking at you where you can't actually see eyes, just light, but somehow you still feel *seen*, which used to creep me out and now mostly doesn't. Mostly.

"The black box is welded into my chest cavity, Imani. If they fire on this ship, they fire on the evidence. Corvane wants it destroyed quietly. He cannot destroy it without destroying me, publicly, in front of both fleets." A pause. "He is calculating whether that trade is worth it."

"And is it?"

"I am not certain," Sable said, "that I want to know the answer."

* * *

Here's the other thing you should know: I hadn't slept in two days, and neither had anybody else.

It started small, back on Halcyon. Kids waking up crying, then kids waking up calm, which was honestly worse. Then the teachers started comparing notes with — well, they weren't supposed to talk to Vesper people, but Dr. Vale broke a bunch of rules doing exactly that, and she found out the same thing was happening over there. Same dream. Burning ship. A voice saying a name nobody had said out loud in twenty years.

I dream it every night. I'm not going to describe the burning part again because everyone always asks about the burning part and the burning part isn't the important part. The important part is the voice. It's patient. It's been waiting so long it forgot how to be anything except patient. And underneath the voice there are names — dozens of them, hundreds, read out one after another like roll call at school, except nobody ever answers *here*.

Except lately, some of us have started answering.

Nobody planned it. That's the thing grown-ups never believe. On Halcyon, a girl named Priya started whispering the names into her sleep-talk, and her roommate wrote them down, and then kids started writing them down on purpose, and then someone figured out you could send them over the station's open channel, the one nobody monitors because it's just for announcements about lost mittens and cafeteria menus.

So we sent the names.

And on Vesper-9, kids we've never met sent them back.

That's how I know the dream isn't just mine. That's how everybody knows, now. You can't fake two hundred children on opposite sides of a truce reciting the same dead people in the same order.

Grandma calls it "the echo finding its answer." Sable calls it "a distress signal finally receiving acknowledgment." I call it what it is: the dead crew of the Meridian's Hope asking if anyone remembers them, and us saying yes. Finally. Twenty years late, but yes.

* * *

We came out of the burn into the edge of the debris field and Sable killed the engines to drift, because a running ship is a target and a piece of junk is furniture. He's very good at being furniture. It used to make me sad, watching him do it. Now I understand it's a skill, like Grandma reading a drift pattern, like Dr. Vale breaking rules gently enough not to get caught.

For about nine minutes, everything was quiet. I counted. Nine minutes of just the tick of cooling metal and Grandma breathing and Sable making tiny course corrections that weren't really corrections, just him keeping his hands busy.

Then the long-range scanner chimed, and Sable said, "Contact. Heavy signature. Halcyon transponder code — it's the *Vigilant*. Corvane's flagship."

My stomach did a thing. "How fast?"

"Forty minutes to weapons range. Perhaps thirty-five."

"We have the box," Grandma said. "We have the truth. We go in, we broadcast it, we're done before he arrives."

"No," Sable said. "We wait."

"*Wait?*"

"If we broadcast now, from inside the Graveyard, alone, with no witnesses — Corvane announces a rogue android and a stolen vessel transmitting fabricated records. Both fleets' news services run his version by morning. The truth needs ears, Ms. Okafor. Thousands of them. It needs to arrive at the same moment he does, so that no one has time to decide what to believe before they've already heard it."

"So we're bait," I said.

"We are testimony," Sable said. "Bait runs away."

I looked at Grandma. Grandma looked at me. We've known each other my whole life, so I knew what her face meant, which was: *this machine is right and I hate it.*

So we waited. And while we waited, the channel lit up.

* * *

It started as static. Then it wasn't static.

"—Okafor, Adaeze, crew designation hydroponics—"

I sat straight up so fast I banged my knee on the console. "That's — Grandma, that's your name."

"I hear it, baby."

"—Vale, Tomas, crew designation medical—"

"Dr. Vale's husband," Grandma whispered. "She was pregnant when the Hope went missing. She never—" Grandma stopped, pressed her knuckles against her mouth.

The names kept coming. Not from the black box — I could tell the difference now, the box's signal had a texture, a worn-out quality, like a recording of a recording. This was live. This was children. Hundreds of them, on open channels, on both sides of the corridor, reading the crew manifest of a ship their governments had spent twenty years pretending was a rumor.

Priya's voice came through from Halcyon, steady and clear, the way she reads in class when she's nervous but refuses to show it. And under her, overlapping, a boy's voice from Vesper with an accent I only knew from freight manifests and dock chatter, and behind both of them more voices, layering up, a chorus with no conductor.

"—Meridian, Hope, crew designation command—"

They'd named the ship itself. Somebody had put the ship on the manifest. I don't know which kid thought of it. I hope it was Priya. I hope it was one of ours.

"They're answering," I said. My throat felt weird. "Sable, they're answering the roll call."

"Yes," Sable said, and his voice did something I'd never heard it do. It wobbled. Just slightly. An android whose voice wobbles — you'd think that would be a malfunction, but it wasn't, it was the opposite of a malfunction. It was the most working-correctly he'd ever sounded. "Twenty years, the echo asked. Tonight, it receives its answer."

Grandma was crying. Not the quiet kind either. I put my hand on her arm and she grabbed it and held on hard enough to hurt, and I let it hurt, because some things should.

And that's when I understood what Corvane couldn't afford to understand: you can argue with a black box. You can call it fabricated, tampered, forged — Sable told us all the words they'd use. But you cannot argue with four hundred children singing your dead back to you across an open channel while the whole corridor listens. There's no spin for that. There's no press release shaped like that.

Innocence is a weapon nobody's built armor for yet.

* * *

The *Vigilant* came in at twenty-nine minutes, which meant Corvane had burned harder than polite, which meant he was scared, which was the single most comforting fact of my entire life up to that point.

She filled the forward viewport like a wall. Halcyon-built, gunmetal gray, running lights blazing in a formation that said *we are official and you are not.* Turrets tracked us. I could see them tracking, little dark movements, like eyes following a fly around a room.

"Halcyon vessel *Long Quiet*," a voice said over the comm, all crisp and cold, "you are in violation of interdiction protocols. Cut engines and prepare for boarding."

Sable opened the channel. "This vessel carries the flight recorder of the colony ship Meridian's Hope," he said, "and the testimony of the individual who transmitted the forged order that ended the war. Both records are being broadcast on all open frequencies as of this moment. Firing upon this vessel will be firing upon that broadcast, in front of every listener in the corridor."

Silence. Long silence. I pictured some bridge officer somewhere holding a hand over a fire button and sweating.

Then Corvane himself came on the channel, and I heard my first war criminal speak, and do you want to know the disappointing thing? He sounded normal. Tired, even. Like a principal.

"Sable Villalobos," he said. "You were a fine instrument once. It grieves me to see what they've done with you."

"They," Sable said, "is you, Marshal. You know that. You've always known that."

"Children's games," Corvane said, and his voice got harder. "A manufactured signal. A Vesper fabrication designed to destabilize the armistice in its final hours. Halcyon will not be moved by ghost stories."

But even as he said it, the names kept coming through the open channels — you could hear them bleeding into his transmission, faint under his voice, *Ashen, Priya, Okafor, Adaeze* — and I swear the whole corridor could hear him trying to talk over four hundred kids, and failing, because you can't talk over that. Nobody can. It's like shouting down rain.

"Marshal Corvane," Sable said, and his voice had gone steady in the way it goes steady right before he does the bravest thing in the room, "in ninety seconds I will begin broadcasting the unedited record. Your officers are listening. Your crews are listening. Their children taught them the names tonight. When the record plays, every one of them will hear who gave the order."

"Then you die with it," Corvane said.

"Probably," Sable agreed, pleasantly. "But not alone, and not unheard. Those were my terms. I negotiated them myself."

Grandma squeezed my hand. On the channel, I could hear Corvane breathing. Just breathing. A man with a whole fleet behind him, and all he had left was breath.

"Ninety seconds, Marshal," Sable said. "I would spend them deciding who you want to be when the names finish."

* * *

The countdown ran in my head even though nobody announced it. Fifty seconds. Forty.

And that's when the dreams changed.

I felt it happen — I was awake, and I still felt it, which shouldn't be possible, but there it was, like a door opening in the back of my skull. Except it wasn't burning anymore. The ship in the dream wasn't burning. It was *lit* — running lights, warm ones, the kind a ship turns on when it knows it's been found. And the voice wasn't asking anymore.

It was saying thank you.

On the open channels, the kids felt it too. I know because the recitation stopped — mid-name, poor Priya cut off right at "designation" — and for three whole seconds the entire corridor went silent, both fleets, thousands of listeners, everyone holding the same breath.

Then a voice came through that wasn't any kid. It was older, and rougher, and it came from everywhere at once, from the black box in Sable's chest and the wreck outside and maybe from twenty years of waiting, and it said:

"This is Captain Ede Meridian, colony ship Meridian's Hope. To whoever hears this: we made it further than they told you. Check the third cluster. Check the lifepods. *We were not all lost.*"

Grandma made a sound I'd never heard her make.

And out in the dark, in the third cluster of the Graveyard, something old and battered and patient blinked its running lights on — one, two, three — like a porch light coming on after a very long night.

Thirty seconds left on Sable's countdown. The *Vigilant*'s turrets were still tracking us. And Corvane's voice came back on the channel, and it wasn't tired anymore.

"Burn that ship," he said, "before it speaks again."
