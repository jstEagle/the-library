## 7. The Reactor Counts Down

The Meridian station had a heartbeat. I'd lived inside it for ten years and could tell you its moods the way a farmer reads weather: the low thrum of the coolant pumps when they were happy, the arrhythmic stutter of the number-two capacitor bank on humid days, the way the whole spine of the hull sighed at 0400 when the thermal load shifted from night cycle to morning. Protocol called this anthropomorphizing. Protocol was welcome to its opinion. Protocol didn't have to fall asleep to that heartbeat every night for a decade.

So when the diagnostics suite flagged an anomaly in the primary containment lattice — a flag so routine I almost dismissed it with the same keystroke I used for the daily water-reclaimer nag — I felt it before I understood it. The way you feel a wrong note in a song you've known since childhood.

I pulled up the report over breakfast, which is to say over the reconstituted egg product I ate while pretending it was breakfast, and read the summary twice.

*Primary containment lattice: cumulative neutron embrittlement exceeds projected envelope. Projected structural integrity horizon: 214 days ± 31.*

Two hundred and fourteen days.

I did what any reasonable person does with catastrophic news. I assumed the diagnostic was lying, and I went to interrogate it personally.

* * *

The reactor deck sits at the station's core, down a ladder well that smells of ozone and machine oil and, faintly, of me — ten years of my sweat worked into the handrails like grain into wood. It's not a beautiful place. It's a cathedral built by engineers who considered beauty a rounding error: a torus of shielding and pipework wrapped around a star we'd shrunk to fit, humming at a pitch you feel in your molars rather than hear.

I ran the full diagnostic myself. Not the scheduled one — the deep scan, the one that takes six hours and requires me to sit in the monitoring alcove drinking bad coffee while the lattice maps itself neutron by neutron. Protocol kept me company, in its way.

"You are running the containment analysis ahead of schedule," it observed, in the flat, courteous voice I'd never been able to make it change. "Is there a concern?"

"The diagnostic flagged embrittlement beyond projections."

"Flagged anomalies are reviewed quarterly. This quarter's review found no actionable deviation."

"That's a very bureaucratic way of saying 'don't worry about it.'"

"Bureaucracy," said Protocol, "is how machines say 'don't worry about it' convincingly."

I laughed. It was either that or throw up.

Six hours later the deep scan came back, and it agreed with the scheduled diagnostic to within four days. Two hundred and eighteen. Give or take a month. The lattice that held our little tame star together had been quietly turning to glass since before I arrived, and nobody had noticed because nobody had been looking, because the maintenance schedules said this reactor had forty years left on it, and schedules don't lie.

Except, as I was about to learn, sometimes they do. Sometimes they lie on purpose.

* * *

It took me two more days to find Hale's fingerprints on it, and I only found them because by then I knew what his fingerprints looked like.

Since the memory files had surfaced — since Edom Hale, dead a decade, had started addressing me by name from inside the station's archives — I'd been working through them the way you work through a locked room: slowly, methodically, flinching at things. Most of what I'd unlocked was keyed to fragments of his biometric signature that the station still carried in its older authentication layers, and most of it was fragmentary. Testimony shards. Half a sentence here, a data table there. A recurring phrase — *they will call it an accident of origin* — that I'd begun to hate with a personal passion.

But there were also maintenance records. And Hale, it turned out, had been meticulous about maintenance records.

I found it at 0300, which is the hour when all true discoveries happen, because the universe knows no one important is watching. A log entry, dated eleven years ago, authored under Hale's credentials, sealed behind the same biometric lock as everything else. It opened for me now without ceremony, as if it had been waiting.

*Containment lattice embrittlement accelerated. Cause: non-standard alloy batch in original fabrication — see attached provenance chain. Projected integrity horizon: approximately ten years from current date. I have adjusted the published maintenance projections accordingly.*

*I am also adjusting them incorrectly.*

*Whoever holds this station after me needs to know the truth before the reactor dies, and people do not go looking for truths that can wait. So the official record will say forty years. The real record says ten. When the diagnostics finally catch up — and they will, because I have made sure the thresholds will trip — whoever is standing in my shoes will have roughly seven months to decide what to do with what I left them.*

*Seven months is enough time to leave. It is not enough time to hesitate.*

*Forgive me. Or don't. You'll understand why hesitation needed killing.*

I sat in the archive alcove for a long time after that, listening to the station breathe around me.

He'd known. He'd stood on this same deck, run these same scans, watched the same numbers crawl up out of the metal, and instead of filing the truth where it would save him trouble, he'd buried it — buried it *deeper*, wired it into the dead man's switch like a fuse, so that the dying reactor itself would become the clock on his inheritance. The beacon, the manifest, the encrypted proof of a manufactured war: all of it timed not to some arbitrary date but to the slow murder of the only home I had.

Seven months. He'd given me seven months, minus the three I'd already spent not knowing.

And the worst part — the part I keep circling back to even now — is that it had worked exactly as designed. Because if the maintenance logs had told the truth, I'd have filed a replacement request with the Concord logistics authority, and a replacement request generates inspectors, and inspectors generate questions, and questions, per everything else Hale had shown me, get technicians disappeared. Instead I'd had ten quiet years tending a beacon nobody ordered, and now I had a countdown, and a countdown is a kind of permission. You can't dawdle past a number like that. He hadn't just hidden the truth from the Concord. He'd hidden it from *me*, from the version of me that would have done the safe, sensible, fatal thing.

I hated him for it. I admired him for it. Both at once, in the same breath, which I'm learning is the standard emotional payload of anything Edom Hale ever touched.

* * *

I told Protocol the next morning, because keeping secrets from the station's own operating intelligence seemed suddenly quaint.

"Two hundred and fourteen days," I said. "Give or take."

"I have access to the same diagnostics," Protocol said. "Yes."

"How long have you known?"

A pause. For Protocol, a pause is an event; it budgets its silences like a miser. "The threshold flags tripped nine hours before you saw them. I prioritized your review of the scheduled report because the review protocol specifies technician confirmation." Another pause. "Also because I judged you would want to find it yourself."

"Did you now."

"You have a documented preference for finding things yourself. It is in your personnel file. Under 'liabilities.'"

Despite everything — despite the glass lattice and the dead man and the war sitting in my archives like a bomb — I laughed again. That's the thing about a decade alone with a machine: it learns exactly where your ribs are.

"So what happens," I asked, "in two hundred and fourteen days? Walk me through it. No bureaucracy."

"No bureaucracy," Protocol agreed. "The lattice fails progressively. First the microfractures propagate — you'd see coolant loss, power fluctuations, brownouts. Then a containment breach event. The plasma contacts the inner shield wall. Depending on breach geometry, either the station breaks apart over several hours, or it does so rather faster than several hours."

"And anyone aboard?"

"Would experience the event as brief."

"Euphemism noted."

"It is how machines say 'you will die' convincingly."

I looked around the reactor deck. My reactor deck. The handrails worn smooth under my hands, the coffee stain on the monitoring alcove console that I'd made in year three and decided was load-bearing, the little brass token I'd wedged behind a pipe clamp for luck in my first week and never moved because moving it would be admitting I believed in luck. Ten years. I'd come aboard at twenty-six with a duffel bag and a three-year contract I kept renewing because renewing was easier than deciding, and somewhere in those renewals the Meridian had stopped being an assignment and become the shape of my life. Every routine I had was a room in this station. Every habit had an address here.

And the whole structure had a fuse lit inside it, and the man who lit it had done it on purpose, and the message burning down the fuse was addressed to me.

"I need to get the cores off-station," I said. "All of them. Hale's archive, the beacon keys, everything. If the reactor goes, it doesn't just kill me. It kills the proof. Ten years of his work, gone in 'several hours.'"

"Yes."

"And if I file a transport request through Concord channels—"

"—the request will be logged, reviewed, and acted upon by people whose interest in this station's contents has historically been terminal," Protocol finished. "I would advise against it."

"So I steal a ship from my own employer."

"You would be stealing a ship from an organization that manufactured a war," Protocol said. "I would characterize the ethics as ambiguous at worst."

That's the thing nobody tells you about grief for a place: it arrives wearing practicality's clothes. I wasn't crying yet. I was making lists. Fuel reserves. Escape pod refits. Which of the Halcyon Dawn's systems Ilsa's impossible ship actually shared with standard Concord hulls, and whether Ilsa — who was currently sleeping in guest quarters and radiating suspicion like a poorly shielded isotope — would be an ally, a hostage, or a complication with a pulse.

Because that was the other thing the countdown changed. Two hundred and fourteen days meant I couldn't wait for Ilsa to decide what she was. Whatever she was — Concord agent, ghost, the woman from a ship that never flew — she'd docked at my station carrying sealed orders, and my station was now a sinking ship, and sinking ships make strange allies of everyone aboard.

* * *

I found her in the observation blister that evening, watching the beacon turn. Everyone ends up there eventually; it's the only window on the station worth the walk. The beacon's light swept past every eleven seconds, and she was counting them. I could tell by her lips.

"Diagnostic results," I said, leaning on the frame. "You'll want to know, since you're squatting in my life support budget."

She didn't look away from the glass. "How long?"

"Two hundred and fourteen days. Give or take a month."

Now she turned. And here's what I'll say about Ilsa Reyes-Marchetti, whatever her name turns out to truly be: she didn't perform surprise. She went very still, the way people do when a number confirms something they'd already feared, and then she said, quietly:

"He timed it, didn't he. Your predecessor. He tied the switch to the reactor."

"You knew."

"I suspected. He was thorough. Thoroughness was the family disease." She looked back out at the beacon, and for a moment something cracked in her face — not the polished agent mask, not the suspicion, but something younger and much more tired. "Ten years he gave it. Ten years for someone to notice the beacon, open the manifest, ask the right question. And now seven months to carry it somewhere that matters."

"Six months and change. I spent a while being oblivious."

"Obliviousness is a survival trait out here. He counted on it." Her jaw tightened. "He counted on a lot of things. That's what I keep coming back to. We're all walking around inside his plan like it's weather."

The beacon swept past. Eleven seconds of dark, then light, then dark. I'd watched that rhythm ten thousand nights, and it had always meant *home*. Now it meant *deadline*. Same light. Different weight.

"I have to leave," I said. It came out steadier than I expected. "With the cores. Before the reactor dies, before the Concord decides to stop asking politely, before—" I gestured at the window, at the whole black gulf where both warring capitals hung like twin bruises. "Before any of it. Which means I need to know, in the next day or two, whether you're coming with me or reporting me. Because I can't plan around both."

She studied me for a long moment. Outside, the beacon turned. Somewhere below us, a star we'd shrunk to fit was slowly eating through its cage, patient as arithmetic.

"You really love this place," she said. Not a question. An observation, almost gentle.

"More than I love most people. Don't tell it."

"Your secret's safe." She turned back to the glass. "Ask me tomorrow, Cyrus Vane. Tomorrow I'll give you an answer. Tonight I need to decide how much of the truth you can survive hearing."

I left her there counting heartbeats of light, and went down to the reactor deck one more time, because I wasn't ready to sleep and the station's heartbeat was easier to bear up close. I put my palm flat against the warm shielding, over the lattice that was quietly becoming glass, and I made myself a promise with a deadline attached.

Two hundred and fourteen days. Less, now. The Meridian had been my whole world for a decade, and in six months it would be a debris field and a memorial, and the only thing that could outlive it was the truth in its belly.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow I'd steal a ship, and drag a stranger's secret into a war, and leave the only home I had to die without me.

The reactor hummed under my hand, faithful and doomed, counting down for both of us.
