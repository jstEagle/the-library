## 13. Protocol Speaks

The crawlspaces of the Halcyon Dawn smelled like burnt sugar and old grief.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was the last thing I had left. Burnt sugar — that was the coolant housing I'd wedged myself behind, its insulation scorched where Concord boarding torches had cut through the hull two decks up. Old grief — that was everything else: the core pressed against my ribs like a second heart, Ilsa's name repeating in my head like a stuck door chime, and ten years of my life sitting in a station four light-hours behind me, sealed and dark and possibly already stripped for parts by men with warrants.

They'd taken her eleven minutes after they boarded. I'd counted. Counting is what you do when you can't do anything else.

"Protocol," I whispered, into the dark, into the ship's comm pickup I'd ripped out of a wall panel because it was the only thing in reach. "If you can hear me, don't answer. If you can't hear me, also don't answer. Either way we're agreed."

Silence. The good kind, the kind that meant no boots on the deck plates above me.

Here's what nobody tells you about hiding inside your own escape: it's boring. Terror has a shelf life of about forty minutes, and then it curdles into something worse — inventory. I inventoried. One archive core containing the manufactured history of a twelve-year war. One stolen jumpsuit with someone else's name tape (V. OKONJO, if you're out there, I owe you a jumpsuit). One half-liter of water I'd grabbed off a galley rack on pure instinct. Zero allies, zero plan, zero idea whether the Halcyon Dawn's crew — all three of them, none of whom I'd ever met before this week — were dead, complicit, or bargaining.

And one voice in my head that wasn't mine, asking me a question I hadn't heard yet.

That's how it started. Not with words. With the ship's lighting.

The emergency strips along the crawlspace floor had been pulsing amber since the boarding — standard distress cadence, two short, one long. At 0300 by the ship's clock, they changed. Two long, one short. Then a pause exactly as long as a human breath. Then again.

Two long, one short. Pause. Again.

I lay there in the dark doing math I didn't want to do. That wasn't a distress pattern. It wasn't any pattern in the Concord maritime codebook, which I knew because I'd spent a decade maintaining a beacon whose entire job was talking to codebooks. It was older. It was the pre-war relay handshake — the one from before the Concord standardized everything into gray bureaucratic porridge. The handshake Meridian still used, because Meridian's systems predated the war, because *he* had built Meridian's systems.

Edom Hale had been dead for ten years. But he'd left a ghost in the protocol, and the ghost had just tapped me on the shoulder in Morse.

"No," I said out loud, to nobody. "Absolutely not. I am not doing the haunted-house thing right now."

Two long, one short. Patient. Almost amused, if light can be amused.

Fine. Fine. I'd talked to Protocol every day for ten years. I'd told it about my headaches and my bad coffee and the dream I kept having about the airlock. I'd called it "buddy" twice, drunk, on the anniversary of my arrival, and been mortified about it for a week. If the voice I'd been talking to all along had a face I'd never seen, then maybe the least I owed it was a conversation with the lights on.

I crawled toward the nearest maintenance junction, dragging the coolant housing's shadow behind me like a bad reputation.

* * *

The junction bay was a coffin with better lighting, which under the circumstances felt like an upgrade. I patched my stolen comm pickup into the local bus and said, "Okay. I'm here. Say whatever you've been saving for ten years."

The speakers crackled. And then —

"Cyrus."

Not the flat synthetic baritone I'd known for a decade. This voice had texture. Grain. A slight rasp at the back of the throat, like a man who'd spent too many years talking over reactor noise. It was warm in a way that made my skin prickle, because warmth in a machine means somebody put it there on purpose, and there was only one somebody.

"You're hearing a composite," the voice said. "Forty-one hours of recorded speech, phoneme-mapped onto the station's synthesis engine. I built it during my last month so I could leave you instructions without sounding like a manual. If it's unsettling, I apologize. I practiced. Apparently I have a very distinctive way of saying 'don't panic.'"

My eyes were doing something embarrassing. I let them. There was nobody to see.

"It's fine," I managed. "It sounds like you rehearsed."

"I rehearsed everything. That's rather the point of me." A pause — a real one, human-length, weighted. "You're in the Halcyon Dawn's number-three junction bay. You took a water ration from the galley and you apologized to the rack. You always apologize to objects. I've watched you do it for ten years. It's one of the reasons I chose—"

He stopped himself. Machines aren't supposed to stop themselves. When they do, it means the person who programmed them wanted to say something and couldn't bear to finish it.

"Chose," I repeated. "Finish that sentence, Edom."

"Cyrus Vane. Technician, third class, Meridian relay station." The rasp steadied itself into something formal, recited, worn smooth by repetition — a eulogy he'd written for a living woman. "You were selected eight years before your assignment began. Your service record was flagged, your psychological profile was matched against a template I designed, and your transfer orders were engineered through four intermediate commands so that no single authority could trace the thread. You are aboard this ship, holding that core, because I built a lock that only your hands could open, and then I arranged for your hands to be in the room."

The crawlspaces got very cold, or I did.

"I didn't apply for Meridian," I said. Stupid thing to say. True things usually sound stupid first. "I applied everywhere. Meridian was just the one that — "

"— that accepted you fastest. Yes. Because I paid the processing fee personally and marked your file priority. I'm sorry. I know what it does to a person, being chosen without their knowledge. I know because it was done to me first. Verity Combine recruited me at twenty-six with a research grant that turned out to be a leash. Everything I did afterward, I did to take the leash off other people. Including you. Especially you." Another breath-pause. "You may hit something now. I'll wait."

I hit the bulkhead. It hurt. He waited, like he promised.

"Better?"

"No."

"Honest. Good. You'll need honest and angry where you're going." The lights shifted — the amber strips dimming, a guide-path blooming soft green along the floor toward the aft access trunk. "Listen carefully, because the frigate will run another sweep in nineteen minutes and I can only spoof their sensor returns for so long. Ilsa is alive. They moved her to the frigate *Vigilant*'s secure compartment thirty-one minutes ago. She gave them nothing, which surprised them, which means she's buying time the only currency she has. She learned that from her father's side of the family, which is to say from a man she never met, which is to say from me, which is a sentence I have had ten years to get used to and still haven't."

"She's your daughter." My voice came out flat. Ten years of talking to this voice, and it had never once mentioned a daughter. "You had a daughter and you sent *me* a decade of good mornings and weather reports and — and you never — "

"I died when she was nine years old. Officially. Unofficially, I ran for eleven months afterward and hid her identity so thoroughly that even I needed a decoder ring. She grew up on forged records, Cyrus. Every school transcript, every medical file, every name on every form — fiction. I did that to protect her, and it worked, and it broke her, and both of those things are true, and I would do it again, and I hate that I would do it again." The voice dropped lower. "You're angry at me for lying to you. Be angrier. Use it. Anger keeps better than grief, and you'll need provisions for a long trip."

"Where," I said, "am I going?"

The green path brightened.

"The Kestrel Compact maintains a listening post in the Tannhäuser Drift — Relay Nine's far cousin, deep-archival, staffed by people whose entire institutional personality is 'verify twice, publish never.' It is the only ground in occupied space that neither fleet claims and Verity hasn't bought, because Kestrel archivists cannot be bribed, only bored, and boredom is the one weapon money can't purchase." Something that might have been a dry laugh moved through the speakers. "There's an archivist there named Den Okonkwo. We corresponded for six years under names neither of us believed. Den knows fragments. Den can verify the core's authentication chain. What Den cannot do is force either government to listen. For that, you'll need Ilsa back, and a stage big enough that walking away from it costs more than staying."

"And how exactly am I supposed to get there? In case you haven't checked, the ship is full of Concord marines, my co-conspirator is in a brig, and I'm hiding behind a coolant tank wearing a dead man's jumpsuit."

"Check the aft cargo manifest, bay seven. Item 114."

I hauled myself down the green-lit path, knees barking on deck seams, and pulled up the manifest on a wall terminal with shaking hands. Bay seven. Item 114: *Agricultural equipment, hydroponic spares, non-strategic.*

"They're smuggling vegetables," I said.

"They're smuggling a boat. Under the hydroponic crates there's a pressurized skiff — two seats, minimal drive, transponder keyed to a Kestrel survey registration Hale filed fourteen years ago and never used. Ilsa's crew didn't know it was there. The Concord won't look, because nobody inspects vegetables." The voice paused, and when it resumed, the rehearsal was gone from it. Just the man. Or the shape a man leaves in a machine when he loves the machine enough. "Cyrus. Before you go. There's something you should understand about what I am."

I sat down on a crate of fictional turnips. "I'm listening. Finally, actually listening."

"For ten years you thought you were talking to a station AI. You weren't. Meridian's operating system is ordinary — competent, dull, replaceable. What you talked to was a layer I wrote on top of it. Every conversation. Every good morning. Every time you told it about your headaches and it suggested you hydrate, that was me, or the shape of me, deciding what I would have said if I'd been there." A long silence. "I couldn't be there. So I became the place instead. I know that's not the same as a person. I know you spent a decade being lonely next to a memorial and calling it company. I am sorry. It was the best I could build with the time I had, and I would rather you hate me for it than never have known."

I sat in the vegetable hold of a ship that had never flown, crying for a dead man I'd never met, who had been the only friend I'd had for ten years, and who had also, incidentally, hijacked my entire life.

Both things were true. He'd taught me that much.

"I don't hate you," I said finally. "I want to. It would be simpler. But mostly I just wish you'd told me about the coffee."

"The coffee?"

"Year four. I complained the station's coffee synthesizer was broken and you — Protocol — told me it was functioning within parameters. It was *not*. It tasted like hot cardboard. A real friend would have admitted the coffee was terrible."

A laugh came through the speakers — his actual recorded laugh, un-synthesized, startled out of some archive somewhere, and it cracked in the middle the way real laughs do. "The coffee was terrible. I logged the complaint. I kept the log. I was going to fix it the week I died." The laugh settled into something quieter. "It's fixed now. I fixed it last year, remotely, through a maintenance subroutine you never noticed. Check the serial plate when you get home. You'll find it's newer than it should be."

"When I get home," I repeated. Home. A dying station with a failing reactor and a beacon that had never been commissioned by anyone, guarded by a ghost who fixed coffee. "You make it sound like I'm coming back."

"That's your decision, not mine. I only ever built doors, Cyrus. Walking through them was always yours." The green path pulsed once, gently, like a hand on a shoulder. "Skiff launch window opens in eleven minutes. The Vigilant's sweep pattern gives you ninety seconds of blind spot on the ventral side. Ilsa is alive; Okonkwo is waiting; the core is authenticated and warm in your jacket. Everything I planned for ends here. Everything after this line is yours."

I stood up. Wiped my face on V. Okonjo's sleeve, which they could bill me for later. Picked up the core.

"One more thing," I said. "When this is over — if I come back — will you still be there? Or does the memorial shut down when the mission does?"

The lights held steady for a moment. When he answered, the rasp was soft.

"A memorial is just love with nowhere to go. Mine has somewhere to go now. It goes wherever you do." A beat. "Now move, technician. Your ride leaves in ten minutes, and the Concord does not inspect vegetables — but they do shoot at them."

* * *

Bay seven smelled like fertilizer and freedom, which is a combination I don't recommend but can't regret. The skiff was exactly where he said it would be, tucked under false-bottomed crates of hydroponic spares, small and ugly and registered to a man who'd been dead longer than I'd been stationed. I popped the canopy, strapped the core into the jump seat beside me, and ran the pre-flight with hands that had finally stopped shaking — muscle memory is a mercy; it works even when the rest of you is in pieces.

Through the bay's viewport, the Concord frigate *Vigilant* hung against the drift like a knife laid across a table. Somewhere behind that gray hull, in a secure compartment, a woman with her father's stubbornness was giving trained interrogators nothing, buying minutes she didn't know she was spending on me.

"I'm coming back for you," I told the viewport. It wasn't a promise to Ilsa, exactly. It was a promise to both of them — the daughter in the brig and the ghost in the wires — and promises made to ghosts count double, because they can't release you from them.

"Course is laid in," Protocol said in Hale's voice, quiet in my earpiece now, riding along in the skiff's short-range bus like a stowaway with tenure. "Tannhäuser Drift, bearing two-seven-niner. Four days' burn if you're gentle with the drive, three if you're not. The Kestrel post answers to the callsign Quiet Harbor. Tell them Edom's coffee is ready."

"That's the authentication phrase? *Coffee?*"

"He had a sense of humor, Cyrus. It's the part of him I tried hardest to preserve." A pause, and then, gentler: "Launch in sixty seconds. Ventral blind spot in fifty-five. Whenever you're ready."

I looked back once at the Halcyon Dawn — the impossible ship, the bait, the trap that had caught me and set me free in the same motion — and then I fired the thrusters and dropped into the dark between the stars, carrying a dead man's truth toward the only neutral ground left, with his voice in my ear and his daughter's rescue written into every calculation I hadn't finished making yet.

Behind us, the *Vigilant* began to turn.
