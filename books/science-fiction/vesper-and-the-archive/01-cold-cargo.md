## 1. Cold Cargo

The checkpoint pinged me at 0400 station time, which was rude of it, because I'd been awake since 0330 and had been counting on at least another hour of pretending the universe didn't exist.

"Freighter *Kestrel's Regret*, this is Fleet Checkpoint Nine. Transmit manifest and prepare for inspection."

I let the request sit in the queue for a full minute while I drank my coffee. This was a tactical decision. Fleet inspectors were like dogs: they could smell eagerness, and they got suspicious of anyone who answered too fast. A slow response said *tired contractor, barely solvent, please don't make my morning worse than it already is*, which happened to be true, and truth was the best camouflage I owned.

"*Kestrel's Regret*," I finally sent back, "manifest transmitting. Cargo: agricultural supplies, forty-one crates, destination Vesper Seed Vault, contract number VC-1147-C. I've got a schedule and a bad attitude, so let's keep this quick."

The last part wasn't strictly necessary, but I'd learned that a little personality made the inspectors file you under *harmless crank* instead of *possible smuggler*. Harmless cranks got waved through. Possible smugglers got their cargo holds opened in vacuum-cold air while teenagers with rifles counted my spare parts.

The manifest was, as always, ninety percent honest. Forty-one crates of agricultural supplies — nutrient substrate, growth-lamp filaments, a replacement pump for the irrigation loop. The other ten percent was a crate of coffee that existed on no official record and would never appear on one, because the Vault's climate control ran cold enough to freeze the pipes and a person needed something to stay human out here. If some inspector wanted to charge me with smuggling stimulants, they were welcome to explain to their commanding officer why Fleet resources had been spent intercepting one woman's caffeine.

"Manifest received," the checkpoint said. "Hold position for scan."

"Holding," I said, and held, and thought about seeds.

That's most of what I did out here, if I'm honest. Thought about seeds. Hauled them, catalogued them, worried about them. Twenty years ago the Earth Fleet had handed me a maintenance contract for the Vesper Seed Vault — a great buried ark of genetic material orbiting in the dead zone between Fleet space and Compact space, built by somebody, funded by nobody, important to everyone in the abstract and to no one in particular. The Vault predated both governments. That was the official line, anyway, delivered in the same bored tone officials used for everything: *the Vault predates both governments, its provenance is under review, your contract is to maintain environmental systems and do not ask questions.*

I'd stopped asking questions around year three. Questions got contracts revoked, and revoked contracts got you dropped into contested orbit without a ship, and without a ship out here you were a corpse with delusions.

The scan swept over me — I felt it as a faint static itch against the hull, the Fleet's way of reminding everyone whose sky this was — and then the checkpoint came back with the verdict.

"You're clean, *Regret*. Proceed on vector seven-niner. And hey — you're the vault lady, right? Hartwell?"

"That's me."

"My grandmother used to talk about that place. Said her mother worked there before the war." A pause, the kind where someone decides whether to say the thing they're thinking. "Anyway. Safe flight."

He cut the channel before I could answer, which was fine, because I didn't have an answer. People occasionally did that — surfaced some half-remembered connection to the Vault, some aunt or grandfather who'd worked the seed decks before everything went to hell — and then looked embarrassed, like they'd mentioned a relative who'd died in disgrace. Nobody talked about the Vault's people. Nobody talked about the Vault at all, really, except as a coordinate to avoid or a resource to fight over someday, when the war got hungry enough.

I fed the thrusters a gentle burn and slid past Checkpoint Nine into the long dark between stations.

* * *

The Vault hung in my viewport two hours later, and I'll tell you the truth: after twenty years, it still made my chest do something complicated.

It wasn't beautiful, exactly. Beauty implies decoration, and the Vault had none. It was a kilometer of dark alloy shaped like a seed itself — a teardrop, blunt end forward, tapered tail trailing solar collectors like a comet's ghost. No running lights. No transponder beacon beyond the minimum required by a treaty neither side honored anymore. From a distance it looked like debris, like something already dead. Up close, you could see the seams where a million seed canisters slotted into the hull like cells in a honeycomb, each one holding the frozen germplasm of a world that mostly didn't exist anymore.

Earth's crops. Earth's forests. Earth's weeds, even — we kept the weeds, because the archivists who built this place had understood something the governments who inherited it never did: you don't get to choose what the future needs. You just save everything and hope.

"Morning, VESPER-9," I said as I docked, because talking to the old security AI was either a sign of loneliness or good discipline, and I'd decided twenty years ago not to examine which.

"Good morning, Caretaker Hartwell," the AI replied, in a voice like a librarian who'd been asked to be friendly once, in 1970, and was still working from those notes. "Your last visit was nineteen days, four hours ago. Environmental systems report nominal. Canister integrity holds at ninety-six point two percent."

"Down from ninety-six point four."

"Yes."

"We'll fix it."

"I have logged your intention," VESPER-9 said, "as I have logged your previous eleven thousand intentions."

There it was. Twenty years of conversations with a machine, and every one of them contained that exact note of gentle, archival despair. I loved the old thing the way you love a grumpy grandparent — which is to say I suspected it loved me too but would rather die than say so, and possibly literally could not.

I suited up and went outside, because the day's work was on the hull: canister row 44-G, where the integrity numbers had been sliding, and the only way to find a microfracture in vacuum-welded alloy was to put your gloved hands on the metal and feel for the cold spots where the insulation had given up. It was slow work. It was the kind of work that ate a whole day and gave you nothing to show for it but a chalk mark and a repair order nobody would ever read.

I loved it. That's the embarrassing confession at the center of my life. I loved the work because the work didn't lie to me. A fracture was a fracture. A failing seal failed. Out here, things broke for honest reasons, and you fixed them or you watched them die, and either way you knew where you stood. It was only the human parts of the universe — the Fleets, the Compacts, the histories, the wars — that ran on fiction.

Row 44-G, canister nine. There. My palm found it before my instruments did: a thread of cold bleeding through the glove, thin as a hair. Microfracture, maybe six centimeters, weeping atmosphere at a rate that would take a decade to matter and mattered anyway, because the whole point of this place was decades. Centuries. The archivists hadn't built the Vault to survive the war. They'd built it to survive whatever came after the war, when somebody, someday, would need to grow food on burned ground.

I sealed the fracture with a patch kit older than I was, chalked the canister, and moved down the row, and somewhere around canister fourteen I caught myself narrating the work aloud to nobody, which was new, or maybe not new, maybe I'd been doing it for years and had only now run out of the energy to pretend I wasn't.

"—and that's why you torque the clamp before you heat it, because heat first means warped flange, and a warped flange means—" I stopped. Listened to my own voice echoing inside the helmet. Laughed, briefly, at a sound that wasn't quite a laugh.

Twenty years. Two decades of hauling cargo through a shooting war's no-man's-orbit, cataloguing seeds for a future that both governments claimed to represent and both intended to own. No family. No crew. No port that knew my name except as a contract number. I had a ship named after regret, an AI who despaired of me gently, and eleven thousand logged intentions.

And the thing about routine — the thing nobody tells you — is that it works. Loneliness disguised as routine stays functional for years. You stop noticing the disguise. You wave at checkpoints, you patch fractures, you drink contraband coffee alone in a cold cockpit, and you call it a life, and some nights it almost is.

Almost.

* * *

I was halfway through the interior rounds — humidity checks in the germination bays, all of them dark and dormant, waiting for a war to end so someone could plant something — when the general distress band lit up.

Not a Fleet channel. Not Compact. The old civilian emergency frequency, the one from before the war, the one every ship was legally required to monitor and every ship had quietly stopped monitoring because it had been silent for forty years.

The signal was weak, degraded, stuttering. But the identifier attached to it came through clean enough, and I sat down slowly in the germination bay's dust because my legs had opinions about standing.

*MV Meridian. MV Meridian. Distress. Life support critical. Crew complement—*

The *Meridian*.

Everyone in this system knew the story, the way everyone knows a ghost story. The *Meridian* had been a deep-space survey vessel, launched by the joint science authority back when Fleet and Compact still pretended to share a sky. Forty years ago — almost to the month — she'd gone out past the system's edge to chart the deep dark, and she'd never come back. No wreckage. No distress call. Just gone, swallowed, and both governments had spent a decade accusing each other of shooting her down before settling into the comfortable arrangement of blaming each other for everything else instead.

Forty years. Her crew would be dead or ancient. Her hull would be scrap.

And she was pinging a distress call on a dead frequency, from somewhere near the system's edge, with a life-support signature that the automated decode flagged as *active*.

VESPER-9's voice came through my suit comm, careful as always. "Caretaker Hartwell. You are receiving the broadcast as well. Shall I log it?"

"Log it," I said. "Don't report it."

A pause. For an AI, VESPER-9's pauses were eloquent. "You are aware that protocol requires reporting all distress signals to the nearest Fleet authority."

"I'm aware that the nearest Fleet authority is Checkpoint Nine, and that whoever answers that signal gets boarded, seized, and disappeared into a debriefing room, and that the *Meridian*'s crew — if there is any crew — deserves better than becoming a classified asset." I was already moving toward the docking ring, heart going faster than it had any right to. "Besides. Protocol also says a contracted caretaker maintains the Vault. Nobody says anything about ignoring forty-year-old ghosts."

"You are rationalizing," VESPER-9 observed.

"I'm excellent at it. It's one of my core competencies. Log it under intentions."

I fired up the *Kestrel's Regret* and burned for the system's edge, chasing a ghost, telling myself it was a detour, a rounding error, a small humane errand on the way back to routine.

Later — much later — I would understand that nothing about that day was a detour. That the distress call was the hinge my whole quiet, hidden, carefully disguised life swung on. But sitting in the cockpit with the coffee going cold beside me and the dead frequency crackling in my ears, all I knew was this: something lost had come back, and every instinct I had — the ones I fed and the ones I starved — said *go*.

So I went.
