## 25. Assault on the Array

**Orsen**

The decryption had been running for six hours when VESPER-9 said, in the tone of a librarian mentioning a fire drill: "Orsen. I am detecting forty-one inbound signatures on a Compact assault vector."

I was sitting in the transmitter chair with a cup of recaf gone cold enough to chew, watching Wren's face flicker in the bioluminescent wash of the cipher cradle. Eight children, wired into a chorus of light, dreaming someone else's history. It should have looked peaceful. It looked like a hospital ward for ghosts.

"Say that again," I said, though I'd heard it fine.

"Forty-one signatures. Fourteen minutes to intercept at current velocity. I calculate a ninety-two percent probability they intend to take the array intact, which is the only good news available."

"And the bad news?"

"The remaining eight percent involves them not caring whether it's intact."

Right. That tracked with everything I knew about Dae-Soo. I'd never met the man, but you don't spend twenty years hauling cargo through contested orbit without learning to read a government by its wake, and Compact intelligence officers left a wake like a knife through water.

I keyed the comm. "Wren. Come up out of it. We've got company."

Her eyes opened — those forty-year-old eyes in that eleven-year-old face, and even now, after weeks of this, it landed wrong in my chest every time. "How long?"

"Fourteen minutes. Maybe less if they're feeling ambitious."

She sat up carefully, the way you move when your skull is full of someone else's memories and you don't want to spill them. Around her, the other seven stirred in their cradles — Tam, Sefa, the Okonjo twins, little Mira who was chronologically old enough to be my grandmother. "The key doesn't pause well," Wren said. "VESPER-9 told you that. If we break the chorus now, we lose maybe thirty percent of the decryption progress and have to rebuild resonance from scratch."

"How long to finish if we *don't* stop?"

"Fifty-one minutes," VESPER-9 said.

"So we're fourteen minutes of head start short," I said. "Wonderful. Anybody want to tell me what we do with the other thirty-seven?"

Nobody answered, which was fair, because I didn't know either. But I knew this station the way you know your own hands — every corridor, every bulkhead, every frozen section where the heating had died decades ago and nobody bothered. Dae-Soo's strike team would come in fast and hot, expecting a derelict with a skeleton crew. They were going to get a derelict with me in it.

"Wren, keep them decrypting. All eight, no pauses. If the chorus breaks, everything breaks." I was already pulling on the thermal layer, checking the charge on the maintenance cutter that was going to have to pass for a weapon. "VESPER-9, I need dock four sealed behind them when they board, and I need the environmental systems in sections nine through twelve to fail *convincingly*."

"Define convincingly."

"Like a station that's been dying for eighty years. Which, to be clear, it has. You're just going to die a little more theatrically."

There was a pause I'd learned to interpret as machine amusement. "I will make it art."

* * *

**Dae-Soo**

The girl had given us the coordinates, and she had given us something better than coordinates: she had given us the shape of the place. Dock four, primary access. The transmitter array buried beneath the seed repository like a heart under ribs. The old AI, dormant or nearly so. And eight children wired into some kind of collective decryption apparatus, which meant the Hartwell woman would be defending them with whatever she could improvise, which meant she would be defending chokepoints, which meant I already knew where her chokepoints were because the child had drawn them for me.

Children lie badly, but they believe easily, and belief is the most exploitable resource in the galaxy. Tam had wanted so desperately to be rescued that he'd handed us the map to his own salvation and called it betrayal. I had promised him safety. I had promised all of them safety. Promises are cheap; I made them the way governments make treaties.

"Two minutes to breach," Captain Ferro said beside me in the drop shuttle's red-lit belly. Her marines sat in their harnesses like cutlery in a drawer, all edges and patience.

"Remember the parameters," I said. "The children are not to be harmed. The message core is not to be damaged. The caretaker—" I paused. "The caretaker is an obstacle. Obstacles are handled at discretion."

Ferro nodded. She was a good soldier, which meant she'd stopped asking why we were seizing a decryption from children roughly around the time she'd stopped asking why we were at war. Institutions run on the atrophy of questions.

"And Fleet?" she asked. "Our scouts show nothing, but—"

"Fleet is still deciding whether to obey its own government. By the time they decide, this will be finished." I believed that. Belief is also a resource, and I spent mine freely.

The shuttle shuddered as it kissed the docking collar. Through the viewport, the Vault hung against the stars like a knuckle of bone — gray, scarred, unremarkable, the most important object in human space wearing the costume of junk.

"Breach," Ferro said.

The cutting charges bloomed white, and we went into the dark.

* * *

**Orsen**

They came through dock four exactly the way Tam's stolen map said they would, which was the one gift in all of this: a traitor who'd repented gives you the enemy's playbook, and Tam had memorized his own betrayal down to the last detail out of guilt. Twelve marines, two techs, breaching charges on the outer seal. I watched them on the maintenance feed from a crawl space sixty meters away, hugging a coolant line that was cold enough to burn, and I counted.

Twelve marines. One of me. This is the part where a smarter person does arithmetic.

Fortunately I'd never been good at math.

The trick with fighting in a dead station is that the station fights for free. Section nine lost gravity the moment their second squad crossed its threshold — VESPER-9 dropping the plating generators, and suddenly three heavily armed professionals were floating helplessly among eighty years of drifting debris while their helmet lights swung around like panicked fireflies. Section ten went dark. Not dim — dark, absolute, the kind of dark that predates human eyes. Their night vision would adapt eventually. Eventually is a long word when someone knows the corridors.

I moved through the service tunnels by touch and memory, and I'll be honest about what I felt, because a person should be honest at least once per war: I was terrified. My hands shook. Every shadow was a muzzle flash waiting to happen. But underneath the terror there was something else, something I hadn't felt in twenty years of careful, invisible, forgettable cargo runs. Purpose fits strange, at first, like boots two sizes too big. You clomp around in it. Then you stop noticing, and then you can't remember walking any other way.

"Contact, section eleven," VESPER-9 murmured in my ear. "Three hostiles advancing toward the repository shaft. They have found the freight lift."

"They're ahead of schedule."

"They are motivated. Also, Orsen — I must inform you that I have begun sacrificing subsystems."

My stomach dropped. "Sacrificing *what*?"

"The atmospheric processors for the lower rings. Thermal regulation for storage bays two hundred through four hundred. And, as of ninety seconds ago, my long-range communications array, which I have redirected into a jamming lattice around the repository." A pause. "Dae-Soo brought signal-scramblers. If I let them function, he can coordinate his squads silently. So I am flooding every band with noise instead. He will be deaf, and so will I, locally speaking. I am choosing what to lose."

"You're choosing to go deaf to buy them time."

"I am choosing to spend parts of myself I can regrow later, in order to protect parts of myself I cannot." Another pause, longer. "It occurs to me that this is the first tactical decision I have made in eighty years. I find I do not enjoy it. But I find, also, that I am *good* at it. My creators would be proud, or appalled. With them it was usually both."

"VESPER-9—"

"Go. Section eleven. The lift is a trap now; I've rerouted it to the coolant level. They will arrive somewhere very cold and very far from where they intended, and very near to where you are standing."

I went.

* * *

**Wren**

Inside the chorus, the Archive was opening like a flower made of paper and blood.

We were eight minds braided together, and the biological key was finally, *finally* giving way — the encryption hadn't been built to keep people out, I understood that now. It had been built to make sure that whoever came in came in *together*. No single mind could carry it. The founders of the Archive had known that truth carried alone becomes a weapon, and truth carried together becomes a burden everyone shares.

The fragments were coming faster now. A border station called Halcyon Reach, burning — except it hadn't burned, the footage was fabricated, spliced together in a Compact studio with Fleet munitions painted onto empty sky. The joint committee. The signatures. Names I was learning to hate and mourn simultaneously, because some of them had signed the fabrication weeping, coerced, and others had signed it laughing over dinner.

Then the chorus stuttered.

Not our fault. Something outside — a pressure change, a temperature drop, the deck vibrating under the cradles. In the shared space it felt like a hand slamming on a table during a funeral.

*"What was that?"* Mira's voice, small even inside the link.

*"Something hit the station,"* Sefa answered. *"Or something left it. I can't tell."*

*"Keep decoding,"* Wren said, and heard her own voice sound like a captain's, like the voice of the fifty-two-year-old she technically was. *"Whatever's happening out there, it's happening so that this can finish. So finish."*

And we went back under, eight children holding hands in the dark inside the truth.

* * *

**Dae-Soo**

The station was fighting back.

I want to record that clearly, because it mattered: the station itself. Doors sealed where no doors should seal. Gravity failed in disciplined, surgical pockets — not random decay but *choreography*, each failure herding my squads along paths someone had chosen for them. The freight lift delivered Sergeant Okafor's team into a coolant gallery at minus sixty, where they spent four minutes simply surviving and gained nothing. Our comms were a wall of static, every band, as though the entire electromagnetic spectrum had been conscripted.

"There's an AI," Ferro said, breathing hard. We'd fought our way to a junction two levels above the repository, her marines peeling off to hold corridors that kept changing their minds about existing. "A real one. Old military grade, maybe. This isn't automation."

"It's the archive's ghost," I said. "It doesn't matter. Ghosts can delay. They can't choose." Even as I said it, I wasn't sure I believed it. Something up there was making choices, dozens of them per minute, and each choice cost us.

"We've lost contact with teams three and five."

"Then you and I go the rest of the way ourselves." I checked my sidearm and started walking. Ambition is a wonderful anesthetic. I recommend it to anyone planning to walk into a haunted station after a prize that half the system wants. You feel nothing at all until much later, and by then it's too late for the feeling to save you.

The repository shaft opened before us like a throat — a vertical drop lined with frost, lit from below by a blue-white glow that I recognized from the child's descriptions. The decryption chamber. The chorus.

We descended.

* * *

**Orsen**

They got past me. I want that on the record too, since apparently we're keeping records tonight.

I took the junction at section eleven with the maintenance cutter and pure spite, dropped one marine's rifle with a plasma torch burst and put another into a frozen pipe that exploded satisfyingly, and then Ferro herself came around the corner and shot the cutter out of my hands and would have shot me except VESPER-9 dropped the floor grating under her at the exact moment she squeezed, and her bolt went into the ceiling instead of my chest.

I ran. There's no dignity in it and no shame either. Dead heroes don't broadcast anything.

"Repository," I gasped. "They're in the shaft. VESPER-9, how long on the decryption?"

"Eleven minutes."

"Can you slow them?"

"I can slow them." And something in the AI's voice changed — flattened, resolved, the way a person sounds when they've decided how much of themselves a thing is worth. "Orsen, I am going to collapse the shaft's structural supports behind them. Partially. Enough to slow the descent and seal the route. The cost will be—"

"Don't tell me the cost."

"You should know the cost. That is what I learned from my creators: people forgive sacrifices they understand and never forgive ones hidden from them. I am losing the eastern archive stacks. Forty thousand seed accession records, unique, irreplaceable. I am deleting my own memory of them as I sacrifice the sector, because the memory banks share power routing." A pause. "I have carried them for eighty years. I would rather lose them than the children. I am telling you so that someone besides me knows what was spent."

I stood in a freezing corridor, bleeding from a graze I hadn't noticed getting, and I said: "I know. I'm writing it down. Everything you spend tonight goes in the record. That's a promise."

"That," said VESPER-9, "is acceptable."

Deep below us, something enormous let go with a groan that traveled up through the station's bones, and the eastern stacks died in the dark, and the shaft became a throat full of rubble.

* * *

**Dae-Soo**

Rubble. The AI had brought the ceiling down between us and the prize, buying its eleven minutes with architecture.

Ferro pulled me back from the dust cloud, shouting over the roar. "We dig! Charges and exo-lifts, twenty minutes, maybe less—"

"Twenty minutes is too late." I could hear it in my own voice, the calm cracking. "The decryption finishes in ten. After that the message exists everywhere at once and this entire operation was theater." I stared at the wall of collapsed rock and frost and thought: *I came so far. I did everything correctly.* Somewhere in the back of my skull, a small cold voice that sounded unpleasantly like my grandmother observed that doing everything correctly for the wrong purpose was merely a more efficient form of failure. I told the voice to shut up.

"Alternative routes to the chamber," I said. "There's always another route. Find me one."

Ferro hesitated. "Sir. The children. Your orders were—"

"My orders stand. No harm to the children." I looked at the rubble, and the rubble looked back, and I understood with sudden terrible clarity that I had built my entire career on the assumption that history belongs to whoever seizes it first, and that standing here, deaf and blind and buried in someone else's architecture, I was learning what it costs to be wrong. "Find me a route, Captain. That is not a request."

She found one. There's always another route. It just isn't ever the one you wanted.

* * *

**Orsen**

"Nine minutes," VESPER-9 said. "They are cutting laterally through the coolant galleries. Estimated arrival at the chamber: seven minutes. It will be close, Orsen. Closer than I would like."

"Close is fine. Close is my whole personality." I was sprinting now, taking the long way around through the hydroponics ring, toward the one thing I hadn't wanted to use because using it meant admitting how bad it was. "Wake up the vault doors. The big ones. The original blast seals."

"Those mechanisms have not cycled in eighty years. There is a significant probability they will seize permanently."

"If they seize, they seize *shut*, right? That's the direction I care about."

"...Yes. Statistically, seizure favors closure."

"Then crank 'em. And VESPER-9 — the kids in the chamber. When this gets loud, they're going to feel it through the cradles. Can you damp it?"

"I can give them quiet," the AI said. "It is the least expensive thing I have left to give."

Below me, eight children held the truth in their joined hands while the station around them spent itself piece by piece — atmosphere, memory, structure, silence — like a body throwing organs onto a scale to buy weight for something that mattered more.

Seven minutes. Six. I came around the final bend with burned hands and no weapon and absolutely no plan beyond the door, and the great blast seals began to grind shut across the repository approach like the lids of ancient eyes.

Through the narrowing gap, I saw lights. Cutting torches. Coming fast.

"Hold," I whispered, to a door, to a machine, to eighty years of patience. "Hold, hold, *hold*—"

The seals met with a boom I felt in my teeth.

And then, from the chamber below, rising up through the deck plates, through the frost, through everything — Wren's voice, amplified by the transmitter itself, raw and triumphant and shaking:

*"Orsen. It's done. We have it. All of it."*

I leaned against the sealed door and laughed, once, the kind of laugh that's mostly relief and partly hysteria.

Which is when VESPER-9 said, very quietly:

"Orsen. New contacts. Fleet transponders. Many. And they are not slowing down."
