## 28. Volunteers

I've spent most of my life doing math I didn't like. Cargo mass against fuel burn. Air scrubber lifespan against crew count. The odds of clearing a Fleet checkpoint with a forged manifest against the odds of not clearing it and spending the rest of my short life in a reeducation facility. Math is honest that way. It doesn't care how you feel about the answer.

So when Wren laid out the problem in the transmitter room — the biological key destabilizing, the reversal process already killing them by millimeters a day, full decryption accelerating it fatally for whoever served as the living cipher — I did what I always do. I started running numbers.

One cipher. One death. Eight children with fifty-year-old eyes standing in front of me, and every single one of them had their hand up before I'd finished translating what VESPER-9 was saying.

* * *

It started, as these things do, with a machine being gentle, which is somehow worse than a machine being cruel.

"The key degradation has reached a threshold," VESPER-9 said, its voice coming through the old intercom with a faint hum underneath, like a hymn sung through a wall. "The neural patterns of the reversed crew are the only viable decryption substrate. However, sustained engagement with the Archive's core cipher will accelerate pattern collapse. I estimate complete neurological failure within six hours of continuous use."

"Meaning what, exactly?" I asked, because I've learned you make machines say the ugly part out loud or they'll let you pretend it means something else.

"Meaning," VESPER-9 said, "that whoever serves as the primary cipher will die."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when everyone already knew and nobody wanted to be the one who said it. The transmitter room is deep in the Vault's bones, all cable conduits and pre-war alloy, cold enough that your breath shows if you breathe hard enough. Eight kids stood in a half circle around the decryption cradle — the thing VESPER-9 had rigged from an old medical suite and enough salvaged hardware to make my teeth ache looking at it. Wren at the front, because Wren was always at the front. Tam hovering at the edge, still carrying the guilt of his betrayal like a stone sewn into his jacket. Priya, Marcus, the twins Ade and Adele — no relation, they'd told me once, laughing, just born on the same station forty-some years ago — and old Halvorsen, who was chronologically the oldest of them at sixty-one and physically maybe twelve, and who had the particular patience of someone who'd already died once, in a sense, and hadn't cared for it.

Wren raised her hand first. Of course she did.

"I'll do it," she said. "I'm the mission lead. It should be me."

"You're eleven," I said.

"I'm fifty-two," she said, in the flat tone of someone correcting a clerical error for the ten-thousandth time. "And the mission was mine before it was yours, Orsen. I carried the message out of the Archive. I'm the one who promised them we'd get it home." She looked around at the others. "All of you are off the list. That's an order."

"You can't order us," said Ade. "You're not our captain anymore. The Meridian's a wreck in a salvage bay."

"I can order you," Wren said, "because someone has to, and I'm the one who remembers how."

And then — this is the part I keep replaying, the part that sits in my chest like a swallowed bolt — they all raised their hands. All seven of the others. Tam last, hand shaking, face blotchy, but up. Halvorsen with the calm of a man signing a form he'd read twice. Priya said something quiet in her family's language, a prayer or a curse or maybe both, and kept her hand up anyway.

Eight volunteers. One chair. One death.

"Do you understand," I said, and my voice came out wrong, scraped, "what you're offering?"

"We understand math, Orsen," Halvorsen said gently, which stung more than it should have, because he was right. They understood math better than anyone alive. They'd lived inside an equation for forty years — time folded wrong, bodies running backward through childhood while their minds ground forward toward old age. They were the only people in the system who truly grasped what it meant to spend a life at a fixed exchange rate.

"We understand," Wren said, "that one of us dies and the war ends and everybody else lives. That's the best trade anyone's offered since the Meridian launched."

* * *

I want to tell you I gave a speech. Something with weight to it, something a person could put in a book someday and feel proud of. I didn't. What I did was walk out of the transmitter room, stand in the freezing corridor for four minutes — I counted, because counting is what I do instead of screaming — and then walk back in and say:

"No."

Wren blinked. "No what?"

"No. Nobody's dying. Pick again."

"There's nothing to pick," she said. "There's one chair and eight of us and the math doesn't—"

"I know what the math says." I planted myself between them and the cradle, which was ridiculous, because they were children and I was a grown woman with a cargo hauler's shoulders, and also because the cradle wasn't going anywhere. "I've been doing ugly math my whole life. Fuel versus distance. Risk versus pay. You know what I learned? The math is only ever right when you accept its terms. And I don't accept these terms."

"Orsen—" Tam started.

"This isn't a negotiation," I said. "This is me telling you how it's going to be. Nobody climbs into that chair alone. Not you, Wren. Not you, Tam, especially not you — you've spent three weeks trying to buy back one bad night, and martyrdom is just another way of hiding. I see you. Don't think I don't."

Tam's face did something complicated. He didn't lower his hand.

"Halvorsen. You've earned rest more than anyone, and that's exactly why you don't get to spend it on this. Priya, Ade, Adele, Marcus — you're not soldiers. You're witnesses. There's a difference, and it matters, and I'll tell you why in a minute." I turned back to Wren. "And you — you've been carrying forty years of other people's survival on a spine that hasn't finished growing. You don't get to finish the job by erasing yourself from the ending. I won't allow it."

"You don't have the authority," Wren said, but her voice cracked on the word *allow*, and I saw it land somewhere under all that fifty-two years: the place where a kid, any kid, wants an adult to say no and mean it.

"Maybe not," I said. "But I'm the only adult in the room, so as far as I'm concerned, I outrank everybody."

Halvorsen laughed. It was a small laugh, tired, but real. "She's got you there, Captain."

* * *

Here's the thing about refusing arithmetic: you owe the universe an alternative. Refusing the terms without producing new ones is just sentiment, and sentiment doesn't decrypt anything. So while they argued — and they argued, eight voices with five decades each behind them, filling that cold room with the noise of a faculty meeting crossed with a schoolyard — I pulled VESPER-9's attention aside on a private channel and asked the only question that mattered.

"The key has to run through a neural pattern. Does it have to run through *one*?"

Silence. Machine silence, which lasts a fraction of a second and feels like a held breath.

"Clarify," VESPER-9 said.

"The cipher load. The fatal load. Whatever it is that burns the pattern out — processing time, bandwidth, whatever we're calling it. Is the damage proportional to throughput?" I was thinking about generators, honestly. Twenty years of hauling meant twenty years of overloading systems that were rated for less than I asked of them. Everything fails the same way: ask one component for everything, and it breaks. Spread the draw across a grid, and it holds.

"Theoretically," VESPER-9 said slowly, and I could hear it running the numbers alongside me, "pattern stress scales with data volume per unit neural engagement. If the cipher process were distributed across multiple compatible substrates—"

"Eight compatible substrates," I said. "Same ship, same anomaly, same reversal. They're not eight different keys, they're eight copies of one key, aren't they?"

"They are... variations on a single original pattern," VESPER-9 said. "The reversal affected the crew uniformly. Their neural architectures remain resonant with one another to a degree I have never observed elsewhere." A pause. "Orsen Hartwell, what you are proposing has never been attempted. Distributed biological decryption would require synchronizing eight minds through the cradle simultaneously. The failure modes are—"

"Worse than one dead kid?"

"...Different," VESPER-9 admitted. "If synchronization fails mid-process, the pattern collapse could cascade across all participants."

"So worst case, we're back where we started, except with worse odds." I looked at the eight of them, still arguing, hands still up. "Best case, nobody dies and the truth gets out. I've taken worse bets with less information. I once flew a cargo contract through a micrometeorite swarm because the client paid double and I needed the air recycler parts."

"That is not reassuring," said VESPER-9.

"It's not supposed to be. It's supposed to be honest." I keyed the room channel back open. "Okay. Everybody shut up for thirty seconds. New plan on the table."

They shut up. Eight pairs of too-old eyes on me. I'd have paid money, real money, pre-war money, for that kind of attention from a Fleet review board.

"One chair kills one of you, and I'm not signing off on that, ever, so take it off the table. But here's what I've got instead." I pointed at the cradle. "That thing needs a neural pattern to chew through the Archive's cipher. Fine. You're not one pattern. You're eight resonant copies of one pattern. So we don't feed it one of you. We feed it all of you — split the load, divide the burn, same way you'd parallel a power draw across a grid instead of frying a single line. Each of you carries one-eighth of the fatal load. Maybe it's survivable. Maybe it's not. But it's a bet where the downside is shared instead of concentrated, and I am telling you, as the only person here who's made a living off long odds, that's the only kind of bet worth placing."

Silence again. Different silence. The kind where people are actually thinking instead of bracing.

"Cascade risk," Halvorsen said finally. He'd been listening to VESPER-9's channel, apparently; fifty-two-year-olds multitask. "If one link fails, does it chain?"

"Possibly," VESPER-9 said.

"And if it chains?"

"All eight patterns collapse."

The silence after that had weight. Tam sat down on a cable spool. Ade and Adele looked at each other — no relation, but they might as well have been, the way twins communicate without words.

"Versus certain death for one," Wren said quietly. "And survivor's guilt for seven, forever."

"Versus certain death for one," I agreed. "Look. I'm not selling this as safe. I'm selling it as *shared*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point. One person in that chair dies alone, and the rest of you carry it until you're old — really old, the normal way. Eight people in that chair either live together or fail together, and either way, nobody carries it solo." I shrugged, and it came out rougher than I meant. "I've been alone a long time. Take it from me: it's overrated. Shared trouble is the only kind that stays bearable."

Priya spoke first. "You'd stay linked with us? Through the whole process?"

"Somebody has to hold the manual overrides," I said. "VESPER-9 can't be in the chorus and conduct it at the same time."

"You'd be exposed too," Marcus said. "Side channels, feedback bleed. You're not keyed to the cipher, but the cradle won't know that."

"Then I'll be the ninth voice in the choir, singing off-key." I met Wren's eyes. "Nobody goes in that machine alone. Not even me. That's the term. That's the price of my sign-off."

Wren studied me for a long moment. Eleven years old, fifty-two years deep, and behind those eyes I watched her do the thing she'd been doing since the day I pulled her out of the Meridian: weighing whether the world was safe enough to trust. I'd spent weeks earning a fraction of that trust. I was about to find out how much of it I had.

"If the cascade risk is real," she said, "we need to know before we commit. Full simulation. Every failure mode VESPER-9 can model."

"Agreed," I said.

"And if the simulation says it kills all eight of us?"

"Then we don't do it, and we find a third option, because there's always a third option. It's just usually uglier and takes longer."

Ade snorted. "That's not how math works."

"That's exactly how math works," I said. "Math gives you answers to the questions you ask. Ask it a better question, you get a better answer. You've all been asking 'which one of us dies.' Wrong question. The right question is 'how do we all survive,' and yeah, it's harder, and yeah, the answer might not exist yet. But questions like that are worth staying alive for."

Tam stood up off the spool. His hand wasn't shaking anymore. "I'm in," he said. "Not because I want to die. Because I want to be there when it works."

Something loosened in my chest that I'd been carrying tight since Checkpoint Nine. One by one, the hands came down — not in surrender, but the way hands come down when a vote passes. Halvorsen nodded slowly, like a man approving a contract he'd read twice. Priya murmured her prayer-word again, softer this time. The twins bumped fists. Marcus cracked his knuckles, which at twelve looks theatrical and at fifty-two looks like a habit worn smooth.

Wren came last. She walked over to me, and up close the height difference was almost comical — the top of her head level with my sternum — and she stuck out her hand like a dockworker closing a deal.

"One-eighth each," she said. "Plus whatever you take as override. Nine-way split. Non-negotiable."

"Non-negotiable," I agreed, and shook it. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

* * *

We worked until shipboard-night cycle, which in the Vault means the lights dim to amber and the old heating coils tick as they cycle down. VESPER-9 ran simulations in the background, thousands of them, cascading failure models scrolling up the transmitter room's ancient displays faster than any of us could read. The early numbers were bad. Then less bad. Then, somewhere past midnight by Earth reckoning — a clock none of us had any reason to care about except that Wren insisted on keeping it, "for morale," she said, though whose morale I couldn't say — the AI went quiet for a long moment and then said:

"I have a configuration. Synchronization stability at ninety-one percent across ten thousand trials. Projected pattern stress per participant: twelve percent of fatal threshold. Margin of safety: significant." A pause. "It may work."

"'May,'" Ade repeated. "Love the confidence."

"Ninety-one percent is extraordinary," VESPER-9 said, with something that in a human would have been offense. "My creators would have called it miraculous."

"My creators," Halvorsen said dryly, "would have called it a court-martial waiting to happen. Which is why we're not asking them."

I caught myself smiling. Actually smiling, in a freezing room buried under a lie of a seed vault, with a war thirty-some hours from reigniting and a Compact assault force presumably crawling toward us through the dark. Smiling like a fool. Like a woman with a crew.

Because that's what they were now. I'd fought the word for weeks — passengers, charges, cargo, anything but crew, because crew meant mine, and mine meant loss, and I'd arranged my whole life to avoid owing anything to anything. Somewhere between Checkpoint Nine and the tether walk and tonight, the arrangement had quietly collapsed, and here I stood owning eight people and being owned by them in return, and the strange thing, the thing I'll carry however long I last, is that it didn't feel like debt. It felt like ballast. Like the ship rides steadier with weight in the hold.

"Get some sleep," I told them. "All of you. Real sleep, not cradle naps. Tomorrow we rehearse the sync sequence until VESPER-9 stops finding things to complain about, and then—" I glanced at the countdown display Okonkwo's people had patched into our system, the truce clock burning down in red digits. "—then we sing."

They filed out. Wren lingered at the door.

"Orsen." She didn't turn around. "Back in the Meridian, when we realized what the reversal had done to us — what it was going to keep doing — we drew lots. Quietly. For who'd go in the chair if it ever came to that. I won. I've been carrying the lot slip for forty years." She pulled something from her pocket: a scrap of foil wrapper, creased soft with handling, marked with a child's handwriting gone adult somewhere along the way. She set it on the console, deliberate, and pushed it toward me. "I don't need it anymore."

She left. I stood there a while, holding a piece of garbage that weighed about as much as a life.

Then the intercom clicked. "Orsen Hartwell," VESPER-9 said, and there was something new in its tone, something almost hesitant. "While modeling the distributed configuration, I found something in the Archive's outer cipher layers. A structure I do not understand. It appears to be... a second message. Nested beneath the first. Addressed — if my parsing is correct — not to the governments. Not to humanity at large." A pause, longer than any machine should need. "Addressed to me. By name. From my creators."

I looked down at the foil scrap in my hand, then up at the dark of the intercom speaker, eighty years of patient grief humming in the walls around me.

"Well," I said. "Ask it what it's been waiting to hear."
