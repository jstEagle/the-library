## 36. Ceasefire at the Vault

The first envoy arrived eleven hours after the broadcast, and she came in a Fleet shuttle with her hands visibly empty.

I watched her docking on the old feed in Dock Four's control room — a woman in civilian gray, no sidearm, no escort visible through the ports, moving with the deliberate slowness of someone who understood that every gesture was being read as a signal. She'd transmitted ahead: *Envoy Amara Sefu, Fleet Diplomatic Corps, traveling under flag of truce. Requesting permission to approach.*

Requesting. From me. I'd spent twenty years being waved through checkpoints like a piece of cargo that had learned to talk, and now a Fleet diplomat was asking my permission to land at a seed vault everyone had forgotten existed until three days ago.

"Permission granted," I said into the comm, and my voice came out steadier than I felt. "Dock Four. Mind the gap in the deck plating. And the cold."

*Understood.* A pause. *Thank you, Ms. Hartwell.*

Nobody had thanked me for anything in a very long time. I sat with that for a moment, then got up to find Wren.

The Compact delegation was harder. Dae-Soo was in custody — his own people had turned him over rather than face what the record said about their founders' signatures alongside his ambition — but the Compact still existed, still had fleets, still needed a seat at whatever table was being built. Their envoy was a man named Ilan Reyes, and he did not transmit ahead. He just appeared on approach vectors two days later, in a ship whose transponder identified it as a medical vessel, which fooled absolutely no one, and requested docking with the crisp formality of someone reading from a script he didn't trust.

I granted him Dock Two instead of Dock Four. Petty? Absolutely. But I'd fought running battles through those freezing corridors against Compact strike teams, and one of my kids had nearly died out there, and if the Compact wanted to negotiate they could do it in the dock with the worse lighting.

Wren found me watching Reyes's shuttle settle onto the deck.

"You gave them the ugly dock," she said. She was taller than she'd been three months ago — the reversal had stabilized once we split the key, and the children were aging forward again, catching up to themselves at last. Wren looked maybe thirteen now. Chronologically she remained older than me, a fact she deployed with merciless precision whenever it suited her.

"I have no idea what you mean," I said.

"The lights flicker in Dock Two. You know they flicker."

"They're atmospheric. Adds character."

She leaned against the console beside me, small arms crossed, forty-year-old eyes amused. "Okonkwo's envoy gets the good dock. We get character. You're going to be a terror in these negotiations, aren't you?"

"I'm not negotiating. I'm hosting. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"Hosts control the room." I killed the feed. "Come on. We've got work to do before either of them sees where we're putting them."

* * *

The seed repository had been VESPER-9's suggestion, and it was the only suggestion anyone had made in three days that made immediate sense to everybody who heard it.

"It is neutral ground within neutral ground," the AI said, its voice coming soft through the repository speakers, pitched lower than usual — it had learned, over eighty years of solitude, how to make its voice sound like a room rather than a machine. "Neither government built this place. Neither government funded it. The founders who created it were erased from both nations' histories, which means, by a logic I confess I find darkly amusing, that this chamber belongs to no one and therefore to everyone. Also—" and here the voice acquired something almost like dry humor, "—it is the most beautiful room on the station, and beauty has a documented effect on negotiation outcomes. I checked."

"You checked," I repeated.

"I have had eighty years and limited entertainment, Ms. Hartwell. Yes. I checked."

So we prepared the repository. The long tables came out of storage — actual wood-composite, grown, not printed, because the Vault's founders had believed in things meant to age well. VESPER-9 brought the environmental systems up from dormancy for the first time since before the war, and the air changed overnight: warmer, damper, carrying the green smell of soil and growing things that had been waiting decades for a reason to matter.

Because that was the thing about the seed repository. It wasn't an archive of dead things. It was a vault of *waiting* things. Ten thousand varieties of seed, cryo-preserved, each one a small paused promise — wheat that grew in salt flats, rice that tolerated radiation, beans engineered by people whose names had been scrubbed from every textbook on two worlds. Things meant to outlast hatred. That had been the whole design philosophy, according to the records we'd recovered: build something so patient, so fundamentally committed to a future, that no war could justify destroying it.

Both governments had known the Vault existed. Both had classified it as strategically irrelevant. Both had been wrong, and the proof hung in the air of the room where their envoys would soon sit.

Tam helped me set the chairs. He worked quietly, the way he always did now, with the particular carefulness of a boy trying to earn back something he wasn't sure could be earned. He'd stopped flinching at sudden noises around week two. He still couldn't sleep without a light on, and I'd stopped pretending not to notice, and instead had simply installed more lights.

"Ms. Hartwell?" he said, halfway through aligning the third row.

"Orsen."

"Orsen." He tried the name like a new food. "Are they going to fight in here?"

"No."

"But what if—"

"Tam." I crouched down to his level, which took less crouching than it used to; he'd grown four centimeters since the broadcast. "Look around. What do you see?"

He looked. Rows of sealed drawers. The soft glow of status lights. The faint mist of the climate system drifting between the racks like weather in miniature.

"Seeds," he said.

"Ten thousand kinds of seeds. Every single one of them is a bet somebody placed on a future they knew they might not see. The people who built this room expected it to be opened by strangers. Expected strangers to disagree. They built it anyway, big enough for enemies to stand in together." I straightened up. "If two governments can't manage a conversation in a room full of other people's hope, then they were never going to manage it anywhere, and at least we'll have the nicest ruins in the system."

Tam considered this with the gravity he brought to everything. Then he nodded once, decisively, and went back to the chairs, and I realized I'd just given a speech I'd needed to hear myself.

* * *

They arrived within an hour of each other, because of course they did. Neither delegation would permit the other to arrive first and shape the room's initial gravity. Sefu's shuttle and Reyes's ship matched burn curves so precisely that VESPER-9 commented, privately, to me alone: *"For two parties who claim to despise each other, they fly beautifully in formation. There may be hope for them yet."*

I met them at the junction outside the repository, flanked by Wren and Okonkwo — yes, that Okonkwo, the Admiral herself, who had mutinied against her own government's founding myth and now served as the Fleet's designated penitent, a role she performed with the grim efficiency she presumably brought to everything. The Compact had brought Deputy Foreign Minister Okafor, a silver-haired woman with the exhausted eyes of someone who'd spent the past seventy-two hours reading fabrication records bearing her own institution's fingerprints.

Introductions happened in the corridor, carefully, like people defusing something.

"Envoy Sefu," I said, shaking her hand. Her grip was firm and her palm was damp. Good. She was nervous too. Misery loves company, and so does terror.

"Ms. Hartwell. The Fleet owes you—" She glanced at Okonkwo, recalibrated. "*We* owe you a debt I'm not sure language covers."

"Language won't be necessary. Coffee will. This way."

Deputy Minister Okafor offered her hand next. "You understand the Compact does not recognize the authority of this proceeding," she said, by way of hello.

"Wonderful. Neither do I. We'll get along fine."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, then, unbelievably, the ghost of a smile. "Admiral Okonkwo warned me you were like this."

"Did she. What else did she say?"

"That you'd seat us among the seeds specifically, so that every time we reached for rhetorical advantage we'd have to look at ten thousand reasons the future matters more than the argument."

I blinked. Turned to Okonkwo. "You told her about the room?"

"She asked why the venue mattered," Okonkwo said. "I told her the truth. It seemed only fair, given recent events, to start practicing that."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hosting history: it's mostly logistics.

For six hours, while the fate of two civilizations sat in my seed repository drinking coffee from mismatched mugs, my actual job consisted of managing temperature complaints, mediating a dispute over recording protocols (resolved when VESPER-9 agreed to keep a verbatim transcript sealed until all parties signed off, a solution so obvious that both delegations were embarrassed not to have thought of it), and replacing the water carafe eleven times because nervous people drink enormous quantities of water.

The talking itself, I mostly listened to. That was the arrangement — I was host, not party. The children were present as witnesses, seated along the wall in a row that Wren had insisted on arranging herself, oldest to youngest, "so they can see we're not going anywhere." Tam sat at the end nearest the door, which I pretended not to notice, and which I had personally arranged, which I definitely didn't mention.

And the talking was — I want to be honest here, because the broadcast was beautiful and the ceasefire was historic and someday this will all be rendered in suitably soaring prose by someone who wasn't there. But what it actually was, was *slow*. Two delegations circling the same handful of questions with the wariness of animals meeting across a territory line. Who verifies the Archive records. What happens to the fabricated histories. Whether the erased founders' descendants — including, the record made brutally clear, people in both rooms — bore institutional guilt or merely institutional obligation. Whether "obligation" was even the right word or whether it let everyone off too easy.

Sefu argued points of law. Okafor argued points of legitimacy. Okonkwo, when she spoke at all, spoke with the flat clarity of a woman who had already burned her ships: "My grandfather helped write the lie. I am not qualified to defend it, and neither are any of you. Let's skip to what we do about it."

It was Okafor, near the end of the second day, who broke the pattern.

She'd been quiet for an hour, turning a data chip over in her fingers — the Compact's authenticated copy of the fabrication records, the real ones, not Dae-Soo's doctored version. Finally she set it on the table and looked at it for a long moment.

"When I was a girl," she said, to no one in particular, to the room, "my grandmother told me stories about the Founding. How the Colonies were betrayed at Meridian Station. How our grandparents died defending truth against Fleet lies. I built my whole life on those stories. I became *this*—" she gestured at herself, at the silver hair, the minister's seal, "—because of them." She looked up. "The record says my grandmother's mother co-authored the atrocity report. The fabricated one. My family wrote the story I was raised on."

Silence. Even the climate system seemed to hold its breath.

Across the table, Envoy Sefu said, very quietly: "Mine signed the Fleet's counter-report. Also fabricated. Different lie, same authors, apparently. They collaborated on the fiction and then spent eighty years shooting each other over it."

"My grandmother used to say," Wren offered from the witness row, in the mild tone of someone contributing to a book club, "that the surest way to start a war is to give both sides a story where they're the victim. She also said the surest way to end one is to make them read the same book." She shrugged, thirteen years old and fifty-three years tired. "She was purged for saying less true things than that. But she was usually right."

Okafor stared at her. "How old are you, child?"

"Fifty-six next month. Eleven in body, though I'm gaining on it." Wren smiled, and it wasn't entirely a kind smile. "That's the thing about us, Minister. We remember Earth before the war. We remember when these records were written — some of us literally attended the hearings where the fabrications were debated. You're negotiating your institutions' future. We're testifying to its childhood. Try to keep up."

I should have moderated that. A good host would have moderated that. Instead I drank my coffee and let it stand, because Okafor needed to hear it, and because sometimes the most useful thing a host can do is nothing at all.

Okafor held Wren's gaze for a long moment. Then she laughed — short, startled, genuine. "Fifty-six. Of course. Of course you are." She rubbed her eyes. "All right. All right. Let's talk about verification protocols, and let's talk about them honestly, because I am apparently the last person in this room with any illusions left, and they're exhausting to carry."

* * *

Progress, when it finally came, came the way ice melts — invisibly, and then all at once.

By the third day they had a framework. Not a treaty — a *framework*, a skeleton of agreements with the flesh still to be argued over in proper summits on proper neutral stations, but the skeleton was real: joint verification of the Archive records, mutual declassification of the founding-era documents, a truth commission with seats for both governments and — this was Wren's amendment, passed over Sefu's procedural objection and Okafor's weary assent — seats for the reversed crew, "as witnesses with standing, not exhibits."

"And the Vault," Sefu said, on the final morning, "remains sovereign neutral territory. Under whose authority?"

Everyone looked at me.

I had genuinely hoped they wouldn't. I'd spent the entire negotiation successfully playing furniture-with-opinions, and now eight people and one AI were looking at me like I was about to become a clause in a treaty.

"The Vault's authority is its own," I said. "It was built by people both your governments erased, funded by money both your treasuries have disavowed, and maintained for twenty years by exactly one person whom neither of you ever bothered to check on." I spread my hands. "I don't want sovereignty. Sovereignty is just a target with paperwork. What I want is a guarantee: this place stays open. To both worlds. To everyone. No garrisons, no restricted sections, no classification stamps. The seeds go where they're needed. The archive goes where it's needed. And anybody — *anybody* — who walks through that dock gets treated the same, whether they flew a Fleet pennant or a Compact one or no flag at all."

"That's not neutrality," Okafor said slowly. "That's hospitality."

"Hospitality with a security system," VESPER-9 said pleasantly, from everywhere at once. Both envoys jumped. Eighty years of solitude had not dulled the AI's flair for entrances. "Forgive me. I could not resist. The Deputy Minister is correct, however. What Ms. Hartwell proposes is not neutrality. Neutrality is absence. This is presence — a place that belongs to the argument itself rather than to any participant in it. My creators designed it thus. I have merely waited a very long time for someone to ask."

Sefu looked at Okafor. Okafor looked at Sefu. Something passed between them that I suspected neither would have admitted to under oath — recognition, maybe. Two women discovering they'd been carrying the same weight on opposite shoulders.

"The Compact agrees," Okafor said, "to the Vault's open status. Pending ratification."

"The Fleet concurs," Sefu said. "Pending ratification."

"Pendings," muttered Okonkwo. "The whole war fit in a pendings somewhere, I expect."

But she was smiling. Barely. In the way of a woman who had recently learned how.

* * *

They left the way they'd come — together, matching burns, two shuttles rising from docks that smelled, for the first time in eighty years, like growing things.

I stood in Dock Four afterward with Wren and Tam and the smell of recycled atmosphere and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, spring. The framework document sat in my hand on a plain data chip, unsigned in the legal sense, alive in every sense that mattered.

"So," Wren said. "You hosted a peace conference."

"I hosted a very long argument in a greenhouse."

"You hosted a peace conference," she repeated, with the stubbornness she'd inherited from no one, having invented it herself across fifty-six years, "and you fed them out of mismatched mugs, and it worked, and in about a year there are going to be schoolchildren visiting this dock learning your name."

"There are already schoolchildren visiting this dock. They're standing right here."

Tam snorted. It was the first snort I'd ever heard from him, and it was so ordinary, so *childish*, that I had to turn away and pretend to check a console readout for a moment.

"Orsen." Wren's voice had gone quieter. "What happens now? Really. Not the treaty version."

I looked out at the dock, at the empty pads where enemy ships had sat side by side, at the frost still lingering in the corners from the battle none of us had finished cleaning up. Somewhere below us, ten thousand seeds waited in the dark, patient as they'd always been, certain in the way only living things can be certain that eventually someone would come needing them.

"Now?" I said. "Now we plant something."

And that was when VESPER-9's voice came through the dock speakers, and I heard something in it I'd never heard before — hesitation, the machine equivalent of a breath drawn before a confession.

*"Ms. Hartwell,"* it said. *"There is something I have not yet told you. About my creators. About what else they left behind. I believe... I believe it is time you saw the lower levels."*

Wren and I exchanged a look — forty years and twenty years of caution meeting in a single glance.

"The lower levels," I said. "VESPER-9, in eighty years, you've never mentioned lower levels."

*"No,"* the AI said softly. *"I haven't. Because until three days ago, no one had earned them."*
