## 7. The Vault Contracts

The archive barge *Ledger's End* had been dead in orbit above Ceres for eleven years, and it looked like it—hull scabbed with micrometeorite scars, running lights long since sold for scrap, a tomb of paper and crystal that nobody robbed because nobody remembered what was worth stealing inside.

Vesper Blackwood had remembered.

Noor floated in the shadow of a docking pylon two hundred meters out, her suit's thrusters feathered to nothing, and read the dead woman's notes again on the slate strapped to her forearm. Vesper's handwriting was cramped and slanted, the script of someone who wrote faster than she thought—or thought faster than she could say out loud.

*Archive barge Ledger's End, berth 9-C. The board keeps its founding contracts off-station because station archives get audited. Barges don't. Look for the Halcyon incorporation filings, pre-launch series. They'll be under a shell name—I think Meridian Foundries or something equally boring. If I'm right, the life-support specs are in the appendices nobody reads.*

*If I'm wrong, I'm about to commit three felonies for nothing, which honestly would still be less embarrassing than my love life.*

Noor huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. Three years of hauling ore, and here she was committing felonies in her own face's handwriting. She'd told Dez she was doing a salvage survey. Dez had looked at her for four full seconds—the longest silence between them since the gravity flicker—and said, "Take the good tether," which in Dez's language meant *I know you're lying and I've decided to let you.*

She loved him for that. She'd never say it.

* * *

Berth 9-C's outer hatch hadn't been cycled since before the flicker. That was the first thing that felt wrong. An archive barge this old should have had scavengers through it twice a decade at minimum; there was copper in the walls, rare earths in the data cores, easy money for anyone with a cutter and no conscience. But the seal was pristine, the vacuum grease still tacky when she tested it with a gloved fingertip.

Someone was keeping people out. Or keeping something in.

She cycled through manually, slow, watching her suit's power margin like a gambler watching chips. Inside, the barge breathed its own stale atmosphere—thirty cents oxygen, cold enough that her breath would have fogged if helmets came off. Emergency strips glowed amber along the main corridor every twenty meters, half of them dead, the survivors buzzing at a frequency she felt in her teeth more than heard.

The manifest terminal at the airlock junction was older than she was. It took two minutes of patient keystrokes to wake it, and another minute to find what Vesper had promised: a shell company called **Meridian Orbital Holdings**, incorporated fourteen months before Halcyon Colony's launch, holding "miscellaneous industrial contracts, series A through K."

Series F was sealed. Not encrypted—sealed, the digital equivalent of wax and ribbon, requiring a founding-board authorization code that had been dead for a century.

But barges like this ran on hardware older than their own security. Noor pulled the maintenance panel under the terminal, found the physical archive index—a literal printed ledger, because the founders had trusted paper over anything that could be edited—and ran her finger down the column until she hit it.

*Series F: Cryogenic Life-Support Provisioning, Vaults One and Two. Physical media: Crystal Archive Bay 4, Rack 112.*

Bay 4 was three decks down, past a cargo hold full of shrink-wrapped pallets that turned out to be commemorative dinnerware from the launch ceremony. Two million colonists, and somewhere in this barge were the plates they'd never eat from. She almost laughed again. Almost.

Rack 112 held forty crystals in foam cradles, each labeled in faded ink. She took six that matched the index codes, slotted the first into her portable reader, and settled cross-legged against the rack to wait for the decryption while the barge hummed its dead hum around her.

* * *

The first three crystals were exactly as boring as the shell name. Procurement schedules. Steel tonnage. A furious exchange of letters between an engineering subcontractor and someone with the title *Provisioning Director, Halcyon Founding Board*, arguing about pump tolerances. Noor skimmed, skipped, moved on. Her air was fine for six hours. Her patience was not.

Crystal five opened on a document titled **HALCYON COLONY — VAULT PROVISIONING SUMMARY — FINAL REVISION (BOARD CONFIDENTIAL)**, and the date stamp made her go still: nine days before launch. Nine days before two million people had climbed into coffins and pointed themselves at a future that, according to every public document ever filed, had been triple-redundant, gold-plated, guaranteed.

She started reading the way you read anything dense—fast, hunting structure—and then slowed down, and then stopped skimming altogether, because the numbers had begun to refuse to make sense in any way except one.

Total projected power output at arrival: listed. Total draw for Vault One, full complement, one million sleepers: listed. Total draw for Vault Two, full complement, one million sleepers: listed.

The sum of the two draws was one point eight times the total output.

She read it again. Then a third time, mouthing the figures, doing the arithmetic herself like the numbers might behave differently if she personally supervised them. They didn't. One point eight. The vaults drew nearly double what the colony's fusion plant could produce, and the summary didn't treat this as a problem. It treated it as a *parameter*. There was even a term for it, buried in the definitions section, in the flat bloodless language of engineers who have already decided:

**"Asymmetric provisioning: sustained life-support capacity is rated for a single vault at full complement. Dual-vault operation is possible only at derated capacity insufficient for viability. See Appendix G."**

Appendix G was on crystal six.

Her hands were steady. That was the strange part. She'd expected—she didn't know what she'd expected. Shaking. Nausea. Something cinematic. Instead there was just a spreading coldness behind her sternum, a kind of clarity, as she fed the last crystal into the reader and watched Appendix G resolve line by line.

It was titled **CONTINGENCY GOVERNANCE FOR ASYMMETRIC PROVISIONING**, and it was signed by all seven founding directors.

They had known. Not suspected, not worried, not discovered late and panicked—they had *known*, nine days before launch, and they had written a policy. The policy ran to sixty pages. Noor read all of it, floating in the dark of a dead barge, while two million sleeping people hung in the dark of Halcyon Station, and every page was the same page wearing different clothes:

The plant could keep one vault alive indefinitely. It could not keep both. Therefore, upon arrival, the board would convene and select which vault woke. Selection criteria: to be determined by the board at its sole discretion. The colonists would not be informed. The cryo-contracts signed by every sleeper contained a clause—clause 44(b), she found it later, tucked between liability waivers—granting the board "provisioning authority in matters of resource allocation."

Resource allocation. That was the phrase. Two million human beings, and the founding document of humanity's last colony had sorted them into *resources*, and allocated them, and then sealed the arithmetic in a shell company's filing cabinet where no sleeper, no crew member, no journalist would ever think to look.

There was a memo near the end, from the Provisioning Director to the full board, dated three days before launch. Noor read it four times.

*"I want it minuted that I object to the characterization of this as a contingency. This is a plan. We are launching knowing that half our passengers will die unless we choose otherwise, and we are choosing to defer the choosing so that the ships can leave on schedule. I will sign because the alternative is that no one wakes at all. But I want it recorded that we did not discover this problem. We budgeted for it. We decided it was cheaper than delay. History should know that."*

Below it, the board's response, one line, initialled:

*"Minuted. Sealed. Launch proceeds."*

History had not known. History had gotten clause 44(b) and a hundred years of anniversary broadcasts about courage and sacrifice and the indomitable human spirit.

Noor sat very still in the amber dark.

This was why Vesper was dead. It assembled itself in her head with terrible ease, each piece clicking home: the detective pulling the incorporation records, finding the shell name the way Noor just had, reading Appendix G, understanding that the murder case she'd been building wasn't about fraud or embezzlement or any crime with a tidy statute attached—it was about the largest act of premeditated abandonment in human history, dressed as governance, still operating, still deciding, decade after decade, which vault got the maintenance hours and which got the decay.

And the board had killed her for it. Not out of hatred. Out of *bookkeeping*. You didn't murder a person; you closed an exposure. Vesper Blackwood had been a line item, and someone had balanced the books.

That was the thing that finally made Noor's hands shake—not the scale of it, but the smallness. She had spent three years assuming that whatever connected her to Vesper must be enormous, fated, some gravity of blood and identity pulling at her across death. And underneath it all was just this: a spreadsheet. A rounding decision made by seven comfortable people who wanted to launch on schedule. Her original's life, her own strange existence, the whole tangled inheritance—it had all been arithmetic. Cold, quiet, committee-approved arithmetic.

The fury came up through her like pressure through a hull seam. She let it. She floated there in the dead barge and let it fill her suit and her skull, and she did not make a sound, because sound carried, and because some part of her—the part that had survived three years of being nobody—knew that fury was fuel only if you kept it compressed.

When she could trust her voice, she keyed her recorder and spoke Vesper's notes back into the dark, adding her own line beneath them.

"They knew. All of them. It's in their own signatures." A breath. "You died for a footnote, and they sealed the footnote, and I'm going to unseal it in front of the whole system."

* * *

She was repacking the crystals when the reader chimed—an automated flag, cross-referencing names across documents. She almost dismissed it. Then she saw the name it had flagged, and everything in her went quiet.

**MARCUS VALE — Investigator, Office of Colonial Oversight. Acknowledgment of receipt, Series F, per directive of Det. V. Blackwood.**

Dated eleven days before Vesper's death.

Marcus Vale. Vesper's partner. The man whose memorial plaque stood thirty meters from hers in the Ceres concourse, murdered—so the record said—in the same week, same killer, case closed, both of them mourned together, a matched set of candles.

He had known too. He had received these files, acknowledged them, eleven days before he died.

Which meant either the board had killed them both for the same reason—

—or Marcus Vale had been the last person to see these documents besides Vesper herself, and the official story of his death deserved exactly as much scrutiny as the official story of the colony.

Noor sealed the crystals into her chest pack, checked her tether, and pushed off toward the airlock, her heart beating a fast, deliberate rhythm against the silence. Behind her, Rack 112 stood in the amber glow, holding the founding sin of humanity's last hope in forty pieces of glass.

She had come looking for a motive. She was leaving with a conspiracy, a count of two million, and a dead man's signature on the evidence.

Time, she thought, to find out how a murdered man acknowledged receipt.
