## 19. Core Ejection

**NOOR**

The core didn't want to leave.

That was the thing no one told you about fusion plants—that after years of carrying you, they developed a kind of gravity of their own. The *Meridian Rose* had been her home, her anonymity, her one honest possession in a life built on forgery, and now Noor stood in the engineering bay with a plasma torch in one hand and the release manual projected on her retinal display, reading the same line for the fourth time because her eyes kept sliding off it.

*Ejection procedure is irreversible. Core loss results in total vessel decommission.*

"Say it out loud," Dez said from the doorway. She had grease on her jaw and something worse than grease in her expression—she'd been crying, or she was about to, and she was fighting it the way she fought everything, with her shoulders up around her ears.

"It's just a ship."

"Don't you dare." Dez crossed the bay in three strides and grabbed Noor's wrist, hard enough to bruise. "Don't you *dare* make this small. That's not a ship. That's every hour you spent pretending to be nobody. You're about to eject your own life into a star's shadow and I will not let you call it 'just' anything."

Noor looked at her hand under Dez's grip. Three years ago she'd have pulled away. Two years ago she'd have made a joke. Now she turned her wrist, laced her fingers through Dez's, and held on.

"Nineteen hours," Noor said. "Two million people. One core that happens to be rated for exactly the load Halcyon's grid needs if we bridge both vaults at reduced capacity." She exhaled. "I did the arithmetic. Vesper would have hated how easily I did the arithmetic."

"You're not her."

"No," Noor agreed. "She'd have found another way. I'm going to do it this way."

* * *

**DEZ**

Flying a ship without a heart was like flying a corpse.

Dez knew it intellectually—the core sat in its cradle amidships, and once it went, everything aft of the bulkhead became dead mass, ballast, a coffin bolted to the cockpit. But knowing wasn't feeling. Feeling was the way the vibration under her palms changed when Noor hit the release clamps, a deep harmonic note dropping out of the hull's song, leaving a silence Dez felt in her teeth.

"Core is free," Noor's voice said over comms, thin and steady. "RCS thrusters responding. I've got maybe forty minutes of maneuvering propellant before I'm just a woman in a suit riding a fusion bottle."

"Then don't waste it." Dez keyed the main drive—a stuttering, dying thing now, running off capacitors—and swung the *Rose* onto Halcyon Station's approach vector. Ahead, the station hung against the dark like a cathedral someone had abandoned mid-prayer: two great vault rings flanking a spine of dead reactors, their running lights pulsing amber, amber, amber. Emergency mode. Countdown mode.

On her scope, three contacts burned toward them from the board's dry dock. Enforcer cutters, military-grade, moving like they had nothing to lose.

"Reyes," Dez said into the open channel. "Tell me you see them."

Static. Then Reyes's voice, clipped and calm in the way that meant she was anything but: "I see them. Vigilant is moving to intercept. Dez—whatever happens next, don't watch. Fly your ship."

Dez watched anyway. She was flying and watching, hands light on the yoke, while the patrol cutter *Vigilant* slid across the void between the enforcers and the *Meridian Rose* like a door closing.

* * *

**REYES**

Captain Ilsa Reyes had spent twenty-two years learning exactly how much disobedience the patrol service would tolerate, and she had never once found the edge of it. Today she intended to find it personally.

"Vigilant, break formation and return to station," said the voice from Halcyon Command—a board liaison named Okonkwo whose voice sounded like a filing cabinet. "You are ordered to permit enforcement action against the vessel Meridian Rose."

"Negative, Halcyon. Meridian Rose is operating under my escort authority. Any cutter approaching weapons range will be considered hostile to a patrol asset." She paused, let the silence do its work. "That's an act of war against the Patrol Charter, gentlemen. I'd check with your lawyers before you commit it over an open channel."

It was a bluff. It was entirely a bluff—Okonkwo could have her stripped of command with a single transmission—but the cutters slowed, their drives flaring as they adjusted course, suddenly uncertain whether the old woman in the aging cutter really was crazy enough to mean it.

She was. She absolutely was.

"Reyes." Okonkwo again, softer now, almost gentle. "You knew Vesper Blackwood. We all grieved her. But this clone—this thing wearing her face—it's not her. Whatever it told you, whatever it promised—"

"She's not Vesper," Reyes said. "That's rather the point. Vesper would have negotiated. This one's just going to do it."

On her tactical display, the *Meridian Rose* fell away behind her, small and dying and stubborn, hauling its ejected core toward the station's grid junction on threads of thruster fire. And ahead of the little ship, cutting across its path with the smooth confidence of a man who had rehearsed this for years, came Marcus Vale's tug.

Reyes had read Vale's file a hundred times. Murdered partner. Heroic victim. She'd drunk to his memory more than once.

*He faked his death,* Noor had told her. *He's been down there the whole time.*

"He's going for the junction," her tactical officer said. "If he reaches it before the core does—"

"I know what he'll reach it with." Reyes rolled the Vigilant, bringing her forward batteries to bear on the tug, and opened a channel she was fairly sure was being recorded by half the system. "Marcus Vale. This is Captain Reyes, Patrol Cutter Vigilant. Power down your drive and prepare to be boarded."

The tug's response was to accelerate.

* * *

**NOOR**

The core rode ahead of her like a tame sun.

She was suited, tethered to it by two lines and a prayer, RCS thrusters puffing in measured breaths as she nudged forty tons of contained starfire toward the grid junction on Halcyon's spine. Through her visor the station filled half the sky—vault rings turning slow as sleeping eyelids, windows dark, two million people dreaming through the last hours of the world's oldest lie.

Her HUD painted the junction: a docking collar and transfer gantry where the station's power trunk met vacuum, designed for ships to plug in during construction, never used since. Forty percent capacity for both vaults. Slow wake. Everyone.

Everyone.

"Sixty seconds to junction," she reported. "Dez, talk to me."

"Enforcers are holding at extreme range—Reyes has them spooked. But there's a second contact, unregistered tug, coming up your six fast. Transponder's spoofed but the drive signature matches Vale's boat from the case file."

Of course it did. Noor felt something cold settle behind her sternum—not fear exactly; more like recognition. Vale had spent nine years inside the station's bones. He knew the junction better than she ever could. He didn't need to stop her. He only needed to be there first, to splice his own control rig into the transfer collar, and then the core wouldn't wake anyone. It would wake whoever Vale decided deserved waking, on whatever schedule served whatever justice he'd been composing alone in the dark for nine years.

"Noor." Vale's voice arrived on the common band, warm as ever, the voice of a man who had once held her original's coffee and laughed at her jokes. "You can still turn around. There's a version of this where nobody gets hurt."

"There's also a version where you shut up and let me work."

"I liked her better when she was sarcastic too," he said, and cut the channel.

His tug swung past her at two hundred meters, close enough that she saw movement along its flank—EVA figures in patched suits, Vale's people, the ones he'd woken early or recruited or owed him. They were carrying cable. Heavy spooling cable, the kind you used to hijack a power feed.

And then the tug fired its harpoon.

The anchor screamed across the gap and buried itself in the junction collar with a clang Noor felt through her tether. A line snapped taut. The tug began reeling itself in, dragging its cable crew toward the junction, toward the collar, toward the connection point she still had four hundred meters to cross.

"Dez," Noor said quietly. "How attached are you to the plan?"

"What plan? The plan where you sacrifice everything? Or a new plan?"

"The new plan. I need you to fly the *Rose* between me and that tug."

A pause. Long enough to hold a whole friendship inside it.

"On my way," Dez said.

* * *

**DEZ**

The *Meridian Rose* had perhaps eleven minutes of capacitor charge left, which meant eleven minutes of thrust, which meant Dez had exactly one burn to spend and no second chances. She spent it all.

The old hauler came around in a graceless arc, running dark except for her nav strobes, and put herself directly into the harpoon line's path. On Dez's scope the geometry was obscene—a dying cargo ship throwing itself across the path of a hijack like a body thrown over a grenade.

"Vale's tug, this is civilian vessel Meridian Rose," she broadcast. "You are dragging a live anchor line through an active approach corridor. Cut it or I'll be forced to—" She stopped. There was no *forced to*. She had no weapons. She had a ship with no engine and a hold full of ore.

So she did the only thing left. She rotated the *Rose* broadside and killed her remaining velocity, letting the tug's own tow line wrap across the hauler's bow.

The cables caught. Metal shrieked—a sound Dez heard through the hull, through her boots, through her ribs—as the harpoon line snagged the *Rose*'s forward mooring points and the two ships jerked together like animals on a rope. The tug's reel screamed, overloaded, and blew its clutch in a spray of sparks visible even from the cockpit.

They were tangled. Both of them. Dead in the water, lashed together, two hundred meters from the junction where Noor was still pushing a star toward a socket.

"Dez," Noor said over comms. Her voice had gone very quiet. "What did you just do?"

"Bought you ninety seconds." Dez's hands were shaking so badly she pressed them flat against the console to stop it. "Maybe two minutes. Use them."

* * *

**REYES**

The enforcers came anyway, because of course they did. Men with arithmetic instead of consciences always came eventually.

Three cutters, spreading into a firing arc around the Vigilant, and Okonkwo's voice on the channel reciting regulations like a rosary. Reyes listened to exactly none of it. She was watching the tangled knot of ships near the junction, watching the tiny bright mote that was Noor Blackwood crawling up the transfer gantry with a fusion core in tow, watching Vale's EVA team swarm along the tug's spine toward the same destination.

"Ma'am," her tactical officer said. "Cutters are charging weapons. If we don't move—"

"We move." Reyes brought the Vigilant about, putting her battered old cutter directly into the enforcers' firing line, between their guns and the junction. Between the board's arithmetic and two million sleeping people. "All batteries, targeting solutions only. Nobody fires unless they do."

"That's not going to hold them."

"No," Reyes agreed. "But it doesn't have to hold them long. It only has to make them hesitate." She thought of Vesper—of the real one, the laughing one, the one who used to steal fries off her plate in the patrol canteen and claim it was evidence-gathering. *You always were good at standing in doorways, Ilsa.* "Hesitation is a weapon. Use it."

The lead cutter fired a warning shot across the Vigilant's bow. Then, blessedly, stupidly, humanly—they hesitated.

In that hesitation, Noor reached the junction.

* * *

**NOOR**

The transfer collar accepted the core's umbilical like a lock accepting a key, and for one perfect moment nothing happened anywhere in the universe except a status light turning green.

"Grid handshake complete," Noor breathed. "Halcyon, I am feeding you a class-four fusion plant on a construction-era interface. Your move."

For three full seconds, silence. Then the station answered.

Power moved. Noor felt it as a change in the hum transmitted through the gantry structure—a vast, waking shudder, like a body remembering circulation. Along the spine, dead conduits lit segment by segment. In the vault rings, amber lights shifted toward white. Somewhere below her feet, cryo systems that had been rationed and triaged and quietly murderous for decades received, for the first time, permission to try.

"Both vaults?" she asked. "Confirm both vaults."

Osei's voice, ragged with something that might have been laughter: "Both vaults. Forty-one percent. Rising. Noor—it's rising."

And then Vale's team hit the junction.

They came over the gantry rail in silence, four suited figures moving with the economy of people who had done ugly things in vacuum before, and the first one reached for the umbilical's manual disconnect. Noor got there first—or rather, she got there differently. She didn't fight for the connector. She put herself between the man and the core, planted her boots on the gantry, and raised both empty hands.

"Go ahead," she said on the local band. "Disconnect it. And then explain to two million people why the man who murdered nothing and saved nothing decided they should die for his principles."

The figure stopped. Behind its visor, Noor saw a young face—too young, someone's kid, someone Vale had fed nine years of righteous grief to.

"Vale says the founders don't get forgiven," the young man said. "Forty percent isn't mercy. It's a slower sentence."

"Forty percent is *everyone*," Noor said. "That's the whole point. That's the thing he can't stand. He wanted a verdict. I'm giving them a reprieve. Reprieves aren't justice. But they're where justice starts."

The young man's hand hovered over the disconnect. And then, faintly, through everyone's helmets, came the sound of the station itself—vault ring one, deep below, cycling its first thaw sequence in thirty years. A mechanical heartbeat. The sound of someone beginning, very slowly, to wake up.

The hand withdrew.

"Noor." Vale's voice, finally, on the private channel, and for the first time it didn't sound warm. It sounded tired. Old. Like a man who had been awake too long. "You're making a mistake."

"Probably," she said. "But it's mine. Get off my junction, Marcus."

A long pause. Then, distantly, the tug's thrusters fired, pulling the tangled ships apart, and Vale's team retreated along the gantry without another word. Noor watched them go and understood that this wasn't surrender. Vale didn't surrender. He relocated. He would be somewhere in the station's depths already, already planning, already certain.

Let him. Tonight, two million people were going to start waking up, slowly, imperfectly, all of them.

"Dez," she said. "Come get your captain home."

"Can't," Dez said, and there was a strange smile in her voice. "Ship's dead, remember. But Osei says the station's got berth space. Noor—we're being *invited in*. All of us. First time in history anybody's invited us anywhere."

Noor looked back once at the *Meridian Rose*, drifting dark and gutted against the stars, her home and her armor and her escape route, given away in nineteen hours flat. It hurt more than she'd budgeted for. She let it hurt. That was hers too.

Then she pushed off from the junction and fell toward the light.

Below her, in the deep cold of vault ring one, the first name on Osei's list began, degree by degree, to warm.
