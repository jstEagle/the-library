## 3. Crew of the Long Answer

The gravity plating in Hold Three had died singing.

Sefa Okonkwo knew that sound better than she knew her own mother's voice—the high, keening whine of a graviton lattice shedding charge all at once, like a choir cut off mid-note. Nine seconds of it. She'd been elbow-deep in the coolant manifold when the floor let go of her, and for nine seconds she had floated in a slow tumble among wrenches and wire spools and Pip's abandoned ration wrappers, listening to the whole ship scream in a hundred small voices: cargo straps groaning, crates thudding against bulkheads, someone—Dov, probably—swearing in three languages at once.

Then gravity had come back like a fist, and everything in the hold had fallen at once, including Sefa, who had landed hard on her hip and lain there for a moment on the cold deck plating, staring up at the overhead lights, counting her own heartbeat.

Nine seconds. Not eight. Not ten. Exactly nine.

That was the part that kept her lying there after the pain registered. Gravity didn't fail *neatly*. Gravity failed like anything else failed—ugly, stuttering, asymmetric, one section lagging behind another while capacitors bled out at different rates. A clean nine-second blackout across an entire ship, and then a snap back to full field strength as if nothing had happened? That wasn't failure. That was *instruction*. Something had told the Long Answer's gravity to stop, and then told it to start again, and whatever could issue that kind of order was either very expensive or very old.

She got up, rubbed her hip, and went to find out which.

* * *

By the third hour she had a map of the damage drawn in grease pencil on the inside of her forearm, which was where Sefa kept all her important documents. The flicker had been hard on the ship in ways that wouldn't show up on any diagnostic readout for days. The ore pallets in Hold Two had shifted forty centimeters starboard and sheared two tie-down bolts; the water recycler had air-locked; and the long spine conduit running aft from the drive section—a piece of salvaged Concord military cable older than anyone aboard—had taken a surge spike that had scorched its insulation black along four meters of its length.

She worked through it the way she worked through everything: methodically, angrily, with music playing too loud in her earpiece. The Long Answer was a good ship. That was the thing people never understood about her until they'd served aboard. She was ugly—a hundred-and-forty-meter slab of mismatched hull plate with a drive section that looked like it had been welded on as an afterthought, because it had—but she was honest. Every scar on her told you exactly what had happened to her. Sefa had spent six years learning to read those scars, and in six years the ship had never once lied to her.

People were another matter.

She was splicing the last of the scorched conduit—stripping carbon off the conductors with a solvent brush, breathing through her mouth because the smell of burnt insulation always reminded her of the refinery fire back on Ceres Station, the one she didn't talk about—when the captain came down into the crawl space behind her.

She heard him before she saw him. Everyone aboard moved with their own signature, and Astrid Dravenmoor's signature was *quiet*. Deliberately quiet, the way a man moves when he learned early that being noticed was dangerous. Most captains clanked. Astrid drifted.

"Status," he said. Not a question. He never made them questions.

"Conduit's cooked but salvageable. Recycler needs a purge cycle, I'll run it tonight. Tie-downs replaced." She didn't look up from the splice. "You?"

A pause. A pause that lasted half a second too long.

"We took it well."

Sefa set down the solvent brush. She turned, slowly, still crouched in the crawl space with conduit dust on her sleeves, and looked up at her captain.

Astrid Dravenmoor was thirty-something by his records, though he carried himself older, like a man wearing a coat cut for someone else's shoulders. Dark hair going gray at the temples too early. Eyes the flat gray-green of deep-space ice, eyes that watched everything and gave back almost nothing. He was handsome in the way of a portrait—correct, composed, every feature in its right place—and Sefa had thought, more than once over six years, that he was handsome the way furniture is handsome. Beautiful. Inanimate. Built according to somebody's design.

But right now the composure had a crack in it. Small. Hairline. The kind of flaw you only saw if you'd spent years reading scars.

His hands were shaking.

Not much. Just a fine tremor at the fingertips as he braced one hand against the conduit housing, the other holding a data-slate he wasn't looking at. The tremor ran through him like current through a live wire, and he was holding himself very still around it, the way you hold still around a wound.

"You felt it too," Sefa said. "The nine seconds."

"Everyone felt it."

"Not everyone's hands are doing that."

He looked down at his own hand as if surprised to find it attached to him. Then he closed it into a fist, and the fist stopped shaking, and the mask came back down over his face so smoothly that if she hadn't been watching for it she'd have missed the seam entirely.

"Adrenaline," he said. "It'll pass."

"Mm." Sefa picked her solvent brush back up and turned to the conduit, giving him the gift of not being looked at. "You know what a clean nine-second flicker means, Captain. Grav systems don't fail like that. They get *told*."

"I know what it means."

"Do you?" She kept her voice light, working-light, the tone she used for talking about coolant pressures and bolt torques. "Because I've been on this boat six years, and I know you keep a locked cabin full of old encryption gear, and I know you flinch every time we pass within scanner range of a Concord buoy, and I know—" she tapped the scorch mark on the conduit with her brush handle, "—that whatever just happened scared you worse than the time we nearly ate a micrometeorite swarm through the forward hull. And that swarm scared *me*, and I don't scare."

Silence behind her. The hum of the ship filling it, the good honest hum of systems doing their work.

"Astrid." She used his first name, which she did maybe twice a year, and she felt him react to it like a touch on a bruise. "Whatever it is—you don't have to tell me. But don't tell me it's adrenaline. I patch this ship with my hands. I know what shaking means."

More silence. Then, quietly, in a voice she'd only heard from him once before—the night he'd pulled her out of a debt contract with a Meridian scrap-broker by paying it off with money he refused to explain:

"It's not the ship I'm worried about, Sefa."

And then he was gone, drifting back up the crawlway on those quiet feet, and Sefa sat alone in the conduit dark with burnt insulation under her fingernails and a cold little knot forming in her stomach.

* * *

The galley of the Long Answer smelled, that evening, like Dov's attempt at curry, which meant it smelled like scorched spice and stubbornness. The whole crew ended up there eventually, the way they always did after something went wrong—as if the room itself were a repair bay for people.

Pip arrived first, seventeen years old and all elbows, still rubbing the bruise on their shoulder from where a cargo crate had introduced itself during the flicker. Then Marta Okoye-Ito, their navigator, who'd spent the blackout strapped into her chair running collision projections and had come out of it with the grim satisfaction of a woman whose paranoia had been validated. Then Dov, bearing the curry like a penance. Then the rest, filtering in by degrees until the little room was full of steam and argument and the particular warmth of eleven people who had chosen each other over whatever lives they'd broken to get here.

Sefa sat at the end of the bench and watched them, and felt the knot in her stomach loosen despite itself.

This was the thing about the Long Answer nobody put in the brochures. Half the crew had come aboard running from something—debt, war, family, law—and the other half had come aboard having lost whatever they'd been running *toward*. And somehow the captain had gathered them all up anyway, this ragtag assortment of damaged goods, and welded them into something that functioned. Not perfectly. They argued about water rations and music volume and whether Pip was old enough for EVA duty. But when the gravity died and the cargo came loose, eleven people had checked on each other before checking on their own bunks. Sefa had seen real families do worse.

And every credit of that, every weld and every shared meal and every absurd tradition—like the birthday they threw for whoever's turn it was that month, since half of them didn't know their real birthdates—traced back to the quiet man at the head of the table who was currently laughing at something Pip said, a low rare laugh, while his eyes stayed somewhere else entirely.

He was doing it again. Laughing with his mouth. Watching with his eyes. His right hand resting flat on the table, pressing down, as if pinning something invisible.

"So I says to the broker," Dov was saying, waving his spoon, "I says, that's not a discount, that's a hostage situation—"

"—and then he signed anyway," Pip finished gleefully, "because the captain did the *face*—"

"What face?" Marta asked.

"The face! You know the face. Where he just looks at you like you're a math problem he's already solved."

Everyone laughed. Even Astrid smiled, briefly, genuinely, and for a moment the crack sealed over and he was simply their captain, sitting in the lamplight with curry steam rising around him, and Sefa let herself believe for one whole minute that whatever had shaken him would shake loose and pass, the way storms passed, the way everything on this ship eventually got patched.

Then the comm panel chimed.

Not the general channel. Not the watch channel. The private queue—the one that almost never sounded, the one wired straight to the captain's cabin with a filter only Astrid had ever configured. The chime cut through the galley noise like a knife through cloth, and every conversation stopped, and eleven faces turned toward the little amber light blinking on the wall.

Astrid had gone completely still.

Sefa watched him look at that light, and what she saw in his face in that unguarded half-second was not surprise. It was recognition. It was the look of a man hearing a voice he'd hoped was dead.

He stood. Smoothed his shirt. Put the mask back on, seam invisible.

"Excuse me," he said, calm as ballast. "I'll take that in my cabin."

And he left, and the galley slowly refilled with noise, and Sefa sat looking at the door he'd gone through, the cold knot back in her stomach, tighter now.

Because she'd caught something else, in that half-second before the mask. Underneath the recognition, underneath the fear, there had been something worse.

Grief. Fresh grief, sharp as the day it happened, dragged up out of wherever he kept it buried.

As if whoever was on that channel had died a long time ago—and had just, impossibly, spoken.
