## 22. A Name That Is Mine

The registry office on Ashfall Station smelled of recycled air and old paper, an anachronism someone in station administration had insisted on after the war-that-wasn't. Real paper, real ink, real ledgers — as if the truce itself needed to be written down somewhere gravity could hold it.

Astrid stood at the counter with his palms flat on the wood and felt his own pulse in his fingertips.

Behind him, the whole crew of the *Long Answer* had crammed into the little office under the pretense of needing to be somewhere else. Sefa was leaning against the doorframe with her arms crossed, trying very hard to look bored and failing spectacularly; her wrench kept twitching in her hand like a dog that wanted to bark. Pip sat cross-legged on the floor with a data-slate balanced on one knee, ostensibly doing inventory, actually watching every second. Old Tammas had brought flowers — actual cut flowers, grown in the hydroponics bay, absurdly fragrant in the sterile air — and was holding them behind his back like a man hiding contraband.

"You don't all have to be here," Astrid said, without turning around.

"We know," Sefa said.

The registrar, a small woman named Odile with silver threading her temples and the patient eyes of someone who had processed ten thousand identities, slid a form across the counter. "Legal name registration," she said. "Given name first. Family name second, if applicable. If no family name, you may leave it blank, or you may claim one." She looked up at him over her spectacles. "It's your choice. It always is, here. That's rather the point of the form."

Astrid stared at the blank lines.

Three years ago he had woken in a growth vat with another man's memories poured into him like ballast — Elias Dravenmoor's childhood on Meridian's green terraces, Elias's first ship, Elias's last morning alive and the face of the woman who killed him. For three years he had treated that inheritance like a disease to be managed. He had hidden in the cargo lanes between two hostile fleets, hauling ore under false transponder codes, answering to nothing because answering to anything felt like surrendering to the echo.

And then there had been nine seconds of falling. And a killer with a ledger. And a summit where he had worn a dead man's face like a mask over his own mouth. And a jump window closing on Veyra Solenne like a lid on a coffin, and the stunned silence afterward, and Sefa's hand on his shoulder in the dark of the gate infrastructure while alarms screamed and stations drifted — *I've got you,* she'd said, *whoever you are, I've got you* —

"Take your time," Odile said gently.

He picked up the pen. It was heavier than it looked. Everything true was heavier than it looked.

In the given-name line, he wrote: **Astrid**.

His hand did not shake. He noticed that, and was quietly astonished by it. Three months ago his hands had shaken so badly Sefa had cornered him in the corridor outside the galley and demanded to know what he was carrying. Now they were steady. Not because the fear was gone — the fear would never entirely be gone; he suspected it was load-bearing now, part of the structure of him — but because the weight had changed. He was no longer carrying a dead man's name like stolen cargo. He was carrying his own.

In the family-name line, he paused.

The room went very quiet. Even Pip stopped fidgeting.

Elias Dravenmoor had built a shipping empire on that name. Had bought fleets with it, married into it, been murdered for it. The name was a fortress and a tomb and a brand burned into everything Elias had touched — including, through the vat and the memory-dump and three years of hiding, Astrid himself.

He could leave it blank. Odile had said so. He could walk out of this office nameless and light, unclaimed by any history, owing nothing to any ghost.

But names weren't only inheritances. They were also addresses. Places where people could find you. And he had spent three years making sure no one could find him, and it had nearly cost him everyone who mattered.

He wrote: **Dravenmoor**.

Not because it was Elias's. Because it was his now — first, not second. Because when Corvane's patrol had boarded the *Long Answer* and the scanner had read his genetics and said *match: Elias Dravenmoor*, he had learned that the universe does not care what you call yourself; it cares what you do with what you are. And because somewhere out beyond the sealed gate, in whatever silence followed Solenne's fall, there were still records, still ledgers, still people who believed the Dravenmoor name meant power and theft and murder. Let them look it up and find this instead: a registry entry on a small station, witnessed by a ragtag crew, belonging to a man who had testified as himself and broken the inheritance clean through.

"Astrid Dravenmoor," Odile read aloud, and stamped the page, and the sound of the stamp was very small and very enormous. "Registered. First of his name."

"There's only one of me," Astrid said.

"That's what makes him *first*," Sefa said from the doorframe, and the crew laughed, and something in Astrid's chest unlocked that he hadn't known was locked.

* * *

They threw the party that evening in the *Long Answer*'s cargo bay, because the crew had decided — by what Sefa described as "a rigorous democratic process consisting of me deciding" — that a man who registers a legal identity deserves a birthday, and a birthday requires a date, and since Astrid had never had one, he would have to choose.

He had chosen the day gravity came back.

Not the day it failed — everyone remembered the nine seconds of falling, the cargo floating free, the screaming, the terrible slam of the world reasserting itself. No. He chose the day after, when the engineers of two formerly hostile fleets had worked side by side in the gate infrastructure to restore the spin and the field and the ordinary miracle of things staying where you put them. The day the coffee had stayed in the cups. The day Pip had run the length of the deck shouting *"it's back, it's back"* like the announcement of a holiday.

"It's not a real birthday," Astrid had protested, when Sefa proposed it.

"Neither is any of them," she'd said. "That's the whole trick. You pick a day and decide it means you survived another year. Congratulations. You survived. Pick the day."

So the cargo bay was strung with lights salvaged from three different ships and none of them matched, and Tammas had somehow produced a cake — a real one, baked in the galley oven that Sefa had repaired twice and cursed daily for six years — and the whole crew was there, plus a handful of guests who had become, without anyone announcing it, family too.

Admiral Corvane arrived out of uniform, which took Astrid a full ten seconds to process, and stood near the hatch holding a drink she clearly didn't intend to finish, wearing the expression of a woman attending a function under diplomatic protest and enjoying it anyway.

"I signed your charter," she said, when he drifted over. "The *Long Answer*'s. Independent registry, Ashfall flag, your name as master. It's official as of today." She paused. "You understand what that means. You're not a smuggler anymore. You're a shipping concern. With a reputation."

"That sounds worse."

"It is," she said, and almost smiled. "Every fleet, every syndicate, every gate authority in four systems is going to know your name now. The man who testified. The copy who refused to be an echo." She turned her glass slowly. "I've commanded a lot of people, Dravenmoor. I've never seen anyone do what you did at that hearing. Standing up in front of both fleets and saying *I am not him* — knowing half of them wanted you to be him, and the other half wanted you destroyed for pretending." She looked at him then, directly, and her voice dropped its admiral's polish. "My intelligence officers flagged your inconsistencies within a day of the summit. I let it stand because I needed the truce more than I needed the truth. You gave me both anyway. I don't forget debts like that."

"You don't owe me—"

"I keep accounts," Corvane said. "Ask anyone. It's a flaw." She drained the drink after all. "Happy birthday, Captain."

*Captain.* The word landed strangely and stayed. He rolled it over in his mind like a stone found on a beach, checking its weight.

Pip rescued him from having to answer, materializing at his elbow with the ferocious energy of a sixteen-year-old who had smuggled a data-slate off a murderer's flagship and never once let anyone forget it. "Speech time," Pip announced. "Sefa says speech time. Sefa made the cake so Sefa gets to make rules."

"I didn't make a cake," Sefa called from across the bay. "Tammas made the cake. I supervised. There's a difference and it matters."

"The speech," Pip insisted, tugging his sleeve. "Come on. Everyone's waiting."

Astrid looked out at the cargo bay — at the mismatched lights and the crooked banner someone had painted reading HAPPY FIRST BIRTHDAY ASTRID (the paint still tacky, Pip's handwriting unmistakable), at Tammas beaming behind the cake with its single candle, at Sefa leaning against the ore loader with her arms crossed and her eyes suspiciously bright, at Corvane pretending to examine a bulkhead, at the whole improbable congregation of people who had every reason to abandon him and had stood in the wreckage of his lie and chosen him anyway.

*I am not him,* he had told the assembled fleets. *I never was. My name is Astrid.*

He cleared his throat.

"I don't have much practice with speeches," he began, and someone — Sefa, definitely Sefa — snorted. "Three years ago I woke up in a vat with a dead man's life in my head, and everything I did after that was either hiding or pretending. And I told myself it was survival. And it was. But it was also cowardice, some of it, and it cost all of you something, and I know that, and I'm not going to stand here and pretend the birthday cancels the debt." He paused. Pip squeezed his sleeve. "But today I registered a name. Legally. Mine. First of its line. And the registrar asked if I wanted a family name, and I want you all to know that I thought about leaving it blank. Starting clean. Owing nothing to anybody."

"And?" Sefa said.

"And I wrote Dravenmoor," he said, "because a name isn't just where you come from. It's where people can find you. And I spent three years making sure nobody could find me, and it nearly got us all killed, and the only reason it didn't is that you people are stubborn enough to break into gate infrastructure and smuggle evidence off warships and follow a lying clone into the dark because—" his voice caught, and he let it, because hiding it would have been the old lie again, "—because you decided I was worth finding. So. Thank you. That's the speech. Someone light the candle before Tammas cries."

"Too late," Tammas said, crying.

They lit the candle. One flame, small and steady in the recycled air of a freighter that had hauled ore through contested orbits and come out the other side with a charter and a name. The crew sang — badly, off-key, Sefa deliberately a half-step behind everyone else out of what she claimed was principle — and Astrid stood in the middle of it with firelight on his face and thought about the man whose memories he carried. Elias Dravenmoor had thrown parties too, according to the inherited recollections: glittering affairs on Meridian's terraces, hundreds of guests, orchestras, fireworks reflected in the harbor. In every memory of them, Elias had stood apart. Watching. Counting allies. Measuring exits.

Astrid was not standing apart. Astrid was in the middle of the noise, with frosting on his sleeve because Pip had ambushed him, and there was nowhere he needed to exit to.

He blew out the candle.

"What'd you wish for?" Pip demanded.

"If I tell you it won't come true."

"That's superstition."

"I'm a man who legally registered an identity this morning," Astrid said. "Superstition is load-bearing."

Pip grinned and ran off to demand cake, and Sefa appeared at his shoulder the way she always did, silent as a failing bearing until she wanted to be heard.

"So," she said. "First day of being officially yourself. How's it feel?"

He considered it honestly, because she deserved honesty; she had bled for honesty, in the corridor where she'd confronted him, in the gate dark where she'd taken his hand, in the long weeks after the confession when trust had to be rebuilt plank by plank like a hull patch.

"Lighter," he said. "And heavier. Both at once. Like—" he searched for it, "—like putting down cargo I was never supposed to carry, and picking up a course I chose myself. Does that make sense?"

Sefa nodded slowly. "That's the most engineer thing you've ever said. I'm proud of you." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Course looks good from here, Captain. Where are we headed?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. The *Long Answer* had a charter now, and a name, and a crew, and the whole chart of four systems opening up like a held breath released. Contracts were already arriving — legitimate ones, strange ones, a request from Concord naval intelligence marked personal, a query from a Meridian historical society wanting to interview "the witness of Vesper Gate," a job offer from a colony out past the far beacon that paid triple rates because nobody else would fly that far.

Anywhere. Everywhere. For the first time in his existence, the answer wasn't *hide* or *pretend*. It was *choose*.

Later, long after the cake was gone and the lights dimmed and the crew had drifted to their bunks, Astrid climbed to the bridge alone and settled into the master's chair — his chair, the upholstery worn to the shape of a body that had spent years avoiding exactly this kind of sitting — and pulled up the navigation display. The starfield turned slowly. Out past the sealed Vesper Gate, the lanes were reopening. Traffic resuming. The ordinary commerce of an ordinary peace, purchased with nine seconds of falling and one act of testimony.

On the console, the ship's registry displayed its new designation:

**LONG ANSWER — Master: A. DRAVENMOOR (registered, Ashfall Station)**

*Astrid Dravenmoor.* First of his name. Echo no longer. Voice.

He keyed the helm and laid in a heading toward the far beacon — the triple-rate contract, the long dark, the unknown — and felt the engines answer beneath him like a heartbeat finally synchronized with his own.

The comm chimed once. A single incoming transmission, routed through Ashfall relay, origin unlisted. He frowned, and opened it.

The text was brief. Unfamiliar cipher, elegant, patient — and addressed not to Elias Dravenmoor, not to the witness of Vesper Gate, but to a name that had existed for less than a day.

**To Astrid Dravenmoor, first of his name:**

**There are others like you. Others who woke in vats with borrowed lives. Some of them are not hiding. Some of them are looking for the person who broke the inheritance — because they want to learn how.**

**We will find you when we're ready. Until then: happy birthday.**

**Choose well.**

Astrid sat very still in the dark of the bridge, the starfield turning, the new name glowing on the registry, and felt the old dread stir — and felt something else rise to meet it, steady and unafraid.

Whoever was out there, they knew how to find him now.

That was the point of a name.

He touched the helm, and the *Long Answer* turned her bow toward the deep black, running quiet and bright and hers — and his — into whatever came next.
