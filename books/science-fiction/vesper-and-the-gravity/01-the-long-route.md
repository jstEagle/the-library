## 1. The Long Route

The station woke Emrys the way it always did: not with an alarm but with a change in the hum. Vesper's spine ran through the bulkhead behind his bunk like a sleeping animal's breath, and when the rotation cycle brought the solar collectors into full face, that breath deepened by a fraction of a hertz. Nine years had taught his body to hear it. He no longer needed the clock.

He lay still for a moment in the grey half-light of the sleep compartment, listening to the station breathe, and performed the small morning inventory he'd never once written down: hull integrity nominal, air scrubbers cycling on schedule, one human being aboard, accounted for.

*Good morning,* said nobody.

He got up anyway.

The galley was four steps from the bunk and took eleven seconds to cross if he didn't hurry. He never hurried anymore. Hurrying was for people who expected something to be different at the other end of the room. He filled the kettle — actual water, heated on an actual coil, because the reclamation system's output tasted like pennies and regret — and stood watching the first threads of steam while the station turned its slow face toward the sun.

Outside the viewport above the counter, the corridor hung in the dark like a river seen from very high up. Two colonies, two civilizations, forty light-minutes to either side of him, and between them nothing but vacuum and the thin geometry of jump lanes that bent through Vesper's patch of nowhere like weather. Once, the traffic had been constant. Freighters in convoys, passenger liners with their running lights strung out like festival lanterns, couriers, tugs, the occasional diplomatic yacht burning expensive fuel to make a point about goodwill. The relays had chattered all day. Emrys had been twenty-two then, newly certified, assigned to the most boring posting in the corridor, and he had complained about it to Liora over a lag-delayed call that cost more than his weekly ration.

*You're a lighthouse keeper,* she'd said, laughing at him across nine seconds of silence. *You keep the light so other people can find each other. Somebody has to love the dark.*

Liora had loved the dark. She'd gone out into it at nineteen, seconded to the diplomatic corps, and the dark had kept her. Eleven years ago now. The war had started eight months later, as if the universe had been waiting for her to be safely out of range before it tore the corridor open.

The kettle clicked off. Emrys poured, carried his tea to the operations deck, and began the day.

* * *

Morning calibration was a liturgy, and like all liturgies its power lay in the fact that nothing changed.

He settled into the operator's cradle — a chair designed for three shifts of technicians and occupied, these days, by one — and woke the consoles in sequence. The relay arrays fanned out beyond the hull on their articulated spars, six great lattice dishes that could catch a whisper from either colony and pass it along, clean and amplified, to the other side. That was the whole purpose of Vesper. A middleman. A throat. For nine years, a closed one.

"Vesper," he said, because he said it every morning, "status."

The station AI answered in its flat, patient voice, a voice assembled from a thousand archived announcements until it sounded like everyone and no one. *"All systems nominal. Relay alignment within tolerance. No scheduled traffic."*

"No scheduled traffic." The words were a joke between them, or would have been, if jokes required two people. There had been no scheduled traffic in eight years, eleven months, and some number of days Emrys made himself stop counting each time he caught himself counting.

He ran the alignment routines anyway. Each dish reported its attitude; each attitude drifted, minutely, under thermal stress and micrometeorite taps; each drift needed correcting by fractions of degrees that no one would ever notice. He corrected them. His hands knew the sequence without consulting him — dish one, dish two, dish three — and while they worked, the rest of him drifted where it always drifted, down the long corridors of memory that the station's silence had worn smooth as river stones.

There had been a final transmission. Of course there had. Every war has a last conversation before both sides decide talking is a form of weakness. Emrys had been on shift when the colonial authorities on both ends pulled their channels simultaneously — coordinated, someone later suggested, which meant even the ending had been negotiated, just not by anyone who wanted peace. One hour the relays were carrying ten thousand voices an hour. The next, nothing. Static with a shape where the voices used to be.

His orders had arrived within the day: maintain the infrastructure. Log all traffic. Await further instruction.

No further instruction ever came. Both governments had apparently decided that a functioning relay station staffed by one junior technician was beneath the dignity of their attention, and so Emrys remained, a comma left in a sentence everyone else had stopped writing.

He finished the alignments. He logged them. The log entry read the same as yesterday's and the day before's, and he entered it with the same care he'd have given a launch manifest, because the alternative was to enter it carelessly, and carelessness was the first crack, and cracks in a man alone don't get patched.

* * *

Midday was traffic watch.

He took it in the observation blister, a bubble of transparent composite capping the station's forward spine, because watching was easier when the watching had somewhere to look. The corridor spread below him in its slow majesty: dust motes of debris catching sunlight, the distant smudge of a nebula the charts called Kestrel's Veil and the colonists, on both sides, had once called simply *the curtain*. Ships came through sometimes. Not many. Not talking ships.

The silent traffic was its own strange ecology. Smugglers mostly, running luxury goods and worse between colonies that officially did not exist to each other. Ghost freighters flying dark, riding the lanes on inertia and nerve. And salvage crews — those he watched with a colder eye — military-registered tenders picking through the war's leavings, stripping dead hulks in the debris fields where whole fleets had gone to die in the corridor's early battles.

Every contact, he logged. Registry, vector, estimated mass, duration in corridor. It was the job. It was also, though he'd have denied it under torture, a kind of company. Out here even strangers were strangers together.

At 1400 station time, a contact bloomed on the passive array: a small vessel, transponder dark, cutting a careful line through the outer lane. Emrys tracked it with the ease of long practice, hands moving across the console, and spoke aloud as he worked, because speaking aloud was another part of the ritual and the ritual was load-bearing.

"Contact, unregistered, bearing two-seven-one relative, velocity low. Running quiet." A pause while the optics resolved it. "Freighter frame, old. Pre-war hull, maybe. Nobody home on any channel I can hear." He typed the entry. "Logged."

"You know," he told the AI, who listened the way furniture listens, "I used to think I'd get used to this. Everyone says you get used to things. That's what they tell you about grief, too. You get used to it." He sipped his tea, gone lukewarm in the way tea always went lukewarm during traffic watch, because drinking it properly would have required leaving the console, and leaving the console felt like abandoning a post even when the post was watching nothing happen. "But you don't get used to it. You just get smooth. Like a stone in a stream. The water doesn't stop. It just stops hurting quite so much, and eventually you can't remember what shape you were before."

The AI said, *"Your heart rate suggests elevated emotional state."*

"Thank you, Vesper. Very comforting."

*"Would you like me to play music?"*

It asked every day. It had asked every day for nine years, drawing from some subroutine written by a designer who had understood loneliness only theoretically. Some days he said yes. Today he said, "Not yet," and the word *yet* sat in the air of the blister, doing more work than he intended.

* * *

Afternoon was maintenance, and maintenance was the true church.

He suited up and went outside, because Tuesdays — and every day was Tuesday if you decided it was — were EVA days. The suit was older than his assignment, patched at the left knee where a spar clamp had bitten it in year three, and it smelled inside like his own exertions recycled through filters until breathing felt like borrowing. He clipped his tether to the spine rail and walked the station's length in slow magnetic steps, and Vesper turned beneath him, and the stars did not move, and it was the closest thing to a walk outdoors that the rest of his life would ever contain.

Dish four had a fouled gimbal bearing. He could have let the software compensate — the compensation was well within tolerance, would stay within tolerance for months — but letting things slide was how stations died, and stations dying meant Emrys dying, and besides, the work was good. Work with your hands, his father had said, back on Earth, in a house with too many children and too little money, work with your hands and the day belongs to you even when nothing else does.

He worked. The bearing came free with a groan of cold metal. He greased it, torqued it, tested it, and for twenty minutes the entire universe consisted of a wrench, a bolt, and the small satisfaction of a thing made right.

From out here he could see the whole station at once: the spine, the hab ring with its single warm window, the six dishes spread like petals. A machine built to connect two worlds, standing in the one place neither world could see. He thought, not for the first time, that there was probably a word for what he was. Relic. Custodian. Fossil-in-progress. Lighthouse keeper, Liora would have said, and grinned, and he would have thrown something soft at her.

He checked the time against the schedule in his head. Call window. Every evening at 1900, station time, he opened a channel to himself — a loop, a recording, thirty seconds of his own voice played back through the relay arrays at minimum power, addressed to no one, aimed at nothing. He'd started it in year one as a systems test. He'd kept it going because stopping felt like admitting something.

He went inside, sealed the lock, and cycled his suit down.

* * *

Evening meal was rehydrated noodles with protein cubes, eaten at the ops console because the mess table seated twelve and he'd stopped sitting at it around year four, when setting a place for one had begun to feel less like eating and more like staging a photograph of absence. At the console he could pretend he was on duty. Duty was a place to put the body while the mind did whatever the mind did in the evenings, which was mostly replay.

Tonight it replayed the last call with Liora. Not the final one — he kept that one locked away, rarely visited, too heavy to lift — but the last *good* one, three weeks before she shipped out. She'd been promoted, she'd been radiant, she'd talked over him twice with excitement and he'd pretended to mind. *When this is over,* she'd said, *when they sign whatever they're going to sign, I'm coming through your corridor on a diplomatic run, and you're going to have to log me like any other contact, and I'm going to make you say my name into your official record.* And he'd laughed and said, *Delacroix, Liora, diplomatic corps, purpose of visit: annoying my brother.*

Eight years, eleven months, and some number of days since anyone had come through his corridor at all.

He washed the bowl. He logged the day's final entries. Hull nominal, air nominal, traffic: one unregistered contact, departed. Systems: maintained. Personnel: one.

Then the ritual he never skipped, the last office of the day. He keyed the internal channel and said, "Vesper, confirm overnight watch parameters."

*"Passive arrays active. Automated logging enabled. Wake protocols armed."* A pause, exactly the length a considerate person might leave. *"Goodnight, Emrys."*

The AI wasn't supposed to use his name. It was a flag in some forgotten config file, a personalization option he'd switched on in year two during a weak moment and never switched off, and every night it said goodnight to him in its voice made of everyone, and every night he said goodnight back, and the exchange held the station together as much as any bolt he'd torqued.

"Goodnight, Vesper."

He dimmed the ops deck, walked the four steps plus eleven seconds to his bunk, and lay down in the grey dark to listen to the station breathe.

Sleep came the way it always came — slowly, reluctantly, circling him like something deciding whether he was worth the trouble — and he was nearly under, nearly at the bottom of the smooth warm water where the days didn't hurt, when the console chimed.

Not the overnight-log chirp. Not the thermal alarm. The docking alert.

Emrys surfaced so fast he cracked his elbow on the bunk frame. He was at the console in bare feet before his mind fully arrived, staring at the approach plot, certain it was a sensor ghost, a reflection, a nine-year-old piece of debris throwing an echo. Contacts didn't dock at Vesper. Contacts *passed* Vesper. In nine years, in eight years, eleven months, and some number of days, nothing had ever asked to come inside.

The plot resolved. Mass: medium, freighter-class. Vector: intercept, decelerating, deliberate. Transponder: none.

And then, because the universe had apparently decided he hadn't suffered enough today, the automated handshake protocol kicked in — the ancient pre-war docking standard, the one every ship in the corridor had once spoken fluently — and the unknown vessel transmitted its approach request, clean and formal and impossible, with a registry code attached.

Emrys looked up the registry with fingers that did not feel like his own.

Decommissioned. Struck from the rolls seven years ago. Listed destroyed with all hands in the second battle of the corridor.

The ship was coming anyway.

And riding in its manifest data, tucked into the passenger field where no passenger should be, one name, spelled correctly, dated correctly, wearing his sister's name like a coat:

**DELACROIX, L.**

Emrys stood in the cold light of the console, tea abandoned, heart hammering out a rhythm the station had never heard, and reached — slowly, deliberately, the way a man touches a wound to learn if it's real — for the airlock controls.

He armed them.

Then he went to meet whatever the night had brought him.
