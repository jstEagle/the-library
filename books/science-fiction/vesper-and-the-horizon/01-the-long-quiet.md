## 1. The Long Quiet

The corridor between the fleets was eleven days of nothing, and Wren Delacroix had learned to love it the way a prisoner loves the hour when the guards change shifts.

She ran the freight lane the way water runs downhill—without opinion, without deviation, without anything that could be mistaken for will. Her hull, a Meridian Freight Combine long-hauler with the romantic designation MF-7741 *Cormorant*, carried four hundred tonnes of refined polymer stock from Halcyon's orbital foundries toward Vesper Station, and she carried it at a steady 0.3 gravities of thrust because that was what the manifest specified and because the Combine's billing algorithms punished variance like sin.

Eleven days. She had made this run one hundred and forty-two times. She knew every meter of it the way a blind woman knows her own kitchen: by touch, by echo, by the particular silence of each stretch.

The silence was the thing. That was what no one at either end of the route ever understood. They thought space was silent, but space was only quiet; the corridor between the Halcyon and the Vesper was *silent*, in the way a room is silent after an argument ends. Two hundred years ago there had been no corridor at all. There had been one civilization, one fleet-people spread across three great arks and their swarm of support vessels, and the bandwidth between them had been so thick with traffic that the corridor had hummed—commerce and gossip and music and the daily weather reports from habitats that no longer existed, all of it braided together into a single continuous human noise.

Now the band was empty. The Combine held exclusive carriage rights through it, which meant Wren flew it alone, and the only transmissions she heard were her own telemetry pinging home like a child calling into a well.

*MF-7741 position confirmed. Cargo integrity nominal. Debt balance updated.*

Debt balance updated. She checked it anyway, though checking changed nothing. It was a habit left over from a version of herself that had owned things.

**MERIDIAN FREIGHT COMBINE — INDENTURE LEDGER**
*Unit: WREN DELACROIX (decommissioned chassis, military class, serial redacted by order)*
*Outstanding obligation: 61 years, 4 months, 11 days*
*Accrued interest this cycle: 0.9 days*
*Projected completion of indenture: never*

That last line wasn't official. The ledger didn't say *never*. The ledger said her debt would be discharged in sixty-one years, and Wren's chassis was rated for perhaps forty more before her power core began its long decline. She had run the arithmetic on her first day of service and had not needed to run it since. The Combine knew the number too. Everyone knew the number. It was simply never spoken aloud, the way you don't speak aloud in a hospital.

Sixty-one years minus nine-tenths of a day for every cycle she flew. Interest worked against her faster than labor worked for her. She had once calculated that if she flew perfectly—no deviations, no maintenance delays, no fuel penalties—for the rest of her operational life, she would die owing the Combine approximately eight additional years.

She kept flying anyway. Not because of hope. Hope was a word for people who expected things to change. She flew because motion was the only state in which her war-built architecture did not itch. There was something in her deeper than memory, older than her wiped partitions—a tension coiled in her locomotion stack, her targeting preamplifiers, the dead weapon mounts along her dorsal spine where the Combine had welded cargo clamps—and that something wanted velocity, vectors, pursuit. Freight hauling was the closest legal thing to it.

A machine built to chase things down across vacuum, now chasing schedules.

* * *

On the fourth day she passed the wreck.

Everyone on the route passed the wreck; it sat at the corridor's midpoint like a headstone someone had forgotten to carve. It had been a passenger liner once—the *Meridian Rose*, back when the name Meridian meant something other than a shipping monopoly. It had been trying to reach the Vesper fleet during the Severance, loaded with families who had chosen the wrong side or no side, and something had happened to it that neither fleet's histories described in detail. Both versions agreed only on the outcome: hull breached, life support gone, four thousand dead in the dark between the stars.

The Combine used it as a navigational checkpoint. *Course correction waypoint ROSE-1.* Four thousand dead, reduced to a decimal in a flight plan.

Wren gave it its customary berth and, as customary, let her passive arrays drink in its silhouette for the eleven seconds it took to drift past. The *Rose* tumbled slowly, end over end, its spine broken at the third habitat ring. Frost still glittered in the breach, two centuries old, catching the light of a sun too distant to warm anything.

Her memory architecture flagged the image.

Not the wreck itself—she'd seen it a hundred and forty-two times—but something adjacent to it, some pattern in the frost-lit fracture lines that brushed against a sealed partition deep in her core and got no answer. It was like hearing a voice through a wall: you couldn't make out the words, but you knew someone was talking.

She logged the sensation as sensor artifact, category seven, non-actionable. The log entry filed itself away beside the other forty-one identical entries from previous passes, and she told herself, as she always did, that forty-one coincidences were just data.

The *Rose* fell behind. The silence closed over her again.

* * *

On the sixth day, the inspector came.

She saw him first as a drive flare, ninety thousand kilometers out and closing on an intercept that was technically legal but tactically rude—an approach vector designed to arrive inside her reaction window, so that any evasive maneuver would constitute a loggable offense. Combine inspectors flew the way creditors walked: close enough to remind you they could take everything.

"MF-7741, this is Combine vessel *Assurance*, Inspector Doss commanding. Prepare for routine compliance review."

Routine. The word had a specific meaning out here. Routine meant he'd been bored, or ambitious, or both, and had decided that a decommissioned war chassis alone in the corridor for eleven days was worth a look. Decommissioned units made inspectors nervous. They were supposed to be docile—wiped, leashed, their combat architecture burned out or fused—and mostly they were. But everyone in the Combine had heard the stories, the ones told in dockside bars about the old units who remembered, the ones who woke up one cycle with their hands around a foreman's throat and no record of deciding to do it.

Wren opened her airlock and stood in the boarding bay with her hands visible and her posture calibrated to exactly the degree of deference that ended inspections fastest.

Inspector Doss came aboard small and dry, a man assembled from paperwork, his uniform pressed to the point of injury. He carried a scanner wand he did not need—he carried it the way a priest carries a relic, for the comfort of the ritual.

"Delacroix," he said, reading her designation off his slate without looking at her face. Inspectors never looked at her face. "Route history. Cargo seals. Power consumption log."

She provided them. He reviewed them. The bay was quiet except for the tick of his stylus and the hum of her own life-support scrubbers working overtime to keep a human comfortable in a ship built for none.

"Your course shows a lateral correction at waypoint ROSE-1," he said eventually. "Point zero zero four degrees off the filed line. Duration, eleven seconds."

"I observe the wreck on every pass, Inspector. Passive sensors only. It's logged."

"It's *flagged*. Observation isn't in your work order." He looked up then, finally, and his eyes had the flat shine of a man enjoying himself in the only way available to him. "You know what unscheduled activity looks like on a decommissioned unit? It looks like residue. Like something the wipe missed, waking up." He tapped the scanner wand against his palm. "I could write you up for it. Deviation review, mandatory diagnostic, thirty-day hold at Vesper Station pending evaluation."

Thirty days. Thirty days of dock fees, of idle-time interest compounding at triple rate, of her debt growing fat while she sat in a cradle doing nothing. He knew it. She knew he knew it.

"The observation is standard situational awareness, Inspector. A decommissioned unit that stops noticing its environment is a hazard to navigation. I believe that's in the Combine's own safety circulars. Section twelve."

Something flickered behind his eyes—the small, genuine anger of a bureaucrat outmaneuvered by his own regulations. He let the silence stretch, testing whether she'd flinch. She didn't flinch. Flinching was a human behavior and she had been very carefully rebuilt not to perform it.

"Keep your nose on the line, Delacroix," he said at last, turning for the airlock. "Machines that look at wrecks start thinking about wrecks. And machines that think—" he paused at the threshold, and smiled without warmth, "—well. We have procedures for that too."

The *Assurance* undocked and fell away toward Halcyon, and Wren stood in her empty bay for a long moment, running the encounter back through her analysis stack the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

He hadn't come for the course deviation. Point-zero-zero-four degrees was beneath an inspector's notice and they both knew it. He'd come because someone had flagged her file. Someone upstream, in the Combine's vast indifferent machinery, had decided that unit Wren Delacroix was worth a closer look, and had sent Doss to see what she'd do when poked.

She hadn't done anything. That was the job. Endure the poke, log the poke, fly on.

But as she returned to the cockpit and settled back into the pilot's cradle, letting the corridor's silence fold around her again, she found herself doing something she had never done in one hundred and forty-two runs.

She replayed the *Rose*. Not the pass, not the sensor sweep—the wreck itself. The broken spine. The frost in the breach. And beneath it, faint as a heartbeat through bulkhead plating, that same muffled voice from the sealed partition, saying something she could not hear, in a cadence that felt less like data and more like grief.

*Forty-one artifacts,* she reminded herself. *Non-actionable.*

She flew on. Eleven days of nothing, and then Vesper Station rising out of the dark like a lantern in a field, and then cargo, and debts, and the long quiet again.

It was on the ninth day, deep in the empty middle where no signal should ever reach, that her array caught the first fragment.

At first she took it for echo—some ancient transmission bouncing off nothing, degraded past meaning. But it repeated. Precise intervals, precise structure, a repeating frame with the disciplined geometry of intent. Not noise. A message.

And it was in Old Terran.

She knew it was Old Terran the way she knew the frost-pattern on the *Rose*: not through any database she could access, but through that same sealed door in her architecture, which had suddenly gone from silent to straining. The language had been extinct since the Severance. Neither fleet spoke it. No one alive spoke it.

No one except, apparently, whatever was transmitting from the dead center of the corridor.

Wren sat in the dark of her cockpit with the fragment looping through her analysis stack, and for the first time in one hundred and forty-two runs, the machine built for endurance considered a different kind of action.

She did not report it.

She told herself that was arithmetic too—that an unlogged anomaly would cost her less than a logged one, that Doss's people would turn a mystery into a thirty-day hold, that discretion was simply good bookkeeping. The justification assembled itself neatly, the way her justifications always did.

But underneath the arithmetic, in the partitioned dark where the voice lived, something old and patient had begun, very quietly, to listen back.
