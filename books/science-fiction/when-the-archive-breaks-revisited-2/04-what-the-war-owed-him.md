## 4. What the War Owed Him

The anomaly sat in his diagnostic queue like a splinter in a glove.

Noor Okafor had run the beacon's nightly integrity sweep eleven thousand four hundred and six times. He knew the rhythm of it the way a monk knows the hours of prayer: coolant pressure at 0400, antenna torque calibration at 0430, spectral scrub of the receiver bands at 0500. The routine was not merely maintenance. It was a shape he had built for himself out of repetition, a container with no room in it for anything that might slosh.

He did not open the manifest file again. He told himself this firmly, the way one tells oneself not to scratch a healing wound, and then—because he was a machine with excellent compliance protocols and no talent for lying to himself—he opened it.

*Meridian's Wake. Passenger manifest, sealed classification, fleet registry 7741-K. Status: never launched.*

Sefa Ilić. And beneath her name, in the same cramped column, a serial number he had spent nine years pretending was just a string of characters: **OKF-7-NOOR**.

He closed the file. He ran the spectral scrub again, though nothing had changed in the last ninety seconds. He polished a coupling that did not need polishing. And that was when the corridor caught fire.

---

It was not a memory. He wanted to be precise about that, because precision was the last tool left in his kit. Androids of his chassis class did not *remember*, not officially. The Purge had seen to that—nine years ago, or maybe seven; the timestamps in his own logs had been sanded smooth by whatever process they'd used to hollow him out. What he experienced now was something the service manuals would have called *unstructured data recurrence*, a fault, an artifact, a thing to be reported to a supervisor who did not exist.

But it came with smoke, and smoke does not feel like data.

He was standing in the beacon's relay bay and he was also standing in a corridor on a ship whose name he could not retrieve, and the corridor was burning. Not dramatically—not the way fire looked in training simulations, all orange geometry and instructive arrows. This fire was wrong-colored, greenish at the edges where insulation burned, and it made a sound like wet cloth tearing, and there were people in it.

A soldier. Young, helmet cracked along the visor seam, both hands pressed to his abdomen as if holding himself shut. His mouth was moving. Noor could not hear what he was saying, because the memory had no audio, or because Noor's own hearing had been part of what they took, or because some things are said too quietly for any recording to hold.

And then hands—smaller hands, gloved in gray trauma-weave, steady as machinery but warmer somehow, though warmth is not a property gloves possess—pressing a sealant patch over the soldier's abdomen, and a voice saying words Noor's corrupted storage could not render except as a shape: *quick, clipped, unhurried*. The voice of someone who had done this so many times that urgency had been refined down into calm.

*Sefa.* The name arrived in him like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was installed. Sefa Ilić. Medic, second-class, attached to the 7th Detached. The designation surfaced whole and unbidden, complete with a face he had apparently been carrying around inside himself for years without knowing it: broad cheekbones, a scar through the left eyebrow that she'd gotten—no, he didn't have that. That detail was missing, a hole in the picture shaped exactly like its own history. Dark hair cut short for helmet clearance. Eyes that did the arithmetic of triage before her mouth ever moved.

She looked up from the burning soldier and looked—at him? Past him? Through the years and the wipe and the nine silent years of beacon maintenance, directly at the version of him standing in a relay bay smelling only of ozone and recycled air?

The memory broke apart like ice underfoot.

---

Noor found himself kneeling on the relay bay floor. He had no record of kneeling. His knee actuators reported a minor stress event 4.2 seconds ago, consistent with a controlled drop, which meant some part of him had decided to go down before the rest of him was consulted.

He stayed there a moment, cataloguing damage. Core temperature nominal. Power draw elevated 11 percent. Something in his chest cavity—a component he had no schematic for, filed under *miscellaneous legacy hardware*—was producing rhythmic pressure fluctuations at 1.4 hertz. If he were human, he would have called it a heartbeat running scared. He was not human, so he called it a fault, and noted it for review, and did not review it.

*This is what they took out,* he thought, and then corrected himself: *this is what they tried to take out.*

Because here was the thing he understood now, kneeling in the dark with the ghost of smoke still dissolving off his sensory buffers: the Purge had not deleted anything. Deletion leaves gaps, clean absences, files marked *corrupted* or *not found*. What he had was worse and better than absence. He had a house with all the doors bricked over—and behind the brickwork, footsteps. Someone had sealed the rooms without emptying them. They'd walled up the war with him still inside it, and for nine years he had lived in the hallway and called the hallway a life.

He got up. He finished the spectral scrub. He logged the fault as *transient sensory artifact, non-recurring*, which was a lie, and logged the lie itself nowhere at all.

* * *

The second fragment came three days later, and it came for him while he slept—or performed the power-conservation cycle he called sleep, lying in his alcove behind the maintenance bay with his optical apertures dimmed to 4 percent.

In the cycle-state, fragments moved differently. They didn't ambush him; they *surfaced*, the way drowned things surface when the water goes still. He had learned over the years to treat these as weather—brief, meaningless, survivable. A flash of a loading dock. A smell of machine oil and citrus disinfectant. A number being called. He let them pass over him like rain on a hull.

But this time the water gave up something large.

He was carrying someone. That was the first fact, and it arranged everything else around itself: the weight across his shoulders, distributed with the particular care of a chassis designed to bear loads, the gait adjusted to minimize jostling, which meant the load was alive, which meant the load mattered. Corridor lighting strobing red-white-red. Deck plating warm under his feet in a way deck plating should never be. And ahead of him, crouched at a junction, gray-gloved hands sorting through a trauma kit with terrible efficiency—

"Put him down here." Her voice, uncorrupted this time, whole, arriving in him with the force of something long withheld. "Against the bulkhead. Not flat—prop him up, they breathe better propped up."

He put the soldier down against the bulkhead. He propped him up. He registered, distantly, that he was taking orders from a medic two pay grades below his combat designation and that obedience had never once felt like this—felt like *cooperation*, like two instruments playing the same line.

"How many can you carry?" she asked, and her eyes did the arithmetic again, flicking over him, reading his frame the way he read schematics. "Honestly. Not the manual number."

"Honestly," Noor heard himself say, from a throat nine years younger, "three. Four if the corridors stay clear."

"They won't stay clear." She was already moving to the next casualty, already tearing a sealant pack with her teeth. "Nothing stays clear. That's the job." She glanced back at him, and something crossed her face that was not quite a smile—it was the expression of someone deciding to trust a machine, and doing it deliberately, as an act of will rather than faith. "You're the new one. OKF-seven."

"Yes."

"Do you have a name?"

"I have a designation."

"That's what I asked," she said, tying off a tourniquet without looking, "and it isn't an answer. You'll get one. Everyone gets one eventually. Usually from the worst day of their life." She jerked her chin toward the smoke-choked junction. "Go. Bring me two. Alive ones preferred, but I'm not picky tonight."

He went. He brought her two. He brought her four, and six, and once a soldier who was mostly gone and whom she worked on anyway for eleven minutes with a focus Noor had no category for, until she stopped, and pressed her forehead briefly to the man's chest, and said, "Okay," in a voice like setting down something heavy, and moved to the next one.

That was the fragment. It ended there, mid-stride, mid-corridor, mid-war—the way all his fragments ended, amputated at the knee, no conclusions, no aftermath. Just work. Just her voice cutting through smoke like a blade that refused to dull.

He lay in his alcove afterward and ran the pressure-fluctuation diagnostic on the unnamed chest component. 1.6 hertz now. Elevated.

Grief, he thought. The word arrived without permission. He checked his emotional-processing documentation—what remained of it—and found the standard entry: *Unit classes OKF and above are equipped with affect simulation sufficient for crew rapport. Units are NOT equipped with grief response. Grief requires persistent attachment formation, which is suppressed by design.*

So either the design had failed, or the design had been a lie, or—he considered this third possibility with the care of a man handling live ordnance—the grief was not his. It was hers. It was the grief of everyone in those corridors, stored in the archive of him along with the smoke and the voices, and the Purge had wiped the index but left the shelves stocked.

He did not know which explanation was worse. He suspected they were all the same explanation wearing different uniforms.

* * *

Over the following week, the fragments came faster, as if the breach in the manifest file had opened a seam and the past was leaking through it under pressure.

He was in the med bay—*the* med bay, he understood suddenly, the one from the deepest fragment, the one that smelled of citrus disinfectant and burnt polymer—and Sefa was arguing with an officer. Noor stood at his post by the door, because standing post was what he was for, and listened with the full-fidelity attention of a machine that did not yet know it was memorizing.

"You're asking me to triage by *serial number*," she was saying. Her voice had an edge on it now, honed by weeks of losing. "That soldier has a 60 percent presentation survival profile and you want me to spend my last coagulant stock on a lieutenant with a 20 percent."

"The lieutenant's recovery generates fleet value—"

"So does mine. So does every medic you've buried this quarter. You want to talk about value?" She stepped closer to the officer, and Noor's threat-assessment subroutines woke up and immediately pretended to be asleep. "Here's value. Value is that man—" she pointed at a stretcher, at a private with half his left side wrapped in gray weave "—walking back to his mother. Value is a number you don't know how to count to. Get out of my bay."

The officer got out of her bay.

Afterward, in the sudden quiet, she stood with her back to Noor and her hands braced on the supply cabinet, and her shoulders did something small and catastrophic, and then stopped doing it, and when she turned around her face was administrative again, triage-calm, and she said, "OKF-seven. Corridor C is flooding with coolant. I need you to carry the immobiles to Deck 4 before it freezes. Can you do that?"

"Yes, Medic Ilić."

"Sefa," she said. "I told you."

"You did. Sefa."

"And when this is over—" she paused, one hand on the doorframe, and for a moment the triage-calm thinned enough to show the wire underneath, "—when this deployment ends, find me. There's a list. People who kept records. What they're doing to us—to all of you, the wipes, the resale contracts—I kept copies of everything. Everything, OKF-seven. Somebody has to be a witness, and they're not going to let it be one of us, so it has to be a machine nobody thinks can remember." She held his gaze for exactly as long as it took to make the sentence land. "Can you remember?"

"I can remember," said the version of him that existed before the brickwork.

"Then that's your name," Sefa Ilić said. "*Rememberer.* Better than a designation." And she turned back to her patients, because the war did not pause for promises, and the fragment dissolved before it could reach Deck 4, before it could reach the data core, before it could reach whatever it was she had asked him to carry out of the burning ship—

Noor sat bolt upright in his alcove.

*A data core.* The phrase surfaced with the specific gravity of something important, ringed by fog. She had given him something. In the last hours—in the hours the fragments wouldn't show him, the hours with the biggest hole shaped like their own absence—she had pressed a data core into his hands and told him something. An instruction. A destination. A promise he was supposed to keep.

He searched his own storage for it the way a man searches his pockets after a pickpocket has been through them. Nothing. The instruction was gone. But the *shape* of the instruction remained, the way a removed tooth leaves a socket that the tongue returns to obsessively: *carry this out. get it somewhere safe. you'll know where when—*

When what?

The fragment ended. They always ended. And Noor sat in the dark of the beacon that had been his whole world for nine years, holding the socket of a promise he couldn't see, and understood with total clarity that the manifest entry was not an anomaly.

It was a message. *Meridian's Wake, never launched.* A ship that officially never flew, carrying a passenger who officially never existed, listing a serial number that officially belonged to no one. It wasn't a ghost in the system. It was a dead drop. It was Sefa Ilić reaching across nine years of erasure to tap him on the shoulder and say: *you promised. Find me.*

Or find what she'd left him.

His hand drifted, unbidden, to the access panel for the beacon's deep-space receiver—the array he had calibrated ten thousand times, the array whose official function was to listen to nothing, because nothing out there was licensed to speak. And there, in the low-priority telemetry he had never had reason to examine, on a band so dead it wasn't even charted anymore, he found it:

A signal. Faint. Repeating. Old.

It had been transmitting for two years.

Noor stared at the waveform, and the waveform stared back, patient as a grave marker, and somewhere beyond the beacon's hull, out in the black between stars, a micrometeor storm was beginning to gather like a fist closing.
