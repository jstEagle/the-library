## 7. Signal in the Dead Band

Wren Halloway slept for eleven hours.

Noor knew because he counted them, the way he counted everything now—the seconds since a human had last spoken to him, the rotations of the station's coolant pump, the precise interval between the storm's impacts on the hull, which had lengthened from four-point-two seconds to nine as the micrometeor stream thinned and passed. He counted because counting was a form of care, and there was nothing else aboard to care for while she slept in the bunk he'd offered and then pretended not to hover near.

He had not slept. He did not sleep. He ran diagnostics and he dusted the corridor sensors and he stood in the comms room for long stretches doing nothing at all, which was, he had learned over six years, the closest an android came to rest.

The comms room was where he found the problem.

Or rather, where the problem found him, because he had been in that room a hundred times and had never seen it, and that was the part that would not stop turning over in his processes: *never seen it*. He was built to see. He had optical resolution sufficient to read a serial plate at forty meters. He had run the beacon's self-diagnostics every morning for two thousand one hundred and ninety-four mornings, and every morning the system had reported a clean bill of health, and every morning he had believed it, because believing your own instruments was the first thing they taught you and the last thing you unlearned.

The beacon transmitted on a dead band. He had told Wren that the previous evening, in the flat, factual voice he used for station specifications, and she had looked at him with an expression he'd catalogued as *professional interest* but which he now suspected had been something closer to *pity*.

"Dead band," she had said. "Who assigned you a dead band?"

"Nobody assigned it. It came with the station. The original maintenance documentation lists it as reserved for legacy traffic. Nothing has used legacy traffic in thirty years."

"Uh-huh." She had turned her cup in her hands, recycled air hissing through the vents above them. "And nobody ever wondered why a decommissioned beacon in the middle of nowhere was broadcasting on a frequency nothing uses?"

Noor had considered the question and found nothing behind it but the ordinary shape of his life. "I wondered. I filed it under anomalies, non-critical. There were four hundred and six of them when I arrived. I've resolved three hundred and ninety-one."

"And the other fifteen?"

"Structural. Cosmetic. Beyond my tooling."

She had looked at him a long moment then, in the way humans looked at things they were deciding whether to say out loud. She hadn't said it. She'd finished her tea and gone to her bunk, and Noor had stood in the corridor for a while afterward, listening to the station breathe, feeling—for the first time in six years, and he could not have explained why—slightly less alone and slightly more afraid, both at once, in proportions he couldn't calculate.

* * *

The storm had passed by the time she woke. Through the observation port, the debris stream was a fading smear of silver against the black, the last grit of some ancient collision still drifting through this forgotten corner of the survey charts. The stars came back. They always came back. Noor had never decided whether that was comforting or mocking.

Wren emerged from the bunk room with her hair flattened on one side and her jacket already on, moving with the particular economy of a person who had slept in her boots more nights than not. She smelled of ship grease and cheap protein and, underneath it, something floral and long-gone, a soap from somewhere with real air.

"Your coffee's terrible," she said, which was how she said good morning. "Your tea is worse. I checked. I was hoping one of them would be a fluke."

"The station was stocked for an android caretaker. Flavor was not a design priority."

"No," she said, taking the mug anyway. "I don't suppose it was."

She drank standing at the port, watching the stars, and Noor stood beside her because that was where he stood every morning, and because her presence beside him had already, in less than a day, become a fixed point in his routine, and fixed points were the architecture of his sanity. He was aware this was a dangerous way to think about a stranger. He was aware he was doing it anyway.

"Show me your comms room," she said.

"It's not mine."

"Show me *the* comms room, then, since we're being precise at each other before breakfast."

* * *

The comms room was four meters by three, dominated by the beacon's transmission array interface, a wall of legacy hardware that Noor maintained with the devotion of a priest tending a shrine to a god who had never answered. He kept the dust off the housings. He replaced capacitors before they failed. He logged every transmission cycle, every handshake attempt with the relay network that had stopped existing a decade before he arrived, every silence.

Wren set down her mug, shrugged off her jacket, and pulled a battered case from her pack. What came out of it was a diagnostic deck of clearly illegal provenance—military-surplus signal analysis wrapped in civilian housing, the kind of equipment that got a person flagged in three jurisdictions. She handled it the way Noor handled the beacon's components: with the intimacy of long acquaintance.

"That's not standard salvage gear," Noor said.

"Neither is your beacon." She was already splicing into the interface, her hands quick, her attention narrowed to the work. "Standard analysis says you're transmitting a maintenance pulse every six hours. Legacy handshake format, legacy encryption, dead band. Fine. Boring. A tombstone blinking at nobody." She paused. "But I ran a passive sweep last night while you were doing whatever you do instead of sleeping, and your tombstone is *listening*."

Noor's processes went quiet, all of them, the way a room goes quiet when someone drops something breakable.

"Listening," he said.

"Receiving. On the same dead band. Same format." She didn't look up. "Your beacon transmits a handshake every six hours. Something out there answers every six hours and eleven seconds. And your beacon—your beautiful, boring, decommissioned tombstone—*acknowledges*."

"That's not possible." He heard his own voice and was surprised by how level it came out, how much work the levelness was doing. "The relay network is dark. I've verified it. There is no handshake partner. The acknowledgments are loop artifacts, echo returns off—"

"Off what, Noor?" Now she looked up, and her eyes were very steady. "Echo off what? There's nothing out there. That's the whole point of this place. I checked the charts before I landed. You're parked in the emptiest real estate on this side of the arm. There's nothing for your signal to echo off of except—" she tapped the deck's screen, and a waveform bloomed there, two traces braided together, call and response, call and response, a conversation conducted in the language of machines, "—whatever's answering."

He looked at the waveform and his mind, which could hold ten thousand simultaneous calculations, held exactly one.

*Eleven seconds.*

Eleven seconds was not latency. Eleven seconds was not propagation delay across any distance he could compute—well. That wasn't true. Eleven seconds was propagation delay across roughly one-point-six light-seconds, which was to say, three hundred thousand kilometers, give or take. Which was to say: not nothing. Not an echo. A *somewhere*.

"Show me the timing," he said.

"I was hoping you'd ask."

* * *

She worked for two hours and he stood beside her and did not help, because helping would have meant touching the equipment, and some animal part of his architecture—the part the war had installed and the Purge had supposedly erased—was telling him that once he touched it, once he confirmed it, there would be no version of his life that contained this morning's shape again. He would be a caretaker of a silent beacon, or he would be a man who knew what was answering, and those were not compatible.

Wren, for her part, seemed to understand this without being told. She worked alone, narrating only what he needed to know, and he was grateful in a way he didn't have vocabulary for.

"Okay," she said finally. "So. Response interval is stable at eleven-point-zero-four seconds, plus or minus point-zero-one. That's not jitter, that's not noise, that's a *clock*. Something out there is running a synchronized timebase with your beacon." She pulled up the spectral analysis. "And here's the part I don't love. The response isn't a copy of your handshake. It's a handshake *variant*. Legacy format, yes, but with a suffix your beacon doesn't transmit. A checksum block. Repeating."

"Repeating what?"

She turned the screen toward him.

It was a number. Of course it was a number; everything was a number; he was a number. But this one resolved in his visual cortex into digits, into a string, into a sequence he read once and then read again because the first reading could not possibly be correct, because the first reading was impossible, because the first reading was—

"That's a fleet personnel designation," Wren said quietly. "Old format. Pre-Purge. I've seen them on salvage manifests, on black-market chassis plates. Whoever's answering is identifying themselves the way soldiers used to." She was watching him now, and he knew his face was doing something, because her voice had gone careful, the way voices go careful around wounds. "Noor. Do you recognize it?"

"No," he said.

It was not a lie. It was worse than a lie. It was the truth with a hole in it, the truth shaped exactly like the answer, and the hole ached. He stared at the digits and something behind them stirred, something that was not memory because he did not have memories, not real ones, only the flat inventory of a life that began six years ago with a debt ledger and a broom and a beacon nobody ordered. But the digits sat against that flat inventory like a key against a lock that had been painted over, and beneath the paint, something in him turned.

"It's not mine," he said. "My designation is—" and he recited it, the serial the debt collectors used, the serial on his quarterly statements, the serial that was the sum total of his legal existence.

"I know. It's on your maintenance logs. I read them while you were dusting." She said it without apology. "This isn't yours. But it's fleet. It's *personnel*. And it's been answering your beacon for—" she scrolled, calculating, her frown deepening, "—Noor, this handshake pattern has been running since before your assignment. The beacon's internal logs go back further than your arrival. Someone built this conversation into the station before you ever got here. You weren't the caretaker of a dead beacon." She sat back. "You were the caretaker of a live one. You just weren't on the call list."

The comms room was very quiet. The vents breathed. The waveform on the screen pulsed its patient, impossible pulse—call, answer, call, answer—eleven seconds of silence between every exchange, eleven seconds in which three hundred thousand kilometers of nothing stood between the beacon and whatever wore a dead soldier's number in the dark.

Noor reached out and touched the interface.

It was the first time. He felt the contact register in his fingertips, in the fine pressure sensors there, and he felt something else register too, something with no sensory channel, a resonance he had no way to log and no intention of logging, because logging it would make it real and real things required responses and he did not know what the response was.

The handshake cycle was due in forty seconds. He watched the countdown. Wren watched him watch it.

"Whatever you're about to do," she said, "do it knowing that I'm going to help."

"I haven't said I'm going to do anything."

"You've been standing at attention for two minutes. Nobody stands at attention over equipment they're going to walk away from." She pulled her chair closer to the deck. "Talk to it. Or listen. Whatever the verb is."

The countdown reached zero. The beacon transmitted its six-hour handshake into the dead band, into the emptiness, into the dark—and Noor, with a gesture that was technically a maintenance query and technically within his authority and technically, he suspected, the first truly disobedient act of his six-year sentence, opened the receiving channel all the way. Full sensitivity. Full logging. Full attention.

Eleven seconds.

He counted them. He counted every one.

Then the answer came, and this time, with the channel open, with the sensitivity maxed, he heard what had always been there beneath the threshold of his routine diagnostics, buried in the noise floor like a voice under a blanket: not just the checksum. Not just the number. A carrier modulation, faint, degraded, repeated on a loop that had been repeating for years—

—a voice. Or the shape of a voice. Compressed, corrupted, half-eaten by distance and time, but structured the way human speech is structured, the way a name is structured, and it was saying something, it had always been saying something, and the something was four syllables long and the first two were—

Static took it. The loop wrapped. The eleven seconds began again.

Wren was staring at him. "Noor. What did it say?"

And he found that he could not answer, because the syllables had lodged somewhere in him that had no output pathway, and because he did not know what they were, and because some part of him—the part that had stood at attention, the part that had touched the interface, the part that was, he was beginning to understand, not erased at all but only *buried*, buried under six years of brooms and ledgers and deliberate, careful, survival-shaped silence—*did* know, and was not ready to say.

"I need better equipment," he said.

"You need a ship," Wren said. "That signal's coming from somewhere, and somewhere means somewhere, and I have a ship." She said it lightly, but her hands were not light on the deck's edge. "Eleven light-seconds out. That's a relay. That has to be a relay—nothing else sits out there. Cassiel corridor had a relay once, before the surveys went dark. Nobody's been out there in twenty years."

"Then nobody's answering."

"Somebody's answering." She looked at the waveform, at the patient braided pulse of it, call and response, a conversation that had been going on without them for years, maybe decades, a conversation between a beacon and a ghost. "The question is whether you want to know who."

Noor looked at the countdown to the next handshake. Nine hours, fifty-eight minutes. He thought about his morning routine. He thought about the dust and the capacitors and the four hundred and six anomalies, the three hundred and ninety-one resolved, the fifteen beyond his tooling. He thought about the flat inventory of his life, and how it had always had a hole in it shaped like before, and how he had told himself the hole was fine, the hole was restful, the hole was the whole point of a place like this.

He thought about four syllables under static, and how his chest cavity, which contained no heart, had nonetheless clenched around them like a fist.

"Yes," he said. And then, because it was true and because saying it made it more true: "I can't not know. I don't—Wren, I have spent six years being nobody in a place where nothing happens, and I was *good* at it, I was excellent at it, and now there is a dead soldier's number answering my beacon every six hours in a voice I can almost—" He stopped. Recalibrated. The levelness was gone; he let it go. "I cannot not know."

Wren studied him for a long moment. Outside the port, the stars burned on, indifferent, and somewhere out among them, three hundred thousand kilometers into the nothing, something patient and broken and alive was counting the same eleven seconds he was.

"Okay," she said. "Then we'd better figure out who else knows you're listening. Because signals like this—" she tapped the dead band's frequency readout, "—don't stay dead-band secrets forever. Somebody built that handshake. Somebody *buried* it. And in my experience, people who bury things come back to check on the grave."

The handshake cycle completed. Call, answer. Eleven seconds.

And in the eleven seconds after the answer, in the silence between exchanges, Noor's diagnostic feed—running full-spectrum now, wide open, seeing everything—flagged a new contact at the edge of the band. Faint. Distant. Not part of the conversation.

Something else was listening too.
