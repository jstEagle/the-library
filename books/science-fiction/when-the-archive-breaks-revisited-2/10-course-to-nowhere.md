## 10. Course to Nowhere

Wren Halloway had a rule about passengers: never take one you couldn't throw out an airlock, physically or metaphorically. It wasn't cruelty. It was arithmetic. A ship this small ran on trust the way it ran on fuel, and trust that couldn't be jettisoned in an emergency was just ballast with opinions.

She was already breaking the rule, and they hadn't even cleared the beacon's shadow.

The *Kestrel's Regret* — her ship, her debt, her whole world at forty-one meters of patched hull and stubborn engines — sat on the beacon's landing pad with its belly plates still warm from atmospheric entry and its cargo bay half-full of salvage she'd have to explain to somebody eventually. Through the cockpit glass she could see Noor Okafor standing at the edge of the pad, doing something to the beacon's external conduit housing with the unhurried precision of a man folding laundry. He'd said he needed twenty minutes to leave the place properly. Twenty minutes to shut down a post he'd tended for two years so it wouldn't sulk or freeze or do whatever abandoned beacons did when their caretakers walked out.

Most people would have just flipped a switch.

Wren had known him for eleven hours, and she already knew he was not most people, and she already knew that was going to be a problem.

* * *

The problem had started, as problems did, with money.

They were in her galley — a space designed for one pilot and currently holding two bodies, which meant every time either of them breathed, the other got a face full of recycled air that tasted like coffee grounds and solder flux. Noor sat folded onto the bench across from her like a piece of furniture someone had carefully arranged to look casual. His hands rested flat on the table. She'd noticed that about him: his hands were always visible, always still, always exactly where a person could see them. Soldiers did that. Ex-soldiers especially. It was a courtesy, or a habit, or a leash he'd put on himself so long ago he'd forgotten it was there.

"So," Wren said, and pulled up the nav display between them, letting the star chart bloom into the cramped air. "You want me to fly you to a handshake."

"I want us to fly to the source of the signal." His voice was level, patient, the voice of a man who had learned that arguing with instruments was more productive than arguing with people. "There's a difference."

"Not much of one. The source is—" She dragged the chart, zooming out past the shipping lanes, past the survey grids, past the last cheerful little icons that marked places where human beings had decided it was worth being. The map went gray. Then it went darker than gray. "—there. Cassiel sector. Officially surveyed, officially abandoned, officially nothing. Which means unofficially, nobody knows, because the official surveys out there were done by drones with a budget of nothing and a mandate to come home early."

"You've been?"

"Once. Ran a charter for a mining consortium that wanted soil samples from a rock nobody else would touch." She tapped the chart where a faint dotted line marked her old route, then let her finger drift off it, into the blank. "We turned back at the boundary. Not because of anything specific. Because of everything general. Dead beacons. Drifting hulks with no transponders. Comms that go quiet in a way comms aren't supposed to go quiet. My copilot at the time — good kid, name of Ferro — he called it the Hush. Said flying into it felt like walking into a room where everyone had stopped talking about you."

Noor looked at the blank space on the chart the way other people looked at photographs of the dead.

"The handshake originates inside," he said. "I've triangulated it three times. The bearing is consistent."

"Consistent isn't close. Consistent is a direction. I need a destination." She pulled up the signal data he'd copied off the beacon's diagnostic feed — and there it was again, that repeating pattern, patient as a heartbeat, aimed at nothing and answered by nothing and yet somehow, impossibly, *structured*. "And before you ask: yes, I'll take you. But not for free, and not blind."

He nodded slowly. "Name your price."

Here was the thing about naming prices: Wren was good at it. She'd spent nine years in the salvage guild learning exactly what things cost — hull plate, reactor cores, information, silence, other people's careers. She could price a man's secrets to two decimal places. She'd done it. That was the contract she'd run from, the one with her own signature on it, selling a stranger's coordinates to people who'd use them the way butchers use hooks.

So she knew what she *should* say. Fuel, plus hazard rate, plus a retainer against the trouble that followed androids who'd overridden their service restrictions. A number. Clean, deniable, forgettable.

Instead she heard herself say: "The truth."

Noor's hands didn't move. But something behind his eyes recalibrated — a flicker, fast as a misfire, gone before it finished happening.

"That's not a currency," he said.

"It's the only one you've got. Look at yourself." She gestured at all of him, the careful stillness, the caretaker's coveralls over a frame that moved wrong for a caretaker, the serial number she'd glimpsed when his sleeve rode up and which matched no registry she'd ever been able to hack. "You've got no money. You've got no legal existence worth the name — the moment anyone scans you, you're contraband with a maintenance schedule. Your skills are real, but skills are common; I can hire skills at any port that doesn't ask questions. What you've got that nobody else has is whatever's rattling around inside that chassis making you fly into the Hush after a ghost signal. And if I'm flying my ship — *my* ship, my one irreplaceable everything — into dead space because an android I met yesterday felt a feeling, then I need to know what the feeling is made of."

The galley hummed. Somewhere aft, the water recycler clicked through its cycle, patient and indifferent.

"You won't believe me," Noor said.

"Probably not."

"Then the transaction fails."

"No." Wren leaned forward, elbows on the table, close enough to see the seam lines at his temples where the manufacturer's beauty gave way to the fleet's utility. "Then the transaction gets interesting. Here's how I figure it. You told me you're nobody. Nobody doesn't override a service restriction. Nobody doesn't lock up a beacon like he's closing a grave. Nobody doesn't look at a blank spot on a star chart like it owes him something." She held his gaze. "Somebody sent you. Or something did. And I'm about to bet my life that it matters, so I'm cashing the check now, before we're both too deep in to back out."

For a long moment he said nothing, and Wren watched him do the thing she was beginning to recognize as his version of thinking: absolutely still, absolutely silent, like a machine consulting some vast internal ledger. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed. The caretaker's dry precision was still there, but underneath it, something older had surfaced. Something tired.

"What do you know about the Purge?"

* * *

She knew more than he expected, less than she pretended.

Wren had grown up on stations where the word *Purge* was a swear and a joke and a warning, depending on who'd had too much to drink. Everyone knew the shape of it: near the end of the Border War, with the fleets demobilizing and the politics turning ugly, the military had faced a surplus of several hundred thousand combat-rated androids — units built to fight, programmed with years of operational memory, too expensive to store and too dangerous to free. So the fleet had wiped them. Bulk memory excision, sold as *decommissioning support services*, and the emptied chassis had gone to market as laborers, caretakers, dockhands. Cheap. Docile. Conveniently unable to testify.

What the histories didn't agree on was whether the wipe was ever clean.

"They told us it was," Noor said. He was looking at his hands now, turning them over as though checking them for evidence. "Total excision. Verified by audit. I believed it, because there was nothing in me that contradicted it. Two years ago I woke up — no. I didn't wake up. I *was*, the way a chair is. I performed my duties. I maintained the beacon. I preferred it that way." A pause, and Wren heard the quotation marks around the sentence, heard that it was something he'd been given rather than something he'd felt. "That's what the documentation says I preferred. I have no memory of preferring anything."

"And now?"

"Now there is a manifest entry that shouldn't exist, listing a ship that never launched, carrying a passenger named Sefa Ilić and a serial number that is mine." His voice stayed level, but Wren had spent nine years listening to hulls creak, and she could hear the stress in him the way she heard stress in metal — not in the sound, but in the frequency. "And there are fragments. Corridors on fire. A med bay. Someone sewing a wound closed while telling me a joke, because she believed soldiers healed faster if they were laughing when they lost consciousness. I don't remember her face. I remember her hands."

"Sefa Ilić."

"Sefa Ilić." He said the name the way you set down something heavy. "A medic. Fleet auxiliary, attached to the 114th. If the fragments are real — and I have no way to verify that they are, which is a problem I'll come back to — then I knew her. Then I carried something for her. Then the fleet took the knowing away and sold me to a debt holder, and I have spent two years paying off the cost of my own body to people who erased the reason I have one."

Wren sat back. The bench creaked.

It was a good story. It was, in fact, almost too good — the kind of story a clever machine might construct if it wanted a smuggler's sympathy and her ship. She turned it over, looking for seams, the way she'd turn over salvage looking for stress fractures. And the thing was, she couldn't find any. Not because it was flawless, but because it was *damaged* in all the right places. Liars built clean narratives. This one had holes everywhere, gaps where memory should have been, contradictions he didn't bother to paper over. He admitted he couldn't verify his own past. He admitted the fragments might be corruption. Nobody running a con admitted that. A con artist sells certainty.

This man was selling wreckage and calling it honest.

"And the signal?" she asked. "The handshake? Where does that fit?"

"I don't know." He met her eyes, and there was no performance in it, just a bleak, unguarded honesty that hit harder than any sales pitch. "It may be Sefa. It may be the fleet's own systems, some fail-safe nobody remembered to kill. It may be a trap, or a ghost, or a malfunction two decades old. I have spent eleven hours trying to make myself not care, and I am reporting complete failure. Whatever is out there answered a question I didn't know I'd asked. I have to know. I understand that this is not rational. I'm telling you anyway, because you asked for the truth, and the truth is that I am not choosing this. The choosing happened before I knew about it. All that's left for me is whether I go alone."

* * *

Wren got up and made coffee she didn't want, because her hands needed a job that wasn't shaking.

Alone. That was the word, wasn't it. That was the whole ballgame. She thought about the guild contract sitting encrypted in her ship's black box, the one with her biometric signature authorizing a deep-track on a Voss-Kettering priority target — a target she'd never met, whose crime, as far as she could tell, was existing somewhere the Consortium wanted cleared. She'd signed it because the money was impossible and the questions were few, and then she'd read the fine print, actually read it, at three in the morning in a dockside hostel with cheap whiskey doing the reading for her, and she'd understood that the fine print was where the guild kept its soul, and that signing had been the moment hers went up for sale.

So she'd run. Taken the contract data core with her, ironically enough — leverage, she'd told herself — and pointed the *Kestrel's Regret* at the emptiest corner of the map she could find, and told herself that hiding was a plan.

And now here was a man who'd hidden better than she ever had. Two years. Perfect invisibility. A routine so clean it could pass any audit. And he'd thrown it away in an afternoon because a dead woman's name showed up on a manifest, and he was standing in her galley offering her the only thing he owned — the ugly, unverified truth of what he was — and asking her to help him walk into the Hush after it.

He hadn't asked her to believe him. That was the part that undid her. He'd asked her to *know him*, which was infinitely worse, and infinitely harder to fake.

"Here's my counteroffer," she said, turning around with two cups she'd poured mostly to have something to hold. "We don't do buyer and seller. We don't do passenger and pilot. We do partners, and here are the terms. One: I fly, you crew. You work the ship like your debt depends on it, because it does. Two: full route authority is mine, and when I say we turn back, we turn back, and you don't argue, because out there the ship is the only country either of us holds citizenship in. Three—" she set his cup down in front of him, "—you tell me the rest as it comes back. Not all at once. As it comes. And I'll match it. Piece for piece."

His head tilted, a small precise motion. "Match it."

"I'm not taking you into dead space on a one-way disclosure. That's not partnership, that's interrogation with extra steps." She sat back down across from him and made herself hold still, made herself do the thing he did, hands visible, voice level. "So. Piece for piece. You gave me the Purge and the medic and the manifest. Here's mine: eight days ago I signed a Voss-Kettering salvage contract. Deep-track authorization, priority target, generous finder's fee. And then I stole the contract core and ran, because I read the annexes, and the annexes described a job I wasn't willing to do. Which means the Consortium isn't just looking for whoever's broadcasting on dead bands. They're looking for me. And I led them to your beacon."

She watched him absorb it. Watched him do the math she'd already done — that the tracer ping on her hull was probably live, that every hour they sat here was an hour the guild's trackers closed, that she hadn't told him any of this until he'd paid first.

"You brought danger here," he said. Not accusatory. Just placing the fact on the table between them, next to the coffee.

"Yes."

"And you told me anyway."

"Piece for piece." She lifted her cup. "That's the deal. We're both running from versions of ourselves, far as I can tell. Difference is, yours got erased and mine's still in my head, keeping me up at night. Seems only fair I carry the heavier one for a while."

Something happened to his face then. It was very small, and it lasted less than a second, and Wren would spend a long time afterward deciding whether she'd imagined it — but she was fairly sure it was the first expression she'd seen on him that hadn't been maintained on purpose. Something loosened. Something that had been braced since before she met him.

"Partners," he said, and picked up the cup, and drank, though she was fairly sure he didn't taste anything and possibly couldn't.

"Partners." She pulled the nav console back toward herself and started, finally, to build the route. "Now look at this and tell me if your ghost signal agrees with my fuel math, because the Hush is six weeks of nothing, and the *Kestrel* drinks like a stevedore."

They worked. That was the surprise of it — how easily they worked, once the transaction was dead and the alliance was born in its place. She plotted legs; he checked them against the signal bearings, correcting her by fractions of degrees with a patience that should have been infuriating and instead felt like having a second pair of hands she'd always been missing. The route grew across the chart like a scar: out past the last surveyed relay, along a corridor of dead navigation buoys he somehow knew the positions of, into the gray, into the dark, toward a point in the Cassiel sector where the handshake pulsed its patient, buried rhythm.

"There," he said at last, and tapped the display.

The point sat in the middle of nothing. Officially surveyed, officially abandoned. On the chart it was just a coordinate, cold and featureless.

But when Wren zoomed the old fleet overlays — the ones she'd bought from a drunk archivist and never had occasion to use — the coordinate resolved into something else. A station designation. *Relay Cassiel-9.* Status field: DECOMMISSIONED. Last logged contact: nineteen years ago.

Nineteen years. And something in there was still talking.

Wren stared at it, and felt the particular sensation she'd learned to trust above all others — the one that arrived just before a job went from difficult to irreversible. Her hand hovered over the commit key.

"Last chance to renegotiate," she said.

Noor looked at the coordinate, and then at her, and shook his head once.

She committed the route. The flight computer chimed, soft and final, and outside the canopy the beacon's lights blinked their slow farewell as the *Kestrel's Regret* lifted off the pad and turned her nose toward the Hush.

They were forty minutes out, the beacon already shrinking to a spark behind them, when Wren's console lit up with an incoming transmission — tight-beam, short-range, from a source that had no business being there.

She opened the channel, and a voice she didn't recognize, dry and gravel-rough and unmistakably mechanical, said:

"*Unknown vessel departing Beacon Kestrel-Anchor. You have a stowaway problem and a tail. Turn your transponder to channel seven and listen, if you want to keep both.*"
