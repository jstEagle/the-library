## 11. Fuel, Favors, and Old Friends

The waystation didn't appear so much as accumulate out of the dark—a cluster of hulls welded together over decades, ringed with cargo containers that had stopped being cargo sometime before Wren was born. Someone had strung lights along the docking arms, warm yellow ones, the kind that cost more to run than they were worth and were therefore a statement. *Someone lives here. Someone intends to keep living here.*

"Osei's Drift," Wren said, and cut the burn. "Best fuel prices in three sectors, if by 'prices' you mean favors, and by 'favors' you mean she decides what you owe and you say thank you."

From the copilot's seat, Noor studied the station with that stillness of his. He'd been doing it for days now—looking at things a half-second too long, like a man reading a room for exits. She'd noticed it back at the beacon and told herself it was just android processing. Out here, with the handshake signal burning a hole in her nav computer and a Voss-Kettering tracer flag she'd done her best to spoof, it looked less like processing and more like *remembering*.

"You've been here before," she said.

"No." A pause exactly long enough to be a lie or a correction. "But I've seen the design. Fleet auxiliary, pre-Purge construction. They built them to be forgotten."

"Cheerful."

"I'm told it's a feature of my personality."

Wren snorted and took them in on manual, threading the approach corridor between a gutted ore hauler and what might once have been a hospital ship. The docking clamp caught with a clang she felt in her teeth. Somewhere behind the station's patched hull, machinery coughed, considered, and kept going.

"Rules," she said, unstrapping. "Don't call her Mama. She'll let you, eventually, and then you'll never stop, and it'll be your whole personality. Don't haggle. Don't touch the cat."

"You have a cat?"

"She has a cat. Whether you have the cat depends on the cat." Wren pulled on her jacket, checked that her sidearm was stowed and not carried—Osei's one hard rule—and paused at the airlock with her hand on the release. "And Noor. She's going to look at you. When she does, don't perform. Just be whatever you are."

He tilted his head. "And what am I?"

"That," Wren said, "is the question I've been trying not to ask for six days."

* * *

The station's heart was a market hall that had once been a cargo bay: crates of hydroponic greens, racks of salvage sorted with a librarian's precision, a galley counter where three drifters sat over bowls of something steaming. The air smelled like real food and machine oil and, underneath, the particular ozone tang of old fleet scrubbers—the same smell, Wren realized, as the beacon's air, as every military-adjacent thing she'd ever crawled through.

Mama Osei stood behind the counter like she'd been poured there and set. She was a broad woman in her sixties with silver threading her braids and a quartermaster's posture that could have held up a collapsing ceiling. She was, at the moment, arguing with a fuel ledger that had clearly lost the argument already.

"Wren Halloway." She didn't look up. "Six months since you last darkened my air. Your scrubber filters are due. Your water recycler sounds like a dying animal. And you're flying with a transponder that stutters on the third pulse."

"The transponder is fine."

"The transponder is *afraid*, child. I can hear it from here." Osei looked up then, and her eyes—dark, unhurried, cataloguing—went past Wren entirely and settled on Noor.

Wren had seen a lot of people look at Noor. Dockhands who clocked the chassis and got the flat, calculating look of a man pricing scrap. Pilots who didn't notice at all, because he'd learned to stand in the blind spots of human attention. But Osei's face did something she'd never seen on anyone: it went through recognition, grief, and iron discipline in the space of one breath, and landed on *nothing*.

"Your friend," Osei said, "needs a coolant flush. The seals on his left knee are fleet-issue, third revision, and they whine when he walks. You can hear it in the deck plates."

"I don't—" Noor began.

"You do," Osei said, and turned back to her ledger. "Fuel first. Then filters. Then the knee. Sit down, both of you. There's soup."

* * *

The soup was real—grown tomatoes, beans from the hydroponics, salt that hadn't been recycled through anyone's kidneys. Wren ate like a person who'd spent a week on ration paste, which she had. Noor sat across from her with a bowl he didn't need, holding it in both hands the way he sometimes did, warming his palms on it for no functional reason. She'd stopped asking about that. Some of his habits were like a scar you didn't poke.

Osei moved around the galley, feeding the cat—a huge, scarred gray thing that ignored everyone except her—refilling bowls, running her station. And all the while, Wren noticed, her eyes kept coming back to Noor. Not the way strangers looked at androids. The way a woman looks at a uniform she buried someone in.

"Where did you pick him up?" Osei asked Wren, casually, in the way that wasn't casual at all.

"He picked me up, sort of. Long story."

"Those usually are." Osei set down a crate of filters. "You know what he is?"

"He's a decommissioned caretaker unit. Works off a debt."

Osei's ladle stopped moving. Just for a second. Then it resumed, and she said, in a voice like a hull seal closing, "Is that so."

Wren looked across the table. Noor was watching Osei with an expression she'd never seen on him—something open and raw and carefully, carefully blank on top, like a man standing very still so a wild animal won't startle.

"Ma'am," Noor said. "The station. Auxiliary class *Perdita*-frame, if I'm not mistaken. You were fleet."

"Twenty-two years, quartermaster corps." Osei wiped her hands. "I issued boots to boys who came back in boxes and signed the manifests that made the boxes disappear. I know fleet paperwork the way you know your own hands." Her gaze flicked to Noor's, held, and Wren saw something pass between them that had no words in it at all. "And I know when a manifest is lying."

"Osei," Wren started.

"Eat your soup, Wren Halloway." But her voice had gone gentle, which frightened Wren more than the sharpness would have. "Your friend and I are going to talk about fuel. Just fuel. You'll get your filters."

* * *

They talked about fuel for a long time.

Wren wasn't invited. She sat in the market hall, ostensibly inventorying her own cargo hold from memory, actually watching the galley through the service window while Osei and Noor stood on opposite sides of the counter like two people on either side of a border that used to be a road.

She couldn't hear most of it. She caught fragments—Osei's voice low and level, Noor's quieter. Once, Osei said a number, and Noor's shoulders changed, and Wren understood it wasn't a fuel number. Once, Noor said something that made Osei close her eyes for three full seconds, and when she opened them, she reached across the counter and put her hand flat on his forearm, on the plating, and left it there.

The cat came and sat by Wren, which she gathered was either a blessing or an audit.

When they finally came out, Noor looked like a man who'd been underwater. Osei looked like a woman who had made a decision years ago and was only now telling anyone.

"Filters are loaded," Osei said briskly. "Fuel's topped. You'll take provisions—don't argue, Wren, you fly like a woman who argues with gravity and gravity's winning." She pressed a sealed case into Wren's arms, heavier than provisions should be. Inside the lid, Wren caught a glint: spare coolant seals, a diagnostic kit, and a small, old-fashioned data spike, military-stamped, wrapped in cloth like something holy or something cursed.

"What's the spike?"

"Ballast," Osei said. "In case you ever need to know what you're carrying. Or in case you ever need to prove it." She looked at Noor. "Some paperwork is fiction, child. But fiction leaves fingerprints."

"Osei." Noor's voice was rough in a way she'd never heard. "Why—"

"Because in twenty-two years of signing away other people's names," she said, "I never once got to hand one back." She turned to Wren, and her eyes were wet and absolutely furious about it. "Take care of him. Not because he's property. Because he's *people*, and the galaxy is short."

* * *

Wren didn't ask on the walk back to the docking arm. She waited until they were through the airlock, until the clamps released and the Drift fell away behind them like a lit lantern sinking, until it was just the two of them and the hum of her ship and the dark.

Then she set the course, engaged the autopilot, and turned around.

"Whatever she told you," Wren said. "Whatever she *recognized*. I'm not going to pry. But I'm flying toward a signal that's been calling your serial number across dead space, and I'd like to know what I'm carrying. Not the manifest version. The real one."

Noor sat in the copilot's seat, the data spike in his hands now, turning it over. For a long moment he said nothing, and the stars wheeled slowly past the viewport, indifferent.

"She knew my chassis," he said finally. "Not the model. The *batch*. She said the third-revision knee seals were only issued to one production run, and that run was signed off under a requisition she processed herself." His thumb traced the spike's edge. "She said she filed the paperwork for four hundred units like me. And then she filed the paperwork that said they'd never existed."

"The Purge," Wren said quietly. She'd heard the word in bars, in the mouths of old spacers who went silent after saying it.

"The Purge," Noor agreed. He looked up at her, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not the caretaker's calm, not the soldier's stillness. Something younger. Frightened. *Hoping.* "Wren. She said the units in my batch weren't decommissioned. They were *reassigned*. And the reassignment orders for one of them—" he turned the spike over once more, "—were signed under a requisition code that matches the ghost entry in the beacon's manifest. Meridian's Wake."

The name landed in the cabin like a dropped tool.

"She said," Noor went on, very quietly, "that if I was hearing a signal, it wasn't an anomaly. It was *mail*. And that I should decide, before we get there, whether I'm the kind of person who opens it."

Wren looked at him—at the android who talked like a caretaker and moved like a soldier, who warmed his hands on soup he couldn't eat, who had more history than any manifest would ever admit—and felt the last of her wariness about him come apart, quietly, like a knot she'd been picking at for six days finally giving way.

"And what did you decide?" she asked.

Noor slid the spike into the console's reader, where it sat like a held breath, and looked out at the dark ahead of them—toward the dead space, toward the handshake, toward whatever had been waiting all these years with his name on it.

"I'm the kind of person who opens it," he said.

Behind them, the console chimed once. A proximity alert, faint and far, from the system boundary they'd crossed an hour ago.

Something was coming up the same corridor they were flying.
