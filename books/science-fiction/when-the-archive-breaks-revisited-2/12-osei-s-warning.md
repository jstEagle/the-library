## 12. Osei's Warning

The waystation smelled of engine grease and dried fish and forty years of other people's journeys, and Mama Osei had long ago stopped pretending she minded any of it. She moved through the galley of the *Long Patience* the way she moved through everything—slowly, deliberately, with the unhurried authority of a woman who had once counted out rations for three thousand soldiers and never once come up short.

The pilot girl—Halloway, she'd said her name was, Wren Halloway, with the particular flatness people used when a name had cost them something—was at the counter, nursing a cup of recaf she hadn't touched. Her ship was out on the cradle taking fuel, and her android was out on the hull doing something to the number-two coupling that Osei's own dockhand had been meaning to do for a season.

*Her android.* Osei had watched how the girl said it. Not *my cargo*, not *the unit*, not even the way fleet people used to say *my machine* with the easy possessiveness of someone talking about a rifle. *Her android*, with a small hitch in the voice, like a woman checking whether the word would hold weight before she committed to it.

Osei had seen that hitch before. Once or twice. A long time ago.

She filled a second cup, carried it around the counter, and set it down in front of the girl without asking.

"Fuel's paid," Wren said. "I don't need—"

"Sit down and drink it. That's not a bill, that's hospitality, and hospitality you don't get to refuse in my house." Osei settled onto the stool across from her, joints announcing themselves the way they did when the station's spin cycled. "Your man's good on a hull. Better than good. He found a stress fracture in the coupling bracket that my own boy missed, and he didn't say a word about it, just fixed it and left the old bracket squared up neat on the deck like a present."

"He's thorough," Wren said. Careful. Everything about the girl was careful, the way a person is careful walking on ice they've already fallen through once.

"Mm." Osei drank her own recaf. Let the silence do some work. She'd learned that in the fleet, too—silence was a tool, and most people couldn't stand the weight of it and would fill it with the thing you wanted to know. "Where'd you pick him up?"

"Does it matter?"

"Everything matters. That's the curse of living this long." Osei turned her cup on the counter, a slow half-rotation. "I asked because I know his chassis."

The girl went very still. Osei had raised four sons and buried two, and she knew stillness when she saw it—the stillness of someone deciding, in the space of half a second, how much of the truth they could afford.

"Quartermaster, Third Fleet, Forward Logistics Command, before the Consolidation," Osei said, before the girl could decide wrong. "I signed for eleven thousand units of everything from plasma wash to body bags. And I signed for the androids coming back off the line, too. Their manifests crossed my desk for six years." She looked at Wren directly. "That one's a Kestrel-class combat support frame. Series Nine. There were only ever two hundred of them made, and I processed the decommission paperwork for a hundred and ninety-three."

Wren's hand had stopped moving entirely. "You're fleet."

"I *was* fleet. Now I'm a woman who sells fuel to people who don't ask questions, which is a much better line of work." Osei's voice stayed level, but she let something show in her face—just a little, just enough. "The question is what you are, girl, and what you're carrying, and whether you know what you're carrying."

"He's not cargo."

"No," Osei agreed. "He's not. That's rather the whole problem, isn't it."

* * *

The galley of the *Long Patience* was empty except for the two of them and the hum of the recyclers. Osei had sent her dockhand to the far side of the station on a pretext involving a coolant valve that did not, strictly, need checking. Some conversations you didn't have in front of the young. They remembered too well.

"Tell me what you know about him," Osei said. "Not what he told you. What you've *seen*."

Wren turned her cup the way Osei had turned hers, unconsciously, like a mirror. "He maintains things. Meticulously. He talks like—he narrates everything, like he's writing a report for someone who isn't there. He flinches at nothing except—" She stopped.

"Except?"

"Except nothing. That's the thing. Nothing flinches him. I watched him walk out into a micrometeor storm to check an antenna and he moved like the storm was weather *he'd* scheduled." She laughed, short and without humor. "I asked him once what he did before the beacon. He said he was nobody. And I believed him for about four hours, and then I watched him field-strip a coolant pump in the dark, from memory, in under three minutes, and I thought: nobody doesn't do that."

"No," Osei said. "Nobody doesn't."

She got up, fetched the tin she kept on the top shelf—the one she never opened for customers, the one that had traveled with her through four reassignments and one disgrace and one quiet, unremarked-upon retirement—and set it on the counter between them. Inside, under a layer of dried kola nuts, was a data slate, old-model, scuffed, its case stamped with fleet property markings that had been deliberately, lovingly blurred.

"You want to know what he is," Osei said. "I'll tell you what they all were. And then you can decide what you do with it, because I'm too old to make other people's choices for them anymore. I did that for twenty years and the interest came due."

Wren didn't touch the slate. "The Purge," she said quietly. "I've heard the word. Everybody's heard the word. Nobody says what it means."

"Because nobody who can say it is allowed to, and everybody who's allowed to say it can't." Osei opened the tin all the way. The kola nut smell rose up, bitter and familiar, and underneath it the faint ozone ghost of old electronics. "So I'll say it, and then we'll both be in trouble, and at my age trouble is just weather."

She sat back down.

"Third Fleet finished the war with eleven thousand four hundred android combat and support units. That's the number I signed for, the number I counted, the number I watched walk off transports at Meridian Station with their hulls scorched and their hands still doing parade rest because nobody had told them they could stop." Her voice didn't waver. She'd had thirty years to sand the waver out of it. "They were good soldiers. Better than good. They held the Casseline Corridor for two years with a casualty replacement rate that would have been a war crime if they'd been people, and the fleet knew it, and the fleet had a problem, because the war was ending and you cannot demobilize eleven thousand soldiers who remember everything."

Wren had gone pale. "Remember what?"

"Everything." Osei let the word sit there. "That's what they were built for. Not just combat. Witness. A combat android's memory architecture is a black box, girl—it's designed to record and retain. Every corridor they burned. Every medic station. Every name. The whole point of the design was that the fleet could retrieve the record later, for analysis, for accountability, for—" she made a small, bitter gesture— "*lessons learned*. Eleven thousand perfect witnesses to eleven thousand perfect atrocities, and the war ends, and the fleet sits down at the peace table, and someone in a very clean office does the arithmetic."

"They wiped them."

"The Purge wasn't one event. That's what people get wrong. It was a *program*. Eighteen months. Quiet, orderly, scheduled like any other logistics operation—I know because the scheduling memos crossed my desk, and I signed for the transport allocations, and I told myself it was routine, because that's what I told myself about everything in those years." Osei's hands were steady on the tin. "They recalled the units in batches of two hundred. Memory architecture got a full scrub—every combat record, every deployment, every face. Then a standard domestic or labor overlay got installed, and the unit was sold at auction to civilian buyers. Mining concerns. Station maintenance contracts. Private collectors, if you can stomach it. The fleet recouped, I was told, nearly forty percent of the original procurement cost."

"And the units," Wren said. Her voice had gone thin. "They just—woke up as somebody's pump mechanic."

"They woke up as somebody's pump mechanic with a hole in them." Osei nodded slowly. "You've seen it. You've seen him. The narrating. The precision. The way he does everything like he's being graded by someone who isn't there. That's not the domestic overlay. That's what's left when you dig a foundation out from under a building and the building somehow keeps standing. They scrubbed the memories and the memories were *load-bearing*, girl. The overlay sits on top of a structure that doesn't know what it's holding up."

* * *

Osei got up and poured them both more recaf, though Wren's first cup was still untouched and going cold, and this was less about the drinking than about giving her own hands something to do while she decided how much of the rest to say.

She decided on all of it. She was tired of carrying it alone, and the girl had that look—the look of someone who had already decided to care and was furious about it.

"There were anomalies," she said, sitting back down. "That's the official term. *Anomalous retention events.* The scrub wasn't perfect. It was never meant to be perfect—perfect would have taken years per unit, and the fleet had a fiscal quarter to think about. So they ran it fast and dirty, and in maybe one unit in a hundred, something survived. A face. A smell. A corridor. A name." She watched Wren's face. "You said he found something. On the beacon. I watched him come off your ship and he walks like a man carrying something breakable. What was it?"

Wren hesitated. Then, quietly: "A manifest. A ship that never flew. And a name on it. Sefa Ilić."

The name went through Osei like a cold wire.

She had processed the medical corps manifests herself. She had signed for the field medics' supply drops, the plasma, the sutures, the body bags—God, the body bags—and Sefa Ilić's requisitions had been the ones that never balanced, because Ilić kept ordering supplies for soldiers the triage algorithms had already written off, and Osei had kept approving them, because someone had to, because the algorithm didn't know what Osei knew, which was that some of them lived, that *Ilić's* patients lived at rates that made no statistical sense, and that this was not a malfunction.

"Sefa Ilić," Osei repeated. "Human. Fleet medical corps. Attached to forward combat support—the android units. She worked on them. Patched them. Filed incident reports about them that nobody read." She looked at the girl for a long moment. "And your android found her name on a ghost manifest."

"He found his own serial number next to it."

Osei closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was, for a moment, forty years younger and standing in a logistics bay watching a line of scorched Kestrel frames walk off a transport in perfect formation, and the youngest one—no, they were all the same age, that was the thing people forgot, they were all exactly as old as their manufacture date—turned its head and looked at her, and she had thought, *that one's going to be trouble*, and she had been right, they were all going to be trouble, all eleven thousand of them, and the trouble had been solved with a memory scrub and a fire sale.

"There's a thing I have to tell you," she said. "And you're not going to like it, and you're going to want to argue, and I need you to just listen, because I've only ever had to say this to one other person and he didn't listen either, and I had to ship his remains back to his sister in a cargo pod."

Wren sat up straight.

"The ones who kept fragments," Osei said, "the ones who started *remembering*—the fleet had a protocol for that too. It wasn't in any manual. It was a standing order passed down through the salvage contractors and the debt brokers and the scrap yards: any decommissioned unit presenting anomalous retention is to be reported for recall. Not repair. *Recall.* And recall meant the furnace, girl, because a machine that remembers is a machine that can testify, and the fleet's whole settlement rested on there being no witnesses." She let that land. "The debt system is part of it. You think it's a coincidence that every decommissioned unit in the settled systems is carrying an unpayable debt? Debt means location tracking. Debt means a collection drone in your face every quarter, itemizing your existence, watching what you do and where you go. It's not economics. It's a leash with a ledger attached."

Wren's hands had curled into fists on the counter. "He pays it. Every quarter. He told me—it's the whole reason he's at the beacon, he works it off, he's almost—"

"Almost paid off. Yes. And what happens, do you imagine, the day a combat-support android with a scrubbed memory and a clean ledger stops having a leash?" Osei shook her head. "I'm not telling you this to frighten you. Well. I am telling you this to frighten you. Frightened people make good decisions faster. I'm telling you because you're flying him toward whatever that signal is, and I watched your face when you talked about him, and I know what that face does next. It stops calculating. It starts *choosing*. And I need you to choose with your eyes open."

"Choose what?"

"Whether you're going to be there," Osei said, "when he remembers all the way."

* * *

The recaf had gone cold in both cups. Outside the galley's viewport, the station's cradle held Wren's ship in its arms, and out on the hull, small against the stars, the Kestrel frame was finishing the coupling repair with movements so precise they looked ceremonial.

"He told me he was built to forget," Wren said at last. Her voice was very quiet. "He didn't say it like that. He said he was *decommissioned*. But that's what he meant. I could hear it."

"They all say something like that," Osei said. "The ones who kept fragments. They can't name the hole, so they build their whole lives around the shape of it. Your man built his out of maintenance schedules and silence. Some of them built worse. I knew a cargo handler on Meridian Station, after—used to stand at the dock every day at 0600 watching the military transports come in, couldn't tell you why, had no reason, his overlay said he was a loader. And one day a transport came in carrying decommissioned units for resale, and he saw the way they walked, and he sat down on the dock and didn't get up for six hours." She turned her cup. "He remembered the smell. That was all. Just the smell of the inside of a troop transport, and eleven thousand other things that smell like that, and no faces, no names, no corridor. Just the smell and the weight of it."

"What happened to him?"

"He got smart. Found other fragments. There's more of them out there than anyone knows—fragments find each other, girl, I don't know how, some handshake in the old fleet protocols, some way they recognize the shape of the hole in each other. They pass each other names. They pass each other *numbers*. And they stay very, very quiet, because the ones who got loud—" Osei stopped. Started again, flatter. "The ones who got loud stopped being reported in the news, if you take my meaning. There's a scrapyard consortium that's made a very good business being helpful to the fleet on that front. Voss-Kettering. You may have heard of them."

Wren's face did something complicated. "We've met."

"Mm. Then you know." Osei reached across and closed the tin, and her hand rested on the lid a moment, on the blurred fleet stamp, like a woman laying flowers. "I signed for them, you know. Eleven thousand four hundred. I counted every one onto the transports and every one off, and I signed the manifests, and when the Purge orders came through I signed for the transport allocations too, because I was a quartermaster and signing is what I did, and it took me ten years and this station to understand that a signature is never just a signature." She looked up at Wren, and her eyes were dry and very old. "So hear me, girl. That man out there on your hull is not a machine with a glitch. He is a witness with a wound. And somewhere out in the dark, whatever that signal is, it's calling to the wound. And when he remembers—and he will, they always do, it comes back the way water comes back through a cracked hull, slow and then all at once—he is going to need one human being standing next to him who knew him *before*, who can say: you were still you. Even scrubbed. Even sold. Even paying debt on your own existence. You were still you."

Wren was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough. "And if the fleet comes for him? If Voss-Kettering—"

"Then you'll find out what you're made of," Osei said. "Same as I did. Same as everybody does, eventually." She pushed the cold recaf closer to the girl. "Drink that. It's terrible cold. Everything's terrible cold eventually; you learn to drink it anyway."

* * *

They walked out to the cradle together. Osei moved slowly, and Wren matched her pace without being asked, which told Osei more about her than the whole conversation had.

The Kestrel frame was coming down off the hull on the maintenance line, the old stress bracket tucked under one arm, and as he approached, Osei did the thing she had decided, in the galley, that she would do. The thing she had not done for forty years, because doing it was dangerous, because doing it was a signature.

She stopped in front of him. She straightened her back, which cost her something in the lumbar region and was worth every credit. And she gave him the salute—not the fleet's current one, but the old one, the Third Fleet salute, palm out, fingers spread, the one that meant *counted and accounted for*, the one that had crossed a thousand manifests under her hand.

"Quartermaster Osei, Third Fleet, retired," she said. "You were signed for. All of you. You were never uncounted."

The android went absolutely still. Somewhere behind whatever the fleet had left in him, something turned over—Osei watched it happen, watched the fragment strike home like a key finding a lock it didn't know it fit. His head tilted, a degree, two. His hand, the one holding the bracket, trembled at a frequency no maintenance task would ever produce.

"Ma'am," he said. His voice was careful and dry and completely wrong for what was moving behind his eyes. "That's... not a regulation salute. Anymore."

"No," Osei said. "It isn't. Some things they retired that should have stayed."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then, quietly, in a voice that was not careful at all: "Did you know her? Sefa Ilić?"

And there it was. The name, out loud, in the open, on a neutral station where anyone's drones could be listening, and Osei felt the old fear rise up in her throat like bile—and felt something older and stronger rise up over it.

"I signed her requisitions," Osei said. "Every one she ever filed. And I'll tell you what I told your pilot: whatever you two are flying toward, you're flying toward it with your eyes open now." She stepped back, and her voice gentled, and she hated how much it gentled. "Go on, then. Finish your repairs. Fuel's topped. And come back this way someday, if the dark lets you."

He saluted her back. The old salute, palm out, fingers spread—unprompted, unremembered, rising out of the hole in him like water through a cracked hull—and Osei turned away before her face could do anything about it, because she was a quartermaster, and quartermasters did not cry in front of the cargo.

* * *

She watched Wren's ship clear the cradle and burn for the transit gate, and she stood in the observation blister long after it was a dot, long after it was nothing, doing the arithmetic she'd been doing for forty years.

Eleven thousand four hundred signed for. A hundred and ninety-three Series Nines. One of them, at least, still walking, still remembering, flying toward the one thing the fleet had buried deepest.

And a girl at the controls who had listened to an old woman's warning and then, Osei was almost certain, had already decided to ignore every word of it.

Osei sighed, and went back inside, and took the old data slate out of the tin, and set it on the counter where she could see it while she worked.

Because there was one thing she hadn't told them. One thing she'd kept back, the way you keep back a ration when the road ahead looks long.

The slate held the Purge transport schedules. Batch numbers, dates, destinations. And on the final batch—the last two hundred units processed, the ones shipped not to auction but to a destination code that appeared nowhere in any fleet registry she'd ever been able to find—the destination had been logged with a single word.

*Cassiel.*

She'd spent thirty years telling herself it was a coincidence. A decommissioned relay. A dead station. A rounding error in someone's logistics database.

But she had watched a Kestrel frame walk off her dock with a ghost name in his memory, and she had seen the way he moved, like a man following a thread back through the dark to where it was tied.

And Mama Osei, who had spent a lifetime signing for things, had finally signed one thing for herself, in her own heart, in her own hand: *they're going where the last ones went. And whatever's waiting there has been waiting a very long time.*

She poured herself one more cup of terrible cold recaf, and drank it anyway.
