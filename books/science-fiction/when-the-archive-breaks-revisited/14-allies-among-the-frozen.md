## 14. Allies Among the Frozen

The trick to finding people who had been paid to disappear was, ironically, the settlements themselves.

Money leaves fingerprints. Even money designed to vanish—especially money designed to vanish. The deciders had routed their hush payments through a shell insurance consortium called Meridian Reassurance Group, which was either a joke so dry nobody got it or an act of arrogance so complete it circled back around to being a confession. Either way, every impossible settlement in my files traced back through the same seven shell companies, and every recipient had one thing in common: they'd signed a confidentiality clause, cashed a check that made no actuarial sense, and then spent the rest of their lives being very careful not to ask why.

I knew the type. I'd been the type for twenty years.

So I built the list the way I built everything: quietly, in pieces, across eleven relay stations and three identities I was about to burn down to the studs. Cross-reference the settlement disbursements against the strike-through column of Sefu's ledger copy—the objections filed, then struck—and you get a pattern no committee ever bothered to erase, because committees assume grief keeps people quiet.

Grief does keep people quiet. But grief plus curiosity? That's a different animal entirely. That's me.

* * *

The first call went exactly as badly as I expected.

Her name was Odile Ferran, sixty-one, living on Relay Four under her third legal name, running a hydroponics bay that smelled like basil and honest work. Her father had been a structural engineer on the evacuation fleets—struck from the objection list in year two of the triage, dead within eighteen months of filing his objection, officially killed by a coolant leak that the physics of his own design made impossible.

"Ms. Galloway." Her voice came through the relay scrubbed and flat, the way voices do when someone is deciding how much of themselves to spend on a conversation. "You have thirty seconds before I report this channel as spam."

"I know about your father's objection."

Silence. Long enough that I checked the connection light twice.

"You don't know anything," she said finally. "You know a rumor with good posture. Everyone who comes sniffing around knows something. That's how they find us. That's always how they find us."

"They already found us," I said. "That's rather the point. Your father is dead. My mother is dead. Forty-seven other people filed objections and are dead or frozen without consent. The only question left is whether we're going to be found out or whether we're going to be loud."

"Loud gets people killed. I've done the math on loud. Loud has a body count." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had softened by maybe half a degree—not warmth, exactly, but the ghost of it, like heat radiating off a wall long after the fire's gone. "You sound young. How old are you?"

"Thirty-four."

"Mm. Old enough to be angry, too young to remember what they did to the last people who were angry." She exhaled, and I heard the particular tiredness of someone who has rehearsed this refusal for decades. "I'm sorry about your mother, Ms. Galloway. Truly. But I grow tomatoes now. Tomatoes don't require courage. Find someone braver."

The channel closed. I sat with it for a while, in the rented bunk on Relay Nine that smelled of recycled air and someone else's cigarettes, and I let the refusal settle into my chest where it belonged. Because here was the thing I'd learned about investigating impossible accidents: most people, given the choice between justice and safety, choose safety, and they're not wrong to. Institutions survive on that arithmetic. Movements die of it.

Which meant whatever I was building couldn't be a movement. It had to be smaller. Meaner. Voluntary all the way down.

* * *

The second call surprised me.

His name was Tomas Reyes-Achebe, forty-ish, and he answered on the second ring with the breezy confidence of a man who had never once considered that he might be in danger. He ran a salvage outfit out of Relay Twelve—a one-ship operation, mostly hull plating and dead satellites—and his grandmother had been a logistics coordinator whose objection to the triage had been struck through with the same bureaucratic pen-stroke as everyone else's.

"Let me see if I've got this straight," he said, after I'd laid it out. He was eating something during the call; I could hear it. Crunchy. "There's a secret archive full of frozen people. Half of them are scheduled to wake up. The other half are scheduled to stay asleep forever. And the folks who decided which half is which are still alive, still in charge, and currently trying to murder anyone who reads their homework."

"That's a fair summary."

"And you want—" crunch "—what, exactly? Me and my bucket-of-bolts ship?"

"I want witnesses. People who can stand up when this goes public and say *yes, that's my family on that list, yes, that settlement was bought silence.* The ledger proves the crime. You prove the victims were real."

"Huh." More crunching. Then, unexpectedly quiet: "My gran used to say the dead don't need lawyers, they need listeners. She said that right up until she died in a docking accident that every independent review flagged as statistically impossible. There were four reviews. They buried them all." A pause. "You know what the funny thing is? I never connected it until you just said the word *settlement.* We got paid. Big. I was nineteen and I thought we'd won the sad lottery."

"The sad lottery," I repeated. "That's exactly what it was."

"Yeah, well." I heard him set the food down. When he spoke again, the breeziness was gone, replaced by something steadier underneath, the way bedrock sits under topsoil. "Here's my problem, Ms. Galloway. I believe you. That's the problem. Because if I believe you, then I'm already in it, and the only question is whether I get to pick my moment or whether it picks me." He sighed. "I pick my moment. What do you need?"

"A ship that doesn't appear on any registry. And a man who can salvage a signal out of places signals aren't supposed to go."

"Gran would've liked you," he said. "She hated committees."

* * *

The third call took six days to place, because the woman I needed to reach had made herself even more invisible than I had, and there was a professional courtesy in how long I let her stay hidden before I knocked.

Mirela Okoye. Former archivist, Ceres Station, retired at fifty-two with a pension that didn't match her salary grade—which meant she'd taken a settlement too, though hers wasn't in the ledger. Her sister was on the strike-through list. Mirela hadn't filed an objection; she'd simply noticed, years ago, that certain records kept rearranging themselves overnight, and she'd started keeping copies, and then she'd stopped noticing things out loud, and eventually she'd retired early and moved to a relay station at the edge of nowhere and grown orchids under artificial light.

When I finally reached her, she listened to my entire pitch without interrupting once. It was the most unnerving conversation of my life.

"You forged a clerk's access token to pull the microfilm," she said at last. "On Ceres."

"Yes."

"Sloppy. You left a query signature. If I could find it in an afternoon, so could their security chief."

"I know. I've gotten better."

"Mm." I heard pages turning—or the audio equivalent, the soft rustle of someone consulting notes they'd prepared for a conversation they'd been expecting for years. "Emrys Galloway. Daughter of Adaora Galloway, passenger manifest, Meridian. You've spent twenty years making yourself administratively invisible. Clever aliases, clean trails, no debts, no dependents, no subscriptions to anything with a mailing list." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Do you know what I did for thirty years, Ms. Galloway? Records integrity. Do you know what I know about invisibility?"

"That it's a kind of visibility. To the right search."

"To *any* persistent search. Invisibility isn't absence. It's pattern suppression. And pattern suppression is itself a pattern." She let that land. "You are the single most findable person in this system, because you are the only person working this hard to not exist. I found you in nine days, and I'm retired, and I like orchids."

I stared at the bulkhead of my rented bunk. Somewhere below me, Relay Nine's ventilation system wheezed like an old man climbing stairs.

"So why haven't you reported me?" I asked.

Another pause. Longer. When she answered, her voice had lost its archival crispness and gained something rawer.

"Because last month I dreamed about my sister for the first time in ten years, and in the dream she asked me what I'd done with all the time, and I didn't have an answer." A breath. "Because I'm seventy-one, Ms. Galloway, and I have watched cowardice compound interest for four decades, and I would like, before the end, to make one withdrawal." Papers rustled again. "I have copies. Originals, some of them. Pre-alteration manifests, internal routing memos, and a partial personnel roster of the triage committee dated before they purged it. I've kept them in a cold-storage unit under a false label for thirty-one years. The label says *orchid substrate, do not open until spring.*"

Despite everything—the fear, the exhaustion, the fresh memory of Odile Ferran's tomato-farm refusal—I laughed.

"That's the best cover label I've ever heard."

"I'm glad you approve. It was either that or *deceased aunt's tax records,* but I felt that invited scrutiny. Nobody scrutinizes orchids." The crispness returned, but warmer now, like a lamp turned up a notch. "Now. Tell me your plan, and then tell me everything wrong with it, because I promise you I will find more wrong with it than you will."

* * *

We met in person eleven days later, on Relay Twelve, in the back of Tomas's salvage office, surrounded by hull plating and the smell of machine grease and, inexplicably, cinnamon. Tomas had hung a hand-lettered sign on the door that read CLOSED FOR INVENTORY, which was true in the sense that we were taking inventory of crimes against humanity.

Mirela turned out to be small and sharp-eyed, with white hair cropped close and hands that moved constantly, sorting invisible papers. She'd brought her cold-storage cache in a battered case, and when she opened it, the room went quiet. Thirty-one years of evidence, wrapped in anti-static film and labeled in a neat, unkillable hand.

Tomas picked up a routing memo, read it, put it down, and rubbed his face with both hands. "Okay," he said. "Okay. So this is real-real. Not conspiracy-podcast real. Real-real." He looked at me. "What's the plan? Please tell me there's a plan and it's not just *be loud and hope.*"

"It's not just be loud and hope," I said. "It's be loud *and precise.* Earth's final transmission loop is dying—its power cell fails within the year. But while it lives, it reaches every habitat, every relay, every pod bay's monitoring system. It's the one channel nobody owns anymore, because nobody remembers it matters." I spread the ledger copy on the workbench between us. "We load it with everything. Full manifest. Triage ledger. Names. And we trigger the waking protocol at the same time, so that when the truth arrives, the people it's about are already opening their eyes."

Silence. Mirela was frowning at the ledger with her whole face.

"The waking protocol," she said slowly. "From outside the Archive."

"That's the part I'm still solving."

"That's the part that kills everyone if you solve it wrong," she said, not unkindly. "If security traces your signal before the thaw completes, they cut power to the bays and call it containment. Every sleeping person dies in the dark, and the last thing the system hears is your broadcast naming them." She tapped the ledger. "You're not planning a message, Ms. Galloway. You're planning a hostage rescue conducted over a public address system, with a countdown clock, run by three volunteers and a salvage ship."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad."

"It sounds *accurate,*" she said. "Which is worse." But she was already pulling a chair toward the workbench, already spreading her routing memos beside the ledger, already, irreversibly, in it. "Fortunately, accuracy is my favorite genre. Sit down. Both of you. We're going to find every way this plan can fail, and then we're going to fail slightly less than that."

Tomas grinned and dragged over a crate to sit on. "Gran also used to say something else," he offered. "*Perfect plans are how cowards procrastinate.*"

"Your grandmother," Mirela said, "sounds like she'd have been useful."

"She'd have loved this. She'd have brought snacks."

And that was how it began—not with oaths or manifestos, but with three people who had each been paid to be quiet, sitting around a salvage workbench under a CLOSED FOR INVENTORY sign, arguing about signal latency while Tomas's kettle whistled and the cinnamon smell turned out to be, on investigation, actual cinnamon rolls he'd baked that morning because, in his words, *conspiracy meetings go better with breakfast.*

Odile Ferran never called back. I thought about her sometimes after that—thought about her tomatoes, her careful math, her rehearsed refusal—and I understood her completely, and I forgave her completely, and I hoped with everything I had that history would be gentler to her caution than it deserved to be.

But three was enough. Three volunteers, one dying transmission loop, thousands of sleepers, and a plan full of holes that Mirela was already annotating in red ink.

It was on the fourth night, while threading test pings through the relay chain, that I noticed the ping from Relay Seven coming back four milliseconds slow.

Four milliseconds. Nothing. A rounding error.

Except Mirela had taught me better than that, and by morning I'd learned that nothing, in this business, is ever nothing—and that one of our three had already started answering questions I hadn't asked him yet.
