## 15. Sabotage Ethics

The safehouse was a decommissioned water reclamation module bolted to the underside of Relay Nine, which meant it smelled faintly of recycled regret and had just enough gravity to make coffee possible but not pleasant. Emrys had rented it under the name "M. Okafor, cargo surveyor," one of her last living aliases. She'd begun to think of them as dying relatives—each one she burned left a small cold space where a person used to be.

Three allies sat around a folding table that wobbled on its magnetic feet: Devi Raghunathan, a former power systems engineer who'd spent thirty years maintaining relays nobody thanked her for; Tomas Ilic, a soft-spoken freight pilot with a face like an apology and three children asleep in pods he wasn't supposed to know existed; and Yara Chen, who had been a records clerk on Ceres before Emrys bribed her, and who had turned out to be less a bribe-taker than a co-conspirator waiting for someone to ask.

Between them, projected onto the table's scarred surface, floated the schematic of Earth's final transmission loop.

It looked harmless. A ring of relay buoys in decaying orbit around a dead planet, powered by a single isotope cell that had outlasted its warranty by two decades and would fail within the year. For forty years it had repeated humanity's last messages—the goodbye from the Shanghai launch coordinator, the child singing to her grandmother in Lagos, the flat bureaucratic voice reading evacuation numbers like a lullaby. The whole species' answering machine.

"And here's the problem," Devi said, tapping the cell icon. It pulsed red, obligingly. "The loop runs on a dedicated feed off the Archive's grid. Separate bus, separate breakers, but same reactor. If we push a payload through the loop's uplink at full broadcast strength—and we'd need full strength for it to propagate through the buoy chain—we spike demand on that bus by roughly four hundred percent."

"Roughly," Emrys said.

"I'm an engineer, not a poet. Roughly four hundred percent. The Archive's reactor is running at sixty-two percent capacity because—" Devi's voice did something complicated, "—because half the cryopods are dark. They throttled it down when they stopped waking people. So there *is* headroom. On paper."

"On paper," said Tomas.

"On paper, yes. But that reactor hasn't been load-tested since before the triage. Its control systems are held together by Sefu Okonkwo's prayers and whatever maintenance budget the deciders deigned to approve. You ask it for a four-hundred-percent spike on any bus, and one of two things happens. Either the breakers trip—which means our broadcast dies mid-manifest, halfway through a list of names, which might be worse than never starting—or the bus doesn't trip because the breakers are as tired as everything else down there, and the brownout propagates into the pod bays." She looked up. "And the pods don't brownout gracefully. They die."

The table was quiet. Somewhere above them, a relay dish rotated with a sound like a slow breath.

Emrys stared at the schematic until the numbers swam. Four thousand, six hundred and twelve occupied pods. That was the number she carried now, everywhere, the way other people carried grief or faith. Half of them were already dark—left frozen without power, their occupants in a sleep that had quietly become something else. The other half blinked green on Sefu's maintenance displays, alive, dreaming, one power fluctuation away from becoming statistics.

"So we can't do it," Tomas said. Not accusingly. Almost hopefully. He had three kids in those bays. Emrys had watched him look at the pod manifests the way a man looks at a lottery ticket he's afraid to scratch.

"We can't do it *that way*," Emrys said. "There's a difference."

"There's always a difference," Yara said. "That's what everyone says right before they kill someone."

Devi snorted despite herself. "Cheerful."

"I'm a records clerk. Cheerfulness died in my department years ago." But Yara pulled the schematic toward her, fingers dancing, and began pulling power draw histories. "Show me the loop's actual consumption profile. Not rated. Actual."

Devi fed her the data. Yara studied it, lips moving slightly, doing arithmetic the old-fashioned way because she didn't trust anything she couldn't watch herself think.

"The loop idles at almost nothing," she said finally. "It repeats stored audio. It's not computing, it's not receiving, it's barely transmitting—it's a whisper on a wire. The four-hundred-percent figure assumes we broadcast at emergency propagation strength so every habitat and every ship in the system hears it simultaneously."

"That was the point," Emrys said. "Everyone wakes *and* everyone hears why."

"Yes. And that's also the most expensive version of the truth." Yara looked at her. "What if we don't need everyone to hear it at once?"

* * *

They argued for eleven hours. Emrys kept track because the module's clock chimed on the hour with the gentle insistence of a grandmother reminding you to eat, and by chime nine she'd started flinching.

The first plan was Devi's: stage the broadcast. Feed the payload to the loop in low-power segments over several days, letting each buoy store and repeat, then trigger full propagation only for the final block—the names of the deciders—when the Archive could be temporarily taken off the loop's bus entirely and run on battery reserve for ninety minutes. Ninety minutes of battery on a grid that hadn't been exercised since the triage. Devi called it "a controlled stress test." Sefu, consulted over a jittery one-way link, called it "asking a hundred-year-old to sprint."

The second plan was Yara's, and it was colder: wake the pods first, quietly, using the thaw sequence's own power curve spread across weeks, and broadcast afterward, when there were no longer thousands of lives hanging on the grid's mood. Wake everyone, then tell them. The problem with that plan was that waking took time—weeks of controlled thaw per bay bank—and the moment the first unauthorized pod opened, the Archive's security would know, and security answered to the deciders, and the deciders had demonstrated repeatedly and creatively what they did to people who found the list. Wake first meant getting caught first, with the job half done.

The third plan was Tomas's, and nobody liked it, including Tomas. Sabotage the loop's power cell deliberately—a controlled failure, days before it would fail anyway—and force the Archive to reroute, creating exactly the power gap his calculations said could absorb a full-strength broadcast. "We're not killing anyone," he said, in the voice of a man rehearsing. "The cell has months left. Maybe weeks. We're just... choosing the hour."

"You're choosing the hour of a heart attack," Devi said, "for a patient whose pacemaker shares a wall socket with four thousand life support units."

"I know what I'm saying." His hands were flat on the table, steadying it or himself. "I've done the math four times. My kids are in Bay Twelve. Do you think I'd propose this if the math gave me any other answer? I want the version where nobody pays. There isn't one. So now we're just deciding who pays, and how much, and whether we're honest about it."

Emrys had spent her career among paperwork that decided who lived and died without ever using those words. Actuarial tables. Settlement schedules. Confidentiality clauses. The deciders hadn't murdered anyone, technically—they'd *allocated*. She heard allocation in Tomas's voice and hated it, and understood it, and hated that she understood it.

"No," she said.

Tomas looked at her.

"We're not sabotaging the cell. We're not choosing an hour for a death and calling it scheduling." She stood, because sitting felt too much like a committee. "My mother stood in front of nine people and told them selective waking was murder, and they struck her name through and put her in the dark. If I solve this by running the same math with kinder handwriting, then they win. They win even if we win. Because the lesson everyone learns is that this is how decisions get made—you find the number of deaths you can live with and you file it."

"It's easy to be pure when your mother's already dead," Yara said quietly.

The words landed like a dropped tool. Devi reached out as if to take them back, but Yara shook her head. "No. Someone had to say it. Emrys—your stakes are memory. Mine are records. Devi's are machines. Tomas's are breathing. Of course we disagree about acceptable losses. The question is whether we're building a plan or just finding the version of the bill we personally don't have to pay."

Emrys sat back down. The clock chimed ten.

"Then let's pay all of it," she said. "Out loud. Itemized. Every option, every cost, on the table where we can see them, and nobody gets to hide behind 'roughly.'"

* * *

It took another six hours, and it was the least dramatic work Emrys had ever done that mattered this much. They built a ledger—not the deciders' kind, theirs—of every approach and its body count, estimated honestly, ranges included, uncertainties flagged in yellow because Devi insisted uncertainty deserved a color.

Full-strength immediate broadcast: risk of grid cascade, worst case 4,600 dead, best case zero, probability unknown. Staged broadcast with battery bridge: lower spike, but ninety minutes of untested battery reserve, and if the batteries failed at minute forty, the pods died in the dark during the most-watched transmission in human history. Wake-first-then-tell: safest for the pods, deadliest for the plan, near-certain interception. Controlled cell failure: rejected, unanimously, though Tomas's hand had shaken when he withdrew it, and Emrys had caught his eye and nodded, and that nod had cost her something she couldn't name yet.

It was Devi, somewhere past midnight ship-time, who broke the deadlock, and she did it the way engineers always do—not with inspiration but with stubbornness.

"The loop doesn't have to carry the whole payload at once," she said. She'd been staring at the buoy chain diagram, chewing a stylus. "It's a *ring*. Forty buoys. Each one stores and repeats. What if we treat it like a queue instead of a broadcast? Load the manifest into the buoys over days at idle power—each buoy holds a segment. Then the trigger isn't a power event at all. The trigger is a *routing* command. Tell the buoys to start passing their segments around the ring at full relay gain. The gain comes from the buoys' own transmitters, not the Archive's bus. The Archive only ever sees idle draw plus one routing packet."

Yara sat up. "And the buoys' transmitters are rated for—"

"Emergency propagation. They were built to scream across the system if Earth needed a last word. Nobody ever throttled them down because nobody ever imagined needing to say anything else." Devi smiled, and it was the first time Emrys had seen her do it, and it made her look twenty years younger and very tired. "The last words are already loaded in the machine, friends. We're just teaching it to say ours too."

"Failure modes," Emrys said, because she was who she was.

"One. The routing command gets caught by Archive security before it propagates—we send it from outside the ring, through a relay they don't own, and we fragment it so no single packet contains anything actionable. Two. The buoys' storage is degraded after forty years—some segments may corrupt. So we triple-redundancy the manifest and accept that some names might arrive garbled." Devi's jaw tightened. "Which is unacceptable, so we'll verify every segment twice. Three. The thaw sequence still needs the Archive's own power, and that part hasn't gotten easier. Waking takes eleven hours once triggered, and the trigger has to come from inside or very near the Archive."

"So the broadcast tells everyone *why*, and the thaw tells everyone *that*," Yara said slowly. "We don't have to sequence them perfectly. We have to sequence them closely enough that by the time security understands what's happening, the pods are committed to thaw. Once a pod passes the first threshold, you can't stop it without killing the occupant. Security's choice becomes: let them wake, or become the thing the broadcast says they are, live, in front of everyone."

"Make them choose in public," Emrys said. "Yes."

Tomas had gone very still. "And if they choose wrong? If they cut power anyway?"

"Then the broadcast is already in the air," Emrys said. "Every ship, every habitat, every relay will be hearing the manifest and the ledger and the nine names while it happens. There's nowhere to administer mercy quietly after that. Whatever they do, they do it as the villains of the last transmission humanity ever sends." She looked around the table. "I'm not saying it's clean. I'm saying it's *legible*. Everyone will see exactly what was done and exactly who chose it. No more impossible accidents. After tomorrow, every accident is possible to explain."

The clock chimed two. Devi began writing the routing fragmentation scheme. Yara started triple-copying the manifest. Tomas volunteered for the near-Archive trigger position, and Emrys opened her mouth to refuse and closed it again, because his children were in Bay Twelve and she understood, finally, that she had no right to stand between a parent and that arithmetic.

She drafted the message to Sefu instead. *Eleven hours. One trigger. Hold the grid steady and don't let anyone else hold it for you.* Then, after a moment, she added: *Your duty ends where their waking begins. Let it begin.*

Outside the little module, Relay Nine turned its dishes toward the dark, faithfully forwarding other people's messages. In forty hours, give or take, it would forward hers.

Emrys saved the draft, encrypted it, and sent it—and then sat very still, because the acknowledgment ping came back from the Archive's internal relay in under a second, along with a second signal she hadn't requested: a maintenance query from Bay Twelve, timestamped four minutes ago, requesting priority inspection.

Someone inside the Archive was already asking questions about Tomas's children's pods.
