## 20. Everyone Wakes

* * *

The first thing Halima Osei felt was the cold leaving her hands.

It went in stages—fingertips first, then wrists, then the long slow thaw up her forearms like spring moving through a frozen river—and she thought, with the peculiar clarity of someone who has been asleep for sixty-one years: *I know this feeling. This is the feeling of my mother pulling me out of a snowbank when I was six.*

Then the lid opened, and the light came in, and she screamed for four seconds before remembering that screaming used air and air was apparently still a thing that existed.

A voice was coming from somewhere above her. Not a person—a broadcast, tinny and enormous at once, echoing down the long dark throat of the Archive's Bay Nine. A woman's voice, clipped and tired and absolutely steady, reading names.

"—pod 4471, Vashti Okonkwo-Reyes, passenger, Meridian manifest, status: struck through by order of the Triage Council, objection filed and denied—"

Halima lay there in her foam, blinking at the frost on the inside of the canopy, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing when they'd put her under. She had been thirty-four. Her daughter had been nine. If the girl had lived—if anyone had lived—she would be seventy now. Older than Halima was supposed to be. That was the joke of cryosleep, the one nobody told you: you didn't escape time, you just handed it to your children to hold for you.

She sat up. The bay stretched away from her in both directions, thousands of pods, and along the row she could see lids opening in ragged sequence, like a field of flowers deciding all at once that it was morning. People were sitting up. People were coughing. Somewhere to her left a man was weeping loudly and without embarrassment, and somewhere to her right a woman was already complaining about the temperature, which Halima found so profoundly human that she started laughing and couldn't stop for a full minute.

"—and the following persons," the voice continued, "signed the triage order. I am going to read their names now, because they spent forty years making sure no one else would."

Halima swung her legs off the pod, wrapped herself in the emergency blanket some forgotten protocol had stocked, and listened to every single name.

* * *

In Habitat Concord, which is what the deciders called the station where they lived while half of humanity slept, the morning shift of the security office began the way every shift began: with tea, with grumbling about the recycling scrubbers, and with Officer Petra Lindqvist updating the watch log.

Petra was twenty-six years old. She had been born on Concord. She had been raised on stories about the Sacrifice—the noble, terrible triage that had saved "the essential half" while Earth burned—and she had believed those stories the way you believe the floor beneath your feet, which is to say completely, right up until the moment it isn't there.

At 0612 station time, every screen in the security office changed.

Not crashed. Changed. The watch log replaced itself with a document, formatted plainly, headed with a title that Petra read three times because her brain kept refusing it:

**THE TRIAGE LEDGER — COMPLETE — AS FILED**

Below it, two columns. WAKE. DO NOT WAKE. And below that, a third column she hadn't known existed: OBJECTIONS FILED, with dates, with names, with the word STRUCK THROUGH stamped over each one in red.

Her supervisor's name was in the header block. So was the name of the woman who ran the school Petra had attended. So was the name of the kind old man who played chess in the park module every Thursday and always let Petra's little brother win.

Petra looked around the office. Everyone was staring at their screens. Nobody spoke. The tea steamed.

"Is this real?" said Officer Adeyemi finally, from the corner desk.

"It's sourced," said Petra, who had spent the last ninety seconds checking, because she was twenty-six and thorough and had not yet learned that thoroughness was not always a kindness to yourself. "It's sourced from the Archive mainframe. It's got checksums."

Another silence. Longer.

"So," said Adeyemi. "Our parents put half the species in the dark. On purpose. And then named a playground after themselves."

"I don't think the playground was them," Petra said. "I think the playground was us. We just—" She stopped. She looked at the third column again, at the objections, at the names of people who had stood up in a council chamber forty years ago and said *this is murder* and been erased for it. "We just never asked who got to say no."

The alarm klaxon chose that moment to go off, which was almost a relief, because it gave everyone something to do that wasn't thinking.

By 0700, Petra Lindqvist had made a decision that would define the rest of her life, and the remarkable thing about it—the thing she would tell interviewers years later, once interviews were a thing people wanted to give her—was how easy it was. She simply walked to the detention wing, unlocked the holding cell where an old man named Sefu Okonkwo sat waiting, and said, "Sir, I think you'd better come see the screens."

* * *

Pod 11208 opened onto darkness and the smell of machine oil, and Dev Anand woke up furious.

He didn't know why at first. The fury arrived ahead of the context, riding up out of the deep sleep like a diver surfacing too fast, and for a good ten seconds he lay in his pod being angry at absolutely everything—the cold, the light, the universe, the specific texture of the foam pad against his shoulder blade—before the broadcast voice drifted down the bay and supplied him with a target.

He was a dockworker. Had been. Sixty-two years ago he'd loaded evacuation shuttles with his own hands, hauling families and their ration crates up the gantries while the sky over Mumbai turned the color of a bruise, and he remembered standing at the top of the ramp watching a manifest clerk turn people away. Turn them away politely. With stamps.

"They told us it was capacity," he said now, to the young woman in the next pod, who had woken ten minutes earlier and was looking at him with the wide-eyed caution people reserve for strangers who talk to themselves. "Capacity. We had *empty bays*. I carried cargo past empty bays. I remember thinking, well, they must be saving those for someone important." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Turns out they were saving them for no one. They were saving them for *later*. In case the important people's friends needed a spot."

"That's—" the young woman started.

"That's the whole thing," Dev said. "That's the entire crime. It fits in one sentence and it took forty years to say out loud." He flexed his fingers, testing them, finding them workable. "Right. I've been asleep since before your grandparents met, I'm stiff as a bulkhead, and I am going to find whoever signed my name into the dark column and ask them to explain it to my face. You coming?"

The young woman considered him. Around them the bay was filling with the sounds of several thousand people relearning how to be bodies—coughing, groaning, one man three rows down singing something that might have been a lullaby or might have been a sea shanty, pitch uncertain but conviction total.

"Yeah," she said. "Yeah, okay. Someone should probably walk you there before you fall over."

"Lead the way," Dev said grandly, and immediately stumbled, and was caught, and pretended he hadn't been.

* * *

On Relay Station Seven, Tomas Reyes heard his own name in the broadcast and dropped a wrench on his foot.

He was the relay tech who had sold Emrys Galloway's plan to security for a bay for his family—one bay, one chance, that was the whole arithmetic, he'd told himself, it wasn't betrayal if the alternative was his daughter growing up in the dark—and he had spent the eleven hours since the uplink went live in a state of terror so complete it had achieved a kind of serenity. Whatever happened now, happened. He'd made his bed in the dark column and he could lie in it.

Except.

"—passenger list continues: pod 9034, Marisol Reyes-Vance, age seven at boarding—"

His daughter's pod number. Read aloud. Into the loop. Into forever.

And then, a few lines later, in the flat recitation of the ledger: *Reyes, Tomas — collaborator, coerced; testimony attached.*

Attached. There was a file attached. His statement, the one he'd given Sefu in the Archive, the one where he'd cried and named names and begged—they'd kept it. They'd put it in the record. Not to punish him. To *contextualize* him. Some archivist's instinct, some stubborn insistence that the record be true all the way down, had preserved even the ugly parts, even him.

Tomas sat down on the deck of Relay Seven, cradled his bruised foot, and laughed until he hiccupped.

"You absolute maniac," he said aloud, to no one, to Emrys Galloway wherever she was. "You put *me* in the broadcast."

His comm lit up. Then it lit up again. Then it started doing the thing comms do when everyone you have ever known calls you simultaneously, which is mostly heat and noise. He let it ring. He had, he realized, exactly one call worth making first, and he knew the routing code by heart, because he'd sold it to monsters and the least he could do was give it back to his family.

He dialed his sister.

* * *

Habitat Meridian—named, with a tastelessness that Emrys Galloway would later describe as "almost impressive"—was the deciders' flagship residence, and its evening was not going well.

Councilor Edith Voss, oldest of the nine, watched from her quarters as the habitat's public concourse filled with people. Not an angry mob—she noted that with a professional's disappointment. Something worse. A *reading* crowd. Hundreds of residents standing in small clusters, terminals held up, comparing the ledger against the rosters of the waking schedule she and her colleagues had administered for four decades like a benevolent lottery. Checking the math. Finding the math obscene.

Her comm chimed. Security Chief Aris Danner, whose loyalty she had purchased in promotions and whose silence she had purchased in the same currency.

"Councilor. We have a situation in the concourse."

"I can see the concourse from here, Aris."

"No, ma'am. I mean—we have a situation with *security*." A pause, and when he resumed, his voice had the careful flatness of a man reading from notes he hates. "Third watch has stood down. Second watch is... debating. Two of my officers have arrested me, Councilor. I am currently being detained in my own office, and honestly—" a breath that might have been a laugh, "—honestly, they're being very polite about it."

Edith Voss set down her tea. Forty years ago, in a room with eight other frightened, powerful people, she had signed a document that decided who deserved to see the future, and she had told herself every night since that it had been the hardest thing anyone had ever done, and therefore forgiven. She had built a life on that forgiveness. She had built a *playground* on it.

Now the ledger was on every screen, and the objections were on every screen, and among the objections, dated forty years back, was a recording she had ordered destroyed and which had evidently not been destroyed, of a woman named Vashti saying, in a voice that stayed calm for eleven minutes and then did not: *"You are not choosing who lives. You are choosing who you can bear to look at. Those are different lists, and you know it."*

Edith Voss looked at the door of her quarters, behind which footsteps were gathering.

There were, she understood, two kinds of exits available to her. One led to the concourse, to the crowd, to the reckoning. The other led to the maintenance docks, to a shuttle, to running—which she had spent four decades insisting she was too principled to do, and which, she discovered now, examining herself with the cold honesty she'd always prided herself on, she was absolutely considering.

Down the corridor, someone knocked. Politely. The way you knock on a door when you already know it's going to open.

* * *

In Bay Twelve of the Archive, a pod opened and Ruth Amari woke to hear her best friend's daughter reading names.

She knew the voice instantly. You don't spend fifteen years listening to a child practice violin in the next compartment without knowing her grown-up voice, even filtered through sixty years and a broadcast system. But it was impossible, because Vashti had stayed awake—Vashti had *refused* the pod, had filed her objection, had marched into that council chamber with her chin up—and Ruth had gone under carrying the certainty that her friend would die on the surface with the rest of them, loud and unbowed.

So either the voice was a recording—and it was, part of it was, the manifest recitation was a recording, of course it was—or...

"...objection filed and denied," the recording finished, and then, live, in the same room as the microphone, a second voice joined it. Younger. Rougher. Familiar in a way that bypassed Ruth's brain entirely and went straight to whatever organ handles grief.

"—last entry," the younger voice said. "Correction to the record. Vashti Okonkwo-Reyes did not die in the loss of the Meridian. She died in Chamber Four of Habitat Concord, forty years ago, objecting. The Meridian never flew. My mother's name belongs to no accident. I am Emrys Galloway, and I am signing this correction."

Ruth Amari lay in her pod and put both hands over her mouth.

*Little Emrys*, she thought. *The girl who used to hide under the table during rehearsals.* All this time she'd imagined the child scattered to some foster rotation, ground smooth by the settlements, and instead—

Instead the girl had grown up into a scalpel and spent her whole life quietly cutting toward this exact room.

"Well," Ruth said to the ceiling of the bay, to the thousands of neighbors opening their eyes around her, to the general and specific absurdity of being alive. "Vashti. You would *not* believe the week your daughter is having."

Around her, Bay Twelve woke up laughing, crying, coughing, arguing, singing—one man was definitely singing now, and had committed to it—and Ruth climbed out of her pod on unsteady legs, sixty years late and right on time, and went to find out what needed doing.

Because that was the thing nobody put in the broadcasts, she thought, steadying herself against the cold rail, looking down the endless rows of opening lids: justice didn't arrive finished. It arrived the way the pods were arriving—all at once, everywhere, half-broken, needing thousands of hands.

* * *

And in a cramped cabin aboard a stolen tender, three hours out from the Archive with alarms still chasing her across every channel, Emrys Galloway finally allowed herself to stop moving.

She sat with her back against the hull and listened to the loop repeat itself—the manifest, the ledger, the names, her mother's voice, her own signature at the end of it—and she waited for the feeling she'd expected. Triumph, maybe. Or grief, finally spent. Something with an ending in it.

What came instead was quieter and more annoying: the awareness that nothing had ended. Thousands were waking to a world that had lied to them, and they would need food, law, answers, trials. The deciders were scattering or standing, depending on their nerve. Half the system's institutions were discovering, in real time, that their foundations were a confession. And somewhere in all of it, her name was now on every manifest in human space—not hidden, not forged, not deniable. Visible. Permanently.

Her comm blinked. Priority flag, routed through Relay Seven, origin: the Archive.

She opened it expecting Sefu. It wasn't Sefu.

*"Ms. Galloway,"* said a stranger's voice, formal and exhausted and strangely gentle. *"This is Officer Petra Lindqvist, Habitat Concord security. I have custody of Sefu Okonkwo, and I have custody of something else: eight of the nine signatories, in detention, asking for lawyers nobody knows how to be. The ninth fled on a shuttle, registry Meridian-Adjunct, heading for the long route. Toward you."*

A pause.

*"They're calling it a tribunal. Everyone is. Nobody's sure who gets to judge whom, and frankly, ma'am, you're the only person whose name is on both sides of the record. They want you to come back. Some of them want you to testify. Some of them want you in the dock."*

Emrys closed her eyes and laughed, once, without much humor in it.

*"Which is it?"* the message asked. *"Witness, or accused?"*

She looked at the nav display. The long route stretched out ahead of her, and somewhere on it, a shuttle was running dark with the last of the nine aboard.

"Both," she said to the empty cabin, and turned the ship around.
