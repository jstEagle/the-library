## 21. The Price Itemized

The tribunal convened in the Archive's old hydroponics bay, because it was the only room large enough to hold three hundred people and the only one with working lights on that level. Someone had strung cables from the waking protocol's power trunk, and the grow-lamps burned a soft amber over the benches, so that Emrys stood trial in what had once been meant for tomatoes and was now full of humanity: the freshly thawed, wrapped in emergency blankets printed with shipping logos from companies that no longer existed, their hair still flat from forty years of gel, their eyes still adjusting to the idea of being alive.

They had built her a dock out of cargo pallets. It was, she thought, a very honest piece of furniture. Nothing about it pretended to be anything other than what it was.

The presiding officer was a woman named Dr. Amara Voss, who had gone into cryosleep as a pediatric oncologist and woken up four days ago to discover she was now, by default and acclamation, a judge. She had protested this for approximately six hours before accepting it with the weary grace of someone who had spent her career delivering bad news to parents and understood that sometimes the job finds you.

"Emrys Galloway," Voss said, reading from a tablet that someone had charged off the same jury-rigged grid. "You are not here for the broadcast."

"I know," Emrys said.

"Say it anyway. For the record. For them." Voss gestured at the three hundred, and beyond them, at the feed relays carrying the proceedings to every habitat where pods were opening or had opened or would open tomorrow. "Half the system is watching. They deserve to hear you say what this is."

Emrys looked out at the amber light and the blankets and the faces. Some of them hated her. She could see it plainly—there was no point pretending otherwise, and she had spent too many years being invisible to want to practice invisibility now. But others looked at her the way you look at a surgeon who saved your life and took your leg doing it: grateful and grieving in the same breath, unable to separate the two, unwilling to try.

"This is not a trial for the broadcast," Emrys said. "I would do the broadcast again. I will not apologize for it, and I want that entered into the record so clearly that nobody wastes time asking me later."

A murmur ran through the benches. Voss let it run itself out.

"You are here," Voss continued, "for the deaths. Eleven people died when security stormed Relay Seven during your uplink. Four more died in the Archive when the alarm protocols triggered a coolant purge in Bay Section Twelve—a purge your signal tripped. And one man, a dockworker named Tomas Reyes, died at Ceres Station two days after you left, in what the new interim authorities are calling a reprisal killing." She paused. "Do you dispute these numbers?"

"No."

"Do you dispute responsibility?"

Here it was. The whole trial balanced on this hinge, and Emrys had turned it over every night for the nine days since her arrest, lying awake in a converted supply locker with Sefu's voice in her memory saying *justice outside the law must still answer to the living*, and she had arrived at an answer that satisfied neither her lawyers—she had been assigned two volunteers, a former maritime arbitrator named Okoye and a very young man who had woken up that morning and immediately asked if he could help with something, anything—and certainly did not satisfy the part of her that wanted absolution.

"I dispute the word *responsibility*," Emrys said, "because it's doing too much work. I didn't kill anyone. The deciders' security killed eleven people at Relay Seven; the Archive's own fail-safes, installed by the people who built this lie, killed four in Section Twelve; and whoever murdered Tomas Reyes did it to punish a community, not to stop me—I was already in custody. If you're asking whether I caused those deaths, then yes. My choices put those people in the path of violence, and I knew the risk, and I chose anyway. I plead guilty to the cost. Not the cause."

Voss wrote something down. "That's a fine distinction, Ms. Galloway."

"It's the only distinction I have," Emrys said. "I've spent my career reading accident reports where the cause was always structural and the cost was always personal. The deciders hid behind structure—the protocol, the inheritance, the duty. 'It wasn't us, it was the selection list.' I refuse to hide behind it from the other direction. 'It wasn't me, it was the security forces.' No. I lit the fuse knowing there were people standing on it. Guilty to the cost."

Okoye leaned over to the very young volunteer and whispered something. The young man nodded solemnly, having apparently decided that whatever she'd said was legally significant.

*

The testimony took three days.

They heard from the family of Imani Okafor, the ally captured at Relay Seven, who had survived detention but whose brother had not survived the storming of the relay. They heard from a woman from Bay Section Twelve who had woken up with frost damage in her left hand and a dead sister in the pod beside hers. They heard from security officers—newly unemployed, newly confessing—who described the orders they'd been given and how quickly they'd been given them, which told everyone listening that the deciders' apparatus had not died with its exposure; it had simply changed employers.

Through all of it, Emrys sat on her pallet dock and listened without flinching, because flinching felt like a luxury purchased with other people's blood.

On the second day, a small crisis erupted when the defense attempted to introduce the triage ledger as evidence of mitigating circumstances—the argument being that Emrys had acted under a moral emergency, that the alternative to her broadcast was the quiet murder-by-neglect of half the frozen, that the deaths she caused were real but the deaths she prevented were statistical and therefore invisible, and the law has always been bad at seeing statistics.

The prosecution—an earnest committee of three survivors who had appointed themselves with the same cheerful improvisation that had produced Dr. Voss—objected that the ledger proved premeditation.

Both things were true. Everyone in the hydroponics bay knew both things were true. That was the problem with trials: they wanted one truth, and reality kept handing out pairs.

*

On the third day, they called Sefu Okonkwo.

He walked to the witness stand slowly—not from age, though he had plenty, but from the particular care of a man who had spent forty years moving quietly through a secret and was only now learning to move like his footsteps counted. He was thinner than when Emrys had first met him in the derelict yard. Detention had done that. He'd been held for nineteen days before the woken community's interim council had demanded his release, and he spoke of it the way he spoke of everything, mildly, as weather.

"Mr. Okonkwo," said the prosecution's lead, a blunt, kind-faced former cargo master named Ferro, "you were the Archive's custodian for thirty-one years. You maintained the selection protocol. You kept half the bays powered and half dark. Why should this community trust anything you say?"

"They shouldn't," Sefu said. "Trust is earned forward, not backward. I'm not asking for trust. I'm asking to be heard."

Ferro glanced at Voss, who nodded. "Then be heard. Tell us about Emrys Galloway."

Sefu folded his hands. He looked, Emrys thought, like a man preparing to give a lecture he'd given many times to an empty room.

"When she came to the Archive," he said, "she had every reason to hate me. I was the keeper of the machine that erased her mother. She could have taken the ledger and run. She could have sold it back to the deciders—they offered her a bay, you know. Her mother's pod restored. All she had to do was stay quiet."

"We know about the offer," Ferro said. "We've heard the recording."

"Then you know she refused it in under a minute. What you don't know is what she did next. She stayed up with me for two days designing the broadcast so it wouldn't touch the power grid. Two days without sleep, checking load calculations, because she said—" his voice caught, just slightly, the first crack in the mildness, "—she said the frozen weren't numbers to her anymore. She'd read every name. Out loud. All of them. She said you can't spend that long reading names and then treat any of them as expendable."

"She was wrong," Ferro said gently. "People died."

"Yes," Sefu said. "And she knows it better than anyone in this room, because she keeps the list. Not the manifest—the other list. The cost. She recites it every morning. Eleven names from Relay Seven. Four from Section Twelve. Tomas Reyes. She adds nothing to it and subtracts nothing from it. I've heard her do it through the wall of her cell." He looked directly at the benches, at the families. "I don't know what justice looks like for you. I genuinely don't. I failed at justice for forty years and called it duty. But I know what conscience looks like, because I had one once and let it atrophy, and watching her has been like watching a limb regrow. Whatever you decide, decide it knowing that the person in the dock has never once asked to be forgiven. Only to be counted accurately."

He stepped down. As he passed the dock, he and Emrys did not look at each other, because looking at each other would have undone them both, and there was still a verdict to get through.

*

Deliberation took five hours, which everyone agreed was remarkably fast for a community that had been awake for less than three weeks and had no constitution, no precedent, and no shared legal tradition older than the contents of a few cargo manifests.

Dr. Voss delivered it standing, in the amber light, with the grow-lamps humming overhead like patient insects.

"Emrys Galloway. We find that your broadcast was an act of rescue performed with foreseeable lethal consequences, knowingly accepted. We find that the deaths at Relay Seven, Section Twelve, and Ceres Station are attributable in part to your choices, and we decline to launder that finding through the actions of others, because you yourself refused to. We find you guilty of the cost." She let that sit. "And we find that punishing you further would be the Archive teaching itself its oldest lesson again—that some people can be spent for a greater good. We won't practice what we're prosecuting."

She paused, and something almost gentle came into her voice.

"The sentence is exile. You will leave the Archive and every habitat under interim authority, and you will not return while any member of this community lives who objects to your presence—which, I'll warn you, may be a long time, because we're planning to live. Your aliases are burned; you'll travel under your own name, visible on every manifest, exactly as you've become. That visibility is part of the sentence. You made yourself seen to save the dead. Now you'll stay seen among the living, and you'll carry what that costs."

Emrys stood. Her legs were stiff from three days on the pallet dock, and her voice, when it came, was steadier than she expected.

"I accept the sentence. And I want to say one thing to the families, if the court permits."

Voss nodded.

"I can't give you back your dead, and I won't insult you by explaining why the trade was worth it—you've each done that arithmetic yourselves, in the dark, and some of you got a different answer than mine, and both answers are true. All I can tell you is that I will remember your names longer than I remember my own. That's not comfort. It's just the truth, itemized."

In the third row, the woman from Section Twelve with the frost-damaged hand did not nod. But she did not look away either, and Emrys had learned, over a career of reading faces across tables, that this was sometimes the entire available vocabulary of mercy.

*

They gave her until morning.

She spent the evening in the control spine with Sefu, packing what little she owned: her father's insurance ledger, the objection recording, a printout of the cost list that she folded into her coat pocket over her heart, because sentimentality deserved good engineering.

"You could appeal," Sefu said. "Okoye thinks you'd win a reduced term. Half the council would vote for it."

"And the other half would spend years resenting me for staying. Exile is cleaner. Besides—" she smiled, tired, "—I've spent my whole life between relay stations. It's the only place I actually know how to live. At least now I get to do it honestly, with my name on the log."

Sefu laughed, then sobered. "There's something else. A message came in overnight, routed through the loop—it's still transmitting, did you know? Your payload, repeating. The whole system hears the manifest every ninety minutes. Like a lighthouse." He hesitated. "This message was addressed to you specifically. From a pod. 4471."

Emrys went very still. "My mother's section."

"Not her pod. But nearby. A woman named Adaeze Nwosu. She woke six days ago. She says—" Sefu handed him the tablet, and Emrys saw the first line and stopped breathing for a moment. "She says your mother gave her a letter. Forty years ago, the night before the bays were sealed. She says it was written, quote, *for whoever finished what I started.*"

Emrys read the line again. Then again. Outside the control spine, through the old observation glass, the stars wheeled slowly around the derelict yard, indifferent and enormous, and somewhere in the dark a transmission loop repeated the names of the dead and the guilty to anyone still listening.

"When does the next shuttle leave?" she asked.

"Six hours. Heading out on the long route."

Emrys folded the tablet closed and picked up her bag. "Then I'd better sign the manifest."
