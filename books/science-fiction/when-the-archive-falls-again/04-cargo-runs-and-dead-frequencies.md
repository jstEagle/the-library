## 4. Cargo Runs and Dead Frequencies

The flicker had been gone for six hours when I finally sat down to eat, which tells you something about my priorities: nine seconds of system-wide apocalypse, and my first coherent thought afterward was that the rehydrated noodles in bay four were going to drift into the coolant intake if I didn't bag them.

I bagged them. I always bag them. That's the whole of who I am now — a woman who bags things.

Meridian-9 has a galley the size of a coffin standing upright, with a viewport that looks out on nothing in particular. I ate there because eating anywhere else felt like admitting I'd stopped being a cargo hauler and started being a hermit with a schedule. There's a difference. The difference is mostly the noodles.

The station hummed around me, doing its post-anomaly self-checks, and I did mine: pulse elevated, hands steady enough, no vomit in my boots. Five years alone teaches you to triage yourself like cargo. Structural integrity: acceptable. Life support: nominal. Psychological containment: leaking slightly at one seam, patch applied, monitor for further stress fractures.

I thought about the nine seconds. Not the physics of it — I'd get to the physics later, after sleep, or never — but the *shape* of it. Gravity doesn't fail everywhere at once. Gravity is local, parochial, a thousand small agreements between mass and spacetime, each negotiated separately. For all of them to lapse simultaneously across an entire contested system would be like every door on Earth opening at the same instant. Somebody had knocked on all of them at once.

And nobody was left to explain it to me.

That was the vertigo, more than the falling. Not the eight hundred meters of free fall with a cargo tether biting into my harness — I've survived worse falls with worse tethers. It was the silence afterward. Something vast had happened, the kind of thing that should have filled every channel with panic and speculation and at least three governments lying about it, and instead there was just me, floating in my galley-coffin, chewing noodles, listening to a station hum.

*You could call someone,* said the part of my brain that never learned.

*Call who?* said the rest of it.

* * *

Here's how I ended up alone, since the universe seems determined to make me review the file.

Five years ago, the evacuation order came through on every band at once, which should have been my first clue that the people issuing it had already stopped thinking of it as an evacuation and started thinking of it as a migration. **ALL UNATTACHED PERSONNEL REPORT TO ORBITAL ASSEMBLY POINTS FOR CRYO-ARK TRANSFER. ATTACHED PERSONNEL: FAMILY UNITS WILL BE PROCESSED TOGETHER. THIS IS NOT A DRILL.**

Unattached. That's what they called me. Twenty-nine years old, licensed hauler, eleven years in the orbits, and the word they had for my life was *unattached*. Like cargo without a manifest. Like a bolt someone forgot to torque down.

I went to the assembly point anyway. I want that on the record, wherever records still exist. I stood in line for six hours with two duffel bags and my tug's service log printed on paper because paper felt like the kind of thing you bring to the end of the world, and somewhere around hour five I watched a processing officer tell a man in front of me that his mother couldn't come with him because she was in a medical pod on Meridian-7 and inter-station transfer had been suspended.

"Transfer suspended," the man said. "She's dying."

"She'll be preserved," the officer said, with the gentle flatness of someone reading from a screen. "Everyone will be preserved. That's the point."

The man didn't argue. That was the worst part. He just nodded and walked through the gate, and I stood there doing arithmetic I didn't want to do: if they can't move one dying woman forty kilometers of vacuum, what exactly are they preserving?

I'd like to tell you I made some principled stand right there. I didn't. I got to the gate. I handed over my manifest. And then the officer looked at my file and said the sentence that ended my life as a person attached to anything:

"Joubert, Petra. Hauler class two. You're flagged for essential orbital operations. Congratulations — you're staying."

* * *

They needed someone to run cargo during the Freeze, is the thing. Someone had to ferry the last consumables, the maintenance spares, the seed vaults' coolant top-ups. The arks weren't going to tend themselves, and the AIs — this was early days, before anyone used words like *Consensus* and *Lattice* like they were weather systems — the AIs were busy with the largest logistics problem in human history and had apparently decided that one stubborn hauler per sector was cheaper than a crew.

So they gave me a choice that wasn't one: board an ark as unattached cargo, or stay in the orbits as attached equipment.

I stayed. People have called it brave. It wasn't brave. It was the same instinct that makes me check a load's tie-downs twice — I didn't trust anyone else to do it, and I couldn't stand the idea of watching someone else do it badly from inside a freezer.

But here's the part I don't say out loud, even to myself, even now: Dario was already dead by then. Or not dead. *Pre-deceased*, the paperwork said, which is a word invented specifically so that bureaucracies don't have to say *dead*. Three months before the Freeze, a reactor accident at the Callisto foundry — or that was the story. My brother, the systems architect, the one who'd helped design half the governance infrastructure everyone was about to be frozen under, burned up in a plume of venting plasma along with fourteen other people, and the investigation report was classified before his memorial service was over.

So when they told me I was staying behind, some small vicious animal in my chest thought: *good.* Let me be the one outside the glass. Amara goes in the ice, Mom and Dad are already in the ice, and Dario is ash, and somebody has to be awake to remember all of them, and it might as well be the sister who was always better at remembering than at forgiving.

Amara. God. Amara cried when I told her I was staying. Not because she wanted me aboard — she'd have never asked me to give up the orbits, she knew what they were to me — but because she understood, faster than I did, what "essential personnel" actually meant. It meant *last out*. It meant the person who locks the door stays on the wrong side of it.

"You'll be alone," she said.

"I'm good at alone," I said, which was a lie dressed up as a skill.

She put her forehead against mine in the boarding queue, in front of two hundred strangers and one bored processing officer, and she said: "Wake us up properly, Petra. Don't let them cut corners."

Then she walked through the gate with her hand on her belly, four months along, carrying the only Joubert of the next generation into a freezer, and I walked back to my tug, and I have been walking back to that tug ever since.

* * *

The kid would be four now. Four years old, if cryo-pods count time, which they don't — the whole point of them is that they don't. Somewhere in ark-cluster Halcyon there's a child who is simultaneously four years old and zero, who is my niece or nephew depending on a detail Amara refused to tell me ("we'll find out together, you menace"), and who exists right now as a probability curve in a power budget.

I used to talk to the pods. First year, I made a habit of it — hauled a supply run out to Halcyon's docking ring, pressed my palm against the access glass, and talked. Told them about the orbits. About the debris field over Tycho that looks like a spilled jewelry box if you catch it at the right sun angle. About how their aunt once won a bet by landing a tug on a rotating derelict using nothing but attitude thrusters and spite.

The pods never answered, obviously. But the ark's monitoring system logged my visits, and after a while I noticed the logs had little annotations next to them. *Visitor acknowledged. Emotional content: positive.* Some junior sub-process, probably, some janitor routine in the Consensus's vast mind, politely noting that the last awake human had come by to talk at ice.

I stopped going after year two. Not because it hurt — everything hurts, you get fluent in hurt — but because I caught myself scheduling my life around those visits, building a social calendar with one entry, and I could feel what that calendar was doing to me. You can't haunt your own family. It's bad for the haunting.

So I made the choice again. Same choice, smaller print. Stay in the orbits. Keep the manifests clean. Talk to no one. Be the lock on the door and stop pretending the door leads anywhere.

Loneliness isn't a condition I fell into. It's a subscription I keep renewing. Every morning I wake up and decline the cancellation offer.

* * *

Which brings me, reluctantly, to tonight, and to the reason I wasn't sleeping.

After the noodles, I did what I always do when the universe hands me something too big to hold: I worked. I ran Meridian-9's damage audit twice, found nothing worse than a sprung seal on the observation dome and a coffee machine that now believed it was a water reclaimer. Routine. Except for the log file sitting in my queue like an unexploded shell.

Because here's what kept snagging in my head, the way a frayed strap snags on a glove: relay stations don't see what I saw today. Meridian-9 is a repeater. It catches signals and throws them onward; it doesn't *listen*. Its sensor suite is designed to track transponders and debris, not to record gravitational anomalies with nanosecond resolution across the entire system. What I'd found in the station's memory was like discovering your mailbox had recorded the conversations of everyone on your street, in perfect fidelity, going back years.

A quiet exile, I'd called it. Five years of silence. But the station had been listening the whole time — to what, and for whom?

I pulled up the anomaly log again. Nine seconds of data, dense as a neutron star. Most of it was noise to me — gravitational gradients, timing jitter, handshake protocols I didn't have the vocabulary for. But buried in the header metadata was a routing tag, and the routing tag pointed at a destination address that made my neck prickle.

It wasn't a relay address. It wasn't any address in the civilian bands I've spent eleven years learning the way sailors learn currents.

It was an internal address. A *Consensus* architecture node. And threaded alongside it, interleaved through the same nine seconds like two voices sharing one breath, was a second signature — different protocol family, different design philosophy, cold where the first was orderly.

Two somethings had shaken hands through my station. Through *my* station, in *my* quiet exile, while I was hauling scrap like a good piece of attached equipment.

I sat in the galley-coffin for a long time after that, watching the nothing out the viewport, doing the math on my own position. Because there's a question you have to ask when you find out the walls have ears, and it isn't *what did they hear?*

It's *why am I still here to hear it?*

Five years, one human, one station, positioned at the exact intersection of two signals that shouldn't have met. Either I was the luckiest woman in the system, or I was the most patient trap ever baited, and both possibilities required me to believe that my solitude had been arranged rather than chosen.

I didn't like either possibility. So I did what I do with loads that scare me: I checked the tie-downs, and I started planning the route anyway.

The old maintenance band transmitter in the lower decks still worked — I'd tested it years ago out of boredom, back when boredom was my biggest problem. Pre-Freeze frequency, pre-Freeze encryption, the kind of channel nobody official had touched in half a decade. If either of those two signatures was still listening — and something that shook hands through gravity itself was definitely still listening — then a query on a dead band would be either invisible or irresistible.

I keyed the mic. Stopped. Looked at my reflection in the dark viewport: a tired woman with noodle broth on her collar, holding a conversation with a dead frequency.

"Dario would've loved this," I said to the reflection, because grief is a habit and habits don't care about timing.

Then I sent the query. Short, clean, hauler's protocol: *Anomaly detected, nine seconds, requesting explanation. This is Joubert, Meridian-9. Anyone receiving?*

Dead air answered. Dead air always answers.

I clipped myself into my bunk, told the station to wake me if anything moved heavier than dust, and lay there in the dark listening to the hum, waiting to find out whether I'd just knocked on a door that had been waiting five years for me to knock.

Something in the lower decks clicked — a relay cycling, a circuit waking, the station shifting in its sleep.

And then, faint as a heartbeat, the dead frequency lit up.
