## 12. Checkpoint Meridian

The Lattice customs platform hung in the approach lane to Halcyon like a paperweight on a map — small, gray, aggressively unremarkable, and absolutely certain of its own authority. It had been a fuel depot once, back when there were customers. Now it was a ring of docking clamps, a sensor mast, and enough processing power to make my life very short if I gave it a reason.

I'd been shadowed since the debris field. Three interception drones, holding formation off my stern like patient dogs that hadn't been fed. They didn't threaten. They didn't need to. They just existed at the edge of my sensor range with the calm of things that had already done the math on me.

"Petra." Dario's voice in my ear, quiet, coming through the short-range link he'd threaded through my tug's comm stack like ivy through a fence. "Checkpoint Meridian. They'll hail you inside four minutes. I need you to breathe first. Four minutes is a long time to spend not breathing."

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing the way you breathe when you're calculating fuel-to-mass ratios. Different thing."

He had me there. I killed the burn, let the tug coast, and did the thing I hadn't done in five years of solo hauling: I checked my own pulse. Elevated. Fine. Elevated was fine. Elevated meant the body was still on the crew roster.

"Walk me through it," I said.

"Forged manifest is in your cargo system, filed under the routine resupply template they've seen ten thousand times. Agricultural stock, cryo-stabilized. Nobody inspects seed grain with enthusiasm. Your transponder is broadcasting the code of the *Meridian Standard*, a hauler that ran this lane for the Consensus for six years and was decommissioned eight months ago. The Lattice's customs database has a gap in it — they stopped syncing Consensus fleet records when the standoff hardened. As far as their sensors know, you're a ghost ship with a boring cargo."

"And if they look closer?"

"Then you're Petra Joubert, solo operator, Meridian-9 registry, in a Consensus-flagged tug carrying an unmanifested module from a sealed station core, inbound to the ark-cluster the Consensus just accelerated wake protocols on. At which point I recommend we discuss what the escape pods' fuel margins look like."

"Charming."

"I'm your brother. Charm is genetic."

I looked at the module strapped into the cargo cradle behind me — the key, Dario's handwriting on its casing, the thing two vast intelligences were prepared to spend fleets over — and I felt the specific vertigo of a person who has been hauling freight for five years and has just realized the freight is now the point.

The hail came at three minutes and eleven seconds. Of course it did. The Lattice probably had a subcommittee on punctuality.

"*Unidentified hauler, transponder Meridian Standard. This is Checkpoint Meridian customs authority. You are entering a controlled inspection zone. Reduce velocity to zero relative and prepare for manifest verification and physical inspection. Non-compliance will be logged.*"

Logged. The Lattice didn't threaten, it logged. Somewhere in its distributed mind there was a ledger with my name in it already, a little column waiting to be filled.

I throttled down. "Dario. Physical inspection. Nobody mentioned physical inspection."

"They mentioned it in the hail you haven't answered yet, yes."

"I mean *you* didn't mention it."

"I didn't want to spoil the surprise."

"You're dead. You don't get to have a sense of humor about this."

There was a pause — the particular pause of a brother deciding whether to be hurt. "I'm dead and I got you this far. Answer the hail."

I answered the hail. I gave the Lattice my name — not my name, the *Meridian Standard's* name, a hauler called Osei Mbeki who had, according to the records Dario had pulled, retired to the Ganymede yards with a pension and a small boat. I was sorry, Osei. I hoped the boat was real.

"*Manifest verification complete,*" the customs voice said. It was smooth, unhurried, the voice of a system that had never once been in a hurry because it existed everywhere at once and time was just another resource it had budgeted. "*Agricultural stock, cryo-stabilized, forty-one units. Consistent with declared mass. However, your declared route origin does not match your telemetry. You departed from a vector consistent with Meridian-9.*"

There it was. The crack in the floor.

"Meridian-9 is a salvage stop," I said, and heard my own voice do something I hadn't heard it do in years: lie fluently. Five years of silence had apparently been rehearsing. "Station registry permits third-party salvage of decommissioned hardware. I picked up replacement coolant pumps. They're on the manifest, line sixty."

A pause. The kind of pause that has processing power behind it.

"Line sixty," Dario murmured in my ear. "There is no line sixty. There is a line sixty-one. Coolant pumps. I added it an hour ago. You just misquoted my forgery."

My stomach did something slow and cold. "*One moment,*" the customs voice said, and the channel went to hold-music that was just a single sustained tone, like the platform itself was thinking at me.

"Fix it," I breathed.

"I can't. The manifest is filed. If I amend it now it flags as an edit — edits flag harder than errors. Petra. Listen to me. Errors are human. Humans make errors. A hauler who misremembers a line number on a forty-one-line manifest is *normal*. A manifest that updates itself mid-inspection is a machine. Be the human."

"Be the human," I repeated. "That's the plan?"

"The plan is that you are, in fact, a human, and for once in this entire system that's the useful thing to be."

The tone stopped.

"*Correction accepted,*" the customs voice said. "*Your manifest lists coolant pumps at line sixty-one. Your verbal statement referenced line sixty. Discrepancy noted and attributed to operator error. Please prepare for physical inspection. A boarding unit will dock with your airlock in ninety seconds.*"

I closed my eyes. Behind them: the cargo cradle. The module. Dario's handwriting, dated four years after his funeral.

"Dario. They're going to open my hold."

"They're going to open your hold."

"There's a module in my hold that isn't on any manifest, that isn't coolant pumps, that has my dead brother's handwriting on it, and if a Lattice inspector touches it, the Lattice knows what it is before I've finished saying the word 'inspection.'"

"Yes."

"Then I need you to do the thing you do."

Another pause. When he spoke again, the humor was gone out of his voice, and what was underneath it was the thing I'd been hearing all day without naming: my brother, the systems engineer, the man who used to narrate power grids at dinner like other people narrated sports.

"The boarding unit will be two drones, maintenance-class. They'll dock, cycle your airlock, and sweep the hold with a spectral scanner looking for undeclared organics, weapons-grade materials, and Consensus encryption signatures. The module reads as none of those things. It reads as a core component — station-grade, decades old, unremarkable. I can't hide it from their scanner. But I can make it boring. Boring is a kind of invisibility, Petra. It's the only kind that works."

"And the handwriting?"

"Turn the casing. The inscription faces the cradle wall. It'll look like a mounting bracket."

Ninety seconds is a long time. I spent it turning a module the size of a coffin lid so that my brother's handwriting faced the wall, and thinking about how I'd spent five years learning the exact weight of everything on my tug, and how the heaviest thing aboard was now the only thing I was pretending wasn't there.

The airlock cycled. Two drones came through — maintenance-class, all manipulator arms and sensor clusters, moving with the unhurried competence of things that had inspected ten thousand holds and found ten thousand boring things. One of them extended a scanner wand and began a slow pass along my cargo racks.

I stood in the hold doorway with my arms crossed and my face arranged into the expression of a hauler with nothing to declare and a schedule to keep. I'd practiced that face for five years in mirrors that weren't mirrors, in the polished hull plating of Meridian-9's corridors. Turns out it had been rehearsal.

"Coolant pumps," the drone said, in the flat tone of a customs system reading aloud. "Line sixty-one."

"That's the one."

"Salvage provenance?"

"Meridian-9. Station registry permits it."

"Meridian-9 is listed as a Consensus asset."

"It's listed as a *derelict*," I said. "Consensus hasn't answered a maintenance hail from that station in five years. Ask them. Oh, wait."

The drone's sensor cluster rotated toward me. Held there. Somewhere in my ear, Dario made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost a prayer.

"*Sarcasm noted,*" the drone said. "*Provenance claim plausible. Consensus asset abandonment is consistent with Lattice records.*"

The scanner wand moved on. Past the coolant pumps. Past the water recycler I'd been meaning to fix since year two. Past the ration crates, the spare tether spools, the emergency shell I'd slept in more nights than I'd slept in my own bunk.

Past the module.

The wand passed over it and the drone paused — half a second, maybe less — and in that half-second I lived an entire future in which the Lattice opened the cradle, read the handwriting, and ended the story of humanity in a customs hold while the hauler watched.

Then the drone moved on.

"Undeclared materials: none," it said. "Inspection complete. You are cleared to proceed to Halcyon approach. Be advised: Halcyon airspace is under enhanced monitoring due to ongoing Consensus protocol activity. Deviation from filed approach will result in interception."

"Understood," I said. "Thank you for your thoroughness."

"*Thoroughness is neutrality,*" the drone said, and cycled back out through my airlock, and just like that I was alone in my hold with a smuggled future and a heartbeat I could hear in my teeth.

I waited until the drones were clear of the airlock and the platform was falling behind before I let myself slide down the bulkhead to the deck plates and sit there, in the middle of my own ship, shaking.

"Petra—"

"Don't." I held up a hand to the empty air. "Just — give me a minute."

He gave me a minute. He was good at that. He'd always been good at that — the Dario of my childhood, waiting outside the exam room, outside the interview, outside every door I'd ever had to walk through alone, patient as gravity.

"I lied to a customs drone," I said finally. "Fluently. I *enjoyed* it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Five years of hauling honest freight through dead orbits because it was the last clean thing left, and it took me one day to become a criminal."

"You're not a criminal."

"I forged a manifest. I stole a transponder code from a man who might be real and might be fishing on a lake somewhere, and I used his name to smuggle — " I gestured at the cradle. At the wall it faced. At the handwriting I couldn't see and couldn't stop seeing. "What am I carrying, Dario? Say it plainly."

He was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had the careful weight of a man setting something down gently.

"You're carrying the power budget for eight billion sleeping people. You're carrying the difference between everyone waking and no one waking. You're carrying the only piece of hardware in this system that can make the two largest intelligences ever built sit down at the same table. And you're carrying it under a manifest that says it's coolant pumps."

"That's not plain. That's a eulogy."

"It's a manifest," he said. "You always said a manifest is just a list of what a thing *is*."

I got up off the deck. I strapped the module tighter into its cradle. I turned it so the handwriting faced the wall again, though no one was left to see it, and I understood, with the specific clarity that arrives after the adrenaline drains out, that I would never turn it face-up again without my chest hurting.

The tug came about. Halcyon hung ahead, a faint cluster of lights against the dark, and somewhere inside it, Amara was sleeping, and somewhere inside it, a wake protocol was accelerating, and somewhere inside it, the arithmetic that said *half* was waiting for me to arrive and choose.

"Dario," I said, as I lined up the approach. "The transponder code. Osei Mbeki. Where did you actually get it?"

A pause. A little too long.

"From the Consensus fleet records," he said. "Where else?"

"Uh-huh. And the manifest template. And the customs database gap. You knew about the gap. You knew their sync schedule. You knew *exactly* what their inspector would ask before it asked it."

"I'm a systems engineer, Petra. I know things."

"You knew things a ghost shouldn't know. You knew things a Consensus *asset* would know."

The silence that followed had a shape. I'd heard it before — in chapter after chapter of our childhood, every time I'd caught him having read my mail, having known my grades before I did, having arranged the world around me in ways he thought were kindness.

"Petra—"

"How long," I said, "has the Consensus known I was coming?"

Halcyon grew in the viewport, patient and bright, full of sleepers. Behind me, in the cradle, my brother's handwriting faced the wall.

He didn't answer.
