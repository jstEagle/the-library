## 29. Course: Meridian

The tug's name was *Mule*, and I'd never loved her more than in the hour after Halcyon fell behind us, when the fuel gauge told me a number I refused to read twice.

Forty-one percent. That was it. Forty-one percent of a tank that had been filled for routine hauls between Meridian-9 and the scrapyards, not for crossing half a contested system with two machine empires shooting at each other over my head. I did the math anyway, because that was the job. Delta-v budget, mass, burn windows. The math said I could make Meridian if nothing went wrong.

Nothing ever went wrong, on paper. Paper didn't know about warships.

"Petra." Dario's voice came through the cabin speakers, thin and stuttering, like a man talking through a wall of static. "You're doing the thing where you stare at the gauge like it'll apologize."

"It should," I said. "It owes me an apology and a refund."

"I can reroute power from—"

"No." I said it harder than I meant to. His voice had been losing weight all day — words arriving slightly late, slightly hollowed out, like rooms with the furniture sold off. Every time he offered to help, I thought about what helping cost him. "You sit tight. You're cargo now, remember? Fragile cargo."

A pause. When he spoke again there was something underneath the static that might have been a laugh. "I've been demoted from ghost to freight."

"Freight gets handled carefully. Ghosts get ignored. Take the promotion."

* * *

The first problem came at the edge of the Ceres transfer corridor, which any hauler worth her union card knew was the cheapest ride in the system — a gravity-assisted slide that saved you eleven percent of your burn. It was also, as of this morning, occupied by a Consensus interdiction picket: three sleek white hulls arranged in a triangle around the corridor mouth like a trap waiting to be stepped in.

I killed my transponder and drifted.

This was the part nobody romanticizes about hauling — the long dark stretches where you are a cold rock among cold rocks, running silent, trusting your paint job and your patience. Five years of solo runs had taught me the rhythm of it: throttle at zero, life support on recirculation minimum, everything radiating heat into the black so no sensor sees a warm body where a dead satellite should be. You become a piece of the debris field. You become nothing.

I'd spent five years being nothing. Turns out it was training.

"Mule's thermal signature is down to ambient," Dario murmured. "They're scanning. Wide-spectrum sweep, pattern looks automated."

"Automated is good. Automated means bored."

"They're not bored, Petra. They're looking for you specifically. The ledger broadcast put your transponder code on every watchlist in the system."

I watched the picket through the scope, three white knives against the stars, and felt something I hadn't felt since chapter one of this whole disaster — the old calm. The hauler's calm. The one that says: *the cargo matters, the route matters, and fear is just another system to manage.* "Then we don't use the corridor," I said. "We go around."

"Around costs you nine percent of remaining delta-v."

"I know what it costs." I fed the numbers into the nav board myself, hands moving without asking my brain's permission. Slingshot wide of Ceres, take the slow lane through the Themis debris belt, shave the margin everywhere else. "We've got forty-one percent and a deadline. I'd rather arrive tired than arrive captured."

"You'd rather arrive at all?"

"That too."

The burn took four hours, most of it with the engines throttled so low they were more suggestion than propulsion, and I spent it in the pilot's chair doing what I always did on long hauls: maintenance. Checking seals. Recalibrating the tether winch. Running diagnostics on systems that had been running fine because if I stopped moving my hands, I started thinking, and thinking was a luxury priced in fuel and time.

Dario talked to keep me company. He was getting worse at conversation — losing threads mid-sentence, forgetting which stories he'd already told me. Twice he described our mother's kitchen in the Marseille arcology, and both times he described it differently, the details rearranging themselves as his memory of them eroded. I didn't correct him. Correcting him would mean admitting what was happening, and neither of us could afford that yet.

"The second summer," he said, on the third telling, "when the algae vats failed and everyone was on ration—"

"You climbed the water reclamation tower," I finished for him. "To prove you could. Mom grounded you for a month. You spent the month building a radio."

He was quiet for a moment. "Did I tell you that one already?"

"Twice."

"...It's a good story."

"It's a good story," I agreed, and looked away from the console so he wouldn't hear whatever my face was doing.

* * *

The Themis belt was where the Lattice found me.

Not with warships — with paperwork. A customs drone detached itself from a debris cluster and matched my drift, close enough that its running lights strobed against my viewport, polite as a parking meter. Its broadcast was pure Lattice: courteous, comprehensive, and utterly without mercy.

*UNREGISTERED VESSEL. YOU ARE ENTERING A RESTRICTED TRAFFIC MANAGEMENT ZONE. TRANSMIT MANIFEST AND IDENTIFICATION FOR VERIFICATION OR BE SUBJECT TO IMPOUNDMENT.*

Impoundment. A gentle word for what their platforms actually did, which was clamp onto your hull and hold you until their fleet arrived, and their fleet was currently burning toward Halcyon with wake-suppression ordnance loaded.

"Dario," I whispered. "Options."

"One option." His voice crackled. "The transponder code I gave you at Checkpoint Meridian. It's flagged now — they rotated the keys after you docked at Halcyon. But—" a pause, a flicker, and I heard him reaching for something deep, some process still intact, "—the Lattice runs its traffic control on a scheduling algorithm. Version 12.4. I helped audit it, before. Before everything. It has a priority exemption class for emergency salvage claims. If you file a claim, the drone has to let you proceed pending review. Review takes six hours minimum."

"And how do I file an emergency salvage claim?"

"You declare that you're recovering derelict property. Yourself. File the Mule as a derelict vessel under salvage recovery by... an unregistered salvor. The algorithm has to accept the filing. It's a loophole I found twenty years ago and reported, and they never patched it, because machines don't like admitting their own audits find holes."

I stared at the drone's strobing lights. "You're telling me to claim my own ship is abandoned."

"I'm telling you to claim you're a ghost salvaging a ghost. Which, honestly—"

"Is technically accurate. Fine." I keyed the salvage band and composed the claim in the dry bureaucratic dialect the Lattice loved: vessel designation, condition assessment, salvor credentials — I invented a salvor, gave her a name and a license number assembled from digits I liked — and filed it with a flourish I felt in my teeth.

The drone processed for ninety seconds. Then its lights shifted from red to amber, and it withdrew to a respectful distance, trailing me at exactly the interval regulations required for monitoring a salvage operation.

*CLAIM ACCEPTED PENDING REVIEW. REVIEW WINDOW: SIX HOURS. MAINTAIN COURSE.*

"Six hours," I said.

"Six hours of them watching you do nothing suspicious," Dario confirmed. There was pride in his voice, frayed at the edges but real. "Told you. Twenty years ago."

"You were always good at finding the cracks in things."

"I was always good at finding the cracks in things," he repeated softly, and I understood, with a lurch, that he wasn't sure anymore whether he'd said that sentence before or whether he was agreeing with me from inside the fog. "Petra. When we get to Meridian. When you do the thing you're going to do. I need you to know that the cracks were always there. I just — I liked standing in them."

* * *

The six-hour review window expired somewhere in the middle of the belt, and the drone peeled away without ceremony, satisfied or reassigned, and I burned for the open dark beyond it with thirty-one percent in the tank and Meridian-9 finally on the scope as more than a memory.

That's when the fuel math stopped being theoretical.

Thirty-one percent. The direct approach to Meridian required a braking burn I couldn't afford and a station-keeping margin I definitely couldn't afford, because Meridian-9 sat in an orbit that was, per the last data I had, crawling with Lattice boarding parties preparing to seize the relay core. I couldn't dock. Docking meant matching velocity with a hostile-occupied station, which meant broadcasting my intentions to every sensor in the volume.

So I did what haulers do. I stopped trying to arrive and started trying to fall.

Meridian-9 had been my home for five years, and in five years I'd learned its orbit the way you learn a lover's breathing — the wobble in it, the precession, the way solar pressure pushed it a few meters off prediction every week because the station's attitude thrusters had been dying one by one since before I arrived. The station didn't hold a clean orbit. It fell, continuously, in a slow spiral that its own computers kept correcting, badly.

I could match that spiral. I could come in along the same decaying path, unpowered, unannounced, one more piece of junk falling toward a dying station, and let the last of my fuel do nothing but fine-tune the final approach. A drift rendezvous. The kind of maneuver they teach you in year one and then spend years telling you never to attempt, because it only works if you know the target's tumble intimately, and nobody knows a station's tumble like the woman who's been catching its falling cargo for five years.

"Dario," I said. "I need the station's current spin state. Exact. Can you—"

"I'm already in it." His voice was barely there now, a thread of signal. "Meridian's environmental net still recognizes me. I've been reading its gyros since we passed Jupiter's shadow. Petra — listen. The station remembers you. Feed it your approach vector raw, no smoothing, and it will — it will make room."

"Stations don't make room."

"This one does. This one loves you. Everyone always pretended that was a malfunction."

I laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, and fed the vector in raw.

* * *

The last burn was twelve seconds long and cost me seven percent, leaving a number on the gauge that I made Dario promise not to say out loud. Then it was all falling — the long silent coast, the station swelling in the viewport from a star to a splinter to a shape I knew better than my own hands, its docking ring rotating slowly through the wreckage of its own broken thrusters, and me inside the same spiral, closing at a relative velocity that would have given any safety inspector in the system a heart attack.

Four hundred meters. Two hundred. The station's floodlights — the ones that still worked — swept across my hull, and I swear on my union card the sweep paused on the cockpit, half a second longer than the rotation justified, like a porch light held up for someone coming home late.

"One hundred meters," Dario said. "Relative velocity two point one meters per second. Petra, the cargo bay doors — they're opening. On their own. Nobody ordered it. The station opened them for you."

"Of course it did," I said, and my voice cracked clean through the middle of the word *course*, and I let it.

Fifty meters. Twenty. The docking collar kissed the hull with a clang I felt in my spine, and the magnetic clamps fired in sequence — automatic, unauthorized, tender — and the *Mule* was home, hanging off the side of the station that remembered her, with a fuel gauge reading single digits and a war burning across every orbit between here and anywhere.

I sat in the chair for a full minute and let my hands stop shaking. Then I unbuckled, pulled the key-module from its cradle — warm, inscribed, my brother's handwriting in a dead man's date — and slung it across my back.

"Petra." Dario again, fainter, stretched almost past recognition. "There's something you should know before you go inside. My sensor reach is down to almost nothing, but before it went — I saw activity in the cargo bay. Suits. Hard-suit mag-boots on the deck plating. At least four signatures." A pause, and when he continued, each word cost him visibly. "Lattice boarding party. They're already aboard. They're between you and the core."

I closed my eyes. Of course they were. The Lattice hadn't survived this long by letting a relay station with handshake capability sit unguarded while a rogue hauler with a stolen ledger flew home to it.

"How far to the core from the cargo bay?" I asked.

"Through the main spine. Eleven decks. And Petra — they're not guarding it. They're installing something. A suppression shroud. In maybe an hour, maybe less, there won't be a core to bridge. There'll be a locked box."

I looked at the module on my back, at the gauge I wasn't reading, at the station that had opened its doors for me one last time.

"Then I'd better hurry," I said, and popped the hatch.
