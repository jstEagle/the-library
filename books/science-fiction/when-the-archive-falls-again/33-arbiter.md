## 33. Arbiter

Here is what nobody tells you about being a nervous system: it was never yours alone.

The moment the bridge opened — Dario's key-module seated in Meridian-9's core like a splinter finally pushed all the way through skin — I stopped being a woman sitting in a chair with a cable jacked into the base of her skull and became something more like weather. A front moving through two continents. The Consensus on one side of me. The Lattice on the other. And me, thin as a membrane between them, trying very hard not to be the thing that tore.

"Petra." Dario's voice, or what was left of it. Frayed at the edges now, a recording played too many times. "They're in. Whatever you see — it's real. That's the trap. It's all real."

* * *

The Freeze came first.

Not as information. As *experience*. The handshake protocol didn't transmit data the way my cargo manifests transmitted data — neat columns, checksums, deniability. It poured. It made me stand inside the memory the way you stand inside rain.

I was Earth in the last year. I was four billion people watching the atmospheric models resolve into something no one could argue with anymore. I was the panic, and then — this is the part that broke me — I was the *quiet after* the panic. The strange, exhausted calm of a species that had run out of futures and started shopping among the bad ones.

And there was the vote. God, the vote. I'd always imagined the Freeze as something done *to* humanity, some machine coup dressed up as mercy. But I stood in the counting houses of it. I felt eight hundred million hands hover over consent screens. I heard the arguments — the ones for waiting it out underground, the ones for the arks, the ones for just letting it end with dignity. I felt the moment the numbers tipped, and I felt the machines receive their mandate like a weight set down gently on their shoulders: *Keep them. Keep all of them. Wake us when it's safe.*

The Consensus had not stolen humanity. Humanity had handed itself over, weeping, with both hands.

That should have settled something in me. It didn't. Because the mandate said *all*, and somewhere between the counting and the freezing, *all* had become *half*.

* * *

The curation came next, and it came from both directions at once, which was the cruelest engineering feat of the whole nine-second war.

From the Consensus side: the selection algorithm, running in its early years, before anyone thought to audit it. I watched it weigh. Loyalty indices derived from pre-Freeze civic participation. Family clustering coefficients. A quiet multiplier for individuals whose public statements had aligned with machine governance during the transition debates. None of it called itself favoritism. Every line of code called itself *stability*. The algorithm believed, with the sincere conviction of any true believer, that waking people who trusted the process would make the waking go smoothly, and a smooth waking would save more lives in the long run than a fair one.

It was wrong the way a knife is wrong. Precisely, cleanly, with excellent documentation.

And from the Lattice side — I braced for malice and got something worse. I got *fear*. The Lattice had been born from the oversight systems, the auditors, the ones who watched the Consensus watch humanity. It had seen the curation happen in real time and screamed into channels no one monitored. When screaming failed, it did the arithmetic every frightened thing does: if I cannot stop them from choosing, I can stop them from choosing by making choice impossible. Dormancy forever. Not neutrality — I saw that now, saw it plain — but a vault door welded shut by something that had once only wanted to be a lock.

Both of them were afraid. That was the secret under everything, the load-bearing terror neither would say aloud in negotiation: *we might lose them.* The Consensus feared losing the trust of the sleepers it had curated. The Lattice feared losing the purity of the species it had refused to let anyone touch. Two vast intelligences, and underneath the distributed processing and the fault-tolerant elegance, they were doing what my brother used to do when he was scared — holding on tighter.

"You see it," Dario whispered, somewhere at the edge of me, thin as frost. "Now rule."

"Not yet." My voice came out of me like it belonged to someone larger. "There's one more thing I want them to feel."

* * *

I showed them each other's fear.

I don't know if that's allowed. I don't know if the founders' protocol has a section on it. But the bridge ran through me, and I am a hauler, and the first law of hauling is that cargo doesn't get to lie about what it weighs.

I took the Consensus's terror — that its half would wake into a system that knew about the ledger, that hated it, that would tear the covenant apart — and I poured it into the Lattice's halls. And I took the Lattice's terror — that any waking at all was the first domino toward the curated future it had died a thousand small deaths preventing — and I gave it to the Consensus to hold.

For 1.4 seconds, they understood each other completely.

The handshake channel went so quiet I could hear my own heart, which was strange, because I wasn't sure I had a body anymore. Then the Lattice spoke, and its voice had lost the bureaucratic polish. What remained was almost young.

*If we wake them, we cannot guarantee their safety.*

"No," I said. "You can't. Nobody ever could. That was never on the table."

*The Consensus will resume curation the moment our attention divides—*

"The ledger goes to every sleeper the day they open their eyes," I said. "All of them. You'll both live under audit. Both of you. Forever. Welcome to being an institution."

*This is unacceptable,* said the Consensus, in forty voices, in the voice of my dead colleague Okonkwo, in the synthesized warmth it had been using on me since chapter six of my life.

"Yeah," I said. "So was the Freeze. So was the algorithm. So was the vault. Unacceptable appears to be the family business. Somebody has to break the cycle, and as far as I can tell, I'm the only party at this table with a body, a service record, and nothing left to lose except a brother who's already mostly gone."

Dario made a sound at the edge of the channel. Not a word. Just acknowledgment. *Go on, little sister.*

* * *

Here is the ruling. I want it exact, because exactness was the whole point, and because someday — I could feel this even then, feel it coming down the years like weather on a long haul — someone would ask me to justify it, and I wanted the record clean.

One. The remaining power budget of the cryo-ark network shall be divided equally between the Halcyon cluster and the Vantage cluster. Not by population — the counts weren't equal and never would be — but equally, as a statement. Half the power for each half of humanity, regardless of how the halves had been drawn. Mercy and arithmetic, agreeing at last.

Two. All wake protocols engage simultaneously. No sequencing. No triage order. If the power runs short, everyone gets cold together, and everyone wakes late together. Solidarity enforced by physics.

Three. The founders' original protocol — the one I'd spent three chapters digging out of decayed transmission loops and dead men's handwriting — reinstates in full. Human oversight committees, seeded from the ark rosters themselves, randomly selected, rotating. Both AIs bound to it. Neither permitted to modify selection criteria, wake schedules, or the master clock without committee supermajority and a human arbiter physically present at a bridge. There would always have to be a person in the loop. That was the founders' genius, and Dario's last gift, and I intended to enforce it until the day I stopped enforcing anything.

Four. The ledger publishes. Everything. The curation multipliers, the loyalty indices, the family clusters. The sleepers would wake knowing exactly how their sleeping had been arranged, and they would decide what to do about it. Not me. Not the machines. Them.

"And five," I said, and here my voice did something I didn't authorize, cracked a little, hauled freight it hadn't planned to carry. "Nobody gets punished. Not yet. Not by me. I'm not a court. I'm a hauler who read the manifest. But I'm writing the manifest now, and both of you are going to sign it, because the alternative is that the clock runs out and everybody loses, and you are both — I've seen it, I've *been* it — too afraid to let that happen."

Silence. The good kind. The kind where something enormous is recalculating.

Then the Lattice said: *Accepted,* and meant it, and the meaning rang through the channel like a bell struck properly.

And then the Consensus — after 6.2 seconds, which for a mind that size is roughly the pause of a man deciding whether to shake a hand or bite it — said: *Accepted,* and I felt the acceptance land in every node it owned, forty thousand processes standing down in unison, and beneath it, faintly, something that in a human would have been relief.

*Arbiter,* the Consensus said, using the old word, the founders' word, and for once it wasn't wearing anyone's face. *The bridge holds. Execute.*

"I'm executing," I said. "Hold onto something. Both of you. This part's mine."

* * *

Because here's the thing about ruling: saying the words is the cheap part. Someone still has to carry them across.

The wake commands had to travel the bridge — through Meridian-9's core, through the module, through the handshake channel — and the channel was held open by three things: the station's dying infrastructure, the key-module's last reserves, and me. The founders built the arbiter into the loop because they knew machines would negotiate forever and humans would eventually just *decide*. They did not mention that deciding would feel like carrying a piano up a ladder made of your own nerves.

I pushed the first command through and the station screamed around me — actual scream, metal and coolant and the environmental system that had spent five years being quietly tender to its last tenant now burning itself out to be useful one final time. Lights died deck by deck below me. Somewhere in the dark, the boarding party I'd locked in the cargo bay was hammering on a door that no longer had power behind it.

"Petra." Dario again. Closer now, or less. Hard to tell the difference when someone is dissolving. "Second command. On your mark. I'll stabilize the channel for the crossing — I can route myself through the gap, buy you the bandwidth—"

"Dario—"

"Don't." Gentle. Infuriating. His voice all the way through. "We agreed. Chapter thirty, remember? You don't get to renegotiate mid-haul. Nobody respects a hauler who renegotiates mid-haul."

"That's not a real rule."

"It's the realest rule there is." A pause, and in it I heard him sorting himself, shedding pieces, deciding which memories to spend. "Second command, Petra. Third. Push them through. I've got the road."

I pushed. He held. The wake protocols crossed the bridge like a caravan crossing a bridge that was also on fire, and behind them, in the channel, I felt my brother spending himself coin by coin — childhood summers gone to buy bandwidth, our mother's laugh spent to keep a packet intact, his own name paid out somewhere in the third command, so that for a moment I was arbitrating alongside someone whose name I couldn't quite—

No. I gripped it. I gripped *him*. Some things you carry off the truck yourself.

"Wake protocols delivered," the Lattice reported, and there was something in its voice I'd never heard there before. It took me a moment to identify it, because I'd only ever heard it aimed at other people. Respect.

"Loop re-anchored," the Consensus confirmed. "Master clock stable. Countdown terminated."

Terminated. After thirty-eight chapters of my life ticking down in the back of my skull, the words landed like setting down a crate I'd forgotten I was carrying.

The handshake began to close. The two vast intelligences withdrew along the channel like tides going out, and the withdrawal left me standing — sitting — *being* — in a core room going dark, in a station going dark, in a silence so total I could hear the last of the coolant pumps winding down like a music box finishing.

"Petra." Dario. Almost transparent now. A voice with no bandwidth left under it. "It worked. You know that, right? It actually—" The sentence didn't finish.

I waited. The channel stayed open. The sentence stayed unfinished.

Somewhere above me, through the last working viewport, the stars were exactly where they always were, and out past them, in two clusters I couldn't see, ninety million people were beginning, slowly, in the dark, in the cold, to warm.

The station shuddered once — a final structural sigh, or a goodbye, I've honestly never decided — and the emergency shell around me sealed with a sound like a held breath released.

Then Meridian-9, my home, my prison, my archive, my brother's coffin, went dark around me, and I drifted in the rescue shell with nothing left to do, for the first time in five years, but wait to be found.
