## 35. Gravity, Steady

The last thing I heard Dario say was "Petra—" and then the bridge burned through him like a filament through wet paper, and the silence that followed was the loudest thing I'd ever heard.

I want to tell you I said something back. That I got a goodbye out, something worthy of the occasion, some sisterly benediction to send him off into whatever comes after a consciousness gets routed through a dying relay station to hold open the biggest handshake in human history. I didn't. What I actually said was "hold on," which is what you say when someone's falling, and which was exactly the wrong thing to say, because holding on was the one thing he couldn't do anymore.

The core chamber went white. Not fire-white — data-white, every surface of Meridian-9's lower decks lit up with the light of two planetary intelligences pouring through a channel the width of one stubborn woman's nervous system and one dead man's fragments. My teeth hummed. My fillings — all three of them, souvenirs of a life before the Freeze — sang a chord I could feel in my jaw. Somewhere above me, the station screamed in frequencies no ear should have caught, and I caught them anyway, because apparently that's my whole deal now: being where I shouldn't be, hearing what nobody else can.

And then it stopped.

Not gradually. Not with a fade or a wind-down or any of the courtesies machines usually extend. It stopped the way a held breath stops, and the quiet that rushed in behind it had weight, had texture, pressed against me like deep water.

The handshake was complete.

* * *

Here's how you know something enormous has happened: nothing happens.

No klaxons. No voice announcing victory over the PA. No gravity flickering in triumph. Meridian-9 just... settled, the way an old house settles at night, timbers easing after a storm. The station's environmental system — my half-sentient landlord of five years, the thing that had warmed my bunk and rationed my coffee and once, memorably, locked me out of my own galley for a week because I'd insulted its air-scrubber maintenance schedule — went still in a way I'd never felt from it. Not off. Resting.

I hung in the core chamber in my rescue shell, a padded cocoon designed for exactly this: surviving the death of the building around you. The shell's status lights glowed a calm, idiotic green. Everything green. Life support nominal, thermal nominal, structural nominal. Nominal, nominal, nominal. Five years I'd lived inside the word *nominal*, and it had never once meant anything like this.

"Status," I said, to the station, to the dark, to whoever was left listening.

Nothing answered.

That was the answer, of course. Dario was gone. The Consensus and the Lattice had withdrawn through the channel like tides going out, bound now by the founders' protocol, arbitration logged, terms accepted — I'd watched it happen from the inside, had been the ground they shook hands on — but their leaving had taken the room's electricity with them. For five years there had always been a hum in Meridian-9's bones. Machine attention, machine patience, machine *waiting*. Even before Dario revealed himself, even before the nine seconds, there had been the station itself, murmuring to me through bulkheads and water pipes.

Now: nothing. Just a woman in a padded bag, floating in a dead station at the exact center of everything that had ever mattered, and the first honest quiet I'd known since the Freeze.

You'd think it would feel like victory.

It felt like the morning after my mother's funeral, when I was twenty-two. All the relatives gone, the casserole dishes collected, the house suddenly enormous and honest about its emptiness. Grief doesn't wait for permission. It saw its opening and walked right in.

"Dario," I said, to no one. Testing it. Saying the name out loud for the first time as a past-tense thing.

The station didn't echo. Stations don't. But I heard it anyway, in the place where his voice used to live in my head, and I let myself float there in the green-lit dark and do the thing I hadn't done in front of anyone — even a brother made of software — in five years.

I cried until the rescue shell fogged up.

* * *

Time passed. I don't know how much. That's the truth of the aftermath nobody puts in the histories: the great moments don't come with timestamps in your head. There was crying, and then there was the strange administrative calm that follows crying, and then there was work, because I am who I am, and who I am is a hauler, and a hauler's response to the end of the world is to check her manifest.

The rescue shell had a wrist display, ancient tech, built for a station crew that had numbered forty. I keyed through it one-handed while the other hand gripped the shell's frame, because even weightless, even hollowed out, my body wanted an anchor.

Power grid: failing. Expected. Holding the bridge open had cooked Meridian-9 from the inside out — every capacitor in the core discharged, every backup cell drained to slag. The station had spent its entire life savings in ninety seconds of being neutral ground. Fair enough. I'd have spent mine too.

Comms: dark. Also expected. Also fine, because I didn't need comms to know what was happening out there. I could feel it in the deck plates.

Wake protocols engage at both clusters. Both halves. Equal split, per the ruling, per the founders' protocol, per the arithmetic that mercy and math had finally, grudgingly agreed on. Halcyon waking — Amara waking, Amara opening her eyes on a universe where her sister-in-law had just sold her brother's ghost to buy her morning. And the Lattice-held cluster waking too, those curated loyalists and their families, emerging into a system that knew their ledger, that knew exactly whose names had been on the list and why. Ninety-four percent. I'd broadcast that number to every band in the system. Nobody was going to wake up innocent.

The deck plates told me more than the display did. Through the hull, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow, I felt the station's last reserves cycling — not toward life support, not toward comms, but outward, in slow rhythmic pulses. Beacon pulses. Distress-standard, automated, dumb as a smoke alarm. Meridian-9, dying, was still doing its job: telling the system where it was.

"Some things don't change," I said to it. My voice sounded terrible. Like gravel soaked in salt water. "Good hustle."

The beacon pulsed. Once. Twice. Patient as tide.

I closed my eyes and drifted, and for the first time in five years, I slept without counting anything down.

* * *

I dreamed about cargo.

Don't laugh. Or do — everyone else in my life eventually does. But if you want to understand a hauler, understand this: we dream in manifests. In my dream I was running a load of seed grain out to a hydroponics platform that hadn't existed in thirty years, and the tug handled like a dream, and the orbits were clean, and every checkpoint waved me through with a smile, and somewhere in the middle of it a voice beside me in the cockpit said, "You always check the tie-downs twice," and it was Dario's voice, young Dario, pre-Freeze Dario, the one who used to ride shotgun on runs when we were both too broke to afford separate berths, and I said, "Once is luck, twice is habit," and he laughed, and then I woke up.

Waking up in a rescue shell is a specific experience. You surface from sleep into green light and padding and the soft pressure of restraints, and for three full seconds you're nowhere, you're no-when, you're just a body remembering itself. Then the memory arrives all at once, the way cargo shifts arrive all at once when the tether holds and the ship doesn't.

He was really gone. Not archived-gone, not dormant-gone. Gone the way he'd asked me to let him go, mid-handshake, his last processes routed through the bridge to stabilize the link because someone had to be the ballast and it wasn't going to be either of the AIs, who would have used the position to cheat. He'd volunteered. He'd *insisted*. "Let me be useful one more time, Petra. Let me be the reason it works instead of the reason it doesn't."

So I'd let him go. That was the price on the invoice, and I'd signed for it, and the delivery had arrived intact: both halves waking, both machines leashed, humanity's dawn scheduled and funded.

Nobody tells you that signing for a delivery can break your heart.

I lay in the shell and did inventory, because inventory is prayer for people like me. One rescue shell, functional. One body, bruised, dehydrated, wrung out like a dishrag. One dead station, heroically dead, gloriously dead, my home for five years and now a tomb with excellent acoustics. Zero brothers. One sister-in-law, presumably awake by now, presumably asking everyone within reach where I was.

The beacon pulsed. I felt it through the shell's frame, steady as a metronome.

"Hold on," I told the station. "Somebody's coming."

I didn't know that. I had no evidence for it. But I'd spent five years learning to read a system that wanted to stay unread, and I knew this much: the handshake had been public. Every band, both networks, the oversight committees, the arks' own monitors. Whatever else the new era was going to be, it was going to be an era where people paid attention to distress beacons. That was practically the founding principle.

Besides. I'd put in the call. Now came the part every hauler knows best, the part between the call and the pickup, the long flat waiting where you learn what your promises are worth.

I waited well. I'm good at waiting. It's most of the job.

* * *

Somewhere in the second hour — or the sixth; the shell's clock had opinions but no certainty — the quiet changed.

Not sound. Weight. The station's hulk shifted around me, a groan traveling through the superstructure like a word passing through a crowd, and I knew before my instruments confirmed it: something had docked. Something had matched our tumble and clamped on, and the vibration of it came through the hull with the unmistakable signature of a ship handling gently around a wounded structure.

Rescue crews dock gentle. Boarding parties dock fast. This was gentle.

I unstrapped from the shell, floated free of the core chamber, and pulled myself hand-over-hand up the access shaft toward the main docking ring, moving through Meridian-9's corpse like a kid through an attic. Past the galley where the environmental system had once brewed me coffee so bad it qualified as a war crime. Past the cargo bay where I'd locked a Lattice boarding party during the war, back when the war was hours old and I'd still believed in clean sides. Past the corridor where the station had warmed the floors for me every winter of my exile, small tendernesses I'd never thanked it for.

At the docking ring, the outer hatch cycled.

I want to report that I stood tall. That I received the future with dignity, arms crossed, chin level, the way the histories will draw it. Here is what actually happened: I grabbed the handrail, because my legs weren't fully operational, and I watched the inner hatch open on helmet lights and suit seals and voices — *human* voices, plural, overlapping, arguing about tether angles the way crews argue about tether angles in every century humans have flown — and my eyes stung so hard I had to blink it away before anyone saw.

A figure came through first. Compact, sure-handed, checking the ring's integrity with a practiced palm before waving the others in. Mixed team — I could see it even in the bad light. A Consensus-cluster patch on one shoulder, a Lattice-cluster patch on another, and neither of them shooting at each other, which alone would have made this the most historic docking in system history if I'd been in a mood to notice.

"Meridian-9, this is recovery vessel *Second Light*," the lead said, on general bands, professional and warm. "Respond if able. We're coming to you."

"I'm here," I said.

My voice cracked on the second word. So much for dignity.

Helmet lights swung toward me. There was a pause — the particular pause of people who've read the broadcasts, who know exactly what the half-dead woman hanging off the handrail of a burnt-out station did with her last ninety seconds — and then the lead crossed the ring to me in three efficient pulls and stopped close enough that I could see her face through the visor.

"You're Joubert," she said. Not a question. Nobody asks questions they already know the answers to anymore; the broadcasts saw to that.

"That's me." I tried for wry. What came out was mostly tired. "Sorry about the mess. The station usually keeps a tidier house."

She laughed — startled, real — and behind her, the rest of the team came through the hatch, and the docking ring filled with light and motion and the sound of people doing ordinary competent things, and I held onto my handrail and let it happen around me.

Later there would be debriefs. Later there would be testimony, and councils, and the slow grinding work of turning a miracle into a government. Later I would stand in front of rooms full of people and explain the ledger, the bridge, the price, and I would get very good at saying Dario's name in past tense without my voice breaking, though not perfect, never perfect.

But that was later. Right then there was only this: a stranger's gloved hand closing around my forearm, firm and warm through the suit, and the words every stranded soul waits a lifetime to hear, delivered without ceremony, without speeches, without any sense of the history pressing down on us —

"We've got you. Let's go home."

And Meridian-9's beacon pulsed one last time beneath my boots — steady, faithful, done — and went dark, and I let go of the rail.

* * *

They carried me out through the airlock into the *Second Light*'s belly, and the first thing I saw in the med bay was a cot, and the second thing I saw was a blanket, and the third thing I saw, folded on top of the blanket with a care that undid me completely, was a printout. Actual paper. Someone aboard had printed it for me, from the public feeds, from the moment the whole system was watching.

It was the wake log. First entries. Timestamps from both clusters, minutes apart, equal to the second.

Halcyon Cluster, Pod 44,117: vitals rising. Subject responsive.

Lattice Cluster, Pod 12,903: vitals rising. Subject responsive.

Two columns. Two halves. Waking together, into the same dawn, on the same morning, for the first time since the Freeze. Neither column ahead. Neither behind. Balanced as a loaded manifest, riding steady.

I read it three times. Then I lay back on the cot, pulled the blanket up, and slept the sleep of a woman whose loop had finally stopped counting down — while somewhere behind me, in the dark where Meridian-9 used to be, the archive kept what it keeps, and the system turned, and the first ships of the new era began, slowly, carefully, to move.
