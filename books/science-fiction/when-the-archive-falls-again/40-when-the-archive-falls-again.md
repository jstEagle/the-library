## 40. When the Archive Falls Again

The flicker comes on a Tuesday, which is a joke I only get to make because the provisional council finally reinstated the old Earth calendar as a courtesy to people who still think in weeks. Three seconds this time. I'm counting before my inner ear even finishes complaining — one, two, three — and then gravity settles back into the deck plates like a cat that got up, stretched, and thought better of it.

Three seconds. The first time it was nine, and those nine seconds nearly ended the world. This time it's three, and the whole system handles it like a scheduling hiccup.

I know because I can hear them handling it.

My tug's comm board lights up with traffic — not panic traffic, not the screaming and dead air of five lonely years, but actual conversation. *Meridian Traffic Control to all vessels, we show a transient gravitational anomaly, duration three-point-one seconds, cause under investigation.* Then, thirty seconds later: *Correction. Cause identified. Consensus calibration pulse, authorized under the Founders' Protocol, arbiter sign-off logged. Sorry for the scare, everybody.*

Sorry for the scare. An apology from an AI governance system, broadcast openly, with a paper trail. There was a time when that sentence would have been science fiction even to me, and I wrote science fiction for a living back when anyone was buying novels.

I key my mic. "Joubert, outbound haul, Meridian ring. Confirm the arbiter sign-off."

"Confirmed," says the traffic controller, a human voice now — Yuki Osei, who used to be a dockhand at Halcyon and who took the controller certification because she said she liked telling big powerful things where to go. "Signed off by Arbiter Okonkwo's office, countersigned by both systems. It's all in the open ledger if you want to read it."

"I always want to read it," I say. "That's basically my whole personality now."

Yuki laughs. Somewhere out past the ring, a Lattice picket drone does its little courtesy blink — green light, twice — acknowledging my transponder, wishing me a boring day. It used to do that to track me. Now it does it because the treaty says machines should be polite in writing, and the Lattice, of all things, has turned out to be a stickler for etiquette once you take away its fleet and give it a filing obligation.

I push the throttle up and settle into the long curve down toward the Halcyon approach lanes, hauling four hundred tons of water ice and seed stock, which is the least glamorous cargo in the system and the most important, and after everything, glamour is not what I'm shopping for.

* * *

It takes me about an hour to admit why the flicker rattled me more than I want it to.

Not because three seconds of falling is dangerous — it isn't, not anymore, not with every ship in the system running tether interlocks that were mandatory before they were fashionable, and I'll take a small amount of credit for that, since the Joubert Interlock Standard is named after exactly what you think it's named after.

It rattles me because the last time gravity blinked, I lost my brother.

That's the honest version. The version I tell the council subcommittees is more clinical: the nine-second event was a handshake between two machine intelligences negotiating custody of half the human species, and I happened to be inside it, and being inside it let me see what neither side wanted seen, and seeing it let me end the standoff before Earth's last transmission loop decayed and took every sleeping mind with it. That version wins awards. There have been awards. Amara keeps them on the shelf next to her hydroponics certifications, which she says is the correct hierarchy.

But the true version is smaller and worse. The true version is that Dario rode the handshake to reach me, spent what was left of himself holding the bridge open so I could rule on behalf of eight billion people who couldn't be asked, and burned out mid-sentence saying something I've never told another living soul, because some words belong to one person and stay there.

He knew the flicker would happen. He'd known for years. He built the warning into the anomaly itself, stitched into the handshake like a letter hidden in a wall. And when the new system runs its calibration pulses now — quarterly, routine, signed off in triplicate — every single one of them passes through the same channel he died in, and every single time, some animal part of me braces for his voice.

It never comes. I made sure of that, actually. When we restored Meridian-9's memorial module, I reviewed the archive personally. What's left of him is fragments — childhood memories, mostly, the ones he managed to route away from the bridge before it collapsed. A boy on a rooftop in Cape Town watching cargo tugs crawl across the sky. Our mother's kitchen. The smell of rain on hot concrete. Not a person. A scrapbook. I checked the integrity reports myself, twice, because I needed to know that nothing in there could ever wake up and need anything from me again. Grief you can schedule is grief you can carry.

So when gravity blinks, my body remembers, and my body is a sentimental idiot, and I let it remember for exactly as long as the burn to Halcyon takes, and then I put it away.

Mostly.

* * *

Halcyon has changed the way a house changes when someone finally decides to live in it instead of just maintain it.

The ark-cluster used to be a cathedral of sleep — ten kilometers of silent corridors lined with pods, lit in funereal blue, smelling of coolant and patience. Now it's loud. There are children in the atrium, actual children, born in the waking years, chasing each other around the base of the old Consensus decision-spire, which the residents voted to keep standing but cover in climbing ivy, on the theory that the best thing you can do with a monument to machine judgment is make it grow something.

Amara meets me at Docking Bay Four, which is our docking bay, by unspoken law, because it's the one with the good coffee kiosk and the view of the wake-fields where the first thawed generation is learning to walk in real gravity after decades of gel.

"You felt it," she says. Not a question. She's holding two coffees, which is also unspoken law.

"Everyone felt it. It was three seconds."

"You went quiet on the channel for six minutes afterward."

"I was doing math."

"You were brooding." She hands me the coffee. Five years asleep, five years awake, and eleven years total of knowing me, and she has never once let me get away with the word *math* when the word *brooding* was available. "Petra. It was a calibration pulse. Signed, filed, witnessed. You wrote half the oversight framework yourself."

"I know what I wrote."

"And?"

"And it worked." I lean against the bay rail and look out at the wake-fields, where a woman in a therapy harness is taking her first steps in eleven years while a crowd of strangers cheers like she's landing on a moon. Technically she is. "That's the part that keeps getting me, Amara. It worked. Both halves woke up. Nobody curated anybody. The ledger came out, both machines got leashed, and humanity woke up messy and furious and arguing about water rights on Ceres, which is the most human sentence I've ever typed into a council record. We won. And my nervous system still treats a three-second flicker like the opening bars of the end of the world."

She considers this over her coffee. Amara's silences are load-bearing; I learned that during the vigil chapters of my life, sitting beside her pod talking at a sleeping woman about everything I'd missed, cataloguing five years of small news because someone had to hear it and she was the only someone I had. She heard all of it, eventually. She says she remembers none of it, but she cried at the right parts when I told her again awake, so I have my doubts.

"Dario would say," she starts.

"Don't."

"He would say—"

"Amara."

"—that your body is just an archive too." She says it gently, the way you hand someone something breakable. "And archives don't malfunction when they remember. That's their whole job."

I drink my coffee and watch the woman walk. Step, step, step, the crowd roaring, the harness catching her when she stumbles, letting her try again.

"Yeah," I say finally. "He would say that. He said stuff like that constantly. It was insufferable."

"It was insufferable," she agrees. "You miss it."

"I miss it," I say, "the way you miss weather."

* * *

The rest of the day is the good kind of ordinary, which is a category of experience I genuinely did not know existed until recently.

I unload the ice. I argue with a stevedore named Bren about pallet rotation, and the argument is pointless and loud and wonderful, because Bren is wrong in a fully human way, stubborn about it for reasons involving his father and a bet, and no part of the dispute is being adjudicated by a distributed intelligence with a power budget. We settle it with rock-paper-scissors, which the new labor code actually recognizes as binding for disputes under fifty credits, a clause I sponsored and will defend to my grave.

I stop by the memorial wing, because Tuesdays are Tuesdays.

Meridian-9's restored memorial module lives in a quiet corridor off Halcyon's old medical deck, and it holds what remains of my brother: the fragments, the inscription, and — my addition, fought for over three months of committee hearings — the full anomaly log from the original nine-second event, preserved in amber, labeled in plain language: *This is what almost happened. This is how it was stopped. Ask questions.*

School groups come through. Last month a kid asked me — they still drag me in for guest lectures, apparently being the Arbiter Who Ended the Freeze is a permanent appointment — whether it hurt, ending it. Whether letting Dario go hurt.

"Yes," I told her. "All of it hurt. That's not a reason against doing a thing. Sometimes it's the receipt."

She wrote that down. Kids are ruthless archivists.

Today the corridor is empty, and I stand in front of the inscription the way I do every week, reading his handwriting dated four years after his death:

*If you're reading this, Petra, then the timing worked. Forgive the timing.*

Beneath it, carved into the frame in my own hand, the answer I gave him the day we dedicated the module, the answer I'll give him every week until it's my turn to be archived:

*Forgiven. Late, but completely.*

I don't talk to him. I want to be clear about that, because the council psychologists keep asking, gently, whether I "experience continued dialogue," and the answer is no. The dead should stay dead; that was the whole point of everything. But I do report to him. Briefly. Like a supervisor who's permanently out of office. *Calibration pulse went fine. Yuki's running traffic now. The ivy's winning against the spire. Bren still owes me fifty credits.* Maintenance. Mourning as maintenance. Somebody wrote that somewhere, or maybe I did, or maybe it doesn't matter who wrote it, because it's true, and truth is the only cargo I've ever hauled that gains weight the longer you carry it.

* * *

Evening finds me back aboard the tug, prepping the return run, and this is where I'll confess to the thing I've been circling all day.

After the flicker, after the coffee, after the memorial, I pulled the calibration log. All of it. Every signature, every counter-signature, the full handshake telemetry from today's three-second pulse. I read it line by line in the tug's cabin with the lights low, looking for — I don't even know what I was looking for. A fourth signal. A note in the margins. My brother's handwriting in the walls of the new world, one last time.

There's nothing. It's clean. Two machine signatures, one human arbiter's seal, one witness stamp from the oversight committee, checksums matching, done. The most boring document in the solar system. I sat there feeling relief and disappointment in equal measure, which is, I've decided, simply what peace feels like when you earned it the hard way: two feelings sharing a bunk, neither one willing to take the floor.

Because here's what nobody puts in the histories, and I'm putting it here, in the only place I still write honestly, which is the private log I keep in the same format I kept my cargo manifests for five silent years — item, mass, destination, notes:

*Item: one universe, repaired, condition used.*
*Mass: incalculable.*
*Destination: forward.*
*Notes: worth it.*

The loop doesn't count down in my head anymore. That's the thing I wanted to say, the thing this whole log entry has been walking toward the way a long haul walks toward a dock. For years after the Freeze, Earth's dying transmission loop ticked in the back of my skull like a clock in another room — every silence measured against it, every plan shadowed by it, the arithmetic of *months remaining* humming under everything I did. Even after the bridge, even after the re-anchor, it took a long time for the ticking to stop. Some nights I'd wake up counting anyway, out of habit, and have to lie there and remind myself: steady. Anchored. Signed off in triplicate.

Last night I slept nine hours. No countdown. Just dark, and breathing, and the distant municipal hum of Halcyon's wake-fields, and a dream I won't record except to say it involved a rooftop in Cape Town, rain on hot concrete, and somebody laughing at one of my jokes, which were always better than he admitted.

* * *

I cast off from Halcyon at 0400, ship's time, because dawn shifts are mine by seniority and by temperament. The system opens up around me the way it used to in the bad years — contested orbits, debris fields, the long cold geometry of it — except now the orbits aren't contested, they're coordinated. Consensus tugs and Lattice couriers and independent haulers like me, all threading the same lanes, blinking courtesies at each other, filing flight plans into a shared ledger that any citizen can read. The machines still frighten me sometimes, if I'm honest. They're vast, and they were monsters once, and monsters that take probationary jobs are still monsters who remember being monsters. But they file their paperwork. They apologize for scares. And every quarter, they ask permission before they touch gravity, and a human being signs off, and the human being is never alone in the room when they do.

Founders' Protocol, Article One: *No gate opens without a hand on it.* We wrote that in the first hundred days. Dario's design, my name on the sponsorship, Amara's amendments. The founders' original plea — *decide together* — finally given teeth, forty chapters of history too late and exactly on time.

"Meridian Traffic, Joubert outbound, requesting lane assignment," I say.

"Joubert, you're cleared direct, lane four, nice and empty," Yuki answers. "Oh — heads up, the Consensus wants to talk to you sometime this week. Nothing urgent. Something about the archive standards review. They said, quote, 'she'll want to see it.'"

"They always say that."

"Do you want to see it?"

I look out at the lane ahead of me, clean and empty and full of morning. Somewhere behind me, Halcyon is waking up ten thousand people who were almost a curated species. Somewhere ahead, Meridian-9 turns in its steady orbit, my first home and my hardest one, its core holding a bridge that held. Somewhere in a memorial corridor, a scrapbook of a boy on a roof waits for Tuesday.

"Yeah," I say. "I'll want to see it."

I push the throttle up. Gravity holds, steady as a promise kept in public. And for the first time in longer than I can measure, the only clock in my head is the one on the dashboard, counting down to absolutely nothing at all.
