## 1. Contraband of the Mind

The trick to smuggling memories out of Meridian Station, I've learned, is to stop thinking of them as stolen goods and start thinking of them as passengers. They don't weigh anything. They don't rattle. They just sit there in the crystal lattice of a data-shard, humming with somebody's whole life, waiting for you to get them past the door.

The door tonight was Customs Gate Four, and the man behind the scanner was a kid named Okonkwo who'd been on the job eight months and still checked my paperwork like it might bite him.

"Villalobos, Bram. Freight handler, third shift." He didn't look up. "Purpose of transit?"

"Delivering replacement coolant filters to the agri-ring." True, technically. The filters were real, sitting in their crates like honest men at a hanging.

He waved me onto the scan pad. The ring of the gate hummed, and I stood there with my arms slightly away from my body, the way you do, feeling the scanner read me the way a customs officer reads a face — looking for the thing you're hiding by not thinking about it.

Here's what he wasn't supposed to find: forty-one memory-records sealed in a shielded case bolted under the floor panel of my freight cart. Forty-one lives. A schoolteacher from Ring C who'd filmed the water rationing riots before the Concord scrubbed them from every public archive. A dockworker who kept a list of the two hundred colonists who died in the decompression "accident" that was not an accident. A grandmother who remembered what Meridian looked like before the war made it a word nobody said — *abandoned* — as if thirty thousand people were a rounding error.

Officially, Meridian Station was empty. Officially, the war between Earth's Concord and the Outer Reach had passed us by. Officially, the station had been evacuated for its own protection, its people relocated to "resettlement zones," which is a bureaucratic way of saying somewhere else, anywhere else, don't write to us.

I lived here. So did maybe four thousand others who'd refused the relocation order or been deemed too unimportant to actually move. The Concord declared us abandoned the way you'd declare a room abandoned after locking the door from the outside. No supply runs except the ones they controlled. No communications traffic out except through Concord relays, where everything got read and most things got lost. And no records — that was the part that ate at people. Every archive on the station had been "consolidated" six years ago, which meant loaded onto a transport that left and never came back.

You can starve a station slowly and call it logistics. But when you take its memory, you're telling it that it never happened. That's the thing I couldn't forgive, and the reason I do what I do.

Because here's my confession, since we're being intimate: I'm not a hero. I'm a freight handler with a bad knee and a talent for paperwork who figured out, about four years ago, that the same shielding crates used to move medical isotopes past customs scanners work just as well for moving memory-records. People started coming to me the way people come to a well. The old woman with her photographs converted to neural captures. The miner whose brother vanished into a Concord labor battalion. They'd press a shard into my hand and look at me with an expression I've never gotten used to — hope, but the tired kind, the kind that's already braced for disappointment.

They wanted their lives sent downwell, out of the war zone, to relatives, to anyone. Proof they existed. Proof Meridian existed.

So I carried what others wanted erased. That was the whole code. It fit on a coin, if coins meant anything anymore.

"Scan's clean," Okonkwo said, and then, because the universe hates a smooth night, his console chirped. He frowned at it. "Secondary sweep flagged an anomaly. Hold position."

I held position. My heart did not hold position; it went somewhere near my tonsils and stayed there.

"Anomaly," I said, keeping it bored. "On a cart full of coolant filters."

"The flag's on you, not the cart." He turned his screen toward himself, typed something, frowned harder. "Residual neural activity. You've got an implant?"

"Old injury. Station medics put a regulator in after a cargo arm clipped me." Also true. I have the scar and the limp to sell it. What I didn't have was an explanation for why the implant currently contained a memory-record I hadn't finished transferring yet — the one I'd taken in an hour ago, the one that changed everything.

Her name was Amara Diallo, and she was dying.

Not dying like the station was dying, slow and deniable. Dying like a woman whose lungs were filling with fluid the clinic's dwindling medicine cabinet couldn't touch. She'd come to my berth that evening leaning on her son's arm, gray as ash, and she'd said, "Bram. I need you to carry something, and I need you to carry it inside yourself."

The record she handed over wasn't testimony. It wasn't grievance. It was eleven minutes of footage, captured on a civilian relay array before the Concord seized the arrays, of a Concord diplomatic vessel meeting a Reach envoy in neutral space — three years ago, before the war had a name everyone used. On that footage, officers signed something. Exchanged tokens. Smiled the smiles of men concluding business. A peace accord, negotiated and accepted, at least provisionally, by both sides.

And then nothing. No announcement. No treaty. Just the war, rolling on, eating ships and stations and sons, year after year, while somewhere in a Concord vault sat the document that could have ended it.

"I was a relay tech for thirty years," Amara told me, coughing between sentences. "I know what I saw. I know what they buried. Everyone who saw it is dead now except me, and I'm next." She pressed the shard into my palm and closed my fingers over it. "Don't let them be right. Don't let it have never happened."

I'd meant to transfer it to the shielded case with the others. But the case's seal was finicky, and the transfer was half-done when my door chime warned me of a spot inspection — random, they claimed — and I'd done the only thing my hands knew: I'd slotted the shard into my implant's auxiliary port and let the record nest against my own neural architecture, encrypted, dormant, indistinguishable from scar tissue to any scanner below forensic grade.

Okonkwo's scanner, apparently, had opinions above his pay grade.

"Step through to secondary screening," he said, and there was an apology in his voice, faint, professional. He was a decent kid. That was almost worse.

* * *

Secondary screening was a booth with a chair, a deeper scanner, and a woman named Inspector Voss who had the eyes of someone who'd found contraband in enough bodies to stop seeing them as bodies.

"Neural implant, class-two medical regulator," she said, reading off a slate. "Registered to you. Standard maintenance logs show..." She paused. Her thumb hovered. "Your last diagnostic sync was fourteen months ago. That's noncompliant, Mr. Villalobos."

"Station's short on technicians. The clinic does what it can."

"Mm." She looked at me over the slate. "We're going to run a deep scan. It takes ninety seconds. If your implant contains anything other than regulatory firmware, you'll want to tell me now, because undeclared data in a neural implant is classified as willful concealment, and willful concealment gets referred to Concord Intelligence. You don't want to meet Concord Intelligence, Mr. Villalobos. Neither do I, honestly."

Ninety seconds. I did the math the way a drowning man does arithmetic. A deep scan would peel my implant open layer by layer. Firmware, yes. Scar tissue, yes. And underneath, wrapped in encryption that would take a machine perhaps forty of those ninety seconds to crack, eleven minutes of a buried peace treaty and a dead woman's last act of witness.

There was an option, of course. There's always an option; that's what makes choices terrible instead of merely hard. I could purge the record. One command, half a second, and Amara Diallo's eleven minutes would scatter into noise, and I'd walk out of this booth clean, and the war would continue exactly as designed, and someday I'd be an old man telling myself a story about the time I almost mattered.

Or.

My implant was old tech, older than regulation, built back when Meridian built its own tools because Earth had stopped sending any. Old tech had features the manuals didn't advertise. One of them was deep-storage compression — a mode meant for archiving a user's personal logs during long hauls, folding data down into the implant's physical lattice itself, below the software layer, into hardware. Nothing scanned hardware. Hardware was assumed to be what it was manufactured as.

Folding a foreign record into the lattice wasn't painless. The interface docs — written by some engineer who'd probably died in the evacuation — described the sensation as "transient proprioceptive displacement." The one other person I knew who'd done it described it as "like being briefly two people."

"Ninety seconds," Voss repeated. "Last chance."

"Run your scan," I said, and gave the command, and became two people.

It's hard to describe without sounding mad. For about ten seconds I was Bram Villalobos standing in a customs booth, and also I was a relay technician named Amara Diallo watching a diplomatic vessel through a scope, feeling her hands on controls I'd never touched, hearing her breath catch as the envoys shook hands — her memory running through my nervous system like a guest walking through my house touching all the walls. I felt her fear. I felt her certainty. I felt, absurdly, her love for a station that had forgotten her, and it settled into me like a coal into kindling.

Then it folded down, down past the software, into the lattice, and I was only myself again, sweating, knees aching, heart slamming.

Voss watched her screen. The deep scan crawled. Layer by layer it reported: firmware intact, architecture standard, no anomalous data structures, no unauthorized processes. The lattice read as lattice. I was, as far as the machine could tell, exactly the man my file said I was — a freight handler with a bad knee and an old regulator, boring as dust.

She studied me a moment longer than the machine required. Maybe she saw the sweat. Maybe she decided a middle-aged smuggler of coolant filters wasn't worth her evening.

"Diagnostic sync within the week," she said, stamping my slate. "Noncompliance noted. Go home, Mr. Villalobos."

I went home. I pushed my cart of honest coolant filters through Gate Four past Okonkwo, who nodded, and along Ring B's dim corridors, where the lights ran at emergency levels to save power nobody was allowed to buy, past the shuttered market stalls and the memorial wall where families chalked names the official record insisted didn't exist.

In my berth, I sat down and put my hand flat against the side of my head, over the implant, the way you'd check a wound.

"You're in there," I said quietly. Eleven minutes of proof that the war was a choice someone made and kept making. A dead woman's passenger, riding in the safest vault on Meridian: the skull of a man nobody important.

I should have felt afraid. I mostly felt what I always felt these days — that weary, stubborn loneliness of being one of the few who remembered, carrying what others wanted erased, with nowhere to put it down.

Then my comm unit lit up with a priority ping from Toma, my contact down in the wreck-salvage yards, a man who hadn't messaged me in two years because messaging me was dangerous for both of us.

The text was four words long:

**The Halcyon came back.**

I stared at it. The *Halcyon* had gone missing eleven years ago, deep-range survey ship, crew of nine, declared lost with honors nobody held. Every child on the station grew up on the story.

Toma sent one more line:

**Bring your kit. Bring the girl-proof locks. You're going to want to see who's flying her.**
