## 2. The Ship That Came Home Young

The message came in on a dead channel at 0300 station time, which is when all the best lies arrive.

*Old friend. The Halcyon came home. Come see before they bury it. — V.*

Vashti hadn't called me old friend since the last time she wanted something and I gave it to her for free. I lay in my bunk aboard the *Kestrel's Regret*—a name I chose because it made customs officers laugh, and laughing officers don't scan as deep—and stared at the ceiling of a ship that smelled like recycled air and other people's secrets, and I told myself I wasn't going.

I went.

Meridian Station had been officially abandoned for six years, which meant the Concord stopped sending food and started sending invoices nobody could pay. Officially abandoned also meant no one was supposed to live here, which meant roughly forty thousand people lived here, because official stories are like that: the less true they are, the more people they have to hold up. I'd spent those six years doing what I did before the war got organized about itself—carrying memory-records out through customs in my skull, carrying contraband medicine back in. A courier of things that never happened, according to Earth. A witness with a freight manifest, according to me.

The Halcyon had been missing eleven years. Deep-range survey vessel, crew of nine, vanished past the Verge during the first flare-up of the war. There were memorials. There was a plaque in the concourse garden, right next to the hydroponic tomatoes that grew bitter from whatever the bombardment drills had done to the water table. People touched the plaque when they walked past, the way you touch a scar you've decided to keep.

Now the Halcyon was home, drifting into Meridian's shadow like a dog that had run off years ago and come back thinner, quieter, wrong in some way you couldn't name until it bared its teeth.

I took a maintenance tram out to Docking Arm Seven, where the customs cordon glowed its cheerful amber *RESTRICTED — CONCORD JURISDICTION* and two enforcers stood looking bored in the way that means they're not bored at all. I didn't go near them. Vashti's tip included a route: Service Spine C, down through the coolant galleries, into the underbelly of the dock where the hull plating met the station's bones. A gap wide enough for a man who'd spent his life making himself small.

*

The Halcyon filled the dock like a whale in a swimming pool. Up close she looked worse than the feeds showed—hull scorched in long streaks, antenna array snapped off at the root, and everywhere that fine gray dust the salvage crews call ghost-fall, the residue of matter that's been somewhere matter isn't supposed to go. My boots left prints in it. Nobody else's did. That should have been my first warning: eleven years missing, and the only footprints on her skin were mine.

I found the maintenance hatch Vashti had described, still sealed with the original code-locks, and I stood there a moment being honest with myself, which is a thing I do maybe twice a year and always regret.

Here's what I knew. Customs had sealed the wreck within four hours of docking. Concord liaison officers had arrived within eight—not investigators, liaisons, which is what they call intelligence officers when they want them to sound friendly. The official story, already circulating on the public channels, was that the Halcyon had suffered a chronal displacement event, an "anomalous temporal shear," and that the crew—alive, recovered, in good health—were being cared for by Station Medical pending reunification with families.

In good health. I'd seen the feed. Nine children between the ages of five and nine, blinking at cameras with faces that matched the crew portraits displayed beside them like before-and-after pictures run backward. Commander Adeyemi, forty-six years old at last record, now a girl of perhaps seven with her mother's heavy brow and her father's crooked smile. The whole crew, aged backward into childhood, wearing their own history like hand-me-downs.

Chronal displacement. Sure. And I'm a licensed pharmacist.

Eleven years missing, and they come back younger? Time doesn't run backward. Time runs forward and takes everything, that's the deal, that's the whole contract. Something had happened to that ship out there in the dark, something the Concord wanted wrapped in jargon and buried under jurisdiction before anyone asked a child a simple question like *what did you see.*

And here was the part I hated about myself: I wanted to know. Not needed—wanted, the way you want to pick at a scab, the way curiosity feels noble right up until it costs someone. I carried other people's memories for a living. I told myself it was service. Some nights I even believed it. But there's a thief at the bottom of every courier, and mine had just smelled something worth stealing.

I cracked the lock—it was old Concord hardware, pre-war, and pre-war Concord hardware opens if you ask it nicely in the right dialect—and slipped inside.

*

The ship smelled like a nursery.

That's not poetry. That's the literal truth: powdered milk and disinfectant and the faint chemical sweetness of pediatric nutrient paste. Someone had stocked the Halcyon's galley with children's supplies in the last day. Crates stamped STATION MEDICAL sat stacked in the corridor, and small blankets, and a toy—a wooden puzzle shaped like the system's planets—that sat alone on a bench as if waiting for a hand.

But underneath the nursery smell was the other smell, the older one: cold metal, stale air scrubbers, and time. Eleven years of time, sitting in a ship that had clearly been empty most of it. No bodies. No logs in the open terminals. Just dust in the drift-patterns of a ship that had flown with no one breathing.

I moved through her the way I move through all forbidden places, quiet and apologetic, saying sorry in my head to every bulkhead. Crew quarters first. Commander Adeyemi's cabin had been stripped, but the mattress bore the impression of a small body, and on the wall, drawn in what I desperately hoped was crayon, was a picture: a big circle with lines radiating out, and stick figures standing around it, and beneath it, in a child's careful block letters:

*WE ALL SAW IT SIGN.*

I stood very still in that little room and felt the hair rise on my arms.

Sign. Not *saw.* Sign. You sign treaties. You sign accords. You stand around a table or a monument or a ring of stones while somebody puts ink to something that ends wars, and witnesses watch, and then the witnesses get to go home.

Except these witnesses hadn't gone home. These witnesses had been made small enough to fit inside a story the Concord could control. Because who believes a seven-year-old? Who hands a microphone to a child who says she remembers being fifty? You call it trauma. You call it temporal shear. You put a toy puzzle in her cabin and wait for the families to cry and the news cycle to turn, and in eighteen months the Halcyon is a sad footnote and the war grinds on, justified, funded, profitable.

That was the shape of it. I couldn't prove any of it yet. But I've spent my life learning to recognize the silhouette of an erased thing, and this ship was wearing one like a shroud.

The hold was aft, down a ladder built for adult legs. I dropped into the dark and clicked on my wrist lamp, and the beam swept across cargo netting, empty racks, a survey drone missing its sensor pod—

—and a data-core, bolted to the deck behind a panel that had been welded shut from the inside.

Welded from the inside. By someone on that ship, after whatever happened out there, who wanted this core to survive even if the crew didn't. Or didn't survive intact. Or didn't survive *as themselves.* The welds were crude, desperate, done in zero-g with a repair torch by someone whose hands shook—I could read the tremor in the bead work, because my father was a welder and he taught me that a weld is a signature.

I put my palm flat against the core's casing. It was warm. Eleven years adrift, four days in dock, and it was warm, drawing power from somewhere, holding something alive inside it the way a seed holds a tree.

"Okay," I said aloud, to the empty hold, because I talk to ships when I'm scared and pretend afterward that I don't. "What are you?"

The core answered the only way a core can: a status light blinked green, once, patient as a held breath.

I had my kit on me—of course I had my kit on me, I always have my kit on me, my ex used to say I'd take a bypass rig to a funeral—and I was already reaching for the extraction clamps when I heard it.

Footsteps. Above. Small ones.

Then a voice, high and clear, drifting down the ladder well with the terrible calm of a child reciting something learned by heart:

"You're the one who listens. She said you'd come. She said, *find the man who carries what others erase.*"

I killed my lamp and stood in the dark of the hold, heart hammering against the warm data-core, listening to small feet descend the ladder toward me.

"I'm Sefa," the voice said. "I used to be the navigator. I remember the signing."

*I remember the signing.* Four words, spoken by a person barely tall enough to reach the table where history had been written. I thought about running. I thought about how much simpler my life would be if I climbed back out that hatch and let the Concord keep its jargon and its jurisdiction and its carefully shelved children.

But I've never once in my life taken the simpler road, and the light on the core blinked green again, steady, like a heartbeat, like a promise.

She stepped into the edge of my beam—small, solemn, eyes too old for the face they'd been issued—and held out one small hand.

"You'd better sit down," said the navigator of the Halcyon, aged seven. "It's a long story, and everyone who remembers it keeps getting erased."
