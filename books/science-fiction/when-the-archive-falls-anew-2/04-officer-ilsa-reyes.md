## 4. Officer Ilsa Reyes

The orders came through at 0400 station time, which in Concord intelligence means they wanted you to feel like the war never sleeps even if you do. *Locate and secure anomalous minors associated with vessel Halcyon. Discretion paramount. Report to Director Voss directly.*

Anomalous minors. Four words doing the work of a nightmare.

I read them three times in my bunk with the lights off, because that's how you learn things on Meridian Station — or what used to be Meridian Station before the Concord declared it abandoned and then quietly kept running it as a customs chokepoint for the whole Reach corridor. Officially nobody lives here. Unofficially, forty thousand people live here, and I'm one of them, and so is a smuggler who sleeps with his boots by the door and his heart somewhere he'd deny having.

I know the official story about the Halcyon. Everyone does: lost with all hands eleven years ago, deep-range survey, tragic loss, moment of silence, next item. Now it drifts back into dock with its crew aged backward into children, and the Concord's response is to seal the wreck, classify the crew manifests, and send me hunting eight-year-olds with the word "discretion" attached.

Discretion, in my line of work, is a euphemism with a body count.

I got up, dressed in the dark, and did the thing I always do when an order smells wrong: I made coffee and thought about Bram Villalobos.

* * *

Here's what you need to understand about loving a smuggler when you're intelligence: you do it knowing the architecture of your life has a fault line running straight through the bedroom. We met two years ago at a customs auction where he was bidding on confiscated cargo with money he definitely didn't earn honestly, and I was there to watch who bid on what, and he bought me a drink afterward mostly, I think, to establish an alibi. It worked. I've been his alibi ever since, and he's been mine, and neither of us has ever once said out loud what we are to each other because saying it would make it evidence.

He knows what I do. He's never asked me to stop. I've never asked him to. That's not romance; that's a treaty. Two small nations maintaining diplomatic relations across a very narrow bed.

And now the Concord wants children found quietly, and Bram — Bram who carries dying colonists' memories in his own skull because someone has to, Bram whose entire moral architecture is built on the principle that nothing people suffered should be erased — Bram is going to be within a hundred meters of those kids if he isn't already.

I could report my personal entanglement. Standard procedure. Conflict of interest, recuse yourself, let Voss hand the file to someone without a lover in the smuggling lanes. The recusal would take four hours to process and the surveillance order on Bram Villalobos would take eleven minutes. I've seen how fast this machine moves when it decides someone is a problem.

Or I could find the children first. And decide, alone, in the dark, with only my conscience for a witness, what "secure" means.

I drank my coffee standing up, which is what you do when sitting down feels like surrender, and I went to work.

* * *

The Halcyon detail was being run out of Customs House by a man named Okonkwo who had the particular stiffness of someone who'd been told the job was simple and had already discovered it wasn't. He didn't want me there. Intelligence officers arriving at your crime scene means either promotion or paperwork, and he'd correctly guessed which flavor he was getting.

"The minors are in protective custody," he said. "Medical wing, east annex."

"Condition?"

"Healthy. Confused." He lowered his voice like the walls had clearance levels. "They keep talking about a signing. A treaty signing. None of them can tell us where or when, and their service records show no such event, and frankly, Officer Reyes, half of them shouldn't have service records at all because half of them weren't born when this ship launched."

"And the other half?"

"Were born after it launched." He rubbed his eyes. "The ship's logs are sealed from my level. Your level, I assume, can open them."

My level couldn't open them either, which I did not say. Sealed above me meant sealed above almost everyone, which meant whatever was in those logs had weight enough to bend the whole chain of command around it.

"I'll need access to the medical wing," I said. "And I'll need it quiet. If these children witnessed something traumatic, the last thing they need is a parade of uniforms."

Okonkwo looked relieved and suspicious in equal measure, which is the correct way to look at me, and signed me through.

* * *

The east annex smelled of antiseptic and children, that specific combination of sterilizer and fruit snacks that no amount of institutional cleaning fully defeats. There were nine of them. Nine small faces arranged in the common room like a jury that hadn't decided yet whether to convict the adult world.

A woman in medical grays — Dr. Adeyemi, according to her tag — intercepted me. "You're the third intelligence officer this week," she said. "The other two asked questions. I'm hoping you'll listen instead."

"That depends on the answers."

"They don't have answers. They have memories." She led me down the row of cots. "Adult memories, Officer Reyes. In children's heads. One of them described the interior of a Concord negotiation chamber in detail she has never physically seen. Another recited the names of everyone aboard at the time of — at whatever happened to them. She said them alphabetically. She was drawing a picture of a dog while she did it."

"Show me the one who talks about the treaty."

Dr. Adeyemi stopped at a cot near the window, where a girl sat cross-legged with a blanket around her shoulders like a general's cloak. Dark skin, close-cropped hair regrown unevenly, eyes that were the worst part — eyes that had done decades of living and were now installed in a face maybe ten years old. She watched me come the way adults watch, not the way children do.

"Sefa," Dr. Adeyemi said gently. "This is Officer Reyes. She works for the government."

"I know what she works for." Sefa's voice was light and young and utterly without a child's softness. "Everyone here works for somebody. That's the whole problem."

I crouched to her eye level, which felt less like rapport-building and more like being audited. "What's the whole problem?"

"You're going to find out, and then you're going to have to choose, and most people choose wrong." She tilted her head. "You have someone. Someone outside the uniform. I can tell. Adults carry a second heartbeat in their face when they have someone."

For one unprofessional moment I thought about Bram with an intensity that probably showed. "Is that so."

"There was a man who came to the dock," Sefa said. "Before they moved us here. He looked at the ship like it was a person. Like it was family coming home. He listened to me for eleven minutes and he didn't flinch once, not even at the bad parts." She hugged the blanket closer. "If you're looking for whoever took the data-core, start with him. And then ask yourself why you hope you're wrong."

I stood up too fast and made myself stand still. "What data-core?"

She smiled, and it was a child's smile wearing an old woman's knowledge, and it was the most frightening thing I'd seen in eleven years of intelligence work. "See? You already know him."

* * *

Bram's berth in the Ring Quarter is behind a noodle shop that launders more than vegetables, and I've knocked on that door perhaps fifty times and every single time there's a half-second where I don't know if he'll open it, or if today is the day the machine catches up with both of us and the door opens onto enforcers instead.

Today it opened onto him. Six days since I'd seen him. He looked tired in the specific way of a man who's been carrying something heavier than cargo, and his left hand had a fresh burn dressing, and none of that mattered as much as the fact that when he saw me his whole face did the thing it does — the guarded thing collapsing into the glad thing, quickly, like a man closing a curtain.

"Ilsa." Just my name, but he said it like contraband.

"Are you going to invite me in, or should we conduct our affair in the corridor for the benefit of your neighbors?"

"My neighbors mind their business. It's their best quality." He stepped back and I came in, and the door shut, and for about ninety seconds we were just two people in a small room, and I let us be that. His mouth tasted like cheap tea and he held me like something he was afraid of breaking or losing, possibly both, and I catalogued the burn on his hand and the new scratch along the freighter patch on his jacket sleeve and filed all of it under later.

Then I pulled back and looked at him, and he looked at me, and the treaty between our nations entered its crisis session.

"You've heard about the Halcyon," I said.

"Heard about it. Seen it. Been inside it." No flinch. Of course no flinch. "Your point?"

"My point is that this morning I was ordered to locate and secure anomalous minors, and tonight I'm standing in the apartment of a man who has already located them, and I need you to tell me — right now, with nothing held back — exactly how deep you are."

He crossed his arms. "Define deep."

"Bram."

"Define it, Ilsa, because I genuinely don't know anymore. I went into a sealed hold to satisfy my curiosity and I came out holding history. That's either shallow or the deepest water there is, depending on who's asking." His voice stayed level but his eyes didn't. "Why are you asking? Is this the job, or is this you?"

"It's both. That's the problem with me. It's always both."

Something in his face eased a fraction — not trust, exactly, but the recognition of honesty, which between the two of us passes for trust. He sat down on the edge of his bunk and told me. All of it. The children, the fragments, the data-core hidden in the hold, Oyelaran the disgraced archivist, the decoding. He told it plainly, without performance, and I understood that he'd decided something while telling it: that I was either the person he hoped I was or the end of everything, and he'd rather know than wonder.

When he finished, the room was very quiet.

"There was a peace treaty," I said slowly. "Eleven years ago. Real, signed, negotiated. And the Concord buried it and started the war anyway."

"That's what the record says."

"Do you understand what that means? Not morally — I know you understand it morally. Operationally. Every death in eleven years, every burned colony, every orphaned kid in the Reach — that's not tragedy. That's policy. And the people who wrote that policy will burn this station to the waterline before they let it broadcast." I heard my own voice shake and hated it. "Voss doesn't send officers to retrieve data-cores, Bram. He sends officers to make sure data-cores stop existing, along with anyone who's touched them."

"So why are you here?" Quietly. Not cruel. Just wanting the truth the way he wants everything — undiluted.

I sat down beside him. Our shoulders touched. Somewhere below us the station hummed its fake-abandoned hum, forty thousand lives pretending not to exist.

"I'm here because I was given a choice between warning you and using you," I said. "And I picked warning you, and I don't know yet if that makes me a traitor or the only honest person in the building, and I've got maybe until morning before Voss notices I spent six hours on a task that should have taken one." I turned to look at him. "But I'm here. That's the answer. I'm here."

He reached over and took my hand — the one with the customs-issue ring, the officer's hand — and laced his fingers through it like he was sealing something.

"Then help me get it out," he said. "Not for me. For them." Meaning the children. Meaning the dead. Meaning everyone the treaty was supposed to save.

And I said yes. I said yes knowing the clock was running, knowing that somewhere in a sealed office Voss was already pulling my file, already noticing the pattern, already writing the word *collaborator* in a draft memo he hadn't sent yet. I said yes because love against duty isn't a choice you make once; it's a choice you make every hour, and I intended to keep making it until the hours ran out.

What I didn't know — what I couldn't have known — was how few hours that would turn out to be, or that the escape window I'd eventually feed him would cost me everything I had left to give.

Outside, in the dark between the station and the stars, a Concord courier drone altered course toward Voss's office, carrying my name.
