## 5. The Buried Accord

Oyelaran's workshop smelled of ozone and old paper, which in the archive district was roughly the smell of respectability. The man himself sat behind a desk built from salvaged shuttle plating, a cyborg's left arm whirring softly as he turned a magnifier over the Halcyon's data-core like a jeweler pricing a stone he already knew was stolen.

"You understand," he said, "that I was cashiered from the Concord Archive Service for insisting that history had footnotes."

"I heard you were fired for being right at the wrong volume."

"Same thing." He set down the magnifier. His eyes, one organic and one a pale prosthetic amber, settled on me with the patience of a man who'd spent thirty years cataloguing other people's lies. "This core is sealed with Concord diplomatic cipher. Wartime grade. Do you know what that means, Villalobos?"

"It means it's worth more than my ship."

"It means it isn't contraband. Contraband is what people are ashamed to want." He tapped the core once, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "This is evidence. There's a difference, and the difference is body count."

I let that sit. Outside his single grimy viewport, Meridian Station turned its slow rotation, the lower rings dark where the war had chewed through power relays nobody would ever repair. Officially we were abandoned. Unofficially, forty thousand people lived in the dark and called it home, and I carried their memories out past the blockade one implant-load at a time because the Concord preferred them forgotten.

The children from the Halcyon were the strangest cargo I'd ever been handed. Sefa — eleven years old now, though her file said twenty-six — had described a treaty signing with the flat certainty only children and witnesses possess. A table on neutral ground. Signatures. Then orders to silence the ship before it could come home.

Oyelaran plugged the core into a decoding rig older than both of us combined and began to work. For six hours he said almost nothing. I drank bad tea and watched the progress bar crawl and thought about Ilsa, who hadn't answered my last three messages, which either meant she was busy or she was doing whatever intelligence officers do instead of saying goodbye. I tried not to think about which.

At hour seven, the rig chimed.

Oyelaran went very still. Then he laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man finding the corpse he'd always suspected under the floorboards.

"Well," he said. "Sit down, smuggler. You'll want to be sitting."

* * *

The treaty existed. That was the first thing. Not a fragment, not a rumor, not a child's broken recollection — the full instrument, text and annexes and signatures, negotiated over fourteen months between the Concord and the Outer Reach delegations aboard the Halcyon itself. Both sides had initialed. Both sides had agreed to ceasefire lines, prisoner exchanges, a shared administration of the border stations.

Meridian was named in it. Article Nine. Relief convoys, restoration of civil infrastructure, an end to the quarantine that had strangled us for years.

"The war ended," Oyelaran said quietly, scrolling. "Right here. Eleven years ago. And then someone ordered the Halcyon silenced and buried this, and the shooting started again within the month."

"Why?" It was a stupid question. I knew why. I'd known since I was old enough to read procurement ledgers, but knowing and having it scroll past your face in official type are different animals.

He showed me anyway. Attached to the treaty file, cross-referenced and annotated in Concord internal cipher, was the money. War contracts. Reconstruction monopolies. Blockade enforcement fees paid to private fleets whose owners sat on the Concord Security Council. Eleven years of conflict generating eleven years of profit for maybe two hundred names, and every one of those names had signed off on burying the peace.

"Ending the war doesn't just stop the killing," Oyelaran said. "It exposes every official who profited from continuing it. They didn't reject the treaty because it was bad. They rejected it because it worked."

I stared at Article Nine. Relief convoys. My mother had died in the third year of the quarantine, waiting on a relief convoy that was never coming because the treaty authorizing it was sitting in a vault. Somewhere in my chest something that had been weariness for a long time quietly finished hardening into something else.

"We copy everything," I said. "Redundant copies. Three formats, three hiding places."

"Already running." He glanced at me with the amber eye. "You should know what happens next, Bram. When they realize this core is loose — and they will realize — they won't send inspectors. They'll send enforcers, and they won't be interested in arrests. An arrest produces testimony. Testimony survives."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I'm seventy-one years old, my career is already dead, and I have no family to make me careful." He smiled, and it was almost warm. "You, however, have a ship, a talent for vanishing, and apparently a child witness who trusts you. One of us should survive to carry this."

"Don't talk like that."

"I've spent thirty years talking like nothing. Indulge me."

* * *

The raid came at 0340 station time, which told us everything about how official it was.

Oyelaran heard it first — his rig flagged the customs network going quiet district by district, a blackout rolling toward us like weather. He killed the lights, swept the copies into a shielded case no bigger than a book, and pressed it into my hands along with the original core.

"Service tunnels behind the stacks," he said. "They lead to the old freight lift. Go."

"Not without you."

"Bram." He took my shoulder with the whirring hand, grip gentle and immovable. "Someone has to answer the door. If nobody answers, they search. If they search, they find the tunnel. I am going to open the door, be extremely rude to some young enforcers, and give you nine minutes. Possibly twelve. I intend to be insufferable; it's my last professional act and I mean to do it well."

"There has to be—"

"There isn't. There never was. That's rather the point of everything we just read." His voice softened by a degree. "Thirty years I filed truth in boxes nobody opened. You're the first person to walk in and actually want one. Let an archivist complete the transaction."

Down in the tunnel, running between shelves of dead records with the case against my spine, I heard the door above breach. Heard Oyelaran's voice, clear and unhurried, quoting some regulation or other at men who'd stopped listening to regulations years ago. Then a shot. Then another. Then nothing, and the nothing followed me all the way down the freight lift.

Nine minutes. He'd bought me nine minutes, possibly twelve, and he'd known exactly what the purchase price was when he quoted it.

* * *

I reached the Halcyon's berth as the sirens finally woke. Sefa was awake too — they always were, the children, sleeping light as birds — and she looked at my face and at the case and understood with that terrible doubled clarity of hers.

"He stayed," she said. Not a question.

"He stayed."

She nodded slowly, and then she did something I wasn't ready for: she put her small hand on my arm, the way adults comfort each other, the way her adult self must have done a hundred times before the ship went dark.

"My mother used to say grief is just love with nowhere to go," she said. "So we should give it somewhere. Give it a job."

So I gave it a job. I gave it the case, and the core, and the names of two hundred men who had traded a world's peace for their portfolios. I gave it a heading out of the archive district while enforcer gunships painted our wake.

And then my comm lit with a message from Ilsa — four words, no signature, sent through channels she should never have touched:

**They know. Run now.**

Behind us, the archive district burned.
