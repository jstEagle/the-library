## 1. Contraband of the Heart

The customs station hung above Ceres Verge like a rusted wedding ring, all corroded gold and bureaucratic promise, and Freya Joubert had been slipping past it for eleven years without once being caught.

She told herself it was skill. Some nights, alone in the dark of her bunk with the drive humming its low lullaby through the deck plates, she admitted the truth: it was also that nobody looked very hard at a woman like her, in a ship like hers, carrying cargo like hers. The *Kestrel's Regret* was forty years old and looked every day of it—hull patched with mismatched plating, one engine cowling painted a slightly different shade of grey than the other, her registry transponder chirping out the legal fiction that she hauled agricultural seed-stock and water reclamation filters to colonies too small to matter. Customs drones had scanned her hull so many times they'd grown bored of her. Boredom was the best camouflage in the system. Better than stealth coating, better than forged manifests. A thing had to be interesting before anyone bothered to look at it closely, and Freya had spent eleven years making sure she and everything she loved were profoundly, aggressively uninteresting.

"Freighter *Kestrel's Regret*, you are cleared for approach lane seven," said the customs channel, in a voice synthesized to sound warm and sounding instead like a man reading a grocery list through a wall. "Maintain speed. Do not deviate."

"Copy, Verge Control. Maintaining." Her voice came out flat and easy, the voice of a tired hauler who wanted nothing more than a hot meal and a bunk.

Inside her chest, her heart kept a different rhythm entirely.

* * *

The memories rode in the coolant lines.

That was the joke she never made aloud, because jokes about your own tradecraft had a way of becoming epitaphs: the coldest place on the ship carried the warmest things aboard it. The primary coolant loop ran through conduits beneath the cargo deck, wrapped in insulation rated for hard vacuum, shielded by three layers of plating and a magnetic shunt that scrambled casual data-sweeps into static. Any scanner that probed those lines would read nothing but refrigerant chemistry and thermal bleed. What the scanners could not know—what no scanner had ever known—was that threaded through the coolant jacket, sealed in cryo-stable memory cores no bigger than a thumbnail, were the last season of a dying fisherman and a mother's lullabies, and eleven years of other people's hearts.

Freya floated in the crawl space beneath the cargo deck, one hand braced against a conduit that hummed cold against her palm, doing the final check. The cores sat in their cradles like sleeping things. She ran her thumb over each seal, feeling for microfractures, checking the status lights: green, green, green.

The fisherman's core was the newest. Old Piet Allemand, from the fishing platforms off Meridian's southern shoals—except there were no southern shoals anymore, not officially, because officially there was no Meridian Colony at all, and Freya had learned to hold two maps of the universe in her head at once: the map the corporations sold, and the map her own eyes drew. On the second map, Meridian's platforms still creaked over grey-green water, and Piet Allemand still went out every morning before dawn to pull nets heavy with silverfish, even as the tumors spread through him like ink through cloth.

He'd come to her six weeks ago with the recorder in both hands, the way people always did, as if handing over something breakable and holy. "My last season," he'd said. "Not my death. My season. I want them to have the mornings."

*Them.* He hadn't specified who. Nobody ever did. That was the secret grammar of her trade: everyone who recorded assumed a future listener, some unnamed descendant or stranger, someone who would press play and feel the salt wind and understand that Piet Allemand had existed, had loved the grey water, had been afraid and gone out anyway. The corporations archived transactions. Freya smuggled the rest.

The mother's lullabies were older cargo, three runs back, from a woman on the Verge ring itself—a hab-block midwife named Ondine whose daughter was being relocated off-world by an employer who did not permit dependents. Ondine had recorded forty-one songs in her kitchen, her voice low and rough, sometimes singing alone, sometimes with the small voice of her daughter joining in on the choruses, sometimes dissolving halfway through into the kind of crying that tries to be quiet and fails. Freya had listened to exactly thirty seconds of that cache when she took custody of it, just enough to verify integrity, and then she had stopped, because some things you couldn't carry and also fly straight.

The cores were going to Juno Reyes on the Verge underlevel, who would broker them onward—to private collectors who paid for authentic grief, yes, but also to the remnant communities scattered through the outer belts, the diaspora pockets, the people who had lost homeworlds and languages and needed, desperately, to hear a human voice from before. Juno called it the empathy trade. Freya called it what it was: smuggling the evidence that people had been alive.

She sealed the access panel and pulled herself up through the maintenance hatch into the cargo bay proper, where the legal cargo sat in plain view—forty crates of seed-stock, honestly declared, honestly boring. Through the forward viewport, customs Station Verge grew from a smudge to a structure: a wheel of docking arms around a central spindle, its hull furred with sensor arrays and antenna masts, floodlights glaring off the ice-dust that clung to everything out here. Ships queued in the approach lanes like patient cattle. Somewhere in that wheel, algorithms chewed through manifests and biometric signatures and emotional-resonance pre-screening flags, deciding who was interesting today.

Freya docked on arm nine, as always. Arm nine's clamps were old and slow, and the drone assigned to it was older still—a customs unit designated V-114, though the dockhands called it Grandfather, because its voice module had degraded into a papery rasp and it moved with the deliberate, creaking patience of something that had been doing this job since before Freya was born. Grandfather was due for decommissioning. Every year it was due for decommissioning. Every year the budget line got cut. And every year, Grandfather scanned the *Kestrel's Regret* with the same four sweeps it had performed a hundred times, and found the same honest seed-stock, and waved her through with the same rasped blessing.

Today, though, there was a second drone waiting beside it.

Freya saw it as the clamps locked home—a sleek new unit, matte black where Grandfather was scuffed white, moving with the fluid economy of current-generation hardware. It bore no customs livery. It bore a corporate mark instead: a stylized sunburst, gold on black.

Halcyon.

Her hands went still on the docking console. Halcyon Salvage Consortium didn't do customs work. Halcyon did salvage rights, colonial charters, extinction clauses—the machinery of erasure, papered and lawful. Its presence on a customs arm meant one of two things: either the Consortium had bought influence here recently, or it was looking for something specific.

Or someone.

"Cargo freighter *Kestrel's Regret*," Grandfather rasped, oblivious, running its first sweep along her port side. "Declare manifest."

"Agricultural seed-stock, forty units. Water filtration spares, six units. Crew of one. Nothing living, nothing perishable, nothing interesting." The litany rolled out smooth. Under the console, hidden from any camera angle, her hand rested on the manual override for the coolant loop's thermal profile—one tap and the lines would flush their buffer charge, wiping the cores' residual magnetic signature down to nothing. It cost her the cores' playback integrity if she held the flush too long. It was the emergency brake. She'd never used it. She let her finger hover near it now.

The black drone detached from its berth and drifted toward her hull.

* * *

They made her wait ninety minutes.

Grandfather completed its sweeps in the usual time, found the usual nothing, and then the black drone requested—and received, from some human supervisor Freya never saw—an enhanced secondary scan. Enhanced secondary scans happened maybe twice a year, usually to ships out of the Kessil Corridor, where the pirate syndicates seeded decoy data-cores in legitimate cargo. Freya sat in her cockpit with a cup of recaf going cold in the holder and watched the drone's sensor eye crawl along her hull centimeter by centimeter, and she did the only thing left to do in such moments, which was to become boring with her whole body.

She yawned. She scratched her jaw. She pulled up a maintenance log on her display and frowned at it with genuine irritation, because the starboard air recycler genuinely did need a filter change and she might as well get work done while she waited to find out if her life was over. She thought about coolant pressure. She thought about the price of deuterium at the Verge depot. She thought, deliberately, about nothing that mattered.

The trick, she'd learned long ago, was that scanners read stress the way sharks read blood—not the data itself, but the body carrying it. Heart rate, pupil dilation, the micro-tremor in a hand. So she gave the cameras a woman annoyed at paperwork. Inside, where the cameras couldn't reach, she kept a small cold room with the door shut, and behind that door the fisherman's mornings and the mother's forty-one songs waited in the dark, patient as seeds.

Ninety-three minutes after docking, the black drone withdrew to its berth without comment. No flag. No query. Either it had found nothing, or it had found nothing worth stopping a seed freighter for.

"Freighter *Kestrel's Regret*," Grandfather rasped. "Sweeps complete. You are cleared for departure and surface transfer. Safe skies, hauler."

"Thanks, Grandfather," Freya said, and only realized she'd said it aloud when the drone's degraded audio processor produced a faint, puzzled click. She covered it with a cough and keyed the release.

The clamps let go. The Verge fell away beneath her, its floodlights shrinking to a scatter of coins on black velvet, and Freya let out a breath she'd been holding for ninety minutes and felt the small, familiar vertigo of survival—the giddy, shaky gratitude of a body that had expected to die and hadn't. She'd felt it a hundred times. She suspected she'd feel it a hundred more. It never quite became routine; that was how she knew she was still human underneath all the armor of habit.

Then she set course for Meridian, and the vertigo faded, and the loneliness came back in behind it like tide into a footprint.

* * *

Eleven hours of transit, coasting dark between the shipping lanes. Freya ate standing up at the galley alcove—rehydrated noodles, tea brewed twice—and afterward she did what she always did on the long hauls. She opened the recording suite.

It wasn't much: a salvaged studio rig wedged into what had once been a supply locker, a good microphone, a screen, and shelves of labeled cores—her own archive, the one nobody knew about, the one that wasn't for sale. She settled into the chair, pulled up the file tree, and scrolled to the folder marked MERIDIAN / SURVEY.

Forty-one entries. Four years of them.

Entry one: the colony's central square, market morning, recorded on a handheld rig she'd worn under her coat. The babble of vendors, the smell of frying doughnuts rendered in the hiss of wind across the mic, children shouting, someone playing a squeezebox badly and joyfully. Entry nine: the same square, eighteen months later, half the stalls shuttered, the squeezebox man gone, the wind louder. Entry twenty-two: the square empty except for weeds coming up through the plaza seams and one old woman—Marta Okonkwo, though the recording only captured her voice—sweeping pavement that no one walked on, because sweeping it mattered to her, because a swept square was still a square.

Entry forty-one, recorded five months ago: the square again. Marta's broom. The wind. And underneath, so faint you had to strain for it, the distant groan of the fishing platforms, still working, still feeding a colony that appeared on no census, received no mail, counted in no ledger. A place being erased in real time, and the only witness a smuggler with a microphone and a habit she couldn't explain even to herself.

Freya pressed record and narrated the transit log in her flat archivist's voice: date, position, customs notes, the black Halcyon drone on arm nine, possible new scanning protocols, watch yourself. Then, because the log was done and the ship was quiet and the stars outside the viewport were the same indifferent handful they always were, she let the recording run a little longer.

"The Verge is getting more corporate eyes," she said. "I don't know what they're looking for yet. Maybe nothing. Maybe me. Maybe—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Meridian looks smaller every time I go back. Not the buildings. The sound of it. There are fewer voices under the wind. I'm going to walk the square this trip. I'm going to count the lit windows. Somebody should count."

She stopped the recording. Labeled it. Shelved it.

That was the whole ceremony. Eleven years of these little entries, filed away in a locker on a ship that officially carried seed-stock, testimony accumulating in the dark with no plan and no audience—just the stubborn, unreasoning conviction that someday, somehow, it would matter that someone had counted the lit windows.

Her grandmother would have understood. Her grandmother, dead twenty years now, who used to sing to her in two languages, one of them half-forgotten even by the old woman herself—snatches of melody in a tongue Freya never learned the name of until later, until she read the histories and found the word *Vellish*, and the word *Purge*, and understood why her grandmother sang those songs only in whispers, only with the windows shut, as if the melodies themselves were contraband. As if memory could be arrested.

Freya touched the shelf of cores lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and killed the cabin lights.

* * *

Meridian rose out of the dark three days later, blue-grey and cloud-streaked, and it looked wrong immediately, the way it always looked wrong now: too few lights on the night side. A living colony glowed. Meridian flickered—scattered constellations of illumination with vast gaps between them, like a city mid-blackout, or a face with teeth missing.

No traffic control hailed her. There hadn't been traffic control in years; the last controller had stayed at his post unpaid for eight months out of loyalty and then taken the shuttle out and never come back. Freya flew her own descent vector, threading the dead satellites, and set down on the cracked apron of Port Meridian, where the beacon still turned its slow red eye over weeds growing up through the runway seams.

She popped the hatch and stood breathing the real air—cold, wet, smelling of salt and kelp and engine oil—and for a moment, as always, she just let it hit her. Then she started unloading seed-stock for the co-op warehouse, because even forgotten colonies ate.

Marta Okonkwo found her at the warehouse door, as Marta always did, small and straight-backed at seventy-nine, her white hair wrapped in a yellow headscarf that had once been bright and was now the color of old bone.

"You're late," Marta said.

"I'm always late."

"You're always late, and I'm always glad, and we say the same things every time." But Marta was smiling, and gripping Freya's forearm with those strong old fingers, and looking up at her with eyes that missed nothing. "Come inside. The kettle's on. And Freya—" her grip tightened, just slightly, "—there's something you should hear. Something strange came through on the old receiver last week. I don't have the ears for it anymore. But I think maybe you will."

Freya followed her into the warmth, and did not yet know that the life she'd built out of being unseen had just begun, quietly, to end.
