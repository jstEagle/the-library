## 3. Signal in a Dead Tongue

The dark between Meridian and the Juno Reach was the kind of dark that had texture. Freya Joubert had spent enough hours floating through it to know its moods: the grainy charcoal of dust lanes, the oily black where nothing at all had ever happened, the bruised violet near the rim of a nebula's afterthought. Tonight—or what she called tonight, ship-time, third watch—the dark outside the *Kestrel's Spare* was the honest kind, empty all the way down, and she loved it the way other people loved their mothers.

She had the autopilot on a long lazy curve around the Kessel Drift, four days of nothing between her last delivery and her next pickup, and she was spending it the way she spent most of her nothing: listening.

The receiver sat in the corner of the cockpit like an animal she'd rescued and never quite tamed. She'd pulled it out of a decommissioned relay buoy eleven years ago, off a scrap hulk in the Verge, and it had cost her three months of runs to rebuild—hand-wound coils, a preamp she'd machined herself from a broken cargo clamp, a tuning array that could hear a whisper across forty light-seconds if the whisper wanted badly enough to be heard. Officially, the receiver didn't exist. Unregistered communications equipment aboard a freighter was a customs violation, one of maybe two hundred violations the *Kestrel's Spare* committed before breakfast. But everyone smuggled something, and Freya had decided long ago that if she was going to be a criminal, she'd be a criminal with good taste.

What she listened for was mostly silence. The shipping bands, the corporate chatter, the automated litanies of buoys and beacons—all of it noise, all of it someone else's business. But underneath the noise, if you were patient, if you learned to hear the way you learn a lover's breathing, there were other things. Ghost carriers from dead stations still transmitting on schedule to no one. The slow arithmetic of pulsars. Once, memorably, six hours of what she could only assume was a private funeral service, broadcast open-channel by mistake from a mining platform, full of weeping in a language she didn't know, and she had listened to every minute because turning it off felt like leaving.

She kept recordings of everything. Not for sale—she wasn't a monster—but because the dark was full of things that would otherwise go unwitnessed, and witnessing was, though she'd have denied it under torture, the closest thing she had to a religion.

So when the signal came, at 0347 ship-time, while she was eating reconstituted noodles out of the pot and watching the Drift turn slowly on the display, she almost missed it for what it was: not noise, not ghost-carrier, not pulsar. A voice.

Her spoon stopped halfway to her mouth.

* * *

It came in on a frequency that shouldn't carry anything—a dead band, de-licensed since before she was born, the kind of frequency that was supposed to be as empty as the space around it. The receiver's auto-scan had flagged it as anomalous harmonic structure, which was the machine's polite way of saying *this doesn't look like physics*. She brought it up on the main speaker and let it fill the cockpit.

A voice. Human, or human-shaped. Thin, distorted by distance and whatever atmosphere or vacuum it had crossed, but unmistakably a voice, rising and falling in patterns that were too regular to be random and too varied to be mechanical. It repeated. She timed it. Every forty-one seconds, the same sequence, roughly nine seconds long, then silence, then again. A distress pattern's skeleton wearing flesh she couldn't name.

She ran it through the translator suite first, because that was procedure, because that was sense. The suite chewed on it for a long time and came back with nothing. No match in trade-tongue. No match in any of the colonial dialects, the corporate cant, the old Earth languages in the standard package. It offered, apologetically, a 12% confidence match on ancient Sumerian liturgical chant, which told her only that the software was grasping at straws made of nonsense.

She should have logged it, tagged it, moved on. That was the rule. Her rules were few but they were iron: never follow distress calls; never answer unknown transmissions; never become visible. She had written those rules herself, in the aftermath of the thing that had made her this way, and they had kept her alive through eleven years of customs drones and consortium patrols and the particular hunger of people who noticed other people. Never follow distress calls, because distress calls were bait more often than not, and even when they weren't, answering them meant becoming someone who could be found.

But she didn't log it. She recorded it instead, clean, at maximum fidelity, and then she played it again. And again. And somewhere around the twentieth repetition, sitting very still in the pilot's chair with the noodles forgotten and cooling in her lap, she recognized it.

Not the words. She didn't know the words. But the shape of them—the music underneath the language, the way the syllables climbed and broke and climbed again, the specific ache in the phrasing, a rise at the end of each line like a hand reaching up out of water—

Her grandmother had sung like that.

* * *

Her grandmother, Ilsa, had died when Freya was nine, in a company dormitory block on Ceres Annex, in a bed too narrow, singing to the end. Freya remembered the songs the way you remember heat from a fire you were carried away from too young to understand it: fragments, mostly. Half-melodies. A lullaby about a boat that was probably not about a boat at all—Ilsa had sung it low, always low, glancing at the door, and young Freya had thought that was just how grandmothers sang. Another song with a word in it that sounded like *vellem*, repeated like a heartbeat, *vellem, vellem*, and Ilsa's eyes closed and her hands moving on the blanket as if smoothing something invisible flat.

Ilsa had called it her mother's language. She'd said the word once—*Vellish*—and then never again, and when Freya, at seven, had asked what Vellish was, her grandmother had looked at her for a long moment with an expression Freya wouldn't understand until decades later, an expression like a woman deciding how much of a wound to show a child.

"It's a language that finished," Ilsa had said finally. "Like a book that got burned before the last page. You don't ask where a burned book goes."

Freya had asked anyway, because she was seven, and Ilsa had held her very hard for a moment and then changed the subject to dinner.

It was only years later, in a station library, in a stolen hour with a public terminal she'd routed through three proxies, that Freya had looked up the Vellish Purge, and even the lookup had felt transgressive, like reading someone else's mail addressed to the dead. Two hundred and twelve years ago. The Vellish Compact—a cultural federation, three systems' worth of settlements, poets, archivists, stubborn keepers of an oral tradition older than their spaceflight—had ended up on the wrong side of a consolidation war, and the winning consortiums had not merely defeated them but *erased* them. Language ordinances. Archive seizures. Forced resettlement, then forced assimilation, then, in the territories that resisted hardest, things the histories called "pacification actions" and the few surviving documents called by plainer names. Within a generation, Vellish was classified as extinct. Not endangered. Extinct—the linguistic equivalent of a species with no living members, no descendants who spoke it, no one left to mourn it in its own words. The archives said so. The archives were very thorough about what they had unmade.

And here, now, in the honest dark, out of a frequency that had been legally empty for two centuries, something was speaking it back into the air.

* * *

She did not sleep. She told herself she was being professional—running spectral analysis, triangulating rough bearing, checking the signal against known beacon registries—and she was, technically, doing all of that. But underneath the professionalism she was playing the recording over and over, and each time it looped, the grief arrived earlier and stayed longer, until by the fourth hour she was crying without drama, without sound, the way she did everything, tears running down into the collar of her flight suit while her hands stayed steady on the console.

Because that was the uncanny thing, the thing she couldn't file anywhere: she had grieved for Vellish already. She hadn't known that's what the grief was—it had been folded inside the larger grief for Ilsa, for the narrow bed, for the songs sung low and glancing at the door—but it had been there, a small sealed room in her chest marked *finished, like a burned book*. And grief for a lost thing has a shape, and part of its shape is finality. You build your life around the absence the way water builds around a stone. The stone does not get to speak.

This stone was speaking. In her grandmother's mother's tongue. On a band that had been dead longer than her whole family line had been poor.

She caught herself whispering along with it—shapeless, wordless, her mouth trying to make the shapes of syllables she'd never been taught—and stopped, embarrassed, alone as she was, in a cockpit four light-minutes from anything human. Embarrassment, she noted distantly, was another luxury of the visible. Out here there was no one to perform composure for. She let herself do it anyway. She mouthed the sounds of a dead language at the dark, and the dark, for once, seemed to be mouthing back.

* * *

Toward morning—toward 0800, rather, when the coffee was real and the noodles were a cold congealed memory—she made herself sit down and think like a smuggler instead of an orphan.

Facts. One: the signal originated from somewhere in the general volume beyond the Kessel Drift, bearing consistent across six hours of observation, which meant either a fixed source or a source moving very slowly. Rough triangulation put it in a region of nothing much—a scatter of ice moons and rock, unclaimed or claim-forgotten, the kind of map notation that read *low value, unsurveyed* and therefore, in Freya's experience, usually meant *hidden on purpose*. Two: the transmission was weak but disciplined. Forty-one-second intervals, nine-second bursts, power profile consistent with a low-yield transmitter running on scavenged or rationed energy. Someone was being careful. Someone was broadcasting just loudly enough to possibly, eventually, be heard, and no louder. Three: it was repeating the same content. Not a conversation. A message. A message designed to be received by anyone, which meant designed to be received by someone, which meant whoever was sending it believed there was someone left who could understand it.

Or—whoever was sending it hoped so. Hope and belief were different animals. She knew that difference in her marrow. Belief was thinking someone would come. Hope was sending the signal anyway.

Four: the Purge had been two hundred and twelve years ago. Languages did not survive their own extinction. If someone was broadcasting in Vellish, then either the archives were wrong—and archives were wrong the way rivers were wet, constantly and by design—or these were people who had done the impossible thing, the thing Ilsa's generation had been hunted for attempting: they had kept it. Kept the tongue alive in throats, in secret, in the spaces between census ships and language ordinances, for eight generations, the way you keep a coal alive under ash.

Freya looked at the bearing on her display. It pointed into the Drift's far side, past the shipping lanes, past the patrol corridors, into a stretch of black that appeared on her charts as a scattering of unlabeled bodies and a single line of text: *NO REGISTERED INTEREST.*

No registered interest. She almost laughed. Somewhere out there, someone had registered no interest so hard they'd built a life on it.

* * *

The rules sat in her head like three stones in a row. *Never follow distress calls.* She picked the rule up and turned it over. It existed to keep her alive, and it had, and she owed it everything. But rules, she thought, were also a kind of archive—they recorded what had hurt you, and they preserved the hurt past all usefulness if you let them. Never follow distress calls, because the last time she'd answered a call for help, she'd been seventeen, and the help had been a trap, and the trap had taught her invisibility the way fire teaches a child the stove.

But this wasn't a call for help aimed at her. This was older than traps. This was a lullaby with a mayday's bones, sent out on a dead frequency by people who had spent two centuries learning exactly the skill she'd built her whole self around: how to be unseen. How to hide so well the universe itself filed you under *no registered interest*. They were her people in a way she had no right to claim and no way to deny—not by blood, or maybe by blood, she didn't know yet, but by craft. By the shared religion of not being found.

And underneath all of it, patient as sediment, the other truth: her grandmother had sung low and glanced at doors, and had died with half a language in her mouth, and the archives said the other half had never existed. Freya had spent eleven years smuggling other people's memories past customs lines because some part of her believed, without ever saying it aloud, that memory was the only property worth stealing—that a dying fisherman's last season and a mother's lullabies were worth more than any cargo manifest, because they were the only things in the system that couldn't be reprinted. And now the dark was handing her the master copy of the whole thesis: a language the archives had declared extinct, alive, calling, forty-one seconds at a time.

If she ignored it, she would spend the rest of her life knowing she'd heard her ancestors' tongue reach out of the grave and had checked her fuel gauge instead.

If she followed it, she might die. Or worse—be seen.

She sat with that for a long time while the coffee went cold and the signal looped, faithful, forty-one seconds, nine seconds, silence, again. Like breathing. Like someone very far away breathing on purpose, so that anyone listening would know they were alive.

"Damn you," she said quietly, to the signal, to Ilsa, to the burned book, to whoever was keeping the coal under the ash. It came out tender, which ruined it.

She fed the bearing into the navigation computer, plotted a course that looked like nothing—a drift route, a salvage meander, deniable three ways if any patrol asked—and engaged the drive. The *Kestrel's Spare* came around her slow and sure, the way she liked, and the honest dark began to slide past the viewport, and somewhere ahead of her, growing by fractions of a degree per hour, hung a bearing and a frequency and a voice.

Forty-one seconds. Nine seconds. Silence. Again.

She set the receiver to record and left it playing softly through the cabin while she ran pre-flight checks, and if her hands were not entirely steady on the throttle, well—there was no one out here to see. There never had been. That had always been the comfort of it.

For the first time in eleven years, it felt less like comfort and more like practice.
