## 6. What the Vaults Hold

The vaults opened with a sound like breathing.

Sefa Vell had heard it every morning of her sixty-one years—the hydraulic sigh of the pressure door, the exhale of geothermal warmth meeting the cold of the ice above—and she had never once thought of it as machinery. Her grandmother had called it *the throat*. The mountain swallowing and giving back, swallowing and giving back, generation after generation, keeping what it was given safe in its belly.

She stood aside now to let the stranger pass through first.

Freya Joubert moved like someone who had spent her life expecting doors to close on her—quickly, shoulders drawn in, eyes cataloguing exits before she'd finished crossing the threshold. Sefa had watched that quality all morning: at the gate, at the long table where they'd fed her fish stew and dark bread, in the way the woman's hands kept finding the seams of her own sleeves as if checking for wires. A smuggler's body. A survivor's posture. Sefa knew it because half the settlement wore some version of it, inherited down through two centuries of hiding, the way other families passed down eye color or a crooked chin.

But the stranger had come when called. That was the thing Sefa kept returning to, turning over like a stone in her palm. The signal had run for eleven months into empty space, and no one had answered, and then this patched-together freighter had dropped out of the dark asking questions in careful, accented Vellish—*Where are you? Who is left?*—and Sefa had made the choice that elders were chosen to make, the choice that could end everything, and had said: *Come down.*

Now she watched Freya Joubert step into the first vault chamber and stop moving entirely.

* *

The chamber was not large. That was always the surprise, Sefa thought; people expected cathedrals. What they got was a room the size of a granary, its walls lined floor to ceiling with memory cores—dark cylinders the length of a forearm, each one sleeved in woven insulation, each one labeled by hand in ink that had faded from black to brown across the generations. The heat of the vents ran through copper pipes along the baseboards, steady as a heartbeat, because cold was the enemy of stored memory the way forgetting was the enemy of a name.

There were four chambers like this one. And beneath them, accessible only through the crawl-tunnels her grandfather's generation had dug with hand tools and terrible patience, there were the deep shelves: the originals, the cores carried out of the Purge itself, wrapped in sealskin and wax and grief.

"Touch nothing yet," Sefa said, though the woman hadn't reached for anything. "First you should know what you're looking at."

Freya turned. In the amber light of the vent-lamps her face was hard to read—not closed, exactly, but weathered, like wood that had been outside too long to show grain clearly. "I'm looking," she said, "at more data than I've ever seen in one place that wasn't a corporate archive."

"Yes." Sefa let the word sit. "And that is precisely why we have never shown it to anyone."

She walked the row slowly, touching each core as she passed, the way her grandmother had touched them, the way her mother had—a fingertip to the sleeve, brief, almost casual, the gesture of a person greeting family in a room full of family. "This shelf is song. Eleven thousand hours of it, give or take what the damp has taken from us. Work songs, mourning songs, the long genealogical chants that used to take three nights to sing whole. This shelf is law—land law, water law, the arbitration records of a people who governed themselves for six hundred years before your charter companies drew their maps. This shelf—" she paused here, as she always paused here, "—is names."

Freya had drifted closer despite herself. Sefa saw the smuggler's discipline war with something older and hungrier in her face, and privately, quietly, she was glad to see the hunger win.

"Names?"

"Every Vellish dead we can account for. From the Purge and from everything after." Sefa lifted a core from its cradle and held it in both hands. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. "Two hundred and nine thousand, four hundred and twelve individuals, recorded by name, parentage, village, and manner of death where it was known. My great-grandmother began the ledger during the evacuation years. She was fourteen. She worked on it for seventy years, and her daughter continued it, and her daughter after her, and I continue it now. We add names still, sometimes. When a grave surfaces in a melt-field. When an old recording is restored and a voice names its own mother, and we realize she was never entered." She set the core back with the care owed to a sleeping child. "Your corporate archives say the Vellish were relocated. Resettled. Assimilated by consent. They say the word *Purge* appears nowhere in any official record. Is that not so?"

"I've moved contraband past customs scanners for nineteen years," Freya said. "I've never once seen a manifest line for a massacre."

"No," Sefa said. "You wouldn't have. Erasure is the most efficient cargo there is. It weighs nothing. It takes up no hold space. It requires only that everyone agree not to look." She gestured at the walls around them, the thousands of sleeping cores, the warm breath of the mountain holding them. "We did not agree."

* *

They went deeper.

The second chamber held the recordings that Sefa found hardest to guide strangers past, and she watched Freya Joubert's face as the woman understood what she was seeing: experience-cores, full sensory captures, the forbidden technology that had been the Purge's stated pretext. *Memory crime*, the charters had called it. The owning of one's own interior life in transferable form. Two hundred years later the corporations recorded everything and owned all of it, and the crime had quietly become an industry, but the old law still stood on the books, and so the Vellish still hid.

"These are testimonies," Sefa said. "Survivors of the burning years, recorded in the third and fourth generations. Some of them are children of children who remembered. Some are the last speakers of dialects that died with them mid-sentence—you can hear it happen, on some of these. An old man telling a story and arriving at a word that no longer exists anywhere in the world, and stopping, and starting again in trade-tongue, and apologizing." Her voice caught, as it always caught, on that particular core, third shelf, fourth position. "He apologized. For losing a word. As if the loss were his fault."

Freya stood very still. Then she said, quietly, "My grandmother sang half-Vellish lullabies. She didn't know what the words meant. She said her own mother told her they were boat songs, from before, and wouldn't say more."

Sefa felt something move through her chest that was not quite hope and not quite pain but had the weight of both. "What were the songs?"

Freya hummed four bars. Low, rocking, the melody shaped like water going out and coming back.

Sefa closed her eyes. "*Ael su venna, ael su korrin.* Sleep, little hull, the sea holds you. It's a launching song. Women sang it when the boats went out, and then when the transports went out, and then when the prison ships went out." She opened her eyes. "It's in our vaults. Third chamber. Your grandmother's mother likely learned it from someone who learned it from these very recordings—or from the same source, before the scattering. There may be a version sung by someone who shared her blood."

For a moment the smuggler's composure cracked clean through, and Sefa saw underneath it a girl, maybe eight years old, being sung to sleep by a woman carrying a language she wasn't allowed to name. Then the crack sealed over, smooth as lake ice, and Freya Joubert said only: "I'd like to hear it. Sometime."

*Sometime.* Sefa noted the word and did not press it. Strangers said *sometime* when they were not yet sure there would be a time.

* *

In the third chamber, Sefa showed her the sickness.

She had debated all morning whether to show it at all—to let the stranger see the vaults strong, inviolate, worth protecting, rather than the truth, which was that they were dying. But Tam had argued otherwise the night before, standing in her kitchen with his stubborn jaw set at the angle his dead father's used to set, and Tam had been right. *If she's going to carry anything for us, Amma, she has to know how heavy it is. You don't ask a porter to lift a crate you've described as light.*

So Sefa knelt at the east wall and pulled back the woven insulation, and showed Freya the bloom.

The corruption spread across the core's casing like frost flowers, white and delicate and utterly wrong. Data-rot. The vaults' climate system had been built from salvaged parts two generations ago, and the parts were failing faster than the settlement's machinists could improvise replacements, and every year the cold spots crept further along the pipes, and every year another handful of cores bloomed white and silent.

"How many?" Freya asked. Her voice had gone flat and professional, and Sefa recognized the tone: it was the voice of someone calculating.

"Of the original Purge-era cores? Perhaps thirty percent compromised beyond recovery in the last decade. Of the full collection, we lose between two and five percent a year, and the rate is accelerating. My engineers buy me seasons, not years." Sefa rewrapped the core gently. "We copy what we can onto newer stock, but new stock means trade goods, and trade goods mean contact, and contact means exposure. So we ration. Every year we choose which memories to save and which to let go white." She straightened, knees aching. "Do you understand what I am telling you, Freya Joubert? I am the keeper of a library that is burning in slow motion, and my choices are which rooms to wet down."

Freya was quiet for a long moment. Her eyes had gone to the ceiling, tracing the pipe runs, the vent architecture, the load-bearing ribs of the mountain itself. Reading the room the way a smuggler reads a ship. "Geothermal loop's undersized for the volume," she said finally. "And your heat exchangers are running on what, secondhand station scrap?"

"You have a good eye."

"I have a paranoid eye. It's kept me alive." Freya crouched beside the corrupted core, studying the bloom without touching it. "Some of this might be recoverable. Partial reconstruction off the error-correction residue. It's delicate work and it needs equipment I don't own, but I know people who owe me favors." She stopped. Sefa watched her hear her own sentence, watch the woman understand what she had just offered and what it would cost. "People," Freya said again, more carefully. "Which brings us to the part of this conversation I've been dreading."

"The drones," Sefa said.

Something shifted in the smuggler's face. "You've seen them."

"Since the spring thaw. At first we thought they were surveying the rare-earth seams—Halcyon has sniffed at this moon twice before, decades apart, and gone away when the extraction costs outweighed the charter penalties. But these don't leave." Sefa led her to the chamber's narrow observation slit, a gap in the rock no wider than a hand, and stood back to let Freya look.

Beyond the slit, the ice plain stretched white to the horizon under a sky the color of old pewter. Far off—at least four kilometers, near the northern ridge where the thermal blooms made mirages dance—a single point of light drifted with the patient, gridlike deliberation of a machine doing arithmetic. It hung. It moved forty meters. It hung again.

"That pattern isn't mineral survey," Freya said softly. "Mineral survey sweeps. That's..." She trailed off. Sefa watched her arrive at it. "That's site characterization. That's what you do when you already know what's there and you're measuring for what comes next."

"We have counted eleven distinct units over the months. They keep their distance. They do not approach the settlement directly. My council says this means they haven't confirmed we exist." Sefa folded her hands inside her sleeves, an old habit, the posture of a woman keeping her fear where her hands could hold it. "My council is wrong. I think it means they have confirmed, and they are deciding."

"Deciding what?"

"What we are worth." Sefa turned from the slit. "Freya Joubert. I am an old woman and I have spent my whole life learning to say true things plainly, so I will say this one plainly. If Halcyon confirms three hundred unregistered descendants of a purged population living above rare-earth deposits, they will not send soldiers. Soldiers make martyrs and paperwork. They will send a clearance order dressed as a geological report. A seismic event. A vent collapse. A tragedy, regrettable, unforeseeable, and the moon will be scraped clean, and the archive will be slag, and in fifty years even the lie will be forgotten because there will be no one left to contradict it." She let that stand in the amber air between them. "Unless the proof leaves this mountain first."

Freya looked back at the walls of cores. All those hours of song. All those names. Two hundred and nine thousand, four hundred and twelve, and counting, and losing.

"The full collection," she said slowly, "is how much storage?"

"At current compression, and including the deep-shelf originals?" Sefa had done the arithmetic so many times it lived behind her teeth like a prayer. "Forty-one petabytes. More, if we recover the corrupted sections."

Freya exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh, entirely without humor. "Forty-one petabytes. I hide stolen memories in coolant lines. I've never moved more than a few terabytes at a stretch. Forty-one petabytes through orbital customs would take convoys. It would take routes I don't have. It would take—" she stopped again, and this time Sefa watched her stop herself, deliberately, the way a person steps back from a ledge.

"Say it," Sefa said gently.

"It would take allies. Contacts. People who know my face and my registry and my habits." Freya's jaw tightened. "Nineteen years I've stayed invisible. Invisible is the only reason I'm alive."

"And visible is the only reason we are." Sefa stepped closer, close enough to see the fine scarring at the woman's hairline, the map of old decisions written on her skin. "I will not pretend to offer you an easy thing. If you carry for us, you become what we are: evidence. Evidence is hunted. But hear me, Freya-of-nowhere-and-no-flag—" she used the old Vellish blessing-form, the address for a person without a house, and saw it land somewhere deep, "—you came down through our sky answering a call eleven months old. No one asked you to. Whatever you decide next, you have already done the hardest thing. You stopped pretending you didn't hear."

Freya was silent for a long time. Out beyond the observation slit, faintly, the drone's light crawled another forty meters and hung.

"There's a boy," Freya said at last, abruptly, as if changing the subject and confessing something in the same breath. "Tam. He cornered me at the gate this morning. Told me he's memorized ten thousand songs and asked me—asked *me*—to take him with me someday." Her mouth twisted. "I told him no. I told him the worst thing I could think of, which was the truth: that the road I travel eats people like him."

"He has been waiting at that gate since the signal first answered," Sefa said. "He believes you are the future arriving. He is fifteen and he is correct about most things, which is the most exhausting quality a grandchild can have." She smiled, and let the smile fade honestly. "He is also right about the vaults. He's the one who insisted I show you the rot. *She has to know how heavy it is,* he said."

Freya looked away. When she spoke, her voice was rougher. "How long do your engineers say the climate system has?"

"A year. Eighteen months if the winter is kind. Less if Halcyon's surveys turn to something else."

"Then I need to make some calls," Freya Joubert said. "Calls I swore I'd never make, to people who'll want to know why, and I'll have to tell them the truth, because lies travel worse than contraband." She turned back toward the vault door, toward the throat of the mountain and the daylight beyond it, and paused with her hand on the frame. "Elder Vell. Start your people copying. Everything. Prioritize the deep-shelf originals and the name-ledger. If I'm going to do this, I'm going to do it properly."

"And if Halcyon decides before you're ready?"

Freya's face did something complicated and final. "Then we'd better hope," she said, "that I'm faster than their paperwork."

After she had gone, climbing back toward the grey light, Sefa stood alone among the singing walls and laid her palm flat against the nearest core, feeling the faint warmth of it, the stored pulse of ten thousand voices. *Hold on,* she told them, in the old tongue, the way her grandmother had told them, the way she told them every night. *Hold on. Help has come down out of the dark.*

Above the ice, unseen, the drone completed its grid and turned, unhurried, toward home.
