## 9. Terms of Smuggling

The vault chamber smelled of cold iron and warm breath. That was the thing about the settlement, Freya was learning: everything down here was a negotiation between the moon's killing cold and the small stubborn heat of three hundred bodies. The geothermal vents breathed through pipes older than anyone living, and the air carried a faint mineral sweetness, like water from a deep well, like something the ground itself was trying to say.

She stood at the edge of the assembly hall while the vote was taken.

It wasn't a vote the way stations voted—no terminals, no tallies flashing on public boards. Elder Sefa stood before the gathered settlement with a carved staff of vent-iron in her hand, and one by one, people came forward and pressed their palms to it. Freya counted without meaning to. Two hundred and six hands before the line began to thin. Some did not come at all. An old man near the back turned his face to the wall when the staff passed, and a woman with a baby on her hip pressed her palm down and then wiped it on her coat, as if she'd touched something hot.

They were voting on her. She understood that much. Whether to hand a stranger the whole of what they were.

*They don't have a word for stranger,* Tam had told her the night before, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the vault antechamber while she pretended to check her ship's manifests and actually listened to him talk the way teenagers talk—endlessly, breathlessly, as if the words had been dammed up for years. *We have a word for someone who hasn't been named yet. It means "not-yet-heard."*

"And if they're never named?"

He'd shrugged, in the way of the young, who had not yet learned what it cost to be unheard. "Then they were never here."

The staff came back to Sefa's hand, and she turned to Freya, and the hall went quiet enough that Freya could hear the vents breathing in the walls.

"The settlement has spoken," Sefa said, in her accented trade-tongue, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "You will be told the terms. Not asked. Told. If you cannot carry them, you will say so now, and you will leave, and we will seal the ice behind you, and no one will speak of you again."

Freya had been threatened by customs officers, pirates, one memorable customs officer who was also a pirate, and a corporate auditor with a voice like wet gravel. None of them had frightened her the way this old woman's gentleness did.

"Tell me," Freya said.

* * *

They laid it out in the vault chamber itself, where the temperature was held just above freezing to preserve the cores, and Freya stood with her arms wrapped around herself while Sefa and the archivists—there were nine of them, keepers, they called themselves, and they moved with the unhurried certainty of people who had done one thing for generations—showed her what she had been voted.

The core was not a thing. That was her first mistake, thinking of it as a thing. She'd imagined a data-slate, a crystal lattice, something she could wrap in insulation and tuck into her coolant lines the way she'd tucked a dying fisherman's last season, a mother's lullabies, a hundred small stolen sunsets. She had smuggled memories for eleven years and she had never once carried more than a few hundred hours.

The Vellish archive was nine hundred years.

"It is not only recordings," said one of the keepers, a stooped man named Eron whose hands trembled unless they were touching the cores, and then went perfectly still. "It is law. It is genealogy. It is the names of every Vellish soul from before the Purge, and every song, and the songs have harmonies that exist nowhere else, and the harmonies have meanings, and the meanings—" He stopped himself, pressed his lips together. Sefa put a hand on his shoulder.

"It is a people," Sefa said simply. "Compressed. You have carried memories before, the settlement knows this. You will tell us honestly: can you carry a people?"

Freya looked at the vault.

It filled a cavern. Racks of memory cores, thousands of them, ran back into the ice in rows that vanished beyond the reach of the work-lamps, and the keepers had told her, with the flat pride of the doomed, that the deep racks were older, that some of the cores at the back had been carried out of the Purge itself, hidden in flour sacks and coffin linings and one notorious case inside a hollowed-out church bell. Nine hundred years of a civilization, and the racks were failing—she could see it herself, the frost-cracked casings, the warning lights that pulsed amber on maybe one core in twenty. Data rot, slow and patient, eating the archive from the edges in. They had been losing their dead a little more every year, and now Halcyon's drones circled overhead, and the losing was about to become total.

"The whole core is—" she did the arithmetic in her head and hated the number. "Four hundred exabytes. Maybe more, if the old formats have to be unpacked. My coolant lines might hide forty hours of high-fidelity memory. The whole vault would take—" She stopped. Said it anyway. "Eleven runs. Minimum. Assuming I had a ship that could lift that mass, which I don't, and a route that didn't exist, which I'd have to invent, and customs scanners that stayed blind, which they never do."

The keepers looked at her the way people look at a doctor who has just said the word *terminal* gently.

"Then it cannot be done," Eron said, and his voice cracked on it.

"I didn't say that." Freya heard her own voice and was surprised by it—steady, dry, the voice she used across negotiating tables. "I said it can't be done the way I've always done things. The way I've always done things is hide small things in a small ship and never be seen. That's over. If I take this on, I stop being a ghost. Ghosts can't carry four hundred exabytes through a customs blockade."

Sefa studied her. The work-lamps caught the deep lines of her face, and Freya thought, absurdly, of the ice outside—of pressure ridges, of what centuries of weight did to a surface.

"Then say what you need," Sefa said. "Not what you wish for. What you need."

Freya had spent eleven years needing nothing from anyone. It was the whole architecture of her life: the patched freighter, the false registry, the fence she paid in cash and lies, the runs planned so that no two contacts ever overlapped. She had built invisibility the way other people built houses, plank by plank, and she had told herself it was freedom. Standing in the cold of the vault, she felt the architecture groan.

"A ship that can lift the mass," she said. "Or a fleet of small ships, moving separately, which means other pilots, which means other people who know. A route that doesn't touch any port Halcyon has a scanner in, which means ports run by people who owe me, or who hate Halcyon, or both. And—" she made herself say it, made herself stand in the open doorway of it, "—a fence. Someone outside this ice who moves cargo and moves money and moves lies, and who I have to tell the truth to, all of it, because you can't smuggle a people through a network you're lying to."

The chamber was silent. The amber warning lights pulsed down the long racks like slow heartbeats.

"You are describing exposure," Sefa said.

"I'm describing the end of the only way I know how to survive."

"And you would do this? For people you met six days ago?"

Freya looked down the rows of cores. Somewhere in the deep racks was her grandmother's language—half of her grandmother's lullabies had been Vellish, sung low so the neighbors wouldn't hear, and Freya had grown up thinking the melodies were just her grandmother's, private, invented, a quirk of one old woman. Then a distress signal in the dark had sung those melodies back to her, and she had learned that what she thought was hers alone had belonged to thousands, and that thousands had been killed for it, and that the killing had never actually stopped—it had just gotten paperwork.

"I'd do it because I've spent my whole life carrying other people's last seasons," Freya said. "The dying fisherman. The mother's lullabies. I take the memories of people who are about to be forgotten and I keep them moving, and I told myself that was enough. It isn't. It's a museum of endings. You're offering me the first thing I've ever carried that isn't dead yet." Her voice did something she didn't authorize. She let it. "Yes. I'd do it. On terms."

"Name them."

* * *

The terms took all night, and most of the next day, and were argued with a ferocity that Freya came to understand was itself a Vellish inheritance—these were people whose law had survived two centuries underground, and they did not agree to anything without tearing it apart first.

She would take copies, never originals. The originals stayed in the ice; if she died, the vault died with her, but the vault would not die *because* of her. The copies would be made in batches of thirty cores, sealed in geothermal-warmed cases the keepers had built for exactly this contingency two generations ago and never used, and the cases would be logged, and every log entry would be recited aloud into the vault so that the archive recorded its own export. Tam, when he heard this, was beside himself with joy—*the archive remembers itself leaving, do you understand how beautiful that is*—and Freya, who understood exactly how beautiful it was and how dangerous, had to look at the frost on the walls for a while before she could speak.

She would decide the routes alone. She would decide the allies alone, though she suspected—knew, with the sick certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant—that her list of allies was one name long. Juno Reyes, who had moved Freya's contraband for nine years, who thought Freya was a small-time runner with a soft heart and a hard head, who was about to learn that her quietest client had been sitting on the largest memory cache in the system. Juno, who would either become the most important person in this whole desperate enterprise or sell her out for enough to clear her debts. Freya had watched Juno lie to a Halcyon inspector with a smile and a tray of drinks, and had watched her cry over a dead courier's unpaid invoice, and she genuinely did not know which of those women she'd be handing the archive to.

That was the terror, she thought, standing at the vent-viewport that night, watching the settlement's lights gutter against the ice. Not the customs scanners, not the eleven runs, not even Kessler's drones that the keepers swore were circling closer every week. The terror was this: to do this, she had to become visible. Every ally was a witness. Every witness was a leak. Every leak was a list of names in a Halcyon file, and the last time Vellish names had been in a corporate file, two hundred thousand people had gone into the ground.

She had built her whole life on a single principle: *unseen is alive*. And here was the arithmetic of the vault saying otherwise. Unseen was alive, yes—but unseen was also, in the end, unread. Unheard. A name never spoken. She thought of the old man who hadn't pressed his palm to the staff, and the woman who'd wiped hers clean, and she thought: *they understand. Some of them would rather be erased than risk being seen. And they voted anyway. Two hundred and six hands.*

Invisibility had never been safety. It had just been a slower, quieter version of the same erasure. She had been practicing her own disappearance for eleven years, one anonymous run at a time, and the vault was offering her the first alternative she'd ever been handed: be seen, and matter, or stay hidden, and be nothing.

The vents breathed. The ice ticked and settled, the way it did at night, the moon's crust contracting in the cold like an old man pulling a blanket up.

"Can't sleep either?"

Tam dropped onto the bench beside her with a mug of something steaming and pressed it into her hands before she could refuse. He was fourteen and had memorized ten thousand songs and had no sense whatsoever of personal space, and over six days he had attached himself to her with the relentless loyalty of a burr.

"I'm planning," Freya said.

"You're staring at ice."

"Planning is mostly staring at ice."

He grinned and drank from his own mug, and for a while they sat in the cold and the quiet, and Freya felt the strange double weight of him—this kid who had already asked her, once, too casually, whether she'd take his memories out if the worst happened, and who was now sitting next to her like she was furniture, like she was permanent, like she was someone who stayed.

"Eleven runs," Tam said. "Eron says it can't be done. Sefa says you said it could."

"Sefa listens better than Eron."

"What did you actually say?"

Freya wrapped her hands around the mug. The heat of it felt almost indecent, down here at the edge of all that ice.

"I said I'd need to stop being a ghost," she said. "And then I said yes anyway."

Tam was quiet for a moment, which for him was a geological event. Then: "My grandfather used to say the Purge didn't kill us. Hiding killed us. Slow. The Purge just dug the grave." He looked at her, and his face in the vent-light was young and old at once, all those songs behind his eyes. "You're the first person who's ever come out of the grave and offered to carry us back up."

"Don't make me a metaphor, Tam. I'm a smuggler. I'm going to get you all killed with paperwork."

He laughed, and the laugh bounced off the ice, and somewhere down the long racks of the vault an amber warning light pulsed its slow pulse, and Freya drank her terrible vent-brewed tea and let herself, for exactly one minute, feel what she was feeling.

Then she stood up.

"Sleep," she told him. "Tomorrow I start building a smuggling route for a civilization, and I have to go tell the only person in the system who can help me that I've been lying to her for nine years."

Tam's grin faded into something more careful. "Is that bad?"

Freya looked at the viewport, at the settlement lights against the dark, at the faint moving star that might have been a survey drone and might have been nothing.

"It's the most honest thing I'll have done in a decade," she said. "That's what scares me."
