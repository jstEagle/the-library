## 13. Festival of Names

Tam Vell had counted the songs ten thousand times, but he had never counted the names. There were too many. That was the point of them.

He stood at the edge of the geothermal terrace in his festival coat—his grandfather's festival coat, actually, the sleeves let out twice and the collar patched with fabric cut from a woman's shawl whose name Tam had never learned—and watched the settlement assemble below him in the blue steam-light of the vents. Three hundred people, give or take the very old and the very new. The old ones came wrapped in thermal wool the color of ash. The new ones—the children born in the last decade, his generation, the generation that had never seen anything but the ice and the vents and the long dark—came barefoot on the warm stone because the stone was warm and because barefoot was how you were supposed to come, though nobody remembered exactly why anymore, and that not-remembering was itself a small wound the festival existed to dress.

The vault mouth stood open at the terrace's center: a black circle in the rock, older than anyone living, its rim worn smooth by two centuries of hands. Recording cores glowed faint amber along its inner ring, hungry, waiting. Tonight they would be filled. Tonight the names would go into the stone the way they went into the stone every year, and the way they would go in every year after, forever, or until someone stopped them, and Tam tried not to think about which of those futures was more likely.

He was fifteen. He had been reciting names since he was four. He knew his own line by heart—Vell, Sefa, Vell, Idris, Vell, and back and back, a rope of names thrown across two centuries of darkness—and he knew the other lines too, most of them, because that was what he did, that was what he *was*, the boy who remembered forward. Elder Sefa said it as a compliment. The other children said it as a joke, mostly, though there was an edge to it sometimes, an edge that meant *you're strange, you're ours, but you're strange.* Tam had made his peace with the strangeness. What he had not made his peace with was the question underneath it, the one that woke him some nights with his heart going like a shuttle engine: *what is all this remembering for, if it never leaves this moon?*

A song is a seed, his grandfather used to say. A seed in a sealed jar is only a seed. Somewhere there has to be soil.

He scanned the crowd for the stranger and found her near the back, standing where the terrace stone gave way to ice, as if she couldn't decide whether she was inside the circle or outside it. Freya Joubert. The smuggler. The off-worlder with the patched freighter and the eyes that never quite stopped moving, cataloguing exits even during a blessing. She wore her ordinary coat, the one with the scorched cuff, and she stood the way a person stands at the edge of a party where they know no one—weight on her heels, hands in pockets, half-turned toward her ship on the horizon.

But she had come. That was the thing. She had been on the moon eleven days and she had kept to the guest quarters and the vault access corridor and her ship, and now, tonight, she had come down to the terrace with everyone else, and she was standing in the cold edge of the warm stone, and she was watching.

Tam went to her. He didn't decide to; his feet just did it, the way they always did around her, and he hated and loved that about himself.

"You're supposed to take your coat off," he said. "Festival rule. Skin on the stone. It's about—" He faltered, because he'd never had to explain it before. "It's about being in contact. With the ground. With the moon. You can't carry names into the vault if you're not standing in the place they died."

Freya looked down at her boots. Looked at the crowd, three hundred pairs of bare or stockinged feet on the stone. Something moved across her face—Tam had learned to read faces the way he read songs, note by note, and what moved across hers was not reluctance exactly. It was more like the look of someone standing at a door they'd forgotten they knew how to open.

"Off-world customs would have my ship impounded for less than this," she said, but she was already unlacing.

"Off-world customs aren't here."

"No," she said, stepping out of her boots, peeling off her socks with the brisk efficiency of a woman used to doing uncomfortable things quickly. "They're just circling."

She said it lightly. Tam felt it land heavy anyway, because everyone on the moon knew about the drones now—the survey drones that had been threading the sky for months, patient as vultures, and everyone knew what survey drones meant, even the children, even if the adults pretended the children didn't know. But this was the festival. This was the one night the fear was supposed to sit outside the circle with the cold. So he didn't answer, just nodded her forward, and the two of them walked onto the warm stone together, and Tam felt a small fierce pride when Freya's bare feet touched the rock and she didn't flinch, didn't even slow down, just kept walking like she belonged on it.

* * *

The recitation began at full dark, as it always did, with Elder Sefa standing at the vault mouth with a single lamp.

Tam knew every part of the ceremony the way he knew his own heartbeat. First the silence—three minutes, one for each century of the dead. Then Sefa's voice, low and cracked as old leather, speaking the opening words in Vellish, the language that existed nowhere else in the settled system, that had been hunted to death everywhere but here, in this bowl of warm stone under the ice: *We were. We are. We will be said.*

Then the lines began.

They went by family, oldest to youngest, each household standing in turn at the vault mouth and speaking the names of their Purge dead into the recording cores while the rest of the settlement stood in the circle with their palms open and their eyes closed. Two hundred years of names. The names of people who had been loaded onto ships that were supposed to be resettlement transports and were never heard from again. The names of people shot in the streets of cities Tam had never seen, on worlds Tam would never walk. The names of people whose entire crime had been their language, their songs, their stubborn refusal to stop saying *we* when the charter worlds had decided there was no such thing.

Tam's household went ninth. He stood at the vault mouth beside his mother and his grandmother and he spoke his rope of names into the dark, *Vell, Idris. Vell, Ansa. Vell, the brothers Okoro-Vell who took the ship called Patient*, and the cores drank the words and glowed a little brighter, and when he finished, the circle breathed out all around him, three hundred exhales in the cold air, and he felt the feeling he always felt, the one he had no trade-tongue word for, the one that was half grief and half something that stood next to grief like a sister.

It was during the fourteenth household that he noticed Freya crying.

She was trying to hide it—head tilted slightly down, jaw set, the posture of a woman who had spent a lifetime learning that tears were a security breach. But the lamplight caught her face, and Tam, who noticed everything about her because he couldn't help it, saw. And what undid him, what he would remember for the rest of his life, was that she wasn't crying the way you cry at a stranger's funeral. She was crying the way you cry when a song you half-remember from childhood turns out to be real.

Because that was the thing about Freya Joubert that Tam had figured out in their first conversation and had been quietly turning over ever since: her grandmother's lullabies had been half Vellish. She had told him that herself, embarrassed, as if it were a confession. Half a language, handed down in fragments, in a cradle, in secret, on some freighter or some station far from here—the way the language had survived everywhere, in pieces, in whispers, in the mouths of grandmothers who shouldn't have been singing it at all. Freya had grown up on the wreckage of the same massacre that built this moon. She just hadn't known it had a shape. Hadn't known the wreckage had gathered itself somewhere, had learned to live under ice, had kept singing.

The households went on. Twentieth. Thirtieth. The steam rose and the lamps burned and the names went into the stone, and the cold pressed in at the edge of the terrace where the ice began, and Tam stood in the circle with his palms open and his eyes closed and listened to his people say *we were, we are* into the dark, and he thought: *soil. This is the soil. She is standing in it.*

* * *

It was near the end, when the oldest names had all been said and only the living remained—the roll of the living, which was the last and most dangerous part of the festival, because a roll of the living was a census, and a census was exactly the kind of record the Purge had been designed to prevent—when Elder Sefa did something that had never happened in Tam's lifetime.

She stopped.

She stood at the vault mouth with her lamp and her list, and she looked out across the circle, and her eyes found Freya Joubert the way a magnet finds north, and she said, in trade-tongue, so that everyone would understand, "There is one among us tonight who has no line here. Who has no names to give and none to receive. Who came to us out of the dark carrying nothing but her ship and her caution." A pause. The steam hissed. "And who has been carrying for us ever since."

Tam's heart slammed. He looked at Freya. Freya had gone very still, the stillness of an animal that has heard something in the brush.

"The old law says," Sefa continued, "that a stranger who stands barefoot on the stone through the whole recitation is not a stranger. The old law says the circle decides. So I am asking the circle."

And the circle—three hundred people, his people, in the blue steam-light—did the thing that circles do. Nobody spoke. Nobody needed to. There was just a sound, a low collective hum, almost subsonic, the sound Tam's grandfather had called *the ground agreeing*, and it rose out of the warm stone and through their bare feet and up into their chests, and Tam felt it in his sternum like a second heartbeat.

Sefa turned to Freya. "In Vellish," she said, "a carrier—one who bears what must not be dropped—is *sava*. And one who carries across the dark, between worlds, is *sava-tenn*. The one who carries over." She held out her hand, palm up, the way you offer a name. "Freya Joubert. Will you take the name Freya-of-the-Carrying? It is not a small name. It is a name with weight in it. It will ask things of you."

Tam watched Freya's face do several things at once. He watched the smuggler reflex—the calculation, the *what does this cost, what does this expose*—flicker and fail, because there was nothing to calculate. He watched the loneliness underneath it, the loneliness she wore like her scorched cuff, like a patch over something, come up to the surface for the first time in maybe years. And he watched her step forward, barefoot on the warm stone, into the lamplight, into the circle, and say, in her off-world accent, in trade-tongue, with her voice not entirely steady:

"I'll carry it."

Sefa took her hands and spoke the naming into the recording cores—*Freya-of-the-Carrying, received under the ice, in the two-hundred-and-eleventh year of the keeping*—and the circle hummed again, the ground agreeing, and people began to move toward Freya from all sides, touching her shoulders, her arms, her hands, the old greeting, and Tam saw the exact moment when Freya Joubert, who had spent her whole life being unseen on purpose, was seen by three hundred people all at once and did not run.

He was close enough to hear her say, half to Sefa, half to herself: "I've never had a name that wasn't on a registry."

"Now you have one that isn't," Sefa said. "Registries can be erased. This one can't."

*Later*, Tam thought. *Later I'll ask her what it felt like.* He wanted to know the way he wanted to know the endings of songs. He wanted to know what it did to a person to stop being invisible in a single night, because he suspected—correctly, as it turned out—that he would need to know someday himself.

* * *

The final recitation came at the end, as it always did: the roll of the living. The children went first, always, oldest custom, because the children were the newest names and the newest names were the most fragile. Tam stood with them at the vault mouth, the whole barefoot crowd of his generation, and when his turn came he spoke his name—*Tam Vell, son of Idris's line, keeper of ten thousand songs*—and then, on impulse, on a whim he would think about for years afterward, he added words that weren't in the script:

"And Freya-of-the-Carrying, received this night, who carries over."

A ripple went through the circle. Sefa's mouth curved, just slightly. The cores glowed.

It was during the last households of the living—during the Osei line, during old Marta's borrowed names, the ones she kept for a colony that no longer officially existed, because Marta had insisted on adding Meridian's dead to the roll two years ago and Sefa had agreed and the sky had not yet fallen—that Tam saw it.

He saw it because he was the boy who noticed things. He saw it because his eyes, even during the recitation, even with his palms open and his heart full, kept doing what they always did: sweeping the horizon, sweeping the sky, cataloguing.

Above the northern ridge, above the ice field where the settlement's heat signature would bloom brightest against the cold, a light was moving. Not a star. Stars didn't move in straight lines. Not a shuttle—shuttles from the settlement announced themselves. It was small and steady and patient, and it crossed the sky the way the survey drones crossed the sky, and it slowed.

It slowed, and it held position, directly above the terrace. Directly above three hundred barefoot people and an open vault mouth and a roll of living names going into the stone.

The recitation faltered. Old Marta stopped mid-name. The hum of the circle died, and in the silence Tam could hear the vents breathing, and his own heart, and nothing else. Three hundred people looked up. The drone's running lights blinked—once, twice—recording, or signaling, or both.

It hung there for the length of ten heartbeats. Then it moved on, unhurried, over the ridge, and was gone.

Nobody spoke. Then Sefa closed the ledger of names with a sound like a small door shutting, and said, in Vellish, the words every child on the moon knew and dreaded: *The keeping continues. But keep your boots by the door.*

Tam looked at Freya. Freya was already looking at the sky, at the place where the drone had been, and her face—the face of a woman who had been given a name an hour ago, who had stood barefoot in a circle of belonging for the first time in her life—had gone hard and flat and professional, and Tam understood, with a cold that had nothing to do with the ice, that the festival was over.

That night, long after the lamps were out, Freya stood at the terrace edge for a long time, looking north. Tam found her there. Neither of them said anything for a while. Then she said, quietly, "How fast can your people pack?"

Tam thought of the vaults. Of the cores, glowing amber in the dark, full of two hundred years of *we were, we are*. Of ten thousand songs, and three hundred people, and one aging shuttle.

"As fast as we have to," he said.
