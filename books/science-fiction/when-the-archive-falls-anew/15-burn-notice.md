## 15. Burn Notice

The message came in at 0347 station time, which was the hour Juno Reyes had once told me she never slept through—the hour when the ledgers balanced and the debts came due and the universe, in her words, stopped pretending to be polite.

I was docked at Ceres Verge, third ring, in a berth I'd paid for under a name that didn't exist, running a diagnostic on the *Kestrel's* port thruster assembly because sleep had become a country I could no longer find the border to. The settlement was eleven days behind me. Eleven days of Tam's voice in my head reciting song after song like he was afraid the silence would eat them, of Sefa's hands steady on the vault door, of the geothermal steam rising off the ice in long white banners that looked, from orbit, like the moon exhaling.

My receiver chimed once. Then again. Then it did something it had never done in nine years of flying: it chimed a third time, using the emergency cadence Juno and I had agreed on back when we were both younger and more frightened and hadn't yet learned to lie to each other about small things.

I crossed the berth in four strides.

The message was text only. No voice, no face. Juno knew better than to put either on a channel, even a buried one, even now. The words sat on my screen in her flat, unadorned style, the style she used when decoration would have been cruelty:

*Clearance order issued 0200. Meridian-adjacent body, your coordinates from the festival. Papered as pre-emptive demolition—unstable rare-earth shaft, "environmental hazard mitigation." Scheduled execution window opens in nine days. Kessler filed the assessment himself. Freya. Nine days. They're calling it an accident before it happens so no one looks for bodies.*

I read it three times. My hands were very still on the console, the way they go still when the rest of me is screaming, and I remember thinking, with a strange floating clarity: *so this is what it sounds like when the machinery finishes assembling itself.* I'd seen the parts for weeks—Kessler's survey drones circling like patient birds, the new scanners at customs with their emotional-resonance readers, the quiet re-flagging of my registry. I'd told myself the noose was loose. I'd told myself a lot of things.

Nine days.

Three hundred people. A shuttle that carried sixty, maybe eighty if you packed children into the spaces between the seats and lied about the mass tolerances. And a clearance order already signed, already scheduled, already dressed up in the language of safety so that when the ice moon burned, the official record would show Halcyon Salvage Consortium performing a public service.

They weren't even going to pretend it was a battle. That was the part that made my teeth ache. A massacre takes witnesses; an accident takes only paperwork. Kessler had learned something from the first Purge after all—not mercy, but method. Two centuries ago they'd needed a war to hide behind. Now a geological survey report would do.

* * *

I don't remember deciding to run. I remember the diagnostic screen going dark because I killed it mid-cycle, and I remember the berth clamps releasing with a sound like a held breath let go, and then I remember Ceres Verge falling away beneath me, its lights smearing into rivers, and my hands doing all the things hands do without any consultation from the person attached to them.

Juno's voice caught me at the second jump point, audio only, compressed and hissing. "Tell me you got the message."

"I'm already out of the berth."

"Of course you are." A pause. In the static I could hear her breathing, quick and shallow, and underneath it the particular hum of her own ship powering up, which meant she was coming too, which meant we were both being idiots together, which was, historically, how our best work got done. "Freya, listen to me. There's a blockade."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped tool. "A blockade."

"Halcyon-backed. Officially it's a 'customs integrity operation'—they've spun up some story about contraband memory trafficking out of the outer colonies, which is funny, isn't it, given who taught half the smugglers in this system the trade." Her laugh had no floor under it. "Four cutters at the Meridian approach corridor. New scanner arrays, the resonance kind. They're not looking for you specifically. They're looking for *anyone* heading toward that moon, because anyone heading toward that moon is either a witness or a problem."

"Then I'll be both."

"Freya—"

"Don't." I said it quietly, and the quiet did what shouting couldn't; she went silent. I watched the jump gate bloom ahead of me, that slow violet unfolding, and I thought about every rule I had ever written for myself, scratched into the inside of the *Kestrel's* cockpit panel where only I could read them. *Never follow distress calls. Never carry anything you can't afford to lose. Never be visible. Never, never let them know what matters to you.* Nine years of rules. Nine years of staying small enough to slip between the fingers of every hand that reached for me.

And here was the thing I understood then, alone in the dark between gates with Juno's breathing in my ears: the rules had never been keeping me alive. They'd been keeping me *absent*. There was a difference, and I had spent nine years refusing to learn it, and three hundred people on a frozen moon were about to pay the interest on my refusal.

"The archive copies," I said. "How many shards made it out before I left?"

"Two. The ones you ran disguised as entertainment media. The rest are still in the vaults." She paused. "You know what that means."

It means that if the moon burned in nine days, everything except two fragments of ten thousand years of human memory would burn with it. Every song Tam had ever memorized. Every name recited at the festival, the ones spoken in Vellish by old mouths and young ones, the litany that Sefa had told me was the spine of their whole survival—*we keep the names, child, and the names keep us.* Gone. Not even erased, because erasure implies something existed first. Just never admitted into the record at all. The same trick they'd pulled with Meridian Colony itself. The same trick, always, forever, unless someone made it expensive.

"I'm coming through the blockade," I said. "I need you to do something worse."

Juno laughed again, and this time there was a floor under it, a bad one, the kind that gives way. "Worse than running a Halcyon cordon?"

"I need you to get me the clearance order. The actual document. Signatures, timestamps, the geological report they're hiding behind—all of it. If I'm going to warn them, I want them to see the paper. I want Sefa to hold the thing that's killing them in her hands and read it aloud, because—" my voice did something I didn't authorize, cracked down the middle, and I let it, "—because the last time, nobody showed them the paper until afterward. Until there was no afterward left to show it to."

Static. Long enough that I checked the channel twice. Then Juno said, "You know what you're asking. That document sits inside Halcyon's internal mesh. Getting it means touching their systems directly, and if I touch their systems directly with the markers they hold over me—"

"They'll own you completely. I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"You skimmed my runs to pay them," I said, and heard her breath catch, because we had never said it out loud, not once, not since the night she confessed it with her eyes on the deck plates. "You sold pieces of us to buy yourself free of them, and it didn't work, and I forgave you anyway, and do you know why? Because found family doesn't mean loving people who never failed you. It means loving them *after*, with the failure sitting right there on the table between you." I watched the gate swallow me, violet light crawling across the cockpit like water. "Get me the paper, Juno. I'll get them the truth. Between us we might make one whole honest smuggler."

She was quiet a moment longer. Then, dry as dust: "One honest smuggler. The system will collapse."

* * *

The Meridian approach corridor had always been a lonely stretch of nothing—four days of rock and radiation with the dead colony hanging at the end of it like a period at the end of a sentence nobody wanted to finish. Now it had company.

I came out of the last jump with my transponder singing a freight identity I'd bought from a man in the Belt whose forgeries were good enough to survive casual inspection and nothing more, and there they were: four Halcyon cutters arranged in a lazy diamond around the corridor's choke point, hulls the gray-white of surgical steel, running lights strobing the regulation pattern of *authorized inspection zone*. Behind them, mounted on a converted ore hauler, sat the scanner array—a dish the size of a cathedral bell, wrapped in cooling shrouds, drinking the dark.

I throttled down to a crawl and became boring. Boring was a language I spoke fluently. I filed a cargo manifest full of agricultural polymer and medical plastics, dull as dishwater, traceable to a supplier who owed me a favor. I set my cabin temperature to simulate a single crew member's metabolic output. I thought about Tam—his ridiculous, stubborn, hopeful face—and deliberately stopped thinking about him, because the new scanners didn't read thoughts, whatever the sales brochures implied, but they read *emotional resonance* in stored memory, and I had learned at terrible cost what that meant. The decoy cache I'd burned at customs had been my childhood: my grandmother's kitchen, the smell of yeast and engine grease, her voice singing half in trade-tongue and half in Vellish, the language I hadn't known I remembered until a dead signal called it out of me across the dark.

That cache was ash. But other caches lived in my holds, in my own recording cores, in the habit of a lifetime—I recorded everything, that was my sickness and my craft—and somewhere in the *Kestrel's* data lattice sat memories of the settlement itself. Sefa's face. The vault doors. Tam's voice, ten thousand songs deep.

If the array touched those, I was finished, and worse, so were they.

So I did the thing every chapter of caution in my body screamed against. I jettisoned them.

Not physically—that would leave debris, and debris tells stories. I scrubbed them. All of them. Every recording core in my hold that had ever touched the ice moon, every backup, every fragment, wiped with a military-grade overwrite while I sat four hundred kilometers from the cutter line and watched nine months of the most important work of my life dissolve into statistically perfect noise. The dying fisherman's last season—gone; I'd already delivered it, but the copy had been mine, a comfort I played on long nights. Marta Okonkwo's oral histories of Meridian, the ones she'd poured into my cores over six winters of tea and failing memory—gone. I kept the originals' checksums in my head, useless without the data, a museum of doorways leading nowhere.

It felt like cutting off fingers to save the hand. I did it anyway, and I did it fast, and when the last core reported clean I discovered I was crying, which annoyed me, because tears blur vision and I needed to fly.

"*Kestrel*, this is Halcyon inspection vessel *Diligent Order*." The hail came crisp and bored, a voice reading from a script. "You are entering a designated customs integrity zone. Reduce velocity to matching, power down lateral thrusters, and prepare for resonance scan. Noncompliance will be construed as—"

"—evidence of intent, yes, I've read the statute," I said, and put as much weary truck-driver into my voice as it could hold. "Scan away. You'll find polymer pellets and cold medicine and a woman who's been awake too long."

The scan took forty minutes. Forty minutes is a long time to sit still while a machine searches your ship for the shape of your love. I kept my hands folded, my breathing even, my mind fixed on the dullest object I owned—a torque wrench, serial number worn off, inherited from a mechanic on Callisto who'd cheated me at cards and died owing me money. I thought about the torque wrench the way monks think about breath. The scanner washed over the *Kestrel* in slow pulses, and I felt them as a faint pressure behind the eyes, like weather changing, like the moment before a storm decides.

"*Kestrel*, scan complete. Residual resonance within tolerance. Proceed through the corridor at assigned vector. Deviation will trigger secondary inspection."

"Tolerance," I repeated. "That's a kind word for it."

Silence. I flew on.

* * *

The ice moon rose to meet me four hours later, and it looked exactly as it had the day I'd left it—white and scarred and steaming gently at the vents, beautiful in the way of things that don't know they're condemned. But I knew how to look now. I swept the orbital approaches twice and found what I'd feared: survey drones, dozens of them, parked in high sentinel orbits with their sensor suites cold and their transponders dark. Watching. Counting heat signatures. Building the baseline that Kessler's report would later cite—*settlement thermal output consistent with approximately three hundred individuals*—so that afterward, when the questions came, if they ever came, the corporation could produce a tidy number and a tidy explanation and a tidy absence.

I brought the *Kestrel* down through the steam plumes on the far side of the ridge, engines banked low, riding the thermals the way Sefa's people had taught me the vents breathed—in cycles, long inhale, longer exhale—and set down hard on the ice shelf above the settlement with none of my usual care, because care costs seconds and I had eight days and change.

The airlock cycled before I'd finished shutdown. Of course it did. They'd seen me coming; the whole settlement probably had; Tam likely had my descent plotted before I cleared the drones.

Sefa Vell stood in the frost-light, wrapped in her grey elder's coat, and she took one look at my face and did not ask what was wrong. Elders of a hunted people learn that question wastes time.

"Say it," she said.

"Nine days." My voice came out scraped. "Halcyon's issued a clearance order. They're dressing it as a pre-emptive demolition—an unstable mining shaft, environmental hazard. It's already signed, already scheduled. Juno's pulling the document itself; I'll have it to you within two days. But the window opens in nine days, Sefa. After that, they come, and they've built the paperwork so the record will call it an accident."

Behind her, the settlement spread out along the vent lines—three hundred lives in domes and dugouts, smoke rising, someone laughing somewhere, a child's shout carrying thin and bright across the ice. Ordinary evening sounds. The sounds of people who had survived two centuries of erasure by believing the ice would keep hiding them.

Sefa closed her eyes. For three heartbeats she was utterly still, the way the ice is still, the way their whole history had taught them to be still. Then she opened them, and what was in them wasn't despair. It was arithmetic.

"Tam," she said, turning, raising her voice just enough to carry. "Go wake the council. All of them. And send runners to every dome—we gather within the hour."

He was already moving, already halfway to the ladder, and he paused only long enough to look at me—at my red-rimmed eyes, my shaking hands, the wreck of caution I'd become—and nod, once, the way soldiers nod. He'd known. Some part of him had always known; that was why he'd begged me to carry his memories out, why he memorized forward, why his grandfather had dug escape tunnels under the ice for exactly this day.

Sefa turned back to me. Steam curled between us. Somewhere below, the vaults hummed their failing hum, holding ten thousand years of names.

"Nine days," she said. "Then tell me, Freya-of-the-Carrying—how many can you carry?"

And I opened my mouth to give her the honest answer—sixty, eighty, not nearly enough—and found that the honest answer had changed, sometime between the blockade and the landing, between the scrubbed cores and the crying, into a different answer entirely. One I hadn't planned. One that was insane.

"Not enough of us," I said. "Unless we stop trying to carry the archive out—and start throwing it at the sky."
