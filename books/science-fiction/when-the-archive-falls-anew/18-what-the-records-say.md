## 18. What the Records Say

The sirens did not sound like sirens. They sounded like his grandfather's voice.

Tam Vell was in the song-house, third shelf from the ceiling, running his fingers along the spines of the memory cores the way other boys ran their fingers along fence rails, when the settlement's oldest alarm began to sing. It wasn't a klaxon. Sefa had refused klaxons years ago—*we are not a ship, we are a people*—so instead the warning came through every speaker in every building as a recording, made forty years before Tam was born, of old Paavo Vell intoning the names of the Purge dead in a voice like river-stones knocking together. Everyone in the settlement knew what it meant. Everyone under the age of thirty had never heard it for real.

Tam's hands went still on the cores. The names poured out of the speaker, unhurried, patient as snowfall: *Alissa. Alissa. Alissa-ben-Harren. Alissa-ben-Harren-of-the-Deep-Roots.* Three hundred and eleven names, and the alarm would play all of them, and by the time it reached the end, either the danger would have passed or they would all be dead.

He was moving before the second name.

The tunnels were his grandfather's, though his grandfather had been dead nine years and the tunnels had never once been used, and Tam had spent more nights than he would admit crawling them with a hand-lamp, learning every junction, every collapse, every place where the ice wept and the rock breathed. *For the day the sky comes down*, Grandfather Ivo used to say, tapping the tunnel map he kept folded inside his copy of the Law-Songs. *A people that can be found can be erased. A people that can run cannot.* Tam had thought it was just something old men said, the way old men said things. He was sixteen, and the sky was coming down, and his grandfather's voice was in his head so clearly it was almost cruel.

The song-house emptied around him. Elders moved with terrible calm, the calm Tam had watched them practice at every festival, every funeral, every recitation of names—the calm that said *we have survived this before and we will decide now whether we survive it again.* He saw Sefa at the door, and she saw him, and for one moment the whole world narrowed to the look that passed between them.

She didn't tell him to run. She didn't tell him to hide. She looked at the portable cores stacked in his arms—six of them, the ones he'd been cataloguing since dawn, because he always catalogued at dawn, because dawn was when Grandfather Ivo had taught him to catalogue—and she nodded once, the way you nod at someone carrying something that must not be dropped.

Then she turned and walked toward the gate.

* * *

The children were in the school-house, because of course they were, thirty-one of them between the ages of four and eleven, and their teacher, Maren-of-the-Long-Winter, was trying to line them up with the kind of order that shatters the moment you look at it wrong. The little ones were crying. Not all of them—some of the smallest were too young to understand anything except that the adults had gone strange and fast, and strangeness in adults is its own kind of terror—but enough of them that the crying moved through the room like weather.

Tam put down four of his cores and picked up a four-year-old instead.

"Listen," he said, and he used the voice Grandfather Ivo had used on him, the low one, the one that made a game out of gravity. "We're going on a treasure hunt. The oldest treasure hunt. You're going to carry treasure, and if you carry it right, you save everyone."

The boy in his arms—Davi, small and furious and always the first to cry and the last to stop—stopped crying to frown at him. "What treasure?"

"Names," Tam said. "The best treasure there is. Come on."

Maren looked at him like she wanted to argue, and then the ceiling shuddered—far off, deep, the sound of ships entering atmosphere, the sound Tam had heard exactly once before, when a supply freighter came in heavy—and she stopped wanting to argue. She started handing him children.

They went out the school-house's back door, into the blue shadow of the ice ridge, and Tam led them to the seam in the rock that looked like nothing, that had looked like nothing to every survey drone that had ever circled the settlement, because Grandfather Ivo had spent eleven years making it look like nothing—hauling stone, planting lichen, teaching the meltwater to run across the entrance in a curtain so thin and constant that thermal imaging read it as geology. Tam pressed the release his grandfather had built out of salvaged door-servos and said the words under his breath, the old words, the ones that weren't a password exactly, more like a courtesy:

*We are the ones who were waiting.*

The seam opened on darkness and the smell of deep stone.

* * *

The tunnels were not made for children. Tam had known this in the abstract; he knew it now in his shoulders and his knees and the raw skin of his palms, carrying Davi on his back through a crawl-passage that Grandfather Ivo had dug at the exact width of a grown man's shoulders, in the years when his grandfather had still been strong enough to dig. The children came behind him in a rope-line, Maren at the rear, each of the older ones carrying a portable core in a padded sling against their chest like something newborn. Six cores, and then twelve, and then the ones from the secondary vault that Tam had diverted for on the way through the song-house—eighteen cores total, a hundred and forty years of Vellish memory, song and law and recipe and lullaby and the recorded voices of the dead saying their own names so no one could ever again say they had never existed.

The little ones were heavy. The dark was total where the guide-lamps didn't reach. Somewhere far above—muffled by forty meters of ice and rock—the settlement was making whatever sounds settlements make when ships with guns come down out of the sky, and Tam refused to imagine it, and imagined it anyway.

"Tam." That was Ilse, nine, the sharpest of the older children, walking three back with her core hugged tight. "Are the bad people the same ones from the stories?"

Tam kept walking. "What stories?"

"The Purge stories. The ones where the ships come and the soldiers come and the elders stand in the gate and—" She stopped herself, the way children stop themselves when they realize the story they've been reciting has become the room they're standing in.

"Yes," Tam said. "The same ones. And you know how the stories end."

"They don't end," Ilse said. "Grandmother says the stories don't end, they just get carried."

"Then carry," Tam said, and heard his own voice crack, and was grateful the dark hid his face.

They came to the first junction, where the tunnel forked toward the old geothermal gallery and the deep escape route, and Tam did what he had done a hundred times in practice and never once under this sky: he checked the marks. Grandfather Ivo had carved them into the rock in a cipher of his own devising—little notches that meant *air is good here*, *the ice is thick here*, *this way to the vent-field*, *this way out*. Tam read them by lamplight the way he read the old script, fluently, the inheritance settling into his hands like a tool that had always been waiting for him to pick it up.

*This way out.*

* * *

Above them—Tam would learn this later, would piece it together from the drones' own recordings after everything, after the tribunal, after the glass-doored vault on the station the survivors were granted—Sefa Vell walked to the settlement gate alone.

She was seventy-three years old. She walked slowly, because her knees were bad and because she was not afraid, and those are different reasons that look the same from a distance. She wore the festival shawl, the deep-blue one, the one woven with three hundred and eleven white threads for the dead of the Purge, and she carried nothing in her hands. The Halcyon security drop-ships sat on the ice plain beyond the outer marker-stones like beetles, black and jointed and glistening, and between them and the gate stood the people of the settlement—two hundred and eighty-nine of them, every one not underground, every one who had voted that morning, in the terrible arithmetic session that Sefa had run with a voice that never shook, to stand.

The lead security officer—a man in gray composite, visor down, voice flattened by his helmet into something with no age and no home—made the announcement the charter required him to make. *This land is subject to a lawful salvage assessment under Halcyon charter clause nine. All persons present are ordered to vacate. Non-compliance voids protections under the colonial extinction statutes.*

Sefa listened to all of it. She waited for the echo to die off the ice.

Then she spoke in Vellish.

She spoke the elders' refusal—the old refusal, the one from the Purge, the words that had been recorded in a dying woman's whisper two hundred years ago and passed down mouth to mouth, vault to vault, until they arrived here, on this ice, on this morning: *This ground is our mother's tongue made solid. You may break it, but you cannot translate it. We are the ones who were waiting, and we are still here, and we will say our names until there is no one left to say them, and then the stones will say them, because we have taught the stones.*

She said it once in Vellish, and once in trade-tongue, so there could be no argument later about what had been said and what it meant. Every drone above the settlement recorded it. Every camera in every gray helmet recorded it. The relay that two smugglers had hijacked in the cold dark overhead was, at that moment, carrying it to every receiver in the system, though Sefa did not know that yet, and would not know it until the first screams came.

She finished. She folded her hands. And she waited, as her grandmother's grandmother had waited, for the ships to answer.

* * *

In the tunnels, the air went warm.

Tam felt it before he understood it—the deep galleries breathed with the geothermal vents, and the breath had changed, which meant the vents had changed, which meant either the mountain was restless or something above was shaking it. Dust sifted from the ceiling in fine gray threads. Davi, on his back, had gone very quiet in the way children go quiet when they have passed beyond crying into something else.

"Sing," Tam said to the line behind him. His voice came out rough. "Quiet singing. The walking song."

And they did. That was the thing he would remember, later, when he could bear to remember—the thing that would become the first entry in the new vault, recorded in his own voice with his own hands shaking: the children sang. Maren started it, low, the walking song their grandmothers used on the long treks between the old settlements in the years after the Purge, the song about putting one foot and then the other foot and carrying the names, and one by one the children joined, and the little ones who didn't know the words hummed, and the sound moved down the tunnel ahead of them like a lamp.

Eighteen memory cores, thirty-one children, one teacher, one boy who had memorized ten thousand songs and never once believed he would need them for anything except leaving.

The tunnel sloped down, and then along, and then—according to the marks, according to his grandfather's nine years of digging—up, toward the vent-field on the far side of the ridge, where the ice thinned and a shuttle that could not possibly hold everyone might, if the arithmetic broke mercifully, hold thirty-one children and a hundred and forty years of a people's soul.

Tam walked. The names came out of the cores against the children's chests, silent, patient, waiting. Above them the sky was answering Sefa in the only language Halcyon had ever learned.

He did not look back. His grandfather had told him once, in the tunnels, in the lamplight, in the ordinary years when this was all a game: *When the day comes, don't look back. Looking back is how the dead get heavy. You carry them forward instead.*

"Almost there," Tam said, and the children sang, and the mountain breathed, and behind them, far away and getting farther, the settlement gate stood open under a sky full of ships.

Then the first explosion came down through the rock like a fist, and the singing stopped, and Tam started to run.
