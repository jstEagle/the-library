## 3. What the Vault Remembers

The signal had not stopped.

That was the first thing I understood when I woke in the caretaker's cot at 0412 vault time, staring at the ceiling's water-stained panels as if they might explain it to me. Bursts came every eleven hours and forty minutes — I'd timed them through the night without meaning to, my body keeping count the way it kept count of coolant cycles and germination alarms. Eleven hours and forty minutes. Not a distress pattern. Distress patterns were frantic, irregular, human. This was a metronome. A heartbeat with a schedule.

I made tea that tasted like the inside of a filter cartridge and carried it down into Stratum Two, where the communications array lived among the climate servers like an organ transplanted into the wrong body. The waveform still hung on my display, patient as scripture. Old Meridian. My grandmother's voice saying *sleep now, little seed, the dark is only soil* — except this was no lullaby. It was arithmetic dressed in a dead language, and someone, somewhere, was still doing the sums.

"Okay," I said to the empty room, because talking to myself had long since replaced talking to anyone else, and because the vault had taught me that things left unspoken tended to rot. "Let's find out who you are."

The archive was organized the way everything on Meridian was organized: by stratum, by era, by how much embarrassment each layer would cause if it were ever opened. That last criterion wasn't official. Officially, the sealed strata were preserved for *historical integrity* — records too fragile or too format-obsolete for routine access. Unofficially, every caretaker before me had learned the same lesson I had: the deeper you dug, the more carefully someone had buried the shovel.

Strata One through Four were open to me. Fleet dispatches, dispersal manifests, agronomy reports, the cheerful propaganda of the First Dispersal — humanity flinging itself outward in a hundred colony ships while Earth's governments congratulated themselves on their generosity. Strata Five and below required authorization codes from an Office of Archive Integrity that had been defunct for a hundred and sixty years. The codes were supposed to have died with the office.

They hadn't. They lived in a leather-bound ledger in Stratum One, in a drawer labeled HYDROLOGY — LEGACY INSTRUMENTATION, because no one audits hydrology. Caretaker Osei had shown me the trick my first year, with the conspiratorial grin of a man passing on contraband. "The fleet forgets we exist," he'd said, "so we make sure we can remember everything." He'd been dead nine years now, and his grin lived in my memory like a pressed flower.

I keyed in the legacy codes. The locks cycled with a sound like a throat clearing after two centuries of silence.

* * *

Stratum Six smelled different from the upper levels. That sounds like superstition, but it isn't — sealed atmospheres develop their own chemistry, their own weather. The upper strata smelled of ozone and desiccant and my own sweat. Stratum Six smelled of paper that had been important once, and of the faint vinegar sweetness of degrading magnetic tape, which is the smell of memory dying slowly and on purpose.

The retrieval system here was older than anything else in the vault. Physical media. Tape spools, crystal matrices, even — I am not exaggerating — microetched nickel discs, the kind used in the dispersal era because they could survive a hundred thousand years and because the people making them believed the records would matter for that long. Someone had catalogued all of it by hand. Someone had cared.

I started with the dispersal-era ship registries, because the signal had come from deep space and deep space meant a ship, and ships meant registries. The registry for Dispersal Wave Three filled four nickel discs. I loaded them one at a time into the ancient reader, watching entries scroll past in typography that predated my grandmother: hull numbers, crew complements, destination vectors, launch dates.

*Meridian's Daughter.* Lost — reactor failure, year twelve.
*Song of the Lowlands.* Lost — navigational error, year nineteen.
*Cormorant.* Lost — contact ceased, year twenty-three.

Lost, lost, lost. The word repeated down the columns with a regularity that should have comforted me and instead began to itch. I told myself it was grief. Two centuries of grief, compressed into a database field. Of course there were losses. Space is a knife; everyone knew the odds when they boarded. My own ancestors had rolled those dice.

But then I reached the H-section, and the itch became something with teeth.

*Halcyon Reach.*

Hull number WR-3317. Colony-class, Wave Three. Complement: two thousand one hundred and six souls, including cryostasis. Destination: the Gliese corridor, the long slow haul toward the settlement prospects beyond the Breakaway Reach. Launch date recorded. Crew manifest attached — all two thousand names, digitized with loving thoroughness.

And then the status line.

**Status: LOST WITH ALL HANDS. Year 9 post-launch. Cause: catastrophic hull breach, presumed. Confirmation: NONE. Source: FLEET ADJUDICATION 3317-A.**

Presumed. I read the word three times. *Presumed* is a word you use when you don't know. But the entry didn't read like not-knowing. It read like a form filled out by someone in a hurry to be done with it. Every other lost ship in the registry had debris fields logged, last-transmission timestamps, search duration, the name of the officer who signed off on the loss determination. Halcyon Reach had none of that. No debris. No final transmission. No search. Just the adjudication code and the word LOST, stamped down over two thousand lives like a lid over a well.

I pulled the adjudication record itself. It took an hour — the cross-reference led me down into Stratum Seven, into a subdirectory whose access log showed exactly one previous query, dated ninety-one years ago, by caretaker ID OSEI-M.

Hello, old friend, I thought. You saw this too.

The adjudication was four paragraphs long. Four paragraphs to bury a colony ship. It cited "anomalous telemetry consistent with hull failure," which is bureaucratic language for *we saw something and decided not to look closer*. It cited "resource constraints precluding recovery operations." And then it did something I had never seen in any fleet document, and I had spent thirty years reading fleet documents the way other people read novels:

It closed the file. Permanently. Sealed under Archive Integrity authority, classification level that outstripped the adjudicating officer's own rank. The order to seal it came from above him. From far above him.

You don't seal a tragedy. Tragedies get investigations, memorials, inquiries that run for decades. You seal a decision.

* * *

I worked through what remained of the night cycle, and most of the next one, and I want to be honest about what that felt like, because the logs will outlive me and I refuse to lie to them. It felt like the floor of my life tilting. I had built thirty years of meaning on top of a simple architecture: the world above had stopped listening, and my job was to keep faith anyway. Keep the seeds viable. Keep the records intact. Keep the logs recording. Devotion without audience is still devotion. That belief had held up my whole adult life the way load-bearing walls hold up a house.

Now I stood in the basement of that house, holding a cracked foundation stone in both hands, and the house swayed.

Because here is what I found next, and I found it fast, once I knew to look. The Halcyon Reach's telemetry — the "anomalous readings" the adjudication waved away — existed in raw form in Stratum Seven. I pulled the sensor logs from the relay buoys along the Gliese corridor, the ones that had tracked the ship's first nine years. And the anomaly wasn't ambiguous. It wasn't noise. It was a secondary transmission source, moving on a parallel vector to the Reach's last known position, broadcasting on a frequency band that matched — I checked four times, hands shaking, tea gone cold beside the reader — the frequency band of the signal that had arrived in my array two nights ago.

Two hundred years ago, something survived out there. Something kept transmitting. And the fleet's own instruments heard it, wrote it down, filed it under ANOMALOUS, and helped stamp the word LOST over the whole affair.

I sat back in the reader's chair, which groaned under me like it disapproved, and I made myself think it plainly, because plainness is a kind of armor:

The fleet declared the Halcyon Reach lost. The fleet had evidence it wasn't. The fleet sealed the evidence. And now the Halcyon Reach — or its children, or its children's children — was calling out in a language the fleet had let die, using words my grandmother sang to me, asking for help by a name I hadn't decoded yet.

No. Stop. I caught myself there, in the log's honest light. I hadn't decoded the full message yet. I was ahead of my own evidence, running on dread instead of data, and dread makes a poor archivist. What I actually had was: a lost ship, a too-clean loss record, a sealed adjudication, a two-century-old transmission matching a two-day-old one. Correlation. Not yet confession.

So I did what archivists do when the ground moves. I went looking for bedrock.

* * *

Bedrock, in the Meridian Vault, meant the physical strata — the actual objects, not the digital ghosts of them. Stratum Eight was the deepest level I could reach with legacy codes, and it was mostly launch-era hardware: guidance systems, sample containers, the ceremonial hull plate from the first dispersal ship, donated back to Earth like a relic. And in the corner, in a case marked DISPOSAL PENDING — a label that had apparently been pending for two hundred years — a crate of personal effects recovered from decommissioned colony vessels.

I wasn't looking for effects. I was looking for paperwork. Ships carry paperwork the way bodies carry fingerprints: maintenance rosters, provisioning tallies, crew assignment orders. And decommissioning paperwork, if any of these ships had been decommissioned rather than launched, would tell me what a *normal* end looked like — so I could measure the Halcyon Reach's end against it.

The crate gave me better than paperwork. It gave me a person.

Her name was Adaeze Okonkwo-Ferran, and she had been the chief archivist aboard the colony ship *Second Harvest*, decommissioned intact in year thirty-one when its destination colony proved viable and the ship was retired to orbital salvage. Her effects had been boxed for return to family, and the family, presumably, had never claimed them, because the family was probably on Gliese or dead or both. Among her belongings was her professional journal — an actual paper journal, because dispersal-era archivists trusted paper the way I trusted seeds — and in it, in a small precise hand, was a passage I have now read perhaps forty times.

Dated year nine, post-launch of Wave Three:

*"Word reaches us via relay gossip that the Reach has gone silent and the fleet has written her off. I don't believe it. I served with three of her nav officers; they were the best in the wave. More to the point, the silence is wrong. Silence is static and missed check-ins. This is a clean absence, like a page cut from a ledger. I have asked the fleet archive for her telemetry packet and been refused twice, politely, which is worse than rudely. When an archive refuses you politely, it is protecting something. I intend to note this here and let the paper do the remembering, since the fleet has declined the job."*

She'd seen it. Two hundred years ago, a woman on a retiring colony ship had looked at the same clean absence I was looking at, and named it exactly — *a page cut from a ledger* — and put it in paper because she already distrusted the fleet's digital memory. Her journal had sat in a disposal-pending crate ever since, patient as a seed, waiting for someone with a reason to dig.

I photographed every page with the vault's imaging rig, then sat for a long time in the cold of Stratum Eight, listening to the building breathe around me — the coolant pumps, the air handlers, the vast slow metabolism of a place built to outlast neglect.

Adaeze Okonkwo-Ferran had let the paper do the remembering. Now the remembering needed a courier.

* * *

Here is the shape of what I know, laid out as cleanly as I can manage, because the next log entry may need to be more than a monologue, and clarity is a gift you give your future self:

One: the colony ship Halcyon Reach, carrying two thousand one hundred and six people, was declared lost in year nine of its voyage on the basis of an adjudication that cites no wreckage, no final transmission, and no search.

Two: the same sensor network that failed to confirm the loss recorded a surviving transmission source on the Reach's vector — a fact the adjudication acknowledged as "anomalous telemetry" and then buried under a seal order from the highest levels of fleet command.

Three: the seal order's classification exceeded the adjudicator's authority, meaning the decision to lose the Reach was made above him, deliberately, by people whose names the redactions have eaten.

Four: a contemporary archivist independently recognized the loss record as falsified and documented her suspicion in a medium the fleet couldn't edit.

Five: two days ago, a transmission in Old Meridian — extinct, untaught, unrecorded in any living voice except mine and my grandmother's — began arriving at this vault on a schedule, in a format, on a frequency consistent with the Reach's surviving telemetry.

I am a caretaker. Caring for things is the whole of my training: keep them alive, keep them true, keep them safe. And every instinct I have is now screaming that the thing in my care most in danger of dying is not the seeds, not the tapes, not the building. It is the truth about what happened to the Halcyon Reach, and it has been dying slowly for two hundred years in a sealed stratum, and someone — some office, some lineage of offices — wanted it dead enough to kill the language that could have exposed it.

Because that's the part that turns unease into something colder. Old Meridian didn't fade. Languages don't just die; they're allowed to. And the fleet's education reforms in the century after the Dispersal — I've read the circulars — phased it out of every curriculum with remarkable efficiency, always for good administrative reasons, always with a gentle note that the old tongue was "of purely historical interest." A language of purely historical interest. Spoken by the founding generation. Carried on every colony ship of Wave Three.

If the Reach survived, its people spoke Old Meridian. Their descendants would speak it still. Which means the surest way to keep a lost ship lost was to make sure no one alive could understand it if it called home.

Someone is calling home. In the one language the fleet made sure we'd forgotten.

Eleven hours and forty minutes until the next burst. I'm going to be ready for it — and this time, I'm going to have my grandmother's recordings loaded, every lullaby, every counting song, every word she left me. If the Halcyon Reach is speaking, then I am, as of tonight, the only translator it has.

The vault remembers. That was always the job.

I just never imagined it would remember a crime.
