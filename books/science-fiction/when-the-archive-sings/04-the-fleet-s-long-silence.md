## 4. The Fleet's Long Silence

The Vigilant did not sleep, but Ilsa Reyes had learned to pretend that she did.

She stood on the command deck at what the ship's clocks still insisted was 0300, though out here past the last navigational buoys of the Meridian Reach there was no morning to be early for and no night to be late to. The lights were dimmed to their standby amber, the way they always were during the quiet watch, and the deck hummed with the low, patient sound of a warship doing its best impression of a monument.

Nineteen days. The number sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed.

"Status, Lieutenant Okafor."

Okafor didn't look up from his console. He'd stopped looking up weeks ago, which Reyes had decided was either a sign of trust or a sign of surrender, and she hadn't yet worked out which. "All stations nominal, Commander. Breakaway flotilla holding formation at grid seven-ten. No course changes in the last six hours. No emissions beyond standard telemetry." A pause, the kind he'd learned to insert because she hated being handed good news too quickly, as if it might be hiding something. "They're as bored as we are."

"Bored is fine," Reyes said. "Bored is the whole point."

She crossed to the forward viewport, hands clasped behind her back because if she let them hang loose someone would notice them shaking, and looked out at the dark where the enemy — no, not the enemy anymore, the *other side*, the phrase they'd all been drilled into using, *the other side* — hung in its patient constellation of running lights.

Forty-one ships. She could have named every one of them. The Breakaway flotilla had been the fleet once, before the schism, before the Founders' Quarrel curdled from argument into embargo and embargo into the two years of shooting that everyone now pretended had been an aberration rather than an inheritance. Forty-one ships crewed by people who spoke the same language, ate the same reconstituted rations, sang the same half-remembered songs from Earth-that-was, and who would, in nineteen days, go back to killing each other unless something changed.

And nothing was going to change. That was the part nobody said out loud.

The truce had been negotiated eleven months ago aboard this very deck, brokered by envoys who had since rotated home to safer postings, signed by officers who had since been promoted or retired or buried. It had a term — one standard year — and an expiration, and no successor agreement, because the negotiations for one had collapsed in the third month over water rights at Ceres Annex, of all the stupid, human, unbearably small things. Water rights. Two fleets with fusion torches and rail batteries, deadlocked over ice.

"Commander?" Ensign Vashti, at the signals station, her voice carefully neutral in the way junior officers' voices went when they were about to deliver news they'd already decided how you'd feel about it.

"Go ahead."

"Priority packet from Fleet Command, routed through Relay Kestrel. Marked eyes-only."

Of course it was. Everything from Fleet Command was eyes-only now, as though secrecy itself were a strategy, as though the war could be won by making sure no one ever quite knew what anyone else intended. Reyes held out her hand. "Print it. Hard copy only."

Vashti blinked. "Ma'am?"

"You heard me."

The printer chattered in the alcove off the command deck, and Reyes read the message alone, standing under the single white lamp, while the amber light of the deck washed over her shoulders like something warm she wasn't allowed to keep.

It was exactly what she expected, which was somehow worse than a surprise. Fleet Command, in the person of Admiral Sorensen, whose voice she could hear through the flat officialese the way you can hear a face through a door, wished to inform her that contingency planning for resumption of hostilities was to proceed on accelerated schedule. That the Vigilant, as flagship, would be expected to demonstrate readiness posture Delta-Nine by day minus ten. That munitions transfers from the tender *Anvil of Peace* — a name that made Reyes want to put her fist through a bulkhead every time she saw it — were authorized effective immediately. And that, in a paragraph written in the particular passive voice that command used when it wanted to give an order without technically giving one, *it would be advantageous* if the flotilla could be maneuvered into a position from which any incident, should one occur, could be attributed to Breakaway provocation.

*Should one occur.* As though incidents were weather. As though incidents weren't manufactured these days the way everything else was manufactured: deliberately, on schedule, by people with quotas.

Reyes folded the printout along its creases until it was a small hard square, and stood holding it, and thought about throwing it away, and did not throw it away, because she was Commander Ilsa Reyes and she had never thrown anything away in her life, and that, she was beginning to suspect, was going to be the death of her.

* * *

Her cabin was four meters by three, which was generous for a flagship and still felt like a drawer she'd been slid into. She kept almost nothing in it. A photograph of her father's orchard on Thessaly Station, the trees long since cut down for reactor shielding. A brass compass that pointed nowhere useful anymore but had belonged to her grandmother and weighed exactly enough to feel like something. And on the shelf above her bunk, a battered audio player, because music was the one luxury the fleet couldn't confiscate and the one thing that still reached the part of her that the rank had built over like scar tissue.

She played the old recordings while she took off her boots. Her grandmother's voice, thin and bright through two generations of copying, singing in Old Meridian, the dispersal tongue, the language the first colony fleets had carried out from Earth in their holds like seeds. *Fadhe su-lira, fadhe su-vane* — sleep, little boat, the water is wide. Reyes didn't speak it. Almost nobody did. It had died the way languages die, not all at once but by degrees, each generation keeping less, until what remained was lullabies and launch blessings and the names of stars that no longer matched the stars.

She listened anyway. Not for meaning. For the sound of a time when leaving had still meant hope.

The chime came at 0412, which meant it was important, because her standing order was that nothing short of hull breach disturbed her before 0500.

"Reyes."

Captain Ansel Duval's face filled her cabin screen, gray-eyed, gray-tempered, ten years her senior and aging in dog years out here. "You're awake."

"I'm always awake. That's the job."

"The job," Duval said, "is about to get worse. We've got movement in the flotilla. Three ships breaking formation, heading spinward toward the Kestrel relay corridor. Transponders cold."

Reyes was already pulling her jacket on. "Identify them."

"That's the interesting part. Best guess from the profile match, it's the *Meridian's Daughter*, the *Cormorant*, and a tug we think is the *Old Harbor*. Supply ships, Ilsa. Ice haulers and grain carriers. They're not running a raid."

"Then what are they running?"

Duval's silence lasted three seconds, which from him was a speech. "That's what I want you to come tell me."

* * *

The tactical room smelled of recycled air and old coffee, and it was full at 0430 in a way it hadn't been full in months, because boredom was a solvent and the rumor of movement dissolved it instantly. Duval stood at the plot table with his arms folded. Around the table, the night watch crowded shoulder to shoulder with the early shift, and everyone talked at once until Duval raised one finger and the room shut up like a valve closing.

"Walk me through it," Reyes said.

Lieutenant Okafor ran the playback. Three contacts detaching from the flotilla's main body at 0347, accelerating on a clean, economical burn toward the Kestrel corridor — the stretch of empty space between the two fleets where the fleet's deep-space relay stations hung like lonely church bells. Course projection put them passing within forty thousand kilometers of Relay Kestrel itself.

"Not an attack vector," Okafor said. "If they wanted the relay, they'd come at it from the shadow side. This is a transit. They're just... going somewhere."

"Supply run," said Commander Brekke from Logistics, around a mouthful of stimulant gum. "The flotilla's been rationing heavy for two months. Their hydroponics are failing — we've got the thermal signatures to prove it. They're hauling ice and seed stock from the outer depots. It's legal under the truce. Barely."

"It's legal," Duval agreed, "if they file a flight plan. They haven't filed one."

"They haven't filed one," Reyes said slowly, "because filing one means telling us where their outer depots are. Which means telling us where they're weak." She looked at the plot, at the three small lights crawling spinward, and felt the dread arrive ahead of the analysis, the way it always did, like weather from a horizon she couldn't see. "How close do they pass to Kestrel?"

"Thirty-eight thousand klicks at closest approach. Well outside weapons envelope. Well inside sensor resolution."

"So we'll see exactly what they're carrying."

"We'll see exactly what they're carrying," Duval repeated, "and then we'll have to decide what to do about it, and whatever we decide, Sorensen will second-guess, and the flotilla will call it harassment, and nineteen days will become eighteen." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, and for a moment he looked less like a captain than like a man holding up a ceiling. "Ilsa, I've been doing this for thirty years. I know a spark when I see one. Three unregistered ships drifting through the corridor between two armed camps, with both sides' trigger fingers already cramped? That's a spark. Somebody's going to strike it, and it doesn't even matter who."

"Then we don't strike it," Reyes said. "We shadow them at extreme range, we broadcast our identification openly so there's no ambiguity, and we let them pass. If they're hauling ice, we let them haul ice."

"And if they're not hauling ice?"

"Then we'll know that too. But we'll know it with our safeties on."

Duval studied her for a long moment. There was something in his face she couldn't parse — not disagreement, exactly. More like a man watching someone walk a plank and trying to decide whether to warn them about the water. "Your call, Commander. You have the watch. Keep it boring."

"Boring is the whole point, sir."

He almost smiled. It didn't survive contact with his face. "Dismissed. Except you, Reyes. Stay."

* * *

When the room emptied, Duval killed the overheads except the plot light, and the three moving contacts glowed above them like distant windows in a house where someone was still awake.

"I'm going to tell you something," he said, "and then I'm going to deny telling you, and you're going to be smart enough to let me."

"Sir—"

"Sorensen's staff sent a follow-up packet an hour ago. Not to me. To the tactical intelligence cell directly, bypassing my chain." He tapped the table edge, once, twice. "Classified above my clearance, which is a sentence I did not expect to say about my own flagship's intelligence cell. Whatever's in it, they don't want the captain reading it. They especially don't want the commander of the watch reading it. And they routed it through Kestrel."

Reyes felt the stone in her chest shift. "Kestrel. The same relay those ships are drifting toward."

"I noticed that too."

"Coincidence?"

"At our level, Ilsa, there are no coincidences. There's only information we have and information other people have decided we shouldn't." He straightened, and the captain came back into his face like a mask sliding down. "Shadow the supply ships. Keep your safeties on. Log everything — everything — under your personal command seal, not the ship's archive. Your seal. Understood?"

"Understood." She hesitated at the door. "Sir. Nineteen days."

"Nineteen days," Duval agreed. "Get some sleep, Commander. While sleeping is still something people do."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She went back to the command deck, because the command deck was the only place on the ship where her restlessness had a shape, and she stood the watch she didn't need to stand, drinking coffee that tasted like hot metal, watching three small lights crawl across the dark.

The watch turned over. The amber lights cycled through their slow artificial dawn. Okafor brought her updates she didn't ask for: the supply ships holding course, transponders still cold, emissions consistent with ice haulage — compressor cycling, ballast pumps, nothing that could be mistaken for a weapon unless you wanted very badly to mistake it.

At 0917, Ensign Vashti turned from the signals station with an expression Reyes had learned to read the way sailors read cloud banks.

"Commander? We're getting something off Kestrel. A transmission burst." She frowned at her board. "It's flagged as corrupted. Partial packet loss, bad checksum, the whole thing's basically noise. But the routing header—" She stopped, checked it again, as if the board might have changed its mind. "The header's in an old cipher format, ma'am. Pre-schism. Fleet archives list it as obsolete. Nobody's used this protocol in two hundred years."

Reyes set down her coffee. Somewhere below decks, her grandmother's recording was still queued on the player, waiting: *fadhe su-lira, fadhe su-vane.* Sleep, little boat.

"Where's it originating?" she asked.

Vashti's fingers moved. Stopped. Moved again.

"That's the strange part, Commander. Kestrel says it's not coming from the flotilla at all." She looked up, and in the amber light her face was very young. "It's coming from inside the Meridian Reach. From the direction of the old seed vault network. Coordinates match Meridian Prime."

The vault. The buried place, the silent place, the place every chart marked with a symbol meaning *inactive* — inactive for longer than anyone aboard had been alive.

"Play it," Reyes said.

Vashti keyed the audio, and the corrupted burst filled the command deck — static, fragments, a rhythm underneath the noise like a heartbeat or a song — and Ilsa Reyes, who did not speak Old Meridian, who had never spoken it, who had only ever heard it sung, felt the hair rise on her arms, because beneath the corruption, unmistakable, impossible, something in that dead language was saying her name.
