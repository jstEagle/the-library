## 5. A Voice That Shouldn't Exist

The signal came back on the ninth day, and this time it said her name.

Wendell had not slept. She told herself it was the inventory — the vault's atmospheric scrubbers were cycling at sixty percent efficiency and dropping, and someone had to stay awake to hear them fail — but that was a lie she'd been telling herself for three nights now, and the truth was simpler: she was waiting for a dead language to speak again.

She sat in the listening room with her grandmother's lullaby playing through one earpiece and the raw signal running through the other, and she matched them phoneme by phoneme like a woman braiding two strands of one hair. *Ondolei. Ondolei. Sefarai.* Sleep, little one, sleep; the stars are only faraway fires. The signal used the same syllables, but slower, worn, dragged through two centuries of distance until they came out sounding less like a song and more like breathing.

At 0347 vault time, the burst repeated.

She had set the receivers to catch it automatically, but she was there anyway, hands flat on the console, feeling the old machine shudder as it drank the transmission in. The waveform bloomed across her display in amber threads, and threaded through the familiar structure — the same opening cadence, the same lullaby bones — were new fragments. Longer passages. Sentences where before there had been only phrases.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this the way you notice rain starting: distantly, then all at once.

*All right,* she said aloud, to no one, to the room, to whatever was out there past the Kessel drift singing in a tongue that had been buried with the First Dispersal. *All right. Say it again.*

It said it again.

* * *

Decoding went slowly because she refused to let it go fast. Every translation program in the archive could brute-force Old Meridian in minutes — the language had been standardized for colonial administration before the Dispersal, before the fleets, before everything broke — but Wendell found she couldn't bear to let a machine do it. A machine would strip the words down to their informational skeleton and hand her a summary. And this was not information. This was a voice.

So she did it the way her grandmother had taught her, sitting cross-legged on the floor of a habitat module three generations gone, tracing characters in condensation on the viewport glass. She played each fragment against the recordings. She listened for the words that survived unchanged — *water*, *child*, *remember*, *come* — and built outward from them like laying stones across a river.

By dawn of the tenth day she had the first full passage, and she read it eleven times before she let herself believe it.

*To the keeper of seeds. To the one who keeps what was planted. We know your name is written in our books. We ask by the old right: answer us.*

She read it an eleventh time anyway.

*We know your name is written in our books.*

Wendell stood up. Sat down. Stood up again, because sitting felt suddenly like drowning, and walked the length of the listening room with her arms wrapped around herself as though holding her own ribs together. The vault hummed around her, indifferent, its ten thousand seed drawers sleeping in their cold dark, its atmosphere wheezing at sixty-one percent.

They knew her name. Someone two hundred years and half a sector away knew her name, had known it long enough to write it in a book, had kept the book alive through whatever the Halcyon Reach had endured — and the Reach had endured something, because the second passage, decoded an hour later, made that brutally clear.

*Our hull is a wound that will not close. Our gardens grow thin. We have two hundred souls and fewer every season. We were sent out and we were lost, and being lost, we were forgotten, and being forgotten, we learned to call into the dark. The dark never answered. But the archives remember. Answer us, keeper. Answer us before the last of us forgets how to ask.*

Wendell pressed both palms flat against the console, grounding herself in its vibration, and made herself think.

Two hundred souls. The fleet's casualty registries listed the Halcyon Reach at four thousand colonists, lost with all hands during the Second Dispersal convoys — a rounding error in a century of errors, one more ship swallowed by the expansion years. Four thousand people, erased from the ledgers so cleanly that even the archive's own strata showed no inquiry, no search, no salvage claim. She had found that neatness unsettling once. Now it curdled into something worse. Because if two hundred of those four thousand were still alive — still *breathing*, still naming their children in Old Meridian, still keeping books with a caretaker's name written inside —

Then the loss hadn't been a loss. It had been a decision. Or an abandonment dressed up as one. And somewhere in the sealed strata of her own archive, in the records she had sworn to keep and had never fully read, was the reason why.

*Answer us, keeper.*

"I'm answering," she whispered. "I've been answering for forty years and nobody—"

She stopped. Because that wasn't true, was it? Not anymore. Something out there had finally answered *her* — or rather, had been calling her before she ever knew to listen, had carried her name across two centuries of silence like a coin passed hand to hand down a line of the dying. Her grandmother's grandmother might have taught the lullabies to a child who taught them to a child who sang them to grandchildren born in a wounded hull between the stars, and somewhere in that chain, someone had written down the name of the vault, and the name of its keeper, and waited.

Waited for her specifically. For the last caretaker. For the one who keeps what was planted.

Wendell laughed — a short, cracked sound with nothing in it of humor — and wiped her face with her sleeve.

* * *

She spent the middle hours of the day doing what she always did when the world became too large: working with her hands. She checked the moisture seals on drawer section nine. She recalibrated the germination sensors on the heritage wheat. She recorded log entry 4,008 in a voice she was proud to note stayed almost steady:

"Log 4,008. Vault status: declining. Caretaker status: I don't — " A pause. The recorder's level meter trembled with her breath. "Caretaker status: summoned. Receiving a transmission in Old Meridian from the colony ship Halcyon Reach, declared lost 212 years ago. The ship reports survivors. The ship knows my name. End of log."

She almost ended it there. Instead she let the recorder run a moment longer.

"If anyone ever hears these," she said, "I want it noted that I checked the wheat first. Whatever happens next. The wheat comes first. That's the job."

She killed the recording and stood in the quiet, and the quiet felt different now — no longer empty but expectant, like a held breath.

Because here was the thing she kept circling back to, the thought with teeth: the signal had come *to her*. Not broadcast wide. Not relayed through fleet channels or Breakaway buoys or any of the dozen networks that stitched the settled systems together. It had come on a tight, ancient frequency band — one the archive itself still monitored, one that existed in exactly one place in the modern registry: the Meridian Seed Vault's original commissioning codes. Whoever had sent it had reached into a two-hundred-year-old manual and dialed the vault's private number.

Which meant they had the manual. Which meant they had the founding documents, the commissioning records, the full provenance of the vault — records that, according to the official history, had burned with the Reach.

Nothing about this added up, and every way it failed to add up pointed the same direction: toward the fleet, toward the tidy loss report, toward the sealed strata in her own sub-basement that required a caretaker's override to open. She had walked past that door for forty years. She had dusted its frame.

Tonight she was going to open it.

But first — the reply. Because the plea had asked, and she was constitutionally incapable of leaving a plea unanswered; it was the whole architecture of her soul. She spent two hours composing it, cutting it down, cutting it again, until all that remained was this, keyed out in Old Meridian on the transmitter her grandmother had shown her how to use, aimed along the signal's return vector:

*I am Wendell Montrose, caretaker of Meridian. I keep what was planted. I hear you. Tell me what you need, and tell me who left you behind.*

She hesitated over the last clause. It was accusatory. It presumed. It was also, she suspected, the most important question anyone would ask in the next hundred years, and she was done pretending otherwise.

She sent it.

* * *

The acknowledgment came back in under six hours, which meant the Reach was closer than any lost ship had a right to be — close enough that the light-lag barely registered, close enough that Wendell's skin prickled with a new and colder unease. How long had they been drifting in the dark this near to the vault, calling and calling?

The message was short. Two lines, in her grandmother's careful, rounded script of a language.

*We need water, parts, and passage. We need them soon.*

And beneath it, a third line that stopped Wendell's heart mid-beat and left her standing alone in the humming dark of the listening room, staring at the amber waveform as if it might blink first:

*And, keeper — they are coming to make sure we stay lost. We intercepted their orders. You have nineteen days.*

Nineteen days.

Somewhere beyond the vault's walls, past the wheat and the sleeping seeds and the forty years of unanswered logs, a clock had just started running — and Wendell Montrose, who had spent her whole life tending a monument to a world that stopped listening, understood with total clarity that the world was about to start listening whether it wanted to or not.

She turned toward the sub-basement stair, and the sealed door at the bottom of it, and began to walk.
