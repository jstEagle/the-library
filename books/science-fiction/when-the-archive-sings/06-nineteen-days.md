## 6. Nineteen Days

The Vigilant's war room smelled of recycled air and burnt coffee, the way it had for eleven years, the way it would until someone finally won or everyone finally died. Commander Ilsa Reyes stood at the head of the tactical table with her hands flat on its cold surface and watched two fleets pretend they weren't staring at each other across four hundred thousand kilometers of nothing.

"Nineteen days," said Lieutenant Okonkwo, reading the countdown off the main display like a eulogy. "Truce expires at 0600 fleet-standard, day nineteen."

"Thank you, Lieutenant. I can count."

She hadn't meant it to come out sharp. But the room was full of people who could count, and every one of them was doing the same arithmetic she'd been doing since midnight: nineteen days to negotiate what eleven years of posturing hadn't. Nineteen days before the Breakaway flotilla either folded or fought, and before her own fleet — tired, under-supplied, running on pride and inertia — had to decide whether it actually wanted the war it had been rehearsing for a decade.

Nineteen days. She'd slept four hours in the last thirty-six, and the coffee wasn't helping anymore. It never really helped. It just gave her hands something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Status on the Meridian corridor patrols," she said.

"Quiet," Okonkwo said. "Breakaway's pulled their pickets back to the Kestrel line. Nobody's testing anybody."

"Nobody ever tests us when we want them to." She straightened. "Keep the rotation as is. I want eyes on the corridor but I don't want anyone sneezing near the border without my authorization. Dismissed."

The room emptied with the efficient silence of people who knew better than to linger around their commander when she was counting days. Reyes stayed, alone with the holographic map, watching the slow drift of friendly markers and hostile markers and the empty space between them where the shooting would start if the diplomats failed.

Eleven years ago she'd been a lieutenant junior grade on this same ship, back when the truce was new and everyone still believed it was temporary. Back then the Breakaway had been an abstraction — colonists who'd refused the Consolidated Fleet's authority after the Second Charter crisis, a few dozen ships that had sailed east and declared themselves free. Now the abstraction had children born aboard it who'd never seen a Fleet world, and the Fleet had grandchildren who thought the Breakaway were monsters, and both sides had cemeteries.

Her own brother-in-law was in one of them. Marcus. Killed in the skirmish at Halcyon Reach — no. Not Halcyon Reach. The name snagged in her mind like a burr, and she stood there for a moment trying to work it loose. There was something about that name lately. Something in the intelligence digests.

She couldn't place it. She was too tired to place it. She logged off the table and went to her cabin, and slept badly, dreaming of corridors full of water.

* * *

The signal came up from the signals intelligence section two days later, in a folder marked with a classification level that made her eyebrows rise.

"PRIORITY INTERCEPT — EYES ONLY, COMMAND STAFF," read the header. "Origin: outer Meridian system. Nature: narrowband burst transmission, repeating pattern, non-Fleet encryption schema. Assessment: possible Breakaway communications test or probe. Recommendation: no action. Flagged for burial per Directive 9-C."

Burial. That word again. She'd been seeing it more often in the last year — 9-C was the standing order that let Fleet Intelligence classify anything inconvenient into a black box rather than a briefing. Officially it existed to prevent panic. Unofficially, she suspected, it existed to prevent thinking.

She opened the file anyway. Command staff access meant she could open anything, even things flagged for burial; the flag only stopped the file from being *distributed*, not from being *seen*. It was one of those legal fictions the Fleet ran on, like calling the truce a "temporary cessation of hostilities" when everyone knew it was a held breath.

The intercept data filled her screen: a waveform, narrowband, repeating on a ninety-one-minute cycle. Origin triangulated to somewhere in the outer Meridian system — deep space, past the old seed vault network, out where nothing lived except automated systems and dust. The signal wasn't Fleet standard. It wasn't Breakaway standard either. The cryptographers' preliminary analysis called the encoding structure "archaic, possibly pre-Dispersal," which was intelligence-section language for "we have no idea."

And there — buried in the metadata, in a note some analyst had appended and then apparently regretted — was the phrase that had been snagging at her for two days:

*"Pattern match confidence 0.83 against archived transmissions associated with the colony vessel Halcyon Reach (declared lost, First Dispersal era). Analyst recommends verification. Verification request denied per Directive 9-C."*

Reyes sat very still in her cabin, the coffee going cold in her hand.

Halcyon Reach. The lost colony ship. Two hundred years gone, one of the great tragedies of the First Dispersal — the ship that had carried three hundred founding families out beyond the Meridian line and never reported in, the ship whose loss had been the wound the whole eastern diaspora never stopped picking at. The Breakaway's founding mythology was built partly on that loss: *the Fleet sent our grandparents into the dark and didn't even look for them.* Every Breakaway schoolchild learned the names of the Reach's families the way Fleet children learned the Charter.

And now something out past the seed vaults was broadcasting in a pattern that matched the Reach's transmissions, and Fleet Intelligence had looked at it for exactly long enough to bury it.

She read the note again. *Verification request denied.* Denied by whom? The analyst's ID was stripped from the version she could see, which told her it had been stripped deliberately, which told her someone senior had touched this file, which told her this wasn't routine triage. Routine triage didn't deny verification requests. Routine triage didn't care enough.

Reyes set down the coffee. She was, by training and temperament, a woman who believed in institutions. She'd given the Fleet twenty-two years, and the Fleet had given her purpose, structure, a code worth living by. She believed the chain of command was the difference between a military and a mob. She believed most secrets were secrets for good reasons.

But she also believed — had always believed, in the private chapel of herself where no one else was permitted — that an officer's first loyalty was to the truth, because an institution that lied to itself would eventually lie about everything, and then it would deserve to fall.

She didn't know yet whether this was one of those lies. She only knew that a signal matching a dead ship was coming from the direction of the dead ship's grave, and someone above her had decided nobody should look.

*Commander,* she told herself, *you are tired, and you are paranoid, and you have nineteen days of real problems.*

She copied the file anyway.

It took her four minutes. She routed it through a maintenance-log partition, wrapped it in a corrupted sensor archive from a decommissioned frigate, and gave it a filename that looked like debris: *FRG-7712/aux/telemetry-fragment-0043*. Anyone auditing her personal storage would see garbage. Only she would know how to unwrap it. She wasn't sure why she was doing it — that was the honest truth of it, the thing she turned over as she worked. She wasn't disobeying an order, exactly; no order had been given to *her*. She was simply refusing to let a fact be deleted on someone else's authority. Keeping a copy felt less like treason than like holding a door open a crack, in case the building needed evacuating later.

When it was done she sat in the dim of her cabin and listened to the signal itself, converted to audio, played over her cabin speakers at low volume. It wasn't language, or if it was, it was none she knew. It sounded almost like singing — a structured, patient repetition, rising and falling, the way a voice sounds when it has been calling for a very long time and has learned not to expect an answer.

She thought of Marcus again, and of her sister, who had never forgiven the Fleet for writing him off as an acceptable loss. She thought of the Breakaway schoolchildren learning the names of the Reach's dead.

She turned the volume down further, until the singing was barely there, and left it playing while she tried to sleep.

* * *

Three days later she went looking for the analyst.

Not officially. Officially she spent the morning reviewing patrol schedules and signing supply requisitions and sitting through a forty-minute briefing on Breakaway fleet movements that contained no information she didn't already have. But unofficially, commanders had prerogatives, and one of hers was the right to conduct "quality assurance reviews" of subordinate sections, which was a phrase designed to be so boring that no one ever questioned it.

The signals intelligence section occupied a cramped deck two levels below the war room, all hum and flickering displays and analysts who hadn't seen natural light in months. She found the author of the metadata note by cross-referencing shift logs against the file's creation timestamp. Specialist Danae Voss — no relation to anyone important, twenty-six years old, three years in the service, a reputation for being technically brilliant and politically oblivious, which in Reyes's experience was the same thing as being honest.

Voss looked up from her console and went pale in the way junior personnel did when a ship's commander appeared at their elbow unannounced.

"C-Commander. Ma'am. Is there—"

"At ease, Specialist. Walk with me." Reyes nodded toward the corridor, and Voss followed, rigid, into the relative privacy of the passageway outside the section. "I'm conducting a QA review of recent intercept handling. Your file on the Meridian narrowband burst. Tell me about the pattern match."

Voss's mouth opened, closed, opened again. Reyes watched the young woman perform the calculation every junior officer and enlisted specialist performed in moments like this: *how much does she already know, and what happens to me if I say the true thing.*

"The match was point-eight-three confidence against archived Halcyon Reach transmission signatures," Voss said finally. "I requested verification through the historical signals archive. The request came back denied under 9-C. I noted the denial in the file."

"You noted it in a file flagged for burial."

"Yes, ma'am."

"That was either brave or stupid, Specialist."

Voss's chin came up a centimeter. "My instructor at the academy said the record is the last defense. If you write it down properly, it survives even when the decision doesn't make sense."

"And what did the denial say, exactly? Verbatim."

"It didn't say anything, ma'am. That's the thing." Voss glanced down the corridor, then back, voice dropping. "A 9-C denial comes with a justification field. Panic prevention, operational security, source protection — there's always a reason attached. This one came back blank. Just the directive number and a command authentication code. Blank denials mean the person denying it didn't want a reason on the record. Which means—" She stopped herself.

"Say it."

"Which means it isn't panic prevention, ma'am. Panic prevention gets written down. This is concealment. Somebody doesn't want the Reach pattern verified because they already know what it is." Voss's face had gone from pale to flushed, the flush of a person who had just heard themselves say aloud the thing they'd only thought in the dark. "Ma'am, I've run the correlation six times. It's not noise. Something out there is transmitting on a two-hundred-year-old signature, and we're not allowed to say so."

Reyes looked at her for a long moment. Twenty-two years of institutional instinct told her to shut this down — to remind the specialist about the chain of command, about the meaning of a directive, about the nineteen days and the war and the million ways a stray signal could tip everything into fire. That instinct was load-bearing. It had held her up her whole career.

But underneath it, faint and persistent, the singing was still playing. *A voice that has learned not to expect an answer.*

"What's your assessment of the origin point?" she asked.

"Outer Meridian system, ma'am. Past the seed vault network. If the archival coordinates for the Reach's last known heading are accurate, the origin is consistent with the ship's projected drift corridor." Voss hesitated. "Which is to say, ma'am — if the Halcyon Reach survived, that's where it would be."

*If the Halcyon Reach survived.* Five words that rearranged everything. Two centuries of grief, weaponized into the Breakaway's founding grievance. A war's worth of resentment with its root in a grave that might not be a grave.

If the Reach was alive, then the Fleet had spent two hundred years letting the galaxy believe otherwise. And if Fleet Intelligence was burying proof of it *now*, in the last nineteen days of a truce, then someone, somewhere, had decided that the truth was more dangerous than the war.

Or — the colder thought, the one she made herself look at — that the truth might end the war, and someone wanted the war.

"Specialist," Reyes said quietly, "your QA review is complete. Your handling was correct. You'll receive a commendation notation in your file."

Voss blinked. "Ma'am?"

"Don't discuss this conversation. Don't resubmit the verification request. Don't run any further correlations on Fleet systems." She held the young woman's eyes. "There are other archives besides ours. Historical ones. Civilian ones. Some of them are older than the Fleet and answer to no one. Do you understand me?"

Something moved behind Voss's expression — fear, then understanding, then a strange, fierce steadiness. "Yes, ma'am."

"Carry on."

Reyes walked away down the corridor, her boots ringing on the deck plating, and did not let her stride change, did not let her face change, until she was alone in the lift with the doors closed between herself and the rest of the ship.

Then she exhaled, and leaned her forehead briefly against the cool metal wall, and admitted it to the empty car: she had just instructed a subordinate to seek confirmation of a buried signal through unofficial channels. That was not quality assurance. That was the first domino.

* * *

That night she unwrapped the file from its hiding place inside the dead frigate's telemetry and read it again, end to end, the way she'd once read letters from home during her first deployment — hungrily, repeatedly, as though the words might change if she loved them enough.

*Ninety-one-minute cycle. Narrowband. Archaic encoding. Pattern match 0.83. Origin: outer Meridian. Verification denied. Denial justification: blank.*

Outside her viewport, the stars wheeled with the ship's slow rotation, indifferent and ancient. Somewhere out past all of that, past the picket lines and the Kestrel border and the seed vaults falling silent one by one, something old was singing into the dark on a ninety-one-minute heartbeat, asking a question no one was permitted to answer.

Nineteen days until the truce expired. Sixteen, now. And somewhere in the machinery of the Fleet, someone had decided that whatever was singing should stay buried — had decided it firmly enough to leave the justification field blank, because even liars flinch at writing down a lie.

Reyes sat with the copy glowing softly on her private screen, the one piece of evidence in a case that officially did not exist, and understood that she had already chosen. Not consciously, not with a declaration — but chosen nonetheless, the way a river chooses by flowing. When the moment came, and it was coming, she would not be able to stand in front of the truth and pretend she'd never held it.

The question was no longer whether she'd act. The question was what the acting would cost, and who would pay it.

On the screen, the waveform pulsed. Rise and fall. Rise and fall. Patient as tide, stubborn as grief.

She saved it one more time, in one more place, and went to brief the morning watch on patrol rotations, wearing the face of a commander with nothing on her mind but the next nineteen days.
