## 13. Burn the Archive

The vault woke her at 0347 with the sound of engines.

Not the freighter kind. Not Tam's battered hauler coughing its way down from orbit on a courier run. These were military thrusters — the deep, disciplined pulse of a fleet cutter settling onto the landing pad like a predator choosing where to lie down.

Wendell had known they would come back. She'd felt it in the way Auditor Kade's questions had bent, in the last hour of that first visit, away from seeds and toward signals — away from inventory and toward *what else do you have*. She'd spent nine days since then living inside that knowledge, sleeping badly, eating standing up, moving through the stacks with the decoded message folded into a hundred harmless places: tucked into seed-catalog audio files between entries for drought-resistant sorghum and frost-hardy barley, spliced into the calibration tones of the old atmospheric processors, threaded through four thousand log entries like a filament of glass through wet paper.

They could burn it all, she'd told herself. They couldn't find all of it.

But knowing they were coming and hearing them come were different animals. Her hands shook as she dressed in the dark, and she was grateful no one was there to see it. The caretaker did not shake. The caretaker was the last steady thing on a dead world.

She made herself coffee anyway. Ritual mattered. If this was the last morning the vault belonged to her, she would spend it the way she'd spent every other one — tending.

* * *

Kade arrived at the main airlock at 0512 with six soldiers and a data slate held like a warrant.

Wendell met them in the entry hall, beneath the great carved lintel her grandmother's grandmother had helped raise: SEED AND SONG, it read, worn nearly smooth by two centuries of wind. She stood with her hands folded and her spine straight and let them see her waiting.

"Auditor Kade," she said. "You didn't file a visitation notice."

"I'm not here to audit." Kade's voice was flat, professional, and something underneath it was working hard to stay that way. "I'm here under Fleet Emergency Statute Nine. This facility's records are declared a security risk. I have orders to purge all signal-intercept archives, all transmission logs, and all audio recordings generated or stored within the last standard year."

The words landed one by one, like stones dropped down a well.

"Last standard year," Wendell repeated. "That would be every log I've ever recorded here."

"That would be every log you've ever recorded here." Kade wouldn't quite meet her eyes. "Along with the raw signal captures. Everything."

"And the seeds?"

"The seeds stay. The seeds were never the point."

*No,* Wendell thought. *The seeds were never the point. That's exactly what terrifies me.*

"May I see the order?"

Kade handed over the slate. It was genuine — she checked the authentication chain twice, watched the cryptographic seals resolve green, felt the last door in her chest close quietly behind her. Fleet Command itself. Not regional. Not Kade improvising. Someone very high up had looked at the Halcyon Reach's signal and decided that two hundred years of silence was worth preserving by force.

"You decoded it," Kade said quietly. It wasn't a question either. "I've read your intercept logs from my first visit. There are gaps in your access timestamps. Twelve hours of them. You were translating."

"I was listening," Wendell said. "There's a difference. Though I understand the fleet may have forgotten it."

Something flickered across Kade's face — quick, quickly buried. Grief, maybe. Or recognition.

"Wendell." Kade used her first name, and the soldiers glanced at each other. "I've read the historical annexes attached to this order. I know what the Reach carried when it went missing. I know whose families were on it." A pause, controlled. "My orders don't ask me to like any of this. They ask me to do it. You can stand aside, or you can be removed and I'll do it anyway. Those are the options. I'm sorry. I want you to hear that I'm sorry, even if it changes nothing."

"It changes nothing," Wendell agreed. "But I'll remember it."

She turned and walked into the stacks, and because no one stopped her, they followed.

* * *

The purge took three hours.

Wendell sat in the archive core with her coffee going cold and watched soldiers rack out server blades with the careful reverence of people who understood they were destroying something holy and hated themselves a little more with each pull. The signal captures went first — the beautiful, impossible bursts of Old Meridian, gone from primary storage. Then the intercept metadata. Then, blade by blade, the year of her logs: four thousand mornings of talking to a world that never answered.

Soldiers wept doing it. One of them, a young woman with crop-dust still on her boots from some long-ago posting, apologized to Wendell under her breath every time she passed. Wendell nodded to her each time. She cataloged their faces the way she cataloged everything. It was what she was for.

Kade supervised from the doorway, arms crossed, slate in hand, checking items off a list that must have read like a eulogy.

At 0830, the lead technician straightened up. "Core purged, Auditor. Primary, secondary, tertiary storage. Physical media recovered and staged for incineration." He hesitated. "It's done."

"Confirmed," Kade said, and there was no satisfaction anywhere in the word.

Wendell stood. Every eye in the room came to her, though none of them had been ordered to.

"Well," she said. "Thank you for your thoroughness. Before you go — I believe protocol requires the facility caretaker to sign off on the destruction manifest. So the record shows the vault cooperated fully."

Kade frowned. "That's not actually—"

"Humor an old woman, Auditor. Two hundred years of caretakers signed off on everything that happened in this building. I won't be the first who didn't."

It was a small vanity, and Kade allowed it, which told Wendell more about Kade than the whole first visit had. The slate was passed over. Wendell signed with a stylus that trembled only slightly, and while the authentication hash crawled across the screen — eight seconds, she'd timed it during the audit, eight seconds for the fleet's bloated verification handshake — she reached beneath the signing console and pressed the failsafe.

It was a physical switch. Of course it was. The vault's founders had trusted nothing digital, because they had built this place to outlive the people who might betray it. A recessed brass toggle, older than the fleet, older than the war, labeled in Old Meridian characters her grandmother had taught her to read before she could read anything else:

*IF THE SONG MUST DIE, LET IT SING FROM EVERYWHERE.*

The founders' contingency. The network clause. Every seed vault in the Meridian system — forty-one of them, scattered across three worlds and seven moons, most presumed dead, most merely sleeping — linked by a hardline relay lattice nobody had maintained in a century because nobody remembered it existed except the caretakers, and the caretakers had kept faith.

When the toggle engaged, the vault did not resist the purge. It had already let the purge happen. What it did instead was simpler and far worse for everyone involved:

It began to broadcast.

Every log entry. Four thousand of them, mirrored across the relay lattice in the ninety seconds before the soldiers pulled the final blades — Wendell had spent those ninety seconds, during the "sign-off delay," triggering the mirror cascade, and now the lattice carried her voice outward at lightspeed along routes the fleet's own maps no longer showed. Her lonely devotion, her catalogs of dying systems, her 4,000 unanswered mornings. And woven through all of it, unremovable now, replicated across forty-one vaults on three worlds and seven moons, the decoded plea of the Halcyon Reach, asking for help by name.

Asking for help by *her* name.

The console chimed. Broadcast initiated. Relay lattice: 41 nodes responding. 41. All of them. Even the ones officially decommissioned. Even the ones the fleet had bombed.

The young soldier heard it first — a whisper of sound from a wall panel that should have been inert, Wendell's own voice saying *Log 2,847: the eastern coolant loop failed again today, and I fixed it again today, and no one answered* — and looked up in confusion, then dawning horror.

"Auditor," she said. "We're... broadcasting. The whole network is broadcasting. Sir. Ma'am. It's coming from everywhere."

Kade crossed the room in three strides and stared at the console readout, and Wendell watched the color leave her face node by node as the numbers resolved. Forty-one sites. Three worlds. Seven moons. Both fleets' territory. The Breakaway flotilla's listening posts. Open civilian channels. Everything.

"What did you do?" Kade whispered. It was not an auditor's question. It was a human being's question, asked in a room full of soldiers who had just spent three hours destroying copies of a message that now existed in ten thousand places no warrant could reach.

Wendell folded her hands, the way she did before recording a log, and gave the answer to the whole room, because they all deserved to carry it.

"I did my job," she said. "I kept the archive alive. You were two centuries too late to kill it. And now, Auditor — now everyone is listening."

The console chimed again. First response ping: an unidentified vessel, Breakaway transponder codes, requesting full transmission replay.

Then a second ping. Fleet flagship *Vigilant*, priority channel, demanding to know who had authorized the broadcast.

Then a third, and a fourth, and the chimes began to overlap, and somewhere in the stack of them Wendell heard the thing she had waited four thousand logs to hear:

Voices. Answering.

Kade turned to her slowly, and the look on her face was no longer suspicion, and no longer duty, and no longer grief. It was the expression of someone standing on a fault line as the ground begins to move.

"They're going to send ships here," Kade said. "Both fleets. You understand that. Whatever happens next happens *here*."

"Yes," Wendell said. "I'm counting on it."

Outside, above the vault, the sky was beginning to fill.
