## 14. The Network Wakes

The vault had never been loud before.

Wendell stood in the central gallery with her hands pressed flat against the cold of the old failsafe console, listening to the sound she had spent her whole life trying to imagine: her own voice, multiplied. Every seed vault on the network — Meridian, Calloway Deep, the twin silos under the Ashfall Shelf, the drowned archive at Pelagic Nine — was broadcasting her logs in a rolling cascade, each one picking up the stream and hurling it onward on every open channel, every emergency band, every frequency the old caretakers had ever reserved for the end of the world.

Four thousand entries. Four thousand unanswered nights, going out all at once.

She had expected to feel triumphant. What she felt instead was naked. Her voice — young in the early logs, hoarse in the later ones, cracking on entry 3,112 when she'd recorded the death of the last pollinator drone — was everywhere now, uncontainable, undeniable. There was no taking it back. There was no corner of the dark where the fleet could pretend it hadn't heard.

"Entry one," her own voice said from a dozen speakers, overlapping itself out of phase so that it sounded like a chorus, like a congregation. "Day one of my tenure as caretaker of the Meridian Seed Vault. No one is listening to this. I know that. I'm recording it anyway."

*Good,* she thought. *Let them hear how it starts. Let them hear that I knew.*

The signal traffic coming back was immediate and enormous. Fleet pings, Breakaway hails, automated demands for identification, threats wrapped in protocol language. The vault's ancient systems flagged them all and kept broadcasting, patient as a seed.

And threaded through the noise, faint but unmistakable, came something else. A second voice, riding the same channels. Old Meridian, spoken haltingly by someone whose accent was two centuries out of date — the Halcyon Reach, hearing its own history sung back across the dark, answering for the first time in two hundred years.

Wendell closed her eyes and let the dead language and her own living one fill the gallery together.

*They can't unhear this,* she thought. *None of them. Not anymore.*

Then the lights flickered, and somewhere far above the vault's buried heart, she heard the first thunderclap of weapons fire against the outer ice.

* * *

On the bridge of the *Vigilant*, Commander Ilsa Reyes watched the war almost happen in real time.

It began with silence — the worst kind, the kind that meant every officer on the deck was listening to the same broadcast and pretending not to be. Wendell Montrose's voice poured from the comm station, log after log, and Reyes stood at the center of it with her hands clasped behind her back because if she unclasped them they would shake.

"—entry 2,847. Today I found the fleet's original loss report for the Halcyon Reach. It's too clean. Ships don't die cleanly. Someone wrote this report before the ship was ever lost—"

"Commander." Lieutenant Okafor at tactical, his voice carefully flat. "Breakaway flotilla has gone to combat readiness. All batteries. They're demanding we confirm or deny the contents of the transmission."

"Deny what?" said Ensign Brandt, too young, too loud. "It's right there. She has the fleet's own records. She has the Reach's coordinates. We can hear them, sir. We can hear them *talking.*"

Reyes turned. "Mister Brandt."

He shut his mouth, but the damage was done; everyone on the bridge had already heard the Reach's voice, that impossible antique cadence, and no order could take it back out of their ears. That was the genius of what the caretaker had done, Reyes realized. You couldn't bury a broadcast. You couldn't court-martial a frequency. Every crew member on both sides of the truce line had the truth inside their heads now, and fleets were made of people, and people remembered.

"Response to Breakaway," she said. "Standard de-escalation language. We are investigating the source of an unauthorized transmission. Repeat: unauthorized. No hostile action intended or authorized."

"And when they ask us to confirm the Halcyon Reach is alive?" Okafor asked quietly.

Reyes looked at him. He had been with her through nine years of truce patrols, nine years of holding two fleets apart by nothing but discipline and mutual exhaustion. His face gave away nothing, but his eyes did. He'd heard the lullaby fragment too. Everyone had.

"Tell them the truth," she said, and felt something tear loose inside her chest as she said it. "Confirm it. The Halcyon Reach is alive. This command received intercepts of its signal eleven days ago and failed to act on them."

The bridge went very still.

"Commander—" Okafor started.

"That's an order, Lieutenant. Log it. Timestamp it. Put my name on it." She turned back to the viewport, where the distant Breakaway formation hung like a scatter of angry stars. Somewhere out there, Sef Oyelaran was hearing the same broadcast, and whatever happened next would depend on whether fury or grief arrived first. "If we're going to burn, we burn honest."

Nineteen days had been the number on the truce clock that morning. The countdown display over the tactical station read thirteen days, six hours, and falling — and for the first time since the armistice, Reyes found herself hoping the clock didn't matter. Hoping the war would be decided some other way than the one everyone had planned for.

Then the proximity alarm sang, and the tactical plot bloomed with new contacts: three fast movers, fleet transponders, burning hard toward Meridian. Weapons hot.

"Pursuit wing out of Station Kestrel," Okafor read. "Orders are sealed. Destination: the seed vault."

Reyes stared at the plot. Thirteen days, and someone in fleet command had just decided not to wait for any of them.

"They're going to kill her," Brandt whispered. "Aren't they."

Reyes didn't answer. She opened a private channel instead, keyed it to her own quarters' recorder, and began, quietly, to copy everything.

* * *

Auditor Kade heard the broadcast from the cargo hold of the retrieval skiff, forty minutes out from Meridian, and knew before the first log finished playing that her career was already over.

She had been there. She had stood in those stacks, close enough to touch Wendell Montrose, close enough to see the seed-catalog files blinking on the caretaker's console and known — *known* — and let herself be persuaded otherwise. Let herself want to be persuaded. Because the alternative was admitting that the fleet had sent her to audit a lonely woman tending a monument, and that the woman had outplayed her with a hundred-year-old filing system.

Now the woman's voice was in every ear in the fleet, and Kade sat in the skiff's hold surrounded by marines who were listening to it too, and nobody was looking at anybody.

"—the fleet knew. Entry 3,203. The fleet knew the Reach survived, and the truce you're all dying to protect was built on top of them. Two hundred people, left to drift and die so two governments would have something to hate each other about—"

"Cut it off," said Sergeant Imura, though there was no cut-off to find. The broadcast wasn't coming through the skiff's systems alone; it was on the open bands, on personal receivers, in the marrow of the ship. Kade could have recited the regulation for jamming civilian transmissions in her sleep. She also knew that jamming required authorization, and authorization required someone willing to put their name on the order to silence a witness mid-sentence, and that no one aboard wanted to be that person.

Nobody cut it off. The marines sat with their helmets in their laps and listened to a dead world speak.

Kade pulled up her sister's file on her wrist display, the way she did when she needed to remember why she'd joined the fleet in the first place. Mara Kade, Breakaway flotilla, killed in the Second Skirmish, age twenty-six. The war had eaten her sister and given the family a folded flag and a form letter, and Auditor Kade had spent eleven years believing the machinery that produced that letter was at least *honest* machinery. Cruel sometimes. Careless often. But honest.

The broadcast played on, and the honesty died quietly in the hold of the skiff.

"Incoming hail," the pilot called forward. "Fleet actual. Priority black."

Kade went up to the cockpit. The face on the screen belonged to Admiral Voss-Coleman — no relation to Wendell, though the universe apparently enjoyed its jokes — and the admiral's expression was the smooth, terrible calm that Kade had learned to read as a weather warning.

"Auditor. Your team is forty minutes from the vault. Your orders are amended. The facility's data cores are to be physically destroyed. The caretaker is to be detained. Non-lethal force is authorized only if she complies."

Kade heard herself say, "Admiral, the broadcast is already galaxy-wide. Destroying the cores achieves nothing except—"

"It achieves deniability," Voss-Coleman said, "which is the only currency this fleet has left. Thirteen days, Auditor. In thirteen days the truce expires, and if these fleets go to war over a ghost ship while the public believes we acted in good faith, then maybe the ones who come after us can salvage something. That is the arithmetic. Do you understand your orders?"

Behind Kade, in the cockpit doorway, Sergeant Imura had stopped pretending not to listen.

"I understand them," Kade said.

"Then carry them out. Voss-Coleman out."

The screen went dark. The skiff burned on toward Meridian, toward the vault, toward a woman who had trusted Kade enough to argue with her instead of running. Kade looked down at her wrist display, at her sister's face, at the fleet seal stamped across the corner of the file like a watermark over a life.

"Non-lethal," Imura said behind her. It wasn't a question, exactly. It was a man asking permission to hope.

"We'll see," Kade said, and opened a channel she was not authorized to open, and sent four words to the vault's receiving array, unencrypted, in plain fleet code:

*They're coming armed. Run.*

* * *

Sef Oyelaran listened to the whole broadcast standing up, because sitting down felt like a thing you did afterward, once you knew what you were.

The flotilla's command hall was packed — every officer who could find an excuse, and half who couldn't. Nobody had ordered them there. They'd come the way water comes through a cracked hull, drawn by the sound of a voice reading names. Because that was what the logs had become, somewhere around entry two thousand: a litany. Names of seeds, names of ships, names of the lost — and threaded through the last few entries, translated line by line in Wendell Montrose's careful voice, the names of the Halcyon Reach's founding families.

Oyelaran's family among them. Her great-great-grandmother's name, spoken aloud into the dark by a stranger on a frozen world, and answered, two centuries late, by descendants who had never stopped speaking the old tongue.

"Say it again," someone whispered near the front. "The part about the fleet intercepts."

Montrose's recording obliged, patient as stone: the fleet's own internal routing headers, decoded from the archive strata, showing that distress signals from the Reach had been received, logged, and marked *do not acknowledge* — eleven separate times over sixty years. The hall made a sound Oyelaran had never heard a room full of soldiers make. Not anger. Something older and worse. Recognition.

"They used our dead," said Deniz, her tactical lead, very quietly. "They used our dead as fuel for the truce. Both sides. They fed us to each other."

"The truce holds thirteen more days," Oyelaran said. "That's the official position. That's what I'm supposed to tell you."

"And your position?" Deniz asked.

Sef looked at the faces in the hall — the children of the Dispersal, the grandchildren of grievances no one alive had committed, all of them holding their breath. On the main display, the fleet formations were moving, and not toward each other for once. Toward Meridian. Toward a lone caretaker and a vault full of voices.

"My position," Oyelaran said slowly, "is that for two hundred years we have been fighting the wrong enemy with the right rage. And I think the woman who woke this up knows exactly where the real enemy keeps its throat." She keyed the fleet-wide channel. "All flotilla elements: new heading. We escort the truth to the Halcyon Reach. If the fleet wants to stop us, they can do it in front of the whole listening galaxy, on every channel, with their names attached."

The hall rose around her like a tide deciding, finally, which way to run.

As the flotilla turned, the long-range array caught a single unencrypted pulse from deep fleet space — small, human, desperate. A warning meant for the vault, relayed now across every receiver in the system: *They're coming armed.*

Thirteen days on the truce clock. Three fleets converging on one frozen point. And somewhere ahead of them all, drifting in the dark with two hundred lives hanging by threads of failing steel, the Halcyon Reach began, for the first time in two centuries, to sing back.
