## 15. What We Buried

The purge order sat in Kade's hand like a stone pulled from cold water.

*Directive 9-Theta. All signal records at Meridian Seed Vault to be expunged. Vault caretaker to be detained for debriefing. No further transmission from the site is to be permitted under any circumstances.*

No further transmission. As if the vault were a wound that had to be cauterized rather than a place where people lived.

Kade stood at the viewport of the retrieval skiff and watched the vault grow beneath them — a gray seed pressed into the shoulder of a dead mountain, its antenna array fanned out like something trying to take root in the sky. Wendell Montrose was down there. The old woman with the ink-stained fingers and the voice that sounded like it had been reading aloud to empty rooms for forty years. The woman whose audio files Kade had searched for six hours without finding anything, and who had poured tea the whole time as if auditors came through every season.

She should have arrested her then. That was the first failure. Or maybe the first failure was earlier — on the *Vigilant*, when Commander Reyes had handed her the intercept summary and said, "Bury it," and Kade had said, "Yes, ma'am," and felt nothing break inside her until much later, alone, when she finally understood what she'd agreed to bury.

A voice from a ship two hundred years drowned. A language her grandmother used to hum over the dishwater.

Kade's sister's name was Anouk. Anouk Kade, born on Ceres Station, lost at nineteen in the second Breakaway engagement — not even an engagement, really, just a patrol boat and a minefield and eleven minutes of static. Their mother had kept Anouk's room sealed for a decade. Kade knew what it was to live beside a door no one opened. She recognized the shape of it now: the fleet had built an entire war out of sealed doors, and she had been one of the hinges.

"Approach vector confirmed," said Specialist Okafor from the cockpit. "Vault's external systems are... active, ma'am. All of them."

"All of them?"

"Every antenna. Every relay. They're broadcasting on open channels. It's the caretaker's logs, I think. Thousands of them. Numbered." Okafor paused. "It's kind of beautiful, actually."

"It's a beacon," Kade said. "For every gun in the system."

But even as she said it, she heard the logs bleeding through the comm filter — Wendell Montrose's dry, patient voice reciting humidity readings, seed viability percentages, the date, the weather outside a mountain that hadn't seen rain in a century. *Log four thousand one hundred twelve.* A woman talking to no one, faithfully, for decades. And now the whole network carrying her voice, because Kade had come with a purge order and Wendell Montrose had answered with a failsafe older than both their careers.

*Burn my archive,* the broadcast seemed to say, *and I will make sure the whole sky hears what you're burning.*

Kade closed her fist around the directive until the laminate bit her palm.

* * *

They landed in the loading bay, and the vault swallowed them.

Kade had been in archives before — the Fleet Historical Division on Thessaly, all glass and climate control and reverence arranged by committee. This was different. This was a living thing grown wrong and wonderful: seed racks rising three stories into the dark, frost-breathing conduits wrapped around everything like veins, catwalks sagging under centuries of maintenance patches. The air tasted cold and green at once, like the memory of a garden.

And everywhere, softly, the logs played. Not from speakers — from the vault itself, from relay nodes strung along the stacks, each one murmuring a different year of Wendell Montrose's solitude so that walking through the archive was like walking through a life.

Okafor and Vashti fanned out toward the signal core with cutting charges. Kade told herself to go with them. Instead her boots carried her deeper, following a thread of sound.

*"...log three thousand nine hundred sixty. The eastern coolant loop failed again today. I fixed it with a clamp from the hydroponics spares and a prayer I don't believe in. The wheat strains are holding at ninety-one percent viability. The corn is at eighty-eight. Nobody has answered in thirty years, but if you're listening: the corn is fine. The corn is always fine..."*

Kade stopped walking.

There was a bench there, bolted to the deck between two seed racks, worn smooth in the middle. A mug sat on it, ceramic, chipped, with a faded stenciled crest — the old Meridian Dispersal Authority, extinct longer than Kade had been alive. Beside the mug lay a stack of data slates, and on top of the stack, squared neatly, facing outward, was a single slate with a label in careful handwriting:

*FOR THE AUDITOR. WHEN YOU'RE READY.*

Kade looked around the empty aisle. Frost ticked in the pipes. Somewhere far off, Okafor's voice crackled on the squad channel — *core access secured, standing by* — and Kade did not answer.

She picked up the slate.

* * *

It was the fleet's own paperwork.

That was the thing that undid her — not smuggled evidence, not decrypted secrets, but the fleet's internal correspondence, filed in its own formats, stamped with its own authentication codes. Kade knew document provenance the way a jeweler knows stones. These were real.

She read standing up, then sitting down, then not reading at all, just staring at the middle distance while the logs murmured around her like tide.

*Memorandum, Office of Fleet Intelligence, dated 2197:* The colony vessel *Halcyon Reach*, listed as lost with all hands following drive cascade during First Dispersal transit, is assessed with high confidence to have survived. Surviving telemetry indicates intact hull, functioning life support, and a population in excess of two hundred. Recommendation: classification maintained. Assessment of strategic impact follows.

*Strategic impact assessment:* Disclosure of *Halcyon Reach* survival would establish precedent for Breakaway claims of fleet abandonment during the Dispersal. The Reach's passenger manifest is disproportionately composed of families later associated with Breakaway separatism. Disclosure at this juncture would provide separatist leadership with a martyr-narrative of significant mobilizing power. Recommendation: maintain loss status indefinitely. Reassess upon cessation of hostilities.

*Addendum, dated 2211, marked REASSESSMENT DEFERRED — HOSTILITIES ONGOING.*

And then, near the bottom, the one that made Kade's hands go cold:

*Directive 4-Alpha (Standing): Any detection of Halcyon Reach-origin signals is to be suppressed at source. Retrieval assets may be employed against civilian archive facilities if necessary to prevent disclosure. This directive supersedes standard rules of engagement.*

*Supersedes standard rules of engagement.* Against civilians. Against seed vaults. Against one old woman and her corn.

Two hundred years. Two hundred years of a ship full of people calling into the dark in a dead language, and every time they were heard, someone in a uniform like hers reached out and turned the volume to zero. The Breakaway fleets weren't fighting over territory or ideology or any of the elegant words in the academy textbooks. They were fighting because the fleet had let their grandparents die in the dark and called it strategy, and the only way to keep that secret was to keep the war going, and the only way to keep the war going was —

The circle closed. Kade saw it whole, clean and terrible, the way you see a trap's geometry only from inside it.

Anouk had died in that circle. Eleven minutes of static, and somewhere behind the static, officers deciding what could be known and by whom. Her mother sealing a door. A generation raised on grief that had been manufactured in a conference room, signed, filed, deferred.

*Reassess upon cessation of hostilities.* The war existed to prevent the reassessment. The reassessment required the war to end. It was not a stalemate. It was a machine, and it ran on silence, and she had spent nineteen years feeding it.

Kade became aware, gradually, that she was crying — not dramatically, just leaking, the way old pipes do — and that Wendell Montrose was standing at the end of the aisle.

The old woman held a second mug. Steam rose off it. She didn't smile. She didn't gloat. She looked at Kade the way she must have looked at failing coolant loops for forty years: assessing, unhurried, already halfway to the fix.

"You read the addendum," Wendell said. Not a question.

"How long have you known what we'd find?"

"Since your first audit. You search like someone looking for a reason to stop searching." Wendell crossed the aisle and set the mug on the bench beside the chipped one. "I hid the files in plain sight because I thought maybe you wanted to be found. People who want to hide things put them behind locks. People who want to be stopped put them where their conscience can trip over them."

"That's a hell of a gamble."

"I've had forty years of gambles. I'm good at odds." She sat down on the bench, joints cracking, and nodded at the slate in Kade's hand. "Directive 4-Alpha. My grandmother's lullabies are classified above her grandchildren's lives. Does that summarize it?"

Kade laughed — a broken, startled sound — and wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "My sister died in the second Breakaway engagement. Anouk. She was nineteen." The name came out of her like something she'd been carrying too long. "I joined the fleet because of her. I thought I was honoring her. And the whole time, the reason she died was sitting in a filing cabinet being renewed every decade by people who — "

"Who believed they were the reasonable ones," Wendell said quietly. "That's how it always works. No one burns an archive believing they're the villain. They burn it believing they're the dam."

Outside, faint through the mountain rock, the skiff's engines idled. Somewhere in the stacks, a log murmured: *the corn is fine. The corn is always fine.*

"The purge team is in your signal core," Kade said. "Cutting charges. Twenty minutes, maybe less."

"I know. I've been listening to them walk around for an hour. They breathe very loudly, your specialists." Wendell sipped her tea. "So. Auditor Kade. What happens now?"

What happened now. Kade turned the question over and found it lighter than any question she'd carried in years. Because there was a version of her — the version from this morning, from the last nineteen years — who would radio Okafor and complete the mission and file the report and carry the stone a little further up the hill. And that version was tired. That version had been tired since the static.

"There's a copy of Directive 4-Alpha in the *Vigilant*'s command archive," she said. "Original, authenticated, unredacted. Reyes keeps the keys. But there's also this." She pulled her personal slate from her jacket — the one auditors weren't supposed to have, the one where she'd kept a copy of the buried intercept since the day Reyes said *bury it*. "I've had it for weeks. I told myself I was preserving evidence. I think I was waiting for permission."

"And now?"

"Now I'm giving it to myself." She set the slate on the bench next to Wendell's tea. "The Reach's coordinates are in here. Partial — the signal triangulation isn't complete, your decoding is better than ours. But it's enough to fly toward."

Wendell looked at the slate for a long moment. Then at Kade. Something moved across the old woman's face that might have been the beginning of trust, tested like ice before weight goes onto it.

"They'll call you a traitor."

"They'll call me worse. I've read the manuals; I helped write some of them." Kade keyed her squad channel, then hesitated. "Your purge team. Okafor and Vashti. They're good soldiers, which means they'll follow me anywhere except into treason. I need you to lock the core down — your failsafe, whatever else you've got. Give me a reason to pull them out that isn't mutiny."

"A mechanical fault. Coolant breach in the core chamber. Very dramatic, very plausible — this place fails beautifully, it's had practice." Wendell was already moving, brisk now, forty years of crisis rhythm waking in her stride. "And after?"

"After, I need a ship. Your freighter pilot. Tam." Kade fell into step beside her, past the racks of sleeping seeds, past the murmuring years. "Because in about six hours, both fleets are going to converge on this mountain, and neither of them is coming to negotiate."

They reached the junction where the aisles split — one way toward the signal core and the cutting charges, one way deeper into the archive, toward the vault's oldest strata, where the truth had been sleeping in the dark for two centuries, waiting for someone to stop burying it.

Wendell paused there, mug still steaming in her hand, and looked at Kade with an expression that was almost gentle.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your sister would have liked the corn."

Kade laughed again — wetter this time — and turned toward the core, keying the channel, lying to her own team with the steadiest voice she'd ever used: *"Okafor, Vashti, fall back to the skiff. Core chamber's showing a coolant breach — Montrose says it's about to vent. Move, now."*

And in the silence after the channel closed, deep in the walls of the vault, the logs went on singing — and for the first time in nineteen years, Auditor Mara Kade listened.
