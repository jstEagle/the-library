## 1. Routine at the Edge of Signal

The station woke her at 05:14, as it always did, with a chime so gentle it might have been an apology.

Lena Nakamura lay in her bunk for exactly ninety seconds—her own ritual, though she'd never have called it that—and listened to Vesper breathe. That was what she'd named it, years ago, in a fit of whimsy she'd never quite outgrown: not Relay Station Vesper, all caps and officialdom, but *Vesper*, lowercase, like a woman's name. Like something alive. The station breathed through its ventilation loops in long, slow cycles, air moving through the habitat ring with a sound she could only describe as tidal. Eleven years and she still found it comforting. Eleven years and she had never once told anyone that.

There was no one to tell. That was the point.

She rose, folded her blanket into thirds, squared it against the bulkhead, and ran through her stretches in the narrow aisle between bunk and locker—twenty minutes, no music, counting breaths instead of reps because numbers were more honest than songs. The hab ring's spin gave her just enough weight to feel her body as hers. At 05:47 she was in the galley, boiling water for coffee that was half real grounds and half recycled chicory, watching the bubbles climb the sides of the pot in slow, fat spirals.

"Good morning," she said to the water recycler.

It gurgled back. It always did. The recycler was a dumb machine, older than her contract, dumber than most, but it had a rhythm when it ran well and a stutter when it didn't, and over eleven years Lena had learned its moods the way a farmer learns weather. This morning it sounded content. She patted its housing twice with the flat of her hand, a gesture she would have died of embarrassment performing in front of another human being, and poured her coffee.

The galley window faced outward, away from Earth, away from everything. Just stars. She drank standing up, one hand wrapped around the warm bulb of the cup, and let the silence sit on her shoulders like a coat that fit perfectly.

This was the life she had chosen. She reminded herself of that every morning, the way other people said prayers. *You chose this. Nobody made you. You are here because you wanted to be nowhere.*

* * *

At 06:30, beacon maintenance.

The beacon was the heart of Vesper—not metaphorically, but structurally, physically: a lattice of emitters and focusing arrays mounted on the station's forward spine, aimed at nothing and everything, broadcasting a navigation pulse on a frequency so old that half the modern charts listed it as abandoned. Her job, per the contract she'd signed in a windowless office on Earth eleven years ago, was simple: keep it transmitting. Check the alignment weekly. Replace components as they failed. Log everything.

Ask no questions.

That last part hadn't been in the contract. It had been in the way her handler, a tired man named Ade Okafor with kind eyes and a bureaucrat's smile, had slid the final page across the desk and said, "You'll find some things on station don't come with documentation. That's normal. Don't let it worry you." He'd said it like a man describing a leaky faucet. And young Lena—thirty-one years old, freshly divorced from a planet that had never wanted her, hungry for a salary that would buy her permanent invisibility—had nodded and signed.

She thought about Ade sometimes. Not often. Mostly when she was doing work that required two hands and no mind.

Today the work was the port emitter array. She suited up in the airlock at 06:32—seals checked twice, always twice—and cycled out onto the spine with her toolkit mag-locked to her hip. The stars were doing their morning performance: hard, white, indifferent. Below and behind her, Earth hung like a marble someone had dropped in mud, blue swirled with brown, beautiful from this distance the way most disasters are.

"Morning, sweetheart," she said to the emitter housing.

The housing said nothing, being a housing, but its status panel glowed amber where it should have been green, and Lena felt the small, private satisfaction of a problem discovered before it became a crisis. She clipped her tether, floated along the array, and found it: a focus actuator running hot, its servo drawing twelve percent over nominal. Not failing. *Thinking about* failing. Machines rarely broke without warning; they complained first, if you knew how to listen.

"You've been grinding on me for three weeks," she told the actuator, pulling its inspection cover. "I heard you. I always hear you."

Inside, the mechanism was dusted with fine metallic particulate—the servo's own blood. She cleaned it with solvent and a brush no bigger than an eyelash, replaced the bearing sleeve from stores, torqued everything to spec, and watched the draw curve settle back to green on her wrist display. Total time: forty-one minutes. She logged it in the maintenance record with a note so precise it read like poetry: *Bearing sleeve 7-C replaced. Residual wear within tolerance. Recommend recheck 90 days.*

Then, because she was thorough and because thoroughness was the only religion she'd ever kept, she checked the other five actuators too. All green. All quiet.

She allowed herself thirty seconds to simply float there, tether taut, looking down the length of the spine toward the beacon's core. The lattice caught the distant sun and threw it back in thin bright lines. Somewhere inside that lattice, a signal pulsed outward every 4.2 seconds—a heartbeat older than her whole life, maybe older than her parents' lives. She'd never been able to trace who it talked to. The commissioning records were sealed; the frequency registry entry was a dead link; the original engineering specs existed only as fragments with the interesting parts redacted. She'd asked, once, early on, and gotten a form letter from an office that probably didn't exist.

So she'd stopped asking. That was the deal. Keep the light on, don't ask who it shines for.

"Who do you talk to?" she murmured to the beacon, the way you'd murmur to a sleeping animal whose dreams you couldn't enter. "Hm? Who's out there?"

The beacon pulsed. Four-point-two seconds. Pulsed again.

She cycled back inside at 08:15, hung her suit, and noted with a small frown that the suit's biometric log showed her heart rate had spiked briefly during the EVA—right around the moment she'd asked her question. Probably telemetry interference. Probably nothing.

* * *

The rest of the morning was audits.

Ration audit, first: a physical count of the long-term stores in Bay Two, cross-checked against the inventory manifest, which she maintained in triplicate—one digital, one printed, one handwritten in a ledger she'd bought herself because she trusted ink more than code. Eleven years of ledgers lined the shelf above her desk, spines labeled by year in her small, square handwriting. Anyone reading them would learn everything about how Lena Nakamura lived: grams of protein consumed, liters of water reclaimed, hours of filter life remaining. Everything except why.

Stores were at 94.1 percent of projected annual need. Slightly rich. She adjusted next quarter's resupply request downward by four kilograms and felt the particular pleasure of a balanced column. There were people on Earth, she knew, who would kill for this kind of order. She had been one of them, once.

Systems audit, second: she walked the habitat ring end to end, tablet in hand, greeting each subsystem by name or near-name. The CO₂ scrubbers ("How are we feeling today, ladies?"), the thermal loop ("Stay cool, big guy"), the comm relay that hadn't carried a live voice in six years ("Still dreaming, old man?"). None of them answered. All of them hummed. It was, she understood, a strange way to be a person—to pour affection into machinery precisely because machinery could never disappoint, never leave, never look at you with the pity that had finally driven her off-planet. But it worked. It had worked for eleven years.

The hairline crack came, as it always did, in Ring Section Nine.

Section Nine held the station's recreation allotment: a table bolted to the floor, two chairs, a shelf of physical books left by crews decades dead, and a viewport wide enough to hold the whole sweep of the inner system. It was designed for eight people. It had been used by one for eleven years, and the arithmetic of that sat differently depending on the day. Today she sat in Chair One, ate her lunch ration—soy protein, pickled vegetables, a square of chocolate she rationed like a miser—and looked out at the stars, and for about four minutes she let herself want something she could not name.

Not company, exactly. Company implied obligation, conversation, the exhausting theater of being perceived. What she wanted was smaller and stranger: she wanted the station to say something back. She wanted, just once, to walk these corridors and hear her own voice returned to her, changed slightly, as if the place itself had been listening all along and had an opinion.

"Sentimental nonsense," she said aloud, to the empty room, and the room agreed with her by saying nothing at all.

She finished her chocolate. She got back to work.

* * *

Afternoon: diagnostics, calibration, the endless patient carpentry of keeping a forty-year-old station alive. She re-torqued a coolant coupling in Bay Three. She recalibrated the spectrometer on the science rack, though no science had been requested of her since year three. She ran the antenna gimbal through its full range of motion and greased the bearings, talking the whole time—"easy, easy, there you go"—in the low voice she'd have used on a skittish horse, if she'd ever met a horse.

At 17:00 she exercised. At 18:30 she ate. At 19:00 she read—currently a battered paperback history of the Collapse era, salvaged from the shelf in Section Nine, full of names and dates and atrocities she read the way you'd read about weather in a country you'd emigrated from. Executives who had sold the atmosphere's future short. Generals who had burned grain belts to protect stock prices. A whole civilization that had known, and looked away, and called it prudence.

"Animals," she muttered at the page, without heat. Then, fairer: "People, I suppose. Same thing, under pressure."

At 21:00 she did her rounds. This was her favorite hour, though she'd have denied it under torture. The station at night—with the habitat lights dimmed to amber and the spin carrying her gently along the ring—felt less like a workplace than a body she lived inside, warm and rhythmic and hers. She checked seals, pressures, temperatures. She whispered goodnight to the scrubbers. In Bay One she paused at the main console, where the station's administrative system displayed its nightly summary:

ALL SYSTEMS NOMINAL.
BEACON: TRANSMITTING.
MAINTENANCE LOG REVIEWED — GOOD WORK TODAY, LENA.

She stopped.

She had written that last line herself, years ago—a joke, a bit of self-administered praise typed into the log template so the nightly summary would end on something kind. She'd forgotten doing it. Seeing it now, glowing in the dark bay, it landed strangely: like finding a note from a stranger who turned out to be yourself, or like hearing your own voice on a recording and not recognizing it.

"Good work today, Lena," she repeated quietly. The words echoed off the console glass and came back thinner.

She stood there a moment longer than the routine required. Then she shook it off, dimmed the display, and continued her rounds.

* * *

At 22:40, before sleep, she performed the last ritual of her day: the signal check.

Every night, she verified the beacon's pulse against the master clock—4.2 seconds, precise to the millisecond, faithful as gravity. Every night, she confirmed the transmission log showed the pulse going out and nothing coming back. Outbound only. Eleven years of outbound only. A lighthouse with no ships, singing to a sea that never sang back.

Tonight, she pulled up the log the way she always did, thumb scrolling through the familiar columns—

—and stopped.

There, at 21:17:03 station time, between two outbound pulses, the log recorded an inbound event. Duration: 0.4 seconds. Source: unregistered. Bearing: deep space, trans-lunar, closing.

An echo. Or an answer. Or a mistake in a forty-year-old logging system that had never once made one.

Lena stared at the line until the screen dimmed, then tapped it awake again. She ran the diagnostic twice. Both times, the entry held. Something out there had heard the beacon—or had been listening all along—and tonight, for the first time in eleven years, it had spoken.

Her heart was doing something complicated. She noticed, distantly, that her hands were perfectly steady, and wondered what that said about her.

"Well," she said to the empty bay, to the sleeping station, to whatever was listening. "Hello."

Out in the dark, 4.2 seconds later, the beacon pulsed on—as if nothing had happened, or as if everything had only just begun.
