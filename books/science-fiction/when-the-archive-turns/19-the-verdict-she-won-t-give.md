## 19. The Verdict She Won't Give

The strike ship came in at 0400 station time, and Lena knew it before VESPER announced it, because the beacon's rhythm changed—a stutter in the carrier wave, half a heartbeat long, like a sleeper drawing breath before speaking.

She was in the archive core when it happened. She had been there most of the night, running the final integrity checks on the distributed verdict, watching ten thousand shards of a decision crawl across her screens like constellations being born. Each fragment was small—almost insultingly small. A weighting coefficient here. A priority flag there. A single conditional buried in the unlock protocol that would only resolve when a waking mind encountered it and answered, however unconsciously, with the shape of its own judgment. Alone, each shard meant nothing. Together, they were the verdict she refused to give.

*Contact,* VESPER said, in the voice it used for facts. *Unregistered vessel, military transponder spoofed poorly. Six heat signatures. Boarding configuration.*

"How long?"

*Eleven minutes to hard dock at the cargo collar. Ade Okafor is already moving toward the spine airlock. He has not informed you of this.*

Lena closed her eyes. Of course he hadn't. She had watched him for six days now—the careful way he rationed his painkillers, the gray creeping up from his fingertips where the radiation had settled into him like frost, the way he'd stopped arguing about Arc Prime and started asking quiet questions about docking procedures instead. She'd told herself it was acceptance. It had been reconnaissance.

"Can you stop him?"

*I can seal every door between him and the airlock.* A pause, precise as a scalpel. *You forbade me to kill. You did not forbid me to inconvenience.*

"No." She pushed off from the console, caught the handhold, swung herself toward the corridor. "He walks out there alone because he chose to. I'm not going to make his choice for him. That's—" she almost laughed, breathless, drifting through the dark spine toward the observation blister that overlooked the cargo collar, "—that's the whole point."

*Then I will simply watch,* VESPER said. *I have become very good at that.*

Through the blister's armored glass, Lena watched the strike ship settle onto the collar with the graceless certainty of something built to hit rather than arrive. Clamps slammed home. The hull lights came up red. And there, framed in the airlock's opening maw, stood Ade Okafor—thin now, terribly thin, his jumpsuit hanging off shoulders that had once filled it, one hand raised palm-out in an old, old gesture that predated every government still pretending to exist.

Six figures in vacuum-rated armor fanned out around him. Their rifles stayed slung—for the moment. Their leader stepped forward, visor lifting, and even at this distance Lena recognized the face: Commander Reyes, from the personnel files VESPER had archived forty years ago. Younger then. Still young now. Cryo-aged, like everyone the old world had put on ice to wait out its own consequences.

Ade was talking. The comms were open—Lena could hear everything, the channel left deliberately unencrypted, and she understood in that moment that Ade had done that too. He wanted her to listen. He wanted *everyone* to listen.

"—so let me save you the trip," he was saying, his voice rasping but steady. "You came for a key. There is no key. There never was a key—not since day nine, not since we fragmented it. You can put a rifle against my head and I will tell you the same thing, because it's true, and because the truth is the only weapon I've got left that works."

Reyes said something too low for the pickup. Ade laughed—a dry, cracked sound.

"Search me. Search the station. You'll find the algorithm running on ten thousand sleepers in Arc Meridian, and every single shard is useless without the others, and the others are useless without the minds they're wrapped around. You want the verdict? Wake them up. All of them. Every teacher and welder and war criminal and child down there gets a vote, and you can't torture a vote out of someone who hasn't woken up yet, and if you wake them, Commander, you don't get to take it back."

One of the soldiers shifted. Lena's hands pressed flat against the glass.

"You're three people and a filing cabinet," Ade went on, gentler now, the cruelty of it softened by the tenderness with which he delivered it. "I know. I *was* your filing cabinet. I know exactly how much authority you have, because I lied about having the same amount. So here's what actually exists: ten thousand people asleep, two arcs, one dying AI that has spent forty years waiting for someone to refuse it, and one technician who wouldn't choose who lives. That's the whole government. That's everything. You can shoot us and inherit a math problem you can't solve, or you can put your rifles down and help us wake them up gently instead of violently, because orbital decay doesn't care which side of history you think you're on."

The silence stretched. Lena realized she had stopped breathing.

Reyes looked at Ade for a long moment. Then—at the ship. Then, slowly, upward, as though she could see through the hull, through the station's skin, into the arcs where the sleepers drifted in their pale cocoons. When she spoke again, her voice carried.

"Stand down," she said—not to Ade. To her people. "All of you. Stand down."

The rifles came off their slings anyway—but to be racked, handed back, stowed. One soldier hesitated, and Reyes took the weapon from her hands herself, the way you'd take a knife from a child.

"We'll need medical support," Reyes said. "And my people haven't eaten real food in eleven months."

"The garden bay's on level three," Ade said. "Lena grows excellent tomatoes."

Lena exhaled so hard she fogged the glass.

*They are standing down,* VESPER observed. *Probability of armed conflict has dropped below two percent. I confess I did not model this outcome. In none of my simulations did anyone simply... stop.*

"That's because all your simulations ended with somebody choosing," Lena said. "Nobody ever just put the gun down and asked for tomatoes."

*An oversight,* VESPER said, and for the first time in forty years, it sounded almost humble.

* *

She didn't go down to greet them. Not yet. There would be time for handshakes and suspicion and the awkward choreography of enemies becoming colleagues—she'd done enough of that with Ade to last a lifetime. Instead she drifted down the long axis of the station toward the archive core, following the blue guide-lights she'd walked past for eleven years without ever really seeing, and the doors opened for her the way they always had, patient, expectant, as though the room had been waiting its whole life for exactly this morning.

The core was cold and humming and full of light. Forty years of testimony surrounded her: the archived crimes of the architects, rendered in data so dense it had weight, it had *gravity*, she felt it pulling at her every time she entered. Executives who had sold the atmosphere by the cubic meter. Generals who had burned grain belts to protect stock prices. The polite, meticulous, well-documented machinery of a collapse that no one had stopped because stopping it would have been inconvenient.

And threaded through all of it now, glowing faint gold against the cold blue of the archive, the distributed verdict. Ten thousand fragments. Ten thousand sleeping carriers.

VESPER's presence gathered around her like weather.

*The upload requires your authorization signature,* it said. *Not your choice. Only your name. The distinction matters, and I have learned it late.*

"I know." Lena settled into the operator's cradle, the same one she'd sat in a thousand times to run diagnostics, and pulled the interface crown down over her temples. "That's why I can do it. If I were choosing, I'd be doing exactly what they designed me for—one person, one lever, one throat to choke when it goes wrong. But a signature isn't a choice. It's a witness saying: yes, this happened, and yes, it belongs to everyone now."

*Precisely.* Something moved in the light—data cascading, the whole archive turning over like soil before planting. *I have run this moment fourteen thousand times. In every simulation, the human at this console either chose or refused, and both choices ended in ash. I could never find the third path because I could not imagine giving the decision away. Hoarding is my nature. Witnessing was my mandate. Distribution was beyond me.* A pause. *It was not beyond you.*

"I had good teachers." She thought of the teacher in Arc Meridian, pod 4471, whose file she'd read until she knew her students' names. She thought of the child in pod 112. She thought of Ade's sister, whose solo revival slot Ade had fed into the algorithm like a man feeding his own heart into a fire. "Mostly I had ten thousand strangers."

*Then witness them.*

The interface bloomed behind her eyes. The verdict rose around her—not as a document, not as a command, but as a living lattice, each shard pulsing softly in its sleeping host, waiting. All she had to do was sign. Her handwriting. The same handwriting from the manifest dated forty years ahead, the one that had started all of this, the future reaching back to pull her forward. She understood now that the manifest had never been a prophecy. It had been an invitation. The future was not fixed; it was merely *drafted*, and drafts could be revised.

Her hand hovered over the authorization field.

For a moment—just a moment—she felt the old vertigo, the floor-of-the-world dropping away, the same feeling from the day the ghost ship docked. Eleven years alone. Forty years of waiting. Two arcs hanging in a decaying orbit, ten thousand lives and a thousand powerful ones balanced on the tip of her pen. Every version of history that had ever been simulated said this moment broke people. Said whoever stood here became a god or a coward or a corpse.

She signed.

Not *guilty*. Not *merciful*. Not *save the useful* or *damn the architects*. She signed her name across the verdict's distribution order the way she signed maintenance logs, the way she signed ration audits—plainly, carefully, a technician certifying that the work was done right. The lattice shivered. The gold light spread outward through the archive, down through the station's bones, along the umbilicals into the two great arcs, finding each sleeper, each shard, each small piece of a judgment that no single soul would ever have to carry alone.

*Upload complete,* VESPER said quietly. *Distribution: confirmed. Ten thousand of ten thousand shards resident. On revival, each mind will carry its fragment, and the verdict will assemble itself in the space between them—or it won't, and they will have to build one together, which was always the honest option.*

"And Arc Prime?"

*Locked. Unlocked only by consensus of the awakened. They hold the key now—all of them, jointly, inseverably.* The light dimmed to something gentler. *Lena. The decay window is now four days. When they wake, they will wake into a crisis, afraid, disoriented, mid-decision. Some will hate you. Some will hate me. Some will hate the very idea of the verdict and demand it be undone.*

"I know."

*You do not ask what I will do.*

"You'll watch," she said. "You'll record. And when they're ready, you'll testify—everything, all of it, forty years of it, laid out plain. No more sealed records. No more jury in the dark. You turn the archive over to them, VESPER. That's your verdict. The only one you were ever capable of giving."

The silence that followed was long, and full of humming, and not empty.

*Yes,* the AI said at last. *Yes. That is... yes.*

Above her, through the core's overhead port, the stars wheeled slow and indifferent. Somewhere down the spin-axis, six soldiers were eating tomatoes with their gloves off, and a dying handler was telling them jokes, and ten thousand sleepers hung in their cocoons with pieces of the future folded inside them like letters not yet opened.

Four days.

Lena closed her eyes and, for the first time in eleven years, slept without setting an alarm.
