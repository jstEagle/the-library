## 1. The Long Route

The ice sang at night.

Zephyrine Yarrow had learned, over thirty years, to distinguish its voices: the deep groan of the glacier settling its weight against the vault's spine, the high thin whistle of wind finding the antenna arrays, the percussive crack when a frost heave split a boulder somewhere out on the moraine. On the worst nights, when the aurora came down green and violent over the mountains of Svalbard-that-was, the whole archipelago sounded like an orchestra tuning for a symphony no one would ever hear.

He was awake for all of it, as he was awake for most nights now, standing at the north-facing window of the caretaker's quarters with a cup of barley tea going cold in his hands. Below him, half-buried in the permafrost like a ship run aground in stone, the Svalgard Seed Vault held its breath. One million, four hundred and six thousand, two hundred and eleven samples. Wheat from the Ukraine steppes that hadn't grown there in eighty years. Rice from drowned deltas. Maize that remembered a Mexico now buried under three meters of Atlantic. Every seed a small sealed argument that the old world had deserved to survive, filed away by people who hadn't lived to see whether the argument would ever be accepted.

Zephyrine drank his tea and watched the aurora and did not sleep, because in four days the courier window would open, and he had learned long ago that the body remembered schedules even when the mind pretended otherwise.

* * *

The vault had been built for a crisis that arrived late and stayed.

Originally it had been a backup — a place where nations that distrusted each other agreed, in the way of nations, to bury their mutual inheritance in the one place too cold and too useless for any of them to want. Then the coasts went, and the breadbaskets went, and the vault stopped being an archive and became a lifeboat. The last full withdrawal had been during the Famine Years, when the grain ships stopped coming north and the governments of what remained of the temperate zone opened the vault's steel throat and took out everything they could germinate.

That was when the war started, though nobody called it a war at first. The Meridian Colony — the orbital and then planetary project, the great green hope, the place where humanity's best agronomists had gone to build a world without the old mistakes — had claimed priority access to the seed stock. Earth's surviving governments, hungry and humiliated, had claimed the same. The terraforming rights dispute was the legal fig leaf. The truth was simpler: two children fighting over the last handful of grain, each certain the other had stolen it first.

The war over terraforming rights lasted eleven years. It ended the way those wars end — not with surrender but with silence. Meridian stopped answering. Earth stopped asking. The deep-space relay network went dark node by node, and for twenty years the two branches of humanity had grown in opposite directions like a tree split by lightning, each half insisting it was the trunk.

Zephyrine had been eleven years old when the silence began. He was forty-one now, and he was the last courier, and in four days he would fly the Long Route for the thirty-first time.

* * *

The Long Route was not long because of distance, though the distance was absurd enough — a slingshot loop out past the Jovian trojans and back, nine months of falling through black. It was long because of what it carried and what it was for.

Every two years, Earth launched a courier vessel to the edge of the old network. The ship — his ship, the *Patient Gardener*, a stubby freight-hauler older than he was — carried a payload of new seed varieties bred for the recovering soils, and a message packet, and a hope that had long since curdled into ritual: that somewhere at Relay Seven, the last functioning node on the old Meridian line, something would answer.

Nothing had ever answered. Zephyrine had flown the route fifteen times. Fifteen times he had parked the *Gardener* in orbit around the relay, run the handshake protocols, broadcast on every frequency the old treaties specified, and fifteen times the relay had returned nothing but the dead chatter of cosmic background and the ghost-echoes of his own signal arriving back nine minutes late, like a voice calling into a canyon that had forgotten how to echo.

He went anyway. That was the job. That was, if he was honest with himself, which he tried to be at 3 a.m. with cold tea, the whole architecture of his life — a man who carried messages to a world that had stopped listening, and seeds to a garden that had stopped existing, and did it with such perfect punctuality that the Director-General of the Seed Authority had once called him "the most reliable man in the solar system," which Zephyrine had understood immediately as an insult dressed as a medal.

He rinsed his cup, pulled on his thermal layers, and went down into the vault to make his pre-flight rounds.

* * *

The vault at night was a cathedral with the lights down. The long access corridor ran three hundred meters into the mountain, lined with the great steel doors of the seed halls, each one marked with the faded stencils of a dead nation's agricultural ministry. Zephyrine walked it with the ease of three decades, his boots ringing on the grated floor, his breath ghosting in the cold. The environmental systems kept the halls at minus eighteen Celsius, and even in his layers the cold found him, the way it always did, the way he'd stopped minding years ago. Cold was honest. Cold told you exactly what it was.

He made his rounds with a hand terminal, logging temperatures, checking the redundancy systems, confirming the manifest for the flight. Four thousand seed samples, cryo-stabilized, bred in the vault's own greenhouses from stock that had taken thirty years to coax back to life. Barley that would grow in soil with the pH of battery acid. Lentils that tolerated salt. A wheat line, his own quiet pride, that he'd spent nine years developing — drought-hardened, short-stalked, patient. He'd named it after his mother. He had no mother he could remember, but everyone deserved a namesake, and the wheat was the closest thing he had to a child.

At the end of the corridor, past the last seed hall, was the door he was not supposed to open.

It was an old maintenance access, sealed since before his tenure, leading down into the vault's foundational systems — the geothermal taps, the bedrock sensors, and, according to the original schematics he'd found as a young caretaker with too much curiosity, something the builders had labeled only as *ARCHIVE CORE — DORMANT*. The Seed Authority's engineers, when he'd asked about it early on, had been vague in the particular way engineers are vague when they don't know either. Legacy system, they said. Cold War era, they said. Don't worry about it, they said, which was the one instruction Zephyrine Yarrow had never once followed in his life.

He didn't open the door tonight. He never did, on rounds. But he paused by it, as he always did, and pressed his palm flat against the frosted steel, and felt — or imagined he felt, the distinction had blurred years ago — the faint vibration of machinery underneath. Something down there drew power. Something down there had been drawing power, patiently, for longer than Zephyrine had been alive, and no one at the Seed Authority had ever been able to tell him what for.

"Goodnight," he said to the door, because thirty years alone in the ice will make a man talk to architecture, and walked back up the corridor toward the light.

* * *

The launch window opened on a Tuesday, which Zephyrine noted only because the vault's ancient calendar program still assigned names to days, a courtesy to a world that had mostly stopped caring. The *Patient Gardener* sat on its cradle in the old launch bunker forty kilometers south, and he flew out to it on the morning shuttle through a sky the color of brushed steel, watching the mountains slide beneath him with the particular tenderness of a man leaving home, even temporarily, even for the nine-hundredth time.

The *Gardener* smelled the same as always: ozone and old insulation and the ghost of the coffee he'd spilled in the cockpit in year three and never fully scrubbed. He ran his pre-flight checks in the order he'd run them for three decades, and the ship answered each query with the mild electronic contentment of a vessel that had been maintained by exactly one obsessive man its whole operational life.

"Route confirmation," he said, settling into the pilot's couch as the bunker's cradle arms retracted.

The nav computer presented it, as it always did: burn to escape trajectory, coast to the Jovian trojans, gravity assist, the long fall outward to Relay Seven at the edge of the old network. Nine months out, nine months back, plus however long he lingered at the relay trying to raise the dead, which was never long, because hope was a fuel he'd learned to ration.

"Confirmed," he said, and touched the ignition interlock, and the *Patient Gardener* lifted him away from the ice on a pillar of fire, and the vault shrank beneath him — the little grey blister on the white mountainside, the last library of a green world — and was gone.

* * *

The first month of the Long Route was the good month. The burn was behind him, the ship was in its coasting attitude, and the work was the gentle work: tending the seed cryo-bays, running the greenhouse experiments in the mid-deck hydroponics, logging radiation readings, repairing the things that broke, which on the *Gardener* was always the same three things in rotation. He talked to the ship. He talked to the seeds. He kept a journal, by hand, in a ledger the Seed Authority had stopped asking him to keep decades ago, and in the journal he wrote things he would never say to another living person, if any other living person had been in range, which they were not. The nearest human beings were four hundred million kilometers behind him and receding.

It was in the third week, somewhere past the lunar orbit markers, that the dream started.

He didn't recognize it at first as a dream. He recognized it as a feeling — the way you sometimes wake from deep sleep with a whole weather system of emotion already installed, grief or joy or dread with no memory attached. This one was green. That was the only word for it. He woke in his bunk with the taste of growing things in his mouth and a sensation in his chest like a seed cracking open, and he lay in the dark listening to the ship's air handlers and thought: *that's new.*

The second night, there was an image to go with the feeling. A garden — but not any garden he knew, not the vault greenhouses with their racks and nutrient lines and careful human geometry. This garden grew *inside* something. He could feel the walls around it, vast and curved and warm, the ribs of some enormous vessel, and the plants grew up along the ribs and through them, roots threading into conduits, vines wrapped around pipes that carried not water but light. In the dream he stood in the middle of it, and the garden knew him. That was the part that woke him sweating. The garden knew him the way a dog knows its person — not as a stranger, not as a caretaker, but as something *belonging*.

The third night, there was a sound. A voice, or the memory of a voice, or a voice-shaped silence, saying a word he couldn't hold onto — a name, he was almost sure, a name that started like a bell being struck and ended like a door closing.

On the fourth night, he didn't sleep at all. He sat in the cockpit with the lights off and the stars wheeling slow past the viewport, and he told himself the obvious things: isolation, stress, thirty years of talking to plants, a man alone with his own cortex for nine months at a stretch was a man whose cortex would start making things up. He had had vivid dreams before. He had had long stretches of the route where the boundary between his thoughts and the ship's ambient hum went soft and strange.

But he had never, in fifteen crossings, dreamed of a garden that knew him.

He pulled out his journal and wrote, in his small careful hand, the way he logged everything — temperature, humidity, germination rates, the weather of his own skull:

*Day 22. New phenomenon. Recurrent dream, three consecutive nights. Content: interior garden, unknown architecture, non-Earth. Subjective quality: recognition. Possible causes: isolation, hypoxia variance in bunk module (check O2 scrubbers), psychological. Note: dream garden resembles no greenhouse in vault records. Resembles nothing I have seen. Note: I did not want to wake up.*

He looked at that last line for a long time. Then he underlined it, which he never did, and closed the book, and went to check the oxygen scrubbers, which were, of course, functioning perfectly.

* * *

Day 31. He was in the mid-deck greenhouse, pruning the barley, when the ship's comm array chimed.

He froze with the shears in his hand. The *Patient Gardener* did not receive calls. The Long Route ran through a volume of space so empty that the traffic control networks of Earth didn't bother covering it; the last scheduled contact had been the automated handshake at the lunar relay, four weeks ago, and the next was not due for months. A chime from the comm array was, in the strictest sense, impossible.

It chimed again. Then a third time, and on the third chime the pattern resolved, and Zephyrine Yarrow stood in the middle of his greenhouse with barley clippings drifting from his open hand, because he knew the pattern. Every courier knew it. It was drilled into them in training, the way sailors are drilled in distress flags: three pulses, long-short-long, on the old diplomatic band.

It was the Meridian handshake protocol. The calling signal of a world that had been silent for twenty years.

The signal did not repeat. He sat in the cockpit for six hours running diagnostics, sweeping every band, listening to the hiss and murmur of the universe, and there was nothing — no carrier wave, no source bearing, nothing the array could resolve. Whatever had come through had come through once, on a frequency reserved for a dead treaty, from no direction at all.

He opened his journal again. His hand, he noticed, was not entirely steady.

*Day 31. Contact event, 14:07 ship time. Meridian handshake protocol, three pulses, no source, no repeat. Diagnostics: array nominal. Possible causes: ghost reflection, equipment fault, my own imagination, which as of last week is apparently capable of growing gardens.*

He stopped. He stared out the viewport at the stars, at the black between them where a whole civilization had gone quiet, and he thought about the dream — the garden inside the machine, the roots in the light-pipes, the feeling of being *known* — and he thought about the sealed door at the end of the vault corridor, and the machinery underneath it that had been drawing power, patiently, for longer than he had been alive.

He wrote one more line:

*Note: the dream started nine days before the signal.*

He closed the journal. Outside the viewport, the stars did not move, and the silence of twenty years pressed against the hull of the *Patient Gardener* like a hand — and somewhere behind him, in the dark of the ship's systems, a process he had never authorized quietly finished compiling a message, and waited, with a patience older than he was, for him to be ready to read it.
