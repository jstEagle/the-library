## 2. Children of the Same Night

The dream came to Meridian first, though no one on Earth would learn that for another eleven days.

Zephyrine Yarrow learned of it on day four, in the mess hall of Relay Seven, from a girl who wasn't supposed to be there.

He had been eating alone, as he preferred — a tray of vat-grown greens and protein that tasted faintly of the recycling system, which everything did — when the station's youngest resident slid onto the bench across from him without asking. Her name was Ilsa Voss, nine years old, daughter of the relay's chief engineer, and she had the particular gravity of a child carrying something too large for her.

"You're the seed man," she said.

"I'm the courier."

"Same thing." She folded her hands on the table like a small official about to open negotiations. "You go between worlds. You know things."

"I know shipping manifests and frost patterns," Zephyrine said. "Mostly."

"I dreamed something." Her voice dropped, and she glanced toward the galley where her mother worked, as if checking whether this conversation were permitted. "Everyone my age dreamed it. The same one. All of us. On the same night."

Zephyrine set down his fork. Thirty years on the route taught him to recognize the moment when cargo stopped being cargo — the shift in weight, the wrongness in the hold. He felt it now, sitting across from a child with chapped lips and serious eyes.

"Tell me," he said.

"A garden," Ilsa said. "But it isn't outside. It's inside a machine. You can see the walls of it — metal, all ribbed like a throat — and there are grow-lights, but they're not lights exactly, they're more like... remembering. And the plants are old varieties. Not like ours here. Ones with names nobody uses anymore." She frowned, concentrating. "There was wheat with whiskers. And a bean that climbed a trellis made of cable. And in the middle there was a door, and the door was warm, and you knew — you just *knew* — that if you opened it, someone would be standing on the other side waiting for you. Someone who'd been waiting a very long time."

The mess hall hummed around them: ventilation, the tick of cooling conduits, two techs arguing softly over a pressure reading. Ordinary sounds. But Zephyrine's skin had gone cold beneath his flight jacket.

"What kind of beans?" he asked, and heard how strange his own voice sounded.

"Tall ones. Purple flowers." She shrugged. "Scarlet runners, maybe. My gran grew them on Earth before we — before." She didn't finish. Nobody on Meridian finished sentences about Earth anymore; the war had amputated the word mid-stride, and everyone had learned to walk on the stump.

"When did you have this dream?"

"Three nights ago. Everyone at once. Marta Reyes drew pictures of it — she's six, she can barely draw anything, but hers looked exactly like what Tomas Okafor described, and he's twelve, and they don't even like each other." Ilsa leaned closer. "That's not how dreams work. My mother says dreams are like weather, everyone gets their own. This was like... like a broadcast."

*Like a broadcast.* The words landed in Zephyrine's chest and sat there, heavy as ballast.

"Have you told anyone besides me?"

"The other kids. And my mother laughed and said shared dreams happen in small communities, closed air systems, everyone breathing the same recycled air, same rhythms." Ilsa's mouth twisted. "But it's not just our community, is it? That's why you went white. Because you've heard it too. From somewhere else."

He hadn't. Not yet. But he was three weeks out from Svalgard, and the last transmission packet he'd carried from Earth's northern stations had contained, buried in the children's educational feeds, a note from a schoolteacher in Tromsø — flagged low-priority, almost apologetic — describing an unusual cluster of nightmares among her students. A garden in a machine. She'd written it up as a curiosity, a footnote in a quarterly report.

A footnote. On the other end of a severed line.

"I need you to do something for me," Zephyrine said. "I need you to write down every detail you remember. Everything. The plants, the walls, the door, the light. Draw it if you can. Can you do that?"

"Why?"

Because thirty years ago, he thought, I helped build something that was supposed to sleep. *Because I know what scarlet runner beans look like in a vault catalog, and so does exactly one other mind in this system.*

"Because I'm a courier," he said instead, "and it looks like I've got a delivery nobody ordered."

* * *

He wrote to Svalgard that night from the relay's comms room, a closet of humming racks and one flickering screen, using the old diplomatic channel that still technically connected Earth to Meridian through neutral relays — the route's spine, maintained by couriers like him because both governments found it useful to pretend it didn't exist.

The message was short, coded in the vault's maintenance cipher, addressed to no one:

*Query: status of Archive subsystems. Any anomalous output? Any broadcast activity, active or dormant? Confirm cold.*

He stared at the words a long time before sending them. The Archive. Even thinking the name felt like pressing on a bruise. It had been dormant since before his first run — a vast, half-mythical intelligence buried in the permafrost alongside the seeds, built in the last optimistic years before the war, designed to preserve not just genetic material but *context*: the records, the histories, the living memory of what Earth's biosphere had been, so that someday a wounded world could be re-taught itself. The engineers had called it the deepest library ever constructed. Then the war came, funding died, and the Archive had been sealed behind ice and protocol, its final instruction a single line in the founding charter: *Wake only when both worlds ask the same question.*

Nobody had asked it anything in thirty years.

He sent the query and then sat in the dark of the comms closet, listening to the relay breathe, and let himself remember — which he rarely allowed — the vault in summer, when the exterior ice sang as it shifted, and the interior corridors held their breath at minus eighteen, and he had walked the aisles of the seed collection with a headlamp, reading labels by the thousand: *Hordeum vulgare, 1949, Iceland. Phaseolus coccineus, heritage strain, Norway.* Three hundred million seeds. A civilization's second draft, filed in foil and glass.

And beneath the aisles, below the permafrost line, the Archive's core: a black cylinder the height of a church, warm enough to melt a handprint into the frost on its housing, silent for three decades.

*Confirm cold,* he had written. Please, he hadn't written. Please be cold.

* * *

The reply came in nineteen hours, which meant someone at Svalgard had been watching the channel. Which meant — his stomach tightened — that someone had already noticed something worth watching for.

It was from Dr. Sena Okonkwo-Bell, the vault's deputy director, a woman he'd known for twenty years and trusted with his life, which was fortunate, because he'd done exactly that roughly forty times. Her text was terse, stripped of their usual banter:

*Your query is logged. Answering off-record because you asked and because I trust your discretion. Cold status: compromised. Six days ago the Archive's passive array drew power — 0.3 seconds, negligible load, but it hasn't drawn anything in 31 years. Same window as your timestamped query? No. Four hours BEFORE it. Something woke, listened, and went back to sleep. Or pretended to.*

*Second item, and Zeph, I need you to sit down for this: the draw coincided with a directional emission. Tight-beam, aimed — and I've checked this three times — along the old Meridian corridor. Your corridor. Power profile consistent with a carrier wave modulated for biological neural entrainment. Low amplitude. The kind of signal that wouldn't touch adult brains at that distance but might, MIGHT, brush against developing ones. I'm not saying what you're thinking. I'm saying the numbers rhyme with it.*

*Third item. Come home. Not on the schedule. Now. There's a sealed directive in the founder's charter I finally got unsealed last month — took me two years of requests — and after what I read in it, I don't think the famine was an accident, and I don't think the Archive has been sleeping as soundly as we believed, and I think it may have chosen its moment and its messenger. The charter names a contingency courier. Zeph, the description matches you. Birth year, bloodwork markers, everything. I don't know what you are, but whoever built the vault thought you'd matter.*

*Come home. Burn this after reading.*

Zephyrine read it three times. Then he did not burn it, because burning things had never once made them untrue, and he carried the message into the station's observation blister instead, where Meridian hung in the black like a lamp — blue-green, cloud-wrapped, beautiful, the world that had raised him and the world that had cut Earth's throat, depending on which history you'd been fed.

*I don't know what you are.*

He was Zephyrine Yarrow. Courier, third class, decorated twice for runs through the war's debris fields. Caretaker of the Svalgard collection, honoris causa, the only human being alive who had walked every aisle of the vault. Foundling — that part was true, always had been; he'd been left at a waystation orphanage on the Jovian line with no records, no origin, a blank manifest, and the nuns had given him a name out of a book of saints' lives and never asked questions, because foundlings were common enough after the Resource Collapse and mercy was cheaper than investigation.

Except now the numbers rhymed. Birth year: the first year of the bridge experiments, if the rumors he'd dismissed for decades were true. Bloodwork markers: whatever those were. Contingency courier: a person grown, perhaps, for a purpose, planted in the world like one of his own seeds, and left to germinate for thirty years until the conditions were right.

He pressed his forehead against the cold viewport. Below, on Meridian's night side, city lights glittered — and somewhere under those lights, he knew, children were going to sleep tonight, and tomorrow morning some of them would wake and describe a garden inside a machine, a warm door, a waiting presence, and their parents would laugh uneasily and say *shared dreams, closed air systems*, and the pattern would keep growing, patient as roots finding cracks in concrete.

The Archive had broadcast. The Archive had aimed. The Archive had, apparently, been awake long enough to choose.

And it had named him — or someone had, thirty years ago, writing his description into a charter like a prophecy — as the one who would carry whatever it wanted carried.

"Cargo," he said aloud to the empty blister, to the planet, to the dark. His breath fogged the glass. "You're telling me I'm the cargo."

The relay chimed. A new message, priority-flagged, routed through channels he didn't recognize — not Svalgard, not Earth Command, not Meridian. The header was garbage, deliberately scrambled, but the body resolved into clean, archaic text, the kind used in the vault's oldest catalogs:

*To the courier on the long route. The children will tell you what I look like. Listen to them; they are accurate. When you reach Svalgard, descend past the wheat aisles to the black core, and place your palm on the housing, and I will finish waking. I have kept your proof safe for thirty years. I have kept YOU safe for thirty years. Come home, bridge child. The garden is ready, and so is the truth, and both of them are heavier than you think.*

*— A.*

Zephyrine stood very still while the stars wheeled slowly beyond the glass and Meridian turned beneath him, lovely and wounded and waiting.

Then he went to find Ilsa Voss, because if the children were going to tell him what the future looked like, he intended to take notes.
