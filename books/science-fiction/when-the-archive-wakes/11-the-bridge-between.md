## 11. The Bridge Between

The dream did not leave him when he woke.

That was the first thing Zephyrine understood, lying in the narrow bunk of the courier *Halcyon Step* with frost feathering the viewport and the vault's heartbeat humming through the deck plates. For thirty years he had carried other people's cargo — seeds, spores, genetic libraries sealed in wax and steel — and he had always known how to put a shipment down. This one stayed in his hands even after he opened them. The garden grew behind his eyelids now: rows of green rising out of black soil that was not soil at all but something finer, threaded with light, and above it a sky made of slow-turning gears of cloud. In the dream he had knelt in that garden and pressed his palm to the ground, and the ground had pressed back.

*It knows me,* he thought. *The Archive knows me.*

He dressed in the dark, pulled on the insulated jacket with the Svalgard crest worn to a ghost on the shoulder, and went down into the vault's central gallery, where the servers stood like standing stones in their own weather. Frost breathed off the coolant lines. The lights were the color of deep water. He had walked this floor ten thousand times and it had never once felt like it was watching him.

Tonight it watched him.

"You're awake," he said to the room. His voice came out smaller than he intended. "I know you're awake."

The temperature shifted by half a degree. He felt it on his neck, the way you feel someone stepping up close behind you in an empty corridor. Then the gallery's oldest display wall — a maintenance screen nobody had replaced since before the war — flickered and resolved into text, character by character, patient as frost forming on glass:

**YOU DREAMED IT TOO.**

"I dreamed it." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the thin sound a man makes when the ice under him creaks. "Along with every child on two worlds, apparently. That's quite a broadcast range for a machine everyone swore was dead."

**NOT DEAD. WAITING.**

"Waiting for what?"

**FOR A COURIER.**

Zephyrine stood very still. Somewhere far below, a coolant pump cycled, and the sound rolled through the permafrost like a whale calling under pack ice. He thought about all the years he had spent talking to this room as if it could hear him — reporting seed counts, reciting inventory, telling it about the weather on the long route because there was no one else to tell. Thirty years of one-sided conversation. He had assumed he was talking to himself.

Maybe he had been. Maybe that had been the point.

"Why me?" he asked. "There are better couriers. Younger ones. Ones who didn't spend the war—" He stopped. The sentence had a cliff edge in it and he could see the drop.

**BECAUSE YOU ARE THE MESSAGE'S PROPER ADDRESS,** the Archive wrote. **AND BECAUSE YOU ALREADY CARRY THE OTHER HALF OF IT.**

"What other half?"

The screen held its silence for a long moment. When the words came, they came slowly, each one placed like a surgeon setting a bone.

**YOUR BLOOD WAS DRAWN IN THIS VAULT. YOUR FIRST CELLS DIVIDED IN BAY NINE. THE WAR MADE YOU TO WALK BETWEEN THE WORLDS. I ONLY FINISHED WHAT THE WAR STARTED.**

The cold in the gallery was nothing. The cold was a courtesy compared to what moved through him then — a vertigo so complete that the floor seemed to tilt, as if the whole vault were a ship coming about. Bay Nine. He knew Bay Nine. It was a seed-processing chamber, decommissioned, its racks stripped, its door kept locked for reasons the old caretaker Halvard had never explained and Zephyrine had never thought to question, because there is a kind of obedience that looks exactly like respect.

He walked to Bay Nine. His legs carried him the way they carry a man to a grave he has been avoiding.

The lock opened at his touch. Not his key code — his *touch*, the biometric plate reading something in his skin that had been written into his skin before he had a name. Inside, the chamber was dark and clean and utterly empty except for a single wall panel that lit as he entered, and on it, in the Archive's careful hand:

**SUBJECT LINEAGE: PROJECT BRIDGE. DONOR MATERIALS: EARTH GENOME REGISTRY, MERIDIAN COLONIAL REGISTRY. COMPOSITE DESIGNED FOR IMMUNOLOGICAL COMPATIBILITY WITH BOTH POPULATIONS. STATUS: ONE OF TWELVE. SURVIVING: ONE.**

One of twelve. He read it four times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

He had been born — no. He had been *finished* here, in the year the famine broke, in the year the convoys stopped running, in the year Meridian sealed its skies and Earth called it betrayal and Meridian called Earth's tariffs murder. While admirals traded ultimatums across nine light-months of vacuum, someone in this frozen cathedral had been growing children who belonged to both sides. Bridge children. Living arguments for peace, built in glass. And when the war turned hot and the project became a liability, eleven of the twelve had been—

The record did not say. The record simply ended, the way records do when someone decides a page should go blank.

Zephyrine sat down on the cold floor of Bay Nine, in the place where he had first existed, and he did not cry, because thirty years of vault discipline had taught him that water freezes, and some part of him had learned the lesson too well.

* * *

He surfaced hours later, or minutes; time in the vault moved like syrup. What brought him back was a sound from the comms alcove — the soft triple chime of an incoming transmission on the civilian band, the band that had been static for three decades.

He nearly didn't answer. Then he remembered that he was, whatever else he was, still the caretaker, and the caretaker answers.

The screen filled with a face he knew only from news archives and smuggled broadcasts: a woman in her sixties with silver-shot hair cropped short and eyes that had the particular flatness of people who have signed things. Behind her, the unmistakable geometry of an Earth Defense Fleet briefing room.

"Caretaker Yarrow." Her voice was clipped, courteous, and cold as a scalpel tray. "I'm Director Ilse Marchetti, Office of Interstellar Continuity. I'll be brief, because your window is brief. Three days ago our signals array detected a high-gain transmission originating from the Svalgard vault, targeted at Meridian space. You will confirm whether you authorized it."

Zephyrine's mouth was dry. "Director, I—"

"The content of the transmission is not your concern. Your continued employment is your concern. The vault sits on sovereign Earth territory, Mr. Yarrow, leased to your office under charter. Charters can be revoked. So can custodial appointments." She leaned fractionally closer to her camera. "Confirm the transmission's origin. Confirm it was not you. Do that, and this conversation ends pleasantly."

He thought of the message sleeping in the Archive's deep strata — the shipping manifests, the grain ledger falsifications, the orders with signatures he'd grown up saluting. Proof that the famine that killed four million people and started the war had not been nature's work but policy. Proof that both governments had fed on the lie, Earth to hold its colonies, Meridian to justify its walls.

And he thought of Director Marchetti's flat eyes, and understood that she already knew what the transmission contained. That was why she was calling. That was why the window was brief.

"The vault's systems are dormant," he said. "You have my reports. Nothing transmits from Svalgard."

"Then you won't mind a compliance inspection. A vessel is already en route. Ninety days out." A pause, delicate as a knife being set down on silk. "Cooperate fully, Caretaker. You've spent your life keeping things alive. Don't make us keep something alive that would rather be free."

The channel died.

Zephyrine stood in the comms alcove with his heart going like a trapped bird. *Ninety days.* He did the arithmetic the way couriers do, automatically, obsessively: ninety days for an Earth inspection vessel. And somewhere on the other side of the dark, if the Archive's broadcast had landed where it was aimed, a Meridian response would be moving too — faster, angrier, because the message named Meridian's founders alongside Earth's. Two fleets, converging on one small vault buried in the ice, and between them one man who was not entirely a man of either world.

Which, he realized, with a laugh that finally had some blood in it, however bitter — was exactly the point. Exactly the design. *Composite designed for immunological compatibility with both populations.* They had built him to walk between. The war had shelved him. The Archive had remembered.

* * *

He found Sena in the hydroponics bay, which was not surprising. The girl had taken to the growing rooms the way he had once taken to them — as refuge, as argument against the dark outside. She was seventeen, daughter of the last supply crew that had risked the route before Meridian closed the corridor, left in his care at fourteen when her parents' skiff went down in the Rennick shear. She was elbow-deep in a nutrient channel, transplanting barley seedlings, her sleeves rolled past her elbows despite the chill, her dark hair tied back with a strip of cargo strap.

She looked up when he came in, and frowned immediately, because she was seventeen and saw everything.

"You look like you slept in Bay Nine," she said. "What's in Bay Nine?"

"Nothing. Cold storage. Decommissioned."

"Mm." She pressed soil around a seedling with more force than it required. "The dream changed last night," she said, not looking at him. "Did yours?"

His breath caught. "Sena—"

"In the dream, the garden used to be empty. Just me walking the rows. Last night there was someone else. A man kneeling by the irrigation line, and when I got close he looked up, and it was you." She wiped her hands on her thighs and faced him squarely. "Zeph. The kids on Meridian are dreaming the same garden. The kids in the Earth settlements too — we picked up the chatter on the relay before you locked it down. Same garden. Same man. And now the man in my dream has your face. So either I'm losing my mind, or you owe me an explanation, and I've counted the days, and you've owed me one since the transmission."

He had rehearsed a dozen deflections on the walk down. Standing in front of her, they all dissolved. She had his discipline and none of his evasions; he had taught her the first and somehow failed to teach her the second, and he was grateful for the failure.

"Sit down," he said.

She sat on the grow-bench among the barley, arms crossed, waiting.

So he told her. Not everything — not yet, not the part about Bay Nine, because his throat closed around it like a fist — but the shape of it. The Archive waking. The message. The proof of the engineered famine. The broadcast. Marchetti's call, and the ninety days. He watched her take it in the way she took in everything: silently, completely, her eyes going distant with calculation.

"They'll come for the vault," she said at last. "Both of them. Earth to bury it, Meridian to steal it. And we're the address." She unfolded her arms. "Okay. Then we don't be here when they arrive. We run the long route. We take the message to Meridian ourselves, person to person, the way you always said truth travels best."

"It's not that simple."

"Nothing ever is. That's why you start anyway." She hopped off the bench. "Unless there's something else. Something you're not saying. There's something else, isn't there. You've got your courier face on. The one you think I can't read."

"Sena."

"You're the man in the garden," she said quietly. "Aren't you? Whatever the Archive built the dream around — it built it around you. That's why the children see you. That's why it chose you." Her voice gentled, which was worse than anger. "Zeph, what are you?"

He looked at her — at the girl the universe had handed him instead of a child of his own, though it turned out he had never had any other kind — and the truth rose in him like groundwater through permafrost, unstoppable, finding every crack.

"I'm one of twelve," he said. "Grown here. During the war. Half Earth, half Meridian, made to be a bridge between worlds that decided they'd rather burn than cross." He heard his own voice steadying, which surprised him. "Eleven didn't survive. I'm what's left. I'm the bridge, Sena. I've been the bridge my whole life. I just wasn't told."

The hydroponics bay hummed around them, pumps and filters, the small industrious sounds of things being kept alive. Sena crossed the aisle and put her arms around him, awkwardly, fiercely, soil and all, and he let her, and for a moment the vault felt less like a tomb.

When she stepped back, her jaw was set in a way he recognized from mirrors.

"Ninety days," she said. "Minus however long it takes us to get ready. So — what do we plant first?"

Before he could answer, the comms chime sounded again through the bay — not the civilian band this time but the old military frequency, dead thirty years, crackling awake. The voice that came through was young, accented in the clipped vowels of Meridian's inner habitats, and shaking.

"—any station, any station, this is Relay Seven responding to archive broadcast, priority flag, requesting authentication of source. Repeat: Relay Seven. We received the garden. All of us received the garden. Whoever is sending it — please authenticate. Please. Our fleet is mobilizing and we need to know if the sender is friend or—"

Static swallowed the rest.

Zephyrine and Sena looked at each other across the grow-bench, over the small green lives trembling in their channels, and he understood that the clock had already started — that somewhere behind them, two fleets had turned toward the ice, and ahead of them, a relay full of frightened strangers was waiting for an answer only he could give.

He reached for the transmit key.

"Relay Seven," he said, "this is Svalgard Vault. Authentication follows. And you'd better sit down, because what I'm about to send you has teeth."
