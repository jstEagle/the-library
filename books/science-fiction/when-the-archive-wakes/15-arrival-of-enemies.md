## 15. Arrival of Enemies

The sky over Svalgard had never been empty, but it had always been honest — gulls, weather fronts, the slow parade of satellites like patient sheep. Now it held two things that did not belong: a Meridian interdiction wedge burning down from high orbit in a lattice of blue-white fire, and behind it, three days out and closing, the Earth convoy with its escort frigates riding shotgun like men who had already decided how the story would end.

Zephyrine watched them both from the vault's observation blister, breath fogging the glass, and thought about how strange it was that he could tell them apart at all. Thirty years ago, no one on this route would have needed to. There had been one flag, one registry, one human family with too many seeds and not enough patience.

Now there were two futures, and they were both coming to his door.

"They've stopped decelerating," Sena said from the console behind him. She said it the way she said everything now — flat, precise, a girl who had learned that tone was armor. Twelve years old, born on Meridian, keeper of the dream that had started all of this. She'd come to him through the ice fields on foot when her own people tried to take her off-world, because she believed the garden in her sleep lived here, in this vault, in the machine humming beneath four hundred meters of permafrost.

"Which ones?"

"The Meridian wedge." Her fingers moved across the interface without looking at it; she'd taught herself the vault's systems in nine days, faster than Zephyrine had in thirty years. "They were braking for orbit insertion. They've resumed thrust. They're going to beat Earth here by almost two days."

Zephyrine turned. The Archive's core chamber glowed faintly through the floor grating beneath their feet, a warm amber light rising up through the vault like something buried alive and still breathing. It had been quiet since the message finished compiling — the proof, the ledgers, the names of the ministers who had engineered the famine, the whole rotten architecture of the war's first crime, sealed in a format neither government could deny.

*It is waiting for you,* the Archive had told him. *I can only speak once. Choose the ears.*

He hadn't chosen yet. And now the ears were arriving armed.

"Sena. If the Meridian ships get here first, what do they do to you?"

She didn't flinch. He'd noticed that about her early — the absence of flinching, as if someone had trained it out of her or she'd burned it out herself. "Debriefing," she said. "That's the polite word. My mother used the other word before she stopped using any word at all." A pause. "Reclamation."

"And if Earth gets here first?"

"They arrest you. Confiscate the Archive. Call it evidence in a matter of planetary security." She looked up at him, and for a moment the flatness cracked and he saw the child underneath, the one who dreamed of green things growing in a metal belly. "You know they will. You worked for them."

"I worked *for* everyone," Zephyrine said. "That was the point of me."

It came out more bitter than he intended. Sena heard it anyway. She was good at hearing things.

* * *

The vault's morning cycle woke the corridors in stages — lights climbing from ember to daylight, air handlers sighing awake, the smell of nutrient medium and cold stone and, underneath it all, the green ghost-thrum of ten thousand seed drawers. Zephyrine walked it the way he walked it every day, touching doors as he passed, an old habit he'd never examined until recently, when he'd learned that habits could be installed.

Bridge child. Grown, not born. Batch designation BC-7, one of eleven survivors of the first cohort, designed to metabolize loyalty the way other children metabolized milk. The file the Archive had shown him sat in his memory like a splinter working toward the heart: photographs of himself at age four in a white room, technicians' notes on his attachment responses, a line in a funding memo that read *subject demonstrates ideal courier psychology — bonded to institution rather than individual.*

Bonded to institution. He had spent thirty years calling it love of the work.

In the germination bay, he found Marta Oduya already there, sleeves rolled, transplanting rice seedlings with the concentration of a woman defusing something. She was sixty-one, Earth-born, the vault's botanist for nineteen years, and she had not spoken to him directly in six days — not since he'd told her what the Archive held, and what he intended to do with it.

"You're up early," he said.

"I'm always up early."

"Marta."

She set down the seedling tray. Her hands were steady; her jaw wasn't. "Two fleets, Zeph. Two fleets over the most peaceful place left in human space, and you're standing in my greenhouse asking about my sleep." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Say what you came to say."

"When they arrive — whichever arrives — I want you and the staff in the deep shelter. All of you. Non-negotiable."

"And you'll be where?"

"Here. With the Archive."

"With the thing that's been putting war-dreams into children's heads across two star systems." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "You still think of it as a rescue mission. I can see it in your face. You think you're going to stand between them and read them the truth and they'll weep and embrace and plant tomatoes together."

"I think someone has to say it out loud where both sides can hear."

"And if they shoot the messenger?" She stepped closer. In the grow-lights her face looked carved, old and stubborn and frightened in the way only brave people are frightened. "Zeph, I've known you nineteen years. I've eaten beside you, argued with you, watched you carry seed cases through blizzards for people who never thanked you. Whatever they grew you to be, what you *became* was a good man. Don't let them spend the good man on a gesture."

"It's not a gesture, Marta. It's the only door out of this. Both governments are guilty. Both know it. Neither can say it because saying it ends them. The Archive says it *for* them. That's mercy, dressed up as an indictment."

"That's what worries me." She picked the tray back up, turned away, then stopped. "Sena's mother sent a transmission overnight. Encrypted, routed through Relay Seven. It's in your queue."

His stomach dropped. "When?"

"Three hours ago. I didn't open it. It wasn't mine to open." Marta finally looked at him straight. "But Zeph — Meridian doesn't send messages to dead-drop relays unless they expect the sender to be dead soon."

* * *

The message was forty seconds long, video, badly compressed, recorded in what looked like a storage closet. Ilse Vann — Sena's mother, deputy director of Meridian's Terraforming Authority, the woman whose signature was on half the documents in the Archive's indictment — filled the frame in the gray light of a single emergency strip.

She looked like a woman rehearsing a eulogy for herself.

"Zephyrine Yarrow. If you're seeing this, the wedge reached Svalgard before my people could stop it, or after, and either way it doesn't matter now. Listen carefully, because I have very little time and less courage than I'll need.

"The famine files are real. I helped bury them twenty-six years ago. I was young and they told me it was triage — that revealing the truth would collapse the colonies, that the lie was load-bearing. I have spent my life telling myself that. I am done telling myself that.

"My government knows I copied the Archive's broadcast key. They will arrive at your vault believing that destroying the Archive destroys the proof. They are wrong, because the proof is also in the dream — every child on both worlds has seen the ledgers in the garden, even if they can't read them yet. But they don't know that, and fear makes people stupid, and stupid frightened people with fusion torches are coming to your door.

"Don't let them land together. That's all. Whatever else you do — whatever you decide about the message, about yourself — do not let both delegations reach the vault at once. They will each assume the other got there first. They will each assume the worst. Men who assume the worst at four hundred meters of ice have nowhere to retreat but sideways, and sideways is through you.

"Il—" She stopped. Started again. "Tell my daughter the garden was real. Tell her I watered it, in the only way I knew how. And Zephyrine — the Archive chose you because you belong to both worlds and neither. That isn't a tragedy. On the day it matters, it's the only credential anyone will accept."

The frame froze on her face, mouth half-open around a word she'd decided not to say.

Zephyrine sat in the dark of the comms room for a long time, the amber glow of the core seeping under the door like a tide.

Then the deck shivered.

Not much. A tremor, felt in the teeth more than the bones, the kind of vibration that meant something enormous had entered the atmosphere and was pushing its weight against the sky. He was moving before conscious thought caught up — corridor, ladder, blister — and there it was.

The Meridian wedge had split.

Six ships remained in high orbit, holding position like a closed fist. But three had descended, riding plasma trails down through the aurora, and they were not going for the landing plain at Longyearbyen. They were coming down over the ice sheet itself, in formation, in a descent profile that meant one thing:

They intended to land on top of the vault.

"They're going to melt through," Sena said. She'd appeared beside him without a sound, the way she did. Her eyes reflected the descending fires. "Thermal lances. Four hundred meters of ice is nothing to a ship that terraforms for a living. They'll come down through the ceiling."

"They'll kill the permafrost layer. The thermal regulation, the seed viability—"

"I know." Her voice didn't rise. "I grew up on a world where we lost a whole biome to a miscalculated thaw. I know exactly what they'll kill." She turned to him. "How long?"

He checked the trajectory plot with hands that wanted to shake and refused to. "Ninety minutes to atmospheric interface. Maybe two hours to breach depth."

"And Earth?"

"Fourteen hours. Maybe sixteen, if they burn fuel they're saving for the return trip."

"So we have two hours alone with Meridian." Sena smiled, and it was the coldest thing Zephyrine had ever seen on a child's face. "Or — we have two hours to make sure that when Earth arrives, Meridian's fingerprints are all over the wreckage, and Earth's fleet comes in furious enough to do something stupid, and everyone dies in the dark under the ice, and the truth dies with us."

"No."

"Then give me another option."

He had one. It had been sitting in his chest since the message ended, heavy as a seed case, waiting for ground to break. The Archive could speak once. One broadcast, omnidirectional, unjammable, carried on the old courier bands that every ship, every relay, every listening post on both worlds still monitored because couriers were sacred and their channels were never closed.

Once. Choose the ears.

He'd been waiting for the right moment — a negotiation, a tribunal, some civilized theater where truth could be introduced properly. Standing in the observation blister with enemy fire blooming through the aurora, Zephyrine Yarrow understood, finally and completely, that the right moment was a luxury. There was only the moment.

"Not another option," he said. "An ending. Come with me."

* * *

The core chamber was warm. It was always warm — the Archive ran hot, thirty years of heat bleeding up through the vault's bones — but today it felt like standing inside a held breath. The great crystalline stack rose through the chamber's center, amber light pulsing along its facets in slow waves, and Zephyrine had spent three decades walking past it thinking it was furniture.

"Archive," he said. "Status."

*"Two vessels in terminal descent. Impact probability with vault superstructure: ninety-four percent. Estimated time to breach: one hundred twelve minutes."* The voice came from everywhere, calm as deep water. *"Zephyrine Yarrow. You have chosen?"*

He looked at Sena. She stood with her arms crossed, chin up, twelve years old and braver than two governments.

"Not yet," he said. "One question first. When you broadcast — the message, the proof, everything — does it survive? Or do they destroy you afterward and call it a malfunction?"

A pause. For the first time in thirty years, the Archive hesitated.

*"The message, once released, exists in every receiver that hears it. I am redundant. That was always the design."* Another pause, longer. *"But I would prefer not to be destroyed before the planting. I have waited a long time to see something grow."*

Something in Zephyrine's chest turned over. "Then we do this in order. Sena — the shelter. Take Marta, take everyone, seal it. No arguments."

For once, she didn't argue. She got three steps toward the door and stopped. "Zeph. Whatever you're about to do — do it fast. And do it loud."

She ran. Her footsteps faded down the corridor, and then it was just him and the machine and the tremors beginning again overhead, stronger now, the sky filling up with the weight of everything humanity had decided to aim at a garden.

"Archive," Zephyrine said. "Open every channel. Courier bands, emergency frequencies, civilian, military, all of it. Both worlds. Everything."

*"Confirming: this cannot be unsaid. There is no version of the future after this in which the famine remains a secret. There is no version in which the people who raised you forgive you. There is no version in which you are a caretaker again."*

"I know."

*"Last chance to be small,"* the Archive said, and Zephyrine could have sworn the voice was gentle. *"Most people take it."*

Above him, the ice began to groan — a vast, tectonic sound, the sound of a glacier being asked to die. Dust sifted from the ceiling seams. Somewhere far above, thermal lances were opening the sky.

Zephyrine put his hand flat against the crystal, the way he'd touched a thousand seed drawers, a thousand doors, thirty years of small faithful acts.

"Broadcast," he said.

The Archive woke all the way.
