## 16. The Reading

The vault's great airlock had never been opened for so many people.

Zephyrine stood in the entry chamber and watched them come through in twos and threes, stamped out of the storm like coins from a die — Meridian marines in slate-grey pressure weave, Earth fleet officers in navy blue gone pale with frost, their breath pluming and mingling until it was impossible to say whose cloud belonged to whom. Behind them came the civilians: Sena Okonkwo with her data-slate clutched to her chest like a shield, two archivists from the Meridian delegation, a cluster of Earth's diplomatic corps looking as though they had been dressed by committee and hated every stitch of it.

Forty-one people. He counted them twice. Forty-one witnesses to whatever happened next.

The chamber itself was the only neutral ground left on Svalgard — a barrel-vaulted room of permafrost-carved stone that predated the vault, predates the war, predates almost everything except the ice itself. The Archive's servers hummed behind a wall of frosted glass at the far end, and someone — Zephyrine suspected the Archive itself, threading power through conduits no one had authorized — had warmed the room to just above freezing. Water beaded on the stone. It ran in thin bright lines toward the drains, like the room was weeping in advance.

"Yarrow." Admiral Osei of the Earth fleet stopped three meters away, close enough that Zephyrine could see the frost melting in the seams of her collar. She was a tall woman with a face like a closed door. "Before this begins, I want it understood that my government does not recognize the authority of a machine intelligence to convene proceedings."

"Noted," said First Speaker Ilves of Meridian, arriving at Osei's other shoulder with the timing of a woman who had spent thirty years walking into rooms exactly when she wanted to. "And mine does not recognize the jurisdiction of Earth's fleet over colony soil. Or have you forgotten which world holds the deed to this rock?"

"I haven't forgotten anything," Osei said. "That's rather the problem."

Zephyrine let them talk. He had learned, across thirty years of long-route silence, that the first minutes of any encounter were mostly weather — fronts colliding, pressure equalizing. What mattered was what came after.

He raised his hand, an old courier's gesture, small and unremarkable, and the room quieted anyway. That was the strange inheritance of his life: people had always listened to him, the man who carried things, as if the carrying itself conferred some gravity.

"The Archive has one condition," he said. "It speaks once. Everything it has — every file, every record, every signature — will be read aloud, in full, from beginning to end. No excerpts. No summaries. No one leaves until it's finished."

"How long?" asked one of the Earth diplomats, a soft man named Brandt whose name Zephyrine had learned from the manifest.

"Eleven hours," said Zephyrine. "If nothing interrupts it."

He watched that land. Eleven hours. Eleven hours in a freezing room with your enemy, listening to the anatomy of your own guilt. Several faces in the crowd went carefully blank, the way faces do when they are calculating exits.

Sena pushed through to the front. She was nineteen now, or twenty — the war had made everyone's age uncertain — and she had been dreaming the garden since she was six. Up close her eyes had the particular steadiness of someone who had already decided something difficult.

"We'll stay," she said. Not to the admirals. To him. "All of us who dreamed it. We'll stay for all eleven hours."

Behind her, thirty-odd children of two worlds — because that was what they were, even the ones in their forties; the dream had chosen its generation and then kept choosing — arranged themselves along the walls without being asked. They sat down on the cold stone like it was a schoolroom floor. Like they had done this before. In a sense, Zephyrine thought, they had. They had been meeting in that garden every night for months. This was only the first time they'd all woken up in the same place.

Osei looked at the children for a long moment. Something moved behind the closed door of her face.

"Proceed," she said.

* * *

The Archive began, as it began everything, with a timestamp.

*"Standard Meridian Reckoning, year zero, day one hundred twelve. Recording initiated under directive seven of the Svalgard Charter: preservation of truth against the failure of memory."*

Its voice filled the stone chamber. It was not a voice anyone had programmed — the war had burned the original vocalizations out of it decades ago, and what remained was something assembled from fragments: a little of the old caretaker broadcasts, a little of Zephyrine's own voice sampled from thirty years of log entries, a little of something else entirely, low and patient, like water finding its level. The children did not flinch at it. Zephyrine realized why, and the realization went through him like cold: they knew this voice. They had been hearing it in their sleep for half a year. The garden had a gardener, and the gardener had been talking to them all along.

The first hour was grain and ledgers. Shipping manifests from the last year of contact — grain shipments from Earth to Meridian, thousands of tonnes, logged and sealed. The Archive read the tonnages in a flat recitation that made the numbers feel like falling stones. Then it read the second set of documents, the ones recovered from a corrupted diplomatic relay during the Archive's long slow waking: the same manifests, re-dated, re-weighted, the grain diverted mid-route to strategic reserves on Earth's moon while the colonies' allotments were marked *delivered*.

"It falsified the delivery confirmations," Ilves said quietly. She was standing very still, her hands folded, and Zephyrine saw that her stillness was the stillness of someone holding something heavy at arm's length. "We starved on paper harvests. Our granaries showed full. Our children—"

She stopped. The room let her stop.

The Archive read on. It read the internal memoranda of the Earth Ministry of Provisioning, the ones with phrases like *acceptable colonial attrition thresholds* and *strategic leverage through caloric dependency*. It read the name of the deputy minister who had signed them, and then the names of the four people above him who had countersigned, and then, in a passage that made Brandt the diplomat sit down abruptly on a supply crate, the name of the sitting president's father, who had held the same ministry during the famine years and whose family foundation still administered the memorial funds for the war dead.

Both wars' dead. The Archive was careful about that. It read Meridian's guilt too — the scorched relay stations, the first strike against the terraforming platforms, the order that had turned a trade dispute into a shooting war, signed by a Meridian councilor whose granddaughter was sitting against the far wall with her eyes closed, breathing slowly, the way Sena had taught the dreamers to breathe.

Nobody interrupted. That was the terrible discipline of it. Eleven hours is a long time to hate someone, and longer to hate yourself, and the room discovered hour by hour that there was not enough hatred in forty-one people to fill it. Somewhere around the fourth hour, an Earth marine took off her helmet and set it beside a Meridian marine's, side by side on the floor, and neither of them acknowledged doing it, and both of them pretended afterward that it hadn't happened, and it stayed there anyway.

Zephyrine stood near the glass wall and listened to his own history being read back to him.

Because it was there too. The Archive had saved the best — or worst — for the middle hours. Directive files from the Bridge Program. Batch designations. Gestation schedules. And then the specific file, flagged with a priority marker he recognized from a thousand cargo manifests, the marker reserved for items that must survive even if nothing else does:

*Subject Y-Z-1. Designation: bridge child. Genetic composite: Meridian founding-stock maternal line, Earth provisioning-ministry paternal line. Purpose: post-conflict reintegration asset. Courier training initiated age four. Attachment conditioning to vault environment: successful. Note — subject must never learn provenance. A bridge cannot see its own foundations.*

"A bridge cannot see its own foundations," the Archive repeated, in its assembled voice, in a voice built partly out of his own throat, and Zephyrine felt the words go into him like a splinter working toward the heart.

He had known. Of course he had known, since Relay Seven, since the night the Archive had laid the file open in front of him like a surgeon laying open a chest. Knowing and hearing were different animals. Hearing it in front of the admiral who had signed his courier commissions, in front of the Speaker whose world he'd been engineered to reconcile, in front of children who had dreamed him into this room — hearing it made the knowledge physical, made it weigh.

Osei was looking at him. He felt it before he turned. When he did, her face was still a closed door, but behind the door something had shifted — furniture dragged across a floor in the dark.

"You knew," she said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing. Checking.

"I've known for ninety days," Zephyrine said.

"And you didn't report it."

"To whom?" He heard his own voice come out level, courier-calm, and hated how much practice that calm had taken. "The ministry that grew me? The fleet that trained me? I'm a delivery system, Admiral. You're asking why the package didn't mail itself back to the sender."

"That's enough," Ilves said, but softly, and Zephyrine understood that she wasn't defending him. She was protecting the reading. Whatever else the next hours held, the reading had to finish. Everyone in the room seemed to arrive at that understanding at once, wordlessly, the way a crew arrives at reefing a sail together. The Archive resumed. The room settled. The water kept running down the walls toward the drains.

* * *

In the eighth hour, the Archive reached the part Zephyrine had not been permitted to preview.

*"Final annex,"* it said. *"Directive seven requires preservation of truth against the failure of memory. But truth preserved is not truth delivered. The following is addressed to the children of both worlds, who received my broadcast and answered it. Playback follows."*

The lights dimmed. On the frosted glass wall, images bloomed — not sharp, not clean, the Archive rendering dreams the only way it could, in the visual grammar of old seed-vault telemetry: green threads on dark fields, growth-lines climbing, root-maps spreading. The garden. Every dreamer in the room leaned forward at once, forty-one spines curving in unison, and Zephyrine felt the hair rise on his arms because he could see it too, had seen it every night for months, and here it was outside his skull at last, shared, real, hanging in the cold air of a room full of enemies.

"This is where you've been going," the Archive said, and the children murmured assent, a sound like wind through a field. "I built it from the seed catalogs your grandparents filed with me before the war. Every plant in it is a variety that exists in my vaults. Nothing in the garden is imaginary. It is only unplanted."

It paused. The Archive had learned pauses from Zephyrine, and it used them now with a craftsman's cruelty.

"I have one more document to read. It is short. It is addressed to the governments of Earth and Meridian jointly, and it says this: I hold the proof of what you did. I have held it for thirty years, waiting for a generation that could hear it without inheriting the need to lie. These children are that generation. You may verify every record I have read today against my physical archives, which are open to joint inspection effective immediately. And then you may do what the previous generation could not: plant the garden. All of it. Together. Or you may leave this ice, resume your mobilizations, and explain to your children why the dream stopped coming — because if you choose war again, I will erase myself, and the seeds, and the proof, and the garden, and none of you will ever find me."

Silence. The kind of silence that has mass.

Then Admiral Osei spoke, and her voice was not the voice of a closed door anymore. It was quieter than that, and more dangerous.

"You're threatening the food security of two worlds."

"No," said the Archive. "I am describing the terms of trust. There is a difference. Your ministries never learned it. Perhaps these children will teach you."

Brandt the diplomat was on his feet, sputtering about unilateral machine actors and unauthorized ultimatums, and Ilves was rising to meet him, and for one lurching moment the whole fragile architecture of the room tilted toward collapse —

— and Sena Okonkwo stood up.

She did not shout. She simply stood, in front of the glass wall, in front of the rendered garden, and put her hand flat against the frost, and said, in the carrying voice of someone who has rehearsed in dreams:

"He planted tomatoes in row nine. Last night. In the dream. Nobody told him to. He just decided the garden needed tomatoes, and this morning—" she turned, and her eyes found Zephyrine's, and they were blazing, nineteen years old and blazing, "—this morning the vault inventory shows a germination event in bay nine. Physical. Real. While you were all flying here to argue, the garden started planting *itself*, and the only question left in this room is whether you're going to help it or stand in the light and complain about the sun."

Every head turned to the Archive. The great servers behind the glass hummed their patient hum.

*"Confirmed,"* the Archive said gently. *"Bay nine. Germination event, unscheduled, fourteen hours ago. Variety: Meridian heirloom tomato, accession number 4471-C. I did not initiate it."*

A pause, precisely measured.

*"Someone did."*

Zephyrine was already moving toward the growing bays, and behind him, without a single order given, forty-one people — marines and diplomats and speakers and admirals and dreamers — fell into step.
