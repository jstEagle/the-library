## 1. The Long Route

The dead ship smelled of cardamom.

That was the first thing Ivo Voss noticed when the airlock cycled and the salvage tug's floodlamps cut through the dark. Not coolant, not scorched polymer, not the copper-and-iron tang he'd learned to expect from a hull that had failed its people — but cardamom, faint and domestic, drifting from a galley two decks down where someone had been grinding spice pods for tea when the sky came apart around them.

He stood in the airlock vestibule for a long moment, letting his suit's lamps sweep the corridor, and made himself breathe it in. Most investigators worked from the damage outward: find the breach, find the cause, find the party liable. Ivo worked the other direction. He started with the cardamom. The dead always told you who they were before they told you how they died, if you were patient enough to listen.

"Voss to *Meridian Claims Authority*, comm check." His voice sounded thin inside the helmet. "Boarding the wreck of the *Halcyon Route*, registry MR-7741-Kilo. Beginning survey."

The relay out at Meridian Station took eleven seconds to answer — light-lag dressed up as bureaucracy — and when it did, the voice was flat and bored. "*Survey acknowledged. Reminder: Ceres Reach has lodged a counter-claim on this vessel. Log all findings to shared channel 9. Do not remove personal effects.*"

"Do not remove personal effects," Ivo repeated, and signed off. Five years since the colonies had stopped speaking to each other except through lawyers, and now even a dead freighter had two governments fighting over its corpse. The *Halcyon Route* had been Meridian-flagged, hauling rare-earth concentrates from the belt down to Meridian orbit. But three of her crew had been born on Ceres Reach, and under the old treaties — the ones nobody honored anymore but nobody dared repeal — their families held inheritance rights. So the ship hung in contested space like a body neither morgue would claim, slowly freezing, waiting for an investigator from the neutral guilds to tell both sides what they already suspected: that no one was to blame, or everyone was.

He started in the galley, because that was where people were honest.

The spice grinder sat on the counter, half-full, its lid off. Beside it, a mug with a chipped rim — someone's favorite, kept despite the chip, the way people keep things. A child's drawing was magnetized to the cold-plate behind it: a stick-figure family under a green sun, rendered in the waxy crayons they sold at Meridian's orbital market for prices that should have shamed the sellers. *Come home soon Papa*, someone had written beneath it in careful block letters. Ivo photographed it twice, once for the file and once for himself, though he'd never admit the second exposure existed.

Suit log, timestamp. He moved on.

The galley told him there had been four crew aboard, not the three listed on the manifest. Four mugs on the drying rack. Four indentations worn into the bench cushions. And scratched into the table's surface, half-hidden under a laminate of dried tea, a game board — some grid-based thing played with bottle caps, one cap still sitting mid-square where a hand had left it mid-move. You didn't leave a game unfinished unless you expected to finish it later. People who knew they were going to die tidied up. This crew hadn't known anything.

Which fit the impossible part.

Ivo drifted aft along the spine corridor, gloves trailing the handrails, cataloguing. The *Halcyon Route* was a standard Meridian Lines heavy hauler, forty years old, ugly as a fist, and structurally boring — which was exactly why what had happened to her bothered him. At 0347 ship-time, eleven days ago, her main cargo bay had decompressed. Not a micrometeorite; the breach edges were smooth, melted inward, like something had reached through the hull and opened it. Not a warhead; there was no spall pattern, no residue, nothing either government's munitions would leave behind. Not structural fatigue; the bay's plating had passed full certification eight months prior, and Ivo had spent the morning reading the certification with the suspicion it deserved and finding nothing wrong with it.

A hole in a certified hull, opened from outside by nothing anyone could name, on the busiest silent trade route left between two colonies that pretended each other didn't exist.

*Impossible accidents*, the guild called them. There had been nine on this route in five years. Nine ships, forty-one dead, zero findings of liability, because you couldn't assign liability to a vacuum that behaved like it had intentions. Both governments accepted those findings with suspicious grace. Neither ever asked follow-up questions. Ivo had stopped asking his questions aloud years ago, but he'd never stopped keeping them. They lived in a private ledger in his head, nine entries long, each one ending in the same wordless itch.

He found the first body in the engineering gallery.

The man had been at the coolant manifold when the bay blew, and the blowout had taken the gallery's atmosphere with it in under six seconds — fast enough that he hadn't suffered, the models would say later, fast enough that his last act was preserved in amber. His hands were still on the manifold valves. He'd been closing them. Sealing the section to save the ship, even as the ship died around him, because that was the job and he had done the job.

His name tag read OYELARAN, D. Ivo checked the manifest. Deck engineer, thirty-one years old, born on Ceres Reach, naturalized Meridian citizen — one of the handful of people in the system who still carried papers from both worlds, back when carrying both had been legal rather than suspect. In the pocket of his suit, folded with the care people gave to things they reread often, was a letter in handwriting that mixed two alphabets — Meridian script flowing into Ceres glyphs and back again, the way bilingual children write before anyone teaches them to pick a side.

Ivo photographed the letter without unfolding it. *Do not remove personal effects.* He logged the time, logged the position, logged the small terrible dignity of a man who died doing the right thing with both hands.

* * *

It took him nine hours to walk the whole ship, and he used all of them, because rushing a wreck felt like a kind of theft.

In the second crew cabin he found a woman's spare uniform jacket with a faded patch sewn inside the collar — not a company patch, not a colony flag, but a hand-stitched constellation, seven stars, the thread cheap and the work loving. He photographed it and let himself wonder, briefly, who she'd stitched it for and who had taught her the stitch. In the third cabin, a shelf of paper books — actual paper, absurdly expensive out here, worth more than the cabin's life-support allotment — poetry in three languages, their spines cracked at favorite pages. In the fourth, nothing at all except a bed made with military corners and a single holoframe facedown on the desk, as if its owner had turned it away before the last voyage and never turned it back.

He stood over that frame for a long time. Then he turned it faceup — just to log its contents, he told himself, just for identification purposes — and found a young man in a graduation gown standing beside an older woman whose face wore the particular exhausted pride of someone who had spent everything she had to get him there. The image was tagged in Meridian script: *Mama — first day*. The owner of the fourth cabin was nineteen. The youngest crew member on any hauler Ivo had investigated in six years.

He logged the frame. He set it back facedown, the way its owner had left it, because some arrangements belonged to the dead.

By hour seven he had assembled the shape of it: four people, four lives, four sets of small habits written into the ship's surfaces like watermarks. Captain Reyes-Vance, fifty-eight, who drank her tea bitter enough to strip paint and annotated her navigation charts in pencil so future crews could erase her doubts. Engineer Oyelaran, who died with his hands on the valves. Cargo master Ilse Brandt, who kept the paper books and the stitched constellation and had, judging by the wear patterns, read the same poem so many times the page had gone soft. And the boy — Tomas Ferreira, junior systems tech, nineteen, who played the bottle-cap game with someone (the move patterns suggested Oyelaran) and slept badly (the bunk's compression foam was gouged where he gripped it) and had turned his mother's picture away because looking at it every morning hurt too much, and had boarded the ship anyway, because out here you boarded the ship.

None of which explained the hole.

Ivo ended his survey in the cargo bay itself, standing at the edge of the breach with his boots maglocked to the deck, looking out through the wound in the hull at the slow wheel of stars. The edges of the tear were glassy, fused, precise. Whatever had opened this hull had known exactly where to cut — not the reactor, not the bridge, not the fuel lines. The cargo bay. The one compartment whose loss would kill a ship slowly enough that her crew would have time to be afraid.

He ran his lamp along the fused metal one more time and felt the itch in his private ledger sharpen into something almost like recognition. Nine impossible accidents. Nine breaches, and now that he thought to compare them — really compare them, across five years of files he'd memorized rather than archived — nine cuts with the same glassy signature, nine wounds placed not randomly but *deliberately*, spaced along the route like stitches. Like someone sampling. Like someone testing.

Like a message being written very slowly, in dead ships, across the dark between two colonies that had stopped listening to each other.

"*Meridian Claims Authority*, Voss." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Preliminary survey complete. Cause of decompression: undetermined. Recommend classification per guild protocol seven."

"*Acknowledged,*" said the flat voice, eleven seconds late. "*Transmit your full log. Note: your survey window is terminated early by order of the Claims Directorate. A retrieval team will handle remaining documentation.*"

Ivo frowned. "There are personal effects requiring cataloguing. Families will want—"

"*Your log, Investigator. Now. The Directorate thanks you for your service.*"

He transmitted it. What else could he do? But as the data packet crawled away toward Meridian at the speed of bureaucratic indifference, Ivo stayed at the breach a moment longer, looking at the glassy cut in the hull, thinking about a nineteen-year-old boy who slept badly and a letter written in two alphabets and a game left unfinished.

Then his wrist display chimed — not the relay, not the guild channel, but a direct ping, routed through three anonymous relays, addressed to him by name.

One line of text, no sender:

**INVESTIGATOR VOSS. YOU ARE NOT THE FIRST TO SEE THE PATTERN. COME TO MERIDIAN BEFORE THEY SEAL YOUR FILE. ASK FOR CLAIM NUMBER 0047.**

Behind him, the dead ship drifted in silence, smelling faintly of cardamom, holding its four unfinished lives like a breath no one would ever exhale.

Ivo looked at the message for a long time.

Then he pushed off from the breach and went home the long way, through the dark, carrying more than his log.
