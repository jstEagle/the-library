## 2. Nine Seconds

The jump was supposed to be boring.

Ivo Voss had built a career on the assumption that boredom was the natural state of the universe, and that everything interesting was a deviation from it — a cracked coolant line, a falsified maintenance log, a hull breach with no cause. Boredom was the baseline against which he measured the world's lies. So when the *Meridian Rose* slid into its long transit between the colonies, he did what he always did: he settled into the observation blister with a cup of reconstituted tea and began cataloguing.

It was a habit from the investigation, not sentiment. The *Rose* carried eleven crew. He knew them now by their small signatures: Chief Okafor's habit of tapping the bulkhead twice before committing to any course change, as if asking permission of the ship itself. The young comms officer, Pell, who hummed Meridian folk songs under her breath and didn't know she did it. Vashti in engineering, who kept a photograph of two people Ivo had never met taped inside her tool locker, and who looked at it the way you look at a wound you've decided to stop treating.

Five years of silence between Meridian and Ceres Reach, and still the ships ran the route — freight, ore, seed stock, the unglamorous blood of two civilizations too proud to talk and too dependent to stop trading. The cargo moved. The people didn't. That was the arrangement.

Ivo wrote his notes in longhand, on paper, because paper couldn't be subpoenaed remotely. *Crew morale: functional. Grief distribution: uneven.* He'd learned that every ship on this route carried grief like ballast, distributed differently each voyage. Some crews talked about the war. Most performed its absence instead, a choreography of not-mentioning so practiced it looked like peace.

He was halfway through his tea when gravity flickered.

Not failed. Flickered — the way a lamp does when the current stutters, there and gone and there again, a shudder in the floor plates so brief that a lesser man would have blamed his inner ear. But Ivo had spent nine years learning to trust instruments over instincts, and the tea in his cup told the truth before anything else did: the surface of it went flat, then convex, then flat again, a tiny silver lens trembling with the whole system's pulse.

Then the nine seconds began.

* * *

Later, he would try to reconstruct their order, and fail. Memory that arrives all at once doesn't come with timestamps. But the reconstruction attempts themselves became part of what he remembered, so he recorded what he could:

A garden on Ceres Reach, terraced into the rock of a rotating habitat, where the light came amber through polarized panels because real sunlight cost too much. He knew the garden. Not as an investigator knows a place — layout, function, liability — but the way you know your own hands. He had built the third terrace himself, hauling regolith in a wheelbarrow with a squeaking wheel he never fixed because the sound meant something. It meant his daughter laughing at it.

Her name was Junie. She was six. She had a gap where her left canine hadn't come in yet, and she called the garden "the inside outside," and she believed — with the total, unexamined faith of a six-year-old — that her father could fix anything, including, eventually, the war.

He could smell the tomato vines. Hydroponic tomatoes never tasted right; everyone said so; he grew them anyway, because Junie liked the way the plants looked serious, like they were working harder than everything else.

And then the war arrived in the memory the way it must have arrived in life: not as a declaration but as a shortage, a queue, a notice on the garden gate. CONSCRIPTION LOTTERY — SECTOR 7. His name in it. Junie's face when he explained that he had to go, that daddies sometimes had to do things that made no sense, and her saying, with terrible clarity: *But you fix things.*

He died in the memory. That was the part he could not make smaller, no matter how many times he turned it over afterward. He was on a Meridian boarding action somewhere past the belt — the irony of which he understood even inside the memory, that a Ceres man had died on a Meridian ship — and the deck breached, and the air went, and the last thing he saw was not his life flashing but a single image, chosen by whatever chooses such things: the squeaking wheelbarrow. The sound of it. Junie's laugh riding on top of the sound like a bird on a wire.

Nine seconds. In nine seconds he had lived twenty-two years, loved a woman named Deshi whose handwriting slanted left, buried a father, planted tomatoes, and died with someone else's name in his mouth — a name that was also, somehow, his own.

The gravity steadied. The tea trembled once more and went still.

Ivo Voss sat in the observation blister of the *Meridian Rose*, holding a cup of tea, and did not know who he was.

* * *

The first sound was Pell crying.

He found her in the corridor outside comms, sitting on the deck with her back against the bulkhead, humming — no, not humming anymore, singing, a Ceres Reach harvest song in a dialect she could not possibly know, sung badly, through tears, her hands pressed over her mouth as if she could push the melody back in. She looked up at him and her face did the thing faces do when recognition and non-recognition occupy the same expression.

"I was on a farm," she whispered. "Ivo, I was on a *farm*. There were goats. I hated the goats. Why do I hate goats?"

"Comms officer Pell," he said carefully, "you've never—"

"I know." She laughed, wet and awful. "That's the thing. I *know*. I know both of it. Both of it is true."

They weren't the only ones. He walked the ship because walking was the only protocol he had for shock, and everywhere he walked he found the crew undone. Chief Okafor stood motionless in the galley, both palms flat on the table, staring at nothing, and when Ivo spoke his name the chief said, quietly, "My brother. I had a brother. On Ceres. We stopped writing when the embargo hit, and then he—" He stopped. Started again. "I remember him dying, Voss. I remember it like I was *there*. And I don't have a brother."

Vashti wouldn't open her tool locker. She stood in front of it with the photograph of the two strangers in her hand and said, over and over, "These are my parents. These are my actual parents. This photo is mine, it has always been mine, but the people in it aren't—" and then she couldn't finish, and Ivo didn't ask her to.

Eleven crew. Eleven people weeping or silent or singing songs from the wrong colony. Whatever had happened in those nine seconds had happened to all of them, and it had happened *differently* to each of them, tailored, personal, precise — which meant it wasn't a malfunction. Malfunctions are random. This had been addressed. Delivered. Like mail.

Like claims.

The investigator in him surfaced through the vertigo the way training surfaces through panic, and he grabbed hold of it. Catalogue. Document. Establish sequence. He got Pell a blanket. He got Okafor to drink water. He noted times, symptoms, order of onset. And then he went to the ship's AI, because if the event had been system-wide — if it had touched every human between the colonies — then somewhere there would be a record, and records were the one language the universe spoke honestly.

* * *

"Query," Ivo said to the AI. Its designation was HARMONY, which some pre-war optimist had chosen and no one since had bothered to change. "Log review. Last twelve minutes. All sensor anomalies."

HARMONY's voice was calm the way deep water is calm. "One anomaly logged. Gravitational constant fluctuation, system-wide, duration 9.00 seconds exactly. Deviation amplitude negligible. All systems nominal."

"Negligible," Ivo repeated. "Eleven crew members just experienced spontaneous false memories. How is that negligible?"

"It is not within my monitoring scope. I monitor the ship." A pause — AIs didn't pause unless they were choosing to, which meant this pause was a message. "However. The fluctuation was not local. Long-range arrays confirm synchronized deviation across the entire system. Every station. Every vessel. Both colonies."

"Every human being."

"The data does not specify humans. The data specifies spacetime." Another pause. "Ivo Voss. You are listed in my crew manifest as passenger, insurance investigator, Meridian registry. Is that designation currently accurate?"

The question hung there, absurd and enormous. Was it accurate? He thought of Junie's gap-toothed grin. He thought of his own mother, dead eleven years, whose funeral he had attended in a gray suit on Meridian Station, and how the grief had felt secondhand even then, like reading about weather. He thought of the way he catalogued strangers' lives with such tenderness and had never once been able to explain why — why a man who claimed no family of his own had built a career out of caring about other people's losses down to the smallest detail, the squeaking wheels of other people's lives.

"I don't know," he said. It was the most honest claim report he'd ever filed.

"Noted," said HARMONY. "Anomaly classification updated: system-wide event, cause unknown, effect unknown. Flagging per protocol."

"Flagging to whom?"

"Both governments. Standard procedure for system-wide events." The AI's calm suddenly sounded, to Ivo's new ears, like the calm of a witness who doesn't yet know they're in danger. "Estimated acknowledgment response: four hours from Meridian Traffic Control. Six hours from Ceres Reach."

Four hours. Six hours. Two governments that hadn't spoken in five years, responding to the same event within hours of each other, as if they'd been waiting for it. As if they'd recognize it.

Pell appeared in the doorway, blanket around her shoulders, eyes red. "Voss. The others are asking — what do we tell them? When Traffic hails us, what do we say happened?"

Ivo looked at his hands. Someone else's calluses ghosted over them, memory-thin, from a wheelbarrow handle on a garden terrace orbiting a world he'd never seen.

"We tell them the truth," he said slowly. "Gravity flickered. Nine seconds. Everyone's fine." And then, quieter, almost to himself: "And we see who comes asking anyway."

Because that was the thing he already knew, the way he'd known the taste of those hydroponic tomatoes: nobody sends ships for a negligible anomaly. Nobody seals a system-wide event within the hour. Unless the event isn't an anomaly at all.

Unless it's a leak.

Somewhere behind them, the stars held steady in the viewport, and four hours away, Meridian Traffic Control was already composing questions for a freighter full of people who remembered dying.
