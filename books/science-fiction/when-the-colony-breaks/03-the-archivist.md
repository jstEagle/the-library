## 3. The Archivist

The skiff came in on a Tuesday, which was itself remarkable, because the Vesperine had not had a visitor on a Tuesday in eleven years. It had not had a visitor on any day in eleven years, not counting the supply drones that arrived quarterly like patient, uncomplaining relatives, and the one customs pinnace four years back that had circled twice, found nothing worth taxing, and left.

Ila Voss watched it come from the archivist's berth she had claimed as her own three days earlier, when the long-haul packet *Meridian's Grace* had deposited her at the system's outer marker with a polite request to be collected by whatever passed for local transport. Nothing passed for local transport. She had paid a fuel skimmer's crew an obscene amount of her grant money to make the detour, and they had dropped her at the derelict survey ship's docking collar with the particular silence of people who wanted no part of whatever she was about to do.

She had told them she was researching decommissioned beacon infrastructure. This was even true, in the way that a key is true of a lock.

The skiff approaching now was not hers.

She had felt it first as a wrongness in the docking telemetry—a second transponder where there should have been none, a handshake request sliding against the Vesperine's ancient protocols like a pick in a rusted tumbler. The ship's systems, which Casper maintained with the devotion of a monk tending a single candle, registered the approach and did nothing, because the Vesperine had no weapons, no shields, and no opinions. It only had its cargo and its beacon and its one navigator, and Ila had spent three days learning that this was apparently enough.

She watched the skiff resolve against the black: a salvage frame, stripped and re-stripped, hull plating mismatched like a coat sewn from three coats. No syndicate markings. That meant nothing. The good salvage crews didn't mark their hulls precisely because the marked ones were the ones you could report.

Her hand went to the comm panel in the berth, and then stopped.

*Let him handle it,* she thought, and then corrected herself—*let them handle it*—and then stopped again, because she did not actually know what Casper Crane preferred, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a stone she had swallowed whole.

Three days. Three days aboard the Vesperine, sleeping in a berth that smelled of recycled air and old insulation, eating from Casper's stores with a carefulness that bordered on apology, and she still could not make her hands stop shaking when she saw his face.

*Their* face. She was an archivist. Precision mattered. And precision said: the man who walked these corridors, who wound the beacon every morning at 0600 with a key he kept on a cord around his neck, who spoke in the flat careful cadence of someone who had rehearsed being a person for a very long time—precision said that man was Casper Crane, registered navigator, sole crew, last of a crew of nine according to the manifests she had pulled before coming.

Precision also said that Casper Crane had died eleven years ago.

She had found the record on Meridian Station, in the deep archive, in the course of what she had told her colleagues was a survey of colonial beacon decommissioning protocols. The record was thin—a death certificate, a service number, a date. Navigator Casper Crane, deceased, cause listed as decompression event, body not recovered. Filed from the Vesperine itself, eleven years and two months ago.

And then, four months after she found it, she had requested the beacon's commissioning file—the original authorization, the founding telemetry, the reason a survey ship's navigator had been left alone in a dying system winding a machine that answered to no registry she could find.

And the file had deleted itself.

Not corrupted. Not lost. *Deleted*—across every archive she had access to, in the same hour, with the tidy finality of something that had been waiting to be asked.

She had come out here because of that deletion. She had told herself it was professional rigor. She had told herself a great many things on the long haul out, and all of them had collapsed the moment the airlock cycled and Casper Crane walked into the docking bay—alive, whole, younger than his death certificate, and looking at her with eyes that held no recognition at all.

She had frozen. She knew she had frozen; she had felt her own face go still the way water goes still, and she had watched him notice it, and she had watched him decide not to ask. That was the thing about Casper that unsettled her most, more than the face, more than the death record, more than the way he wound the beacon like a priest performing a rite he did not understand: he noticed everything and asked nothing. He moved through his ship like a man defusing something, and Ila had begun to suspect that the something was himself.

Now the skiff was thirty meters out and closing, and she made herself leave the berth.

* * *

Casper was already in the docking bay when she arrived, standing at the telemetry console with their hands flat on the readout surface, the way they did when they were thinking—palms down, fingers spread, as if holding the ship's data still by touch.

"You saw it," Ila said.

"I saw it." Casper's voice was level. "Salvage frame, no registry, running dark until four minutes ago. They pinged us with a standard approach request and then didn't wait for an answer."

"That's not standard."

"No," Casper said. "It isn't."

Ila came to stand beside them at the console, close enough to feel the warmth off their shoulder, not close enough to be a choice. The skiff filled the external feed now, its hull crawling with patch welds and sensor scars. She had spent enough years around archive footage of salvage crews to read a ship the way she read a document: this one had been in the debris fields around Calloway's Reach, once—there, in the scoring pattern along the ventral spine, the telltale pitting of a system that had been through something no one wrote reports about.

"Are they going to dock?" she asked.

"They're going to dock." Casper said it without inflection, which somehow made it worse. "The collar will accept them. I could refuse the handshake, but the Vesperine's docking systems predate the current protocol standard, and a determined pilot can force a seal. If they force it, they'll breach something. If they ask nicely and I say no, they'll know the ship is worth forcing." A pause. "So I'm going to say yes, and I'm going to be very boring."

"Are you good at that?"

"I've had eleven years of practice."

There it was again—the flatness that was not quite emptiness, the joke delivered like a maintenance log entry. Ila looked at the profile of the face she had loved once, on another station, in another life, and made herself breathe.

Because that was the thing she had not told anyone, the thing she had buried under grant applications and archival methodology: she had known Casper Crane. Not this Casper—the one with the death certificate—but a Casper, on Meridian Station, nine years ago. A navigator between contracts, sharp and funny and restless, who had spent three weeks in the station's archive reading colonial shipping records for no reason he would explain, and who had kissed her under the archive's bad yellow lighting and then left without goodbye, leaving only a message that said *if anyone ever asks you about a beacon, forget my face.*

She had spent nine years forgetting his face. She had gotten very good at it. And then the airlock had cycled and there it was.

"You're doing the thing," Casper said, not looking at her.

"What thing?"

"Where you look at me like I'm a file you're trying to authenticate." Now they did look at her, and their eyes were steady and dark and entirely without memory of her. "I've been trying to decide whether to be flattered or concerned."

"Concerned," Ila said. "Definitely concerned."

"Good. Flattered would complicate things." The skiff's thrusters flared in the external feed, braking. "They'll want to board. Salvage always wants to board. I'm going to let them, and I'm going to show them a dying survey ship with nothing aboard but a navigator, an archivist, and a hold full of—" Casper stopped. The pause was small, but Ila had learned to hear the small pauses; they were where Casper kept everything. "A hold full of old suspension equipment. Decommissioned. Nonfunctional."

"Is it?"

The pause again. Longer.

"I don't know," Casper said, and it was the first time in three days she had heard them say those words, and something in her chest ached at how much it cost them. "I've maintained that hold for eleven years and I couldn't tell you what's inside the pods. I know the maintenance schedule. I know the power draw. I know the sound it makes at night." Their hands flexed once against the console. "I don't know why I know the sound it makes at night."

The skiff kissed the collar with a clang that traveled up through the deck plates and into Ila's teeth.

* * *

The woman who came through the airlock was not what Ila had expected, which was itself a lesson in expectations. She had braced for muscle, for a boarding party with cutters and a manifest of parts to strip. Instead: one woman, alone, in a salvage jacket worn soft with age, gray at the temples, with the unhurried gait of someone who had boarded two hundred ships and been afraid on none of them.

"Vesperine," the woman said, reading the stenciled name on the collar housing with something like fondness. "I haven't seen a hull this old outside of a museum. Ondine Marsh, independent recovery. I'm not here for your ship, navigator—I'm here for what's around it." She turned, and her eyes found Ila first, catalogued her in a half second—the archive-grade tablet in her hand, the Meridian Station issue jacket, the hands of someone who handled documents and not tools—and dismissed her, and came back to Casper. "You're Crane."

"I'm Crane."

"Alone out here eleven years. That's a story."

"It's a job," Casper said. "The story is very boring. Beacon maintenance, systems decay, quarterly supply runs. I could walk you through the maintenance logs if you like. They're extremely thorough."

Marsh smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "Thorough logs. On a ship whose registry file is a sealed stub. On a beacon that doesn't answer to any authority I can find, and I can find most of them." She let that sit. "I'm doing a survey, same as your archivist there. Decommissioned infrastructure. There's a lot of money in knowing where the old machines are and what they're still connected to."

"We're not connected to anything," Casper said.

"Everything's connected to something, navigator. The trick is knowing what." Marsh's gaze drifted, deliberately, toward the hold access at the back of the bay—the sealed hatch, the one Casper had walked past every day of eleven years without opening, the one Ila's own queries had bounced off like light off deep water. "That the cargo bay?"

"Decommissioned suspension equipment," Casper said, in the exact same tone as before, and Ila marveled at it, at the perfect flatness, and hated it, because she could see now that the flatness was load-bearing. "Nonfunctional. I can show you the decommissioning order."

"You do that." Marsh didn't move toward the hatch. That was the thing Ila would remember afterward, the detail that would keep her up that night: Marsh didn't move toward it, didn't push, just let her eyes rest on the sealed hatch for three full seconds, the way a person rests their hand on a door they already own. "I'll be in the system another few days. Running my survey. I may come back. I may not." She turned for the airlock, then paused. "Archivist."

Ila looked up.

"Meridian Station, is it? They keep good records, Meridian." A beat. "You might ask them what happened to the Vesperine's commissioning file. If you can find anyone who admits it ever existed."

The airlock cycled. The skiff disengaged. And the docking bay was quiet again, quiet the way it had been quiet for eleven years, except that nothing about it was quiet anymore.

Casper stood very still, hands flat on the console, and Ila watched the flatness crack—not much, just a seam, just enough to see the light behind it.

"She knew about the file," Ila said quietly.

"I know."

"Which means someone else has asked. Recently. Enough times that the syndicates have heard."

"I know." Casper's voice had gone somewhere far away. "Ila. The hold. The pods. When I said I don't know what's inside them—that's not entirely true." They turned, and their face in the docking bay's amber light was the face from nine years ago, the face from the archive, the face from a death certificate, and none of those faces, and one she was only beginning to know. "I don't know what's inside them. But last night I dreamed about them, and in the dream I knew every one of their names."

Ila's mouth went dry. "Whose names?"

Casper looked at the sealed hatch, and their hand rose halfway to the key on the cord at their neck, and stopped.

"I don't know," they said. "I've never heard them before. But I said them like I'd said them every day for years."

Outside the viewport, far off, the beacon's light turned in its slow patient circle, and Ila thought: *it's answering something. It's been answering something all along.* And she reached for her tablet, and began, with hands that would not entirely stop shaking, to draft the query she had sworn she'd never send twice.
