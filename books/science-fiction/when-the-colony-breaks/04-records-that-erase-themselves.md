## 4. Records That Erase Themselves

The Vesperine's archive room had once been a chapel.

Ila decided this within her first hour aboard, sitting at a console whose housing bore the ghost-letters of some older designation—*MEDITATION / QUIET USE ONLY*—sanded down by decades of hands that no longer existed. Survey ships of this class were built with a small sanctum for crews who would spend years in the dark, and someone aboard had converted it into a records bay without ever quite erasing what it was. The light still came through slatted panels like light through a clerestory window. The chairs still faced each other, two by two, as if waiting for confession.

She found that fitting. She was here to confess something, after all—or to extract a confession, which was nearly the same work with worse manners.

Casper Crane sat across from her, hands folded on the table between them, and watched her with the patience of someone who had spent eleven years being the only audience for their own voice. Up close, in the archive's amber light, the face was unbearable. Not because it was wrong—it was perfect, every line where she remembered it, the small asymmetry of the left eyebrow, the scar at the hairline from a ladder accident on Meridian Station that she had once kissed without thinking about. It was unbearable because it was *right*, and rightness was the one thing Ila Voss could not afford to trust.

"You said you wanted to see my maintenance logs," Casper said.

"I did. I've read them." She had, all night, curled in the guest berth with her slate dimmed to its lowest glow, reading eleven years of entries written in a hand so consistent it made her chest ache. *Wound beacon core. Checked bearing seals. Logged frequency drift: negligible.* Thousands of entries. Not one of them human. "They're very thorough."

"Thank you."

"That wasn't a compliment." She turned her slate toward him. "There's not a single entry in eleven years that tells me who you are. No personal log. No letters home. No record of shore leave, resupply contacts, nothing. You wrote like a machine maintaining itself."

"I wrote like a navigator maintaining a ship." Casper's voice was level, but something moved behind the eyes—a flicker, quickly banked, like a draft finding a crack in a sealed room. "There wasn't anyone to write to."

"There's always someone to write to. Even if they're dead. Especially if they're dead." She heard her own sharpness and didn't soften it. Softness was a currency she'd already overspent on this face, years ago, three systems away, in a life she was beginning to suspect had been arranged for her rather than lived. "That's actually why I'm here. Not the logs. The commissioning file."

Something changed in the room. It wasn't anything Casper did—the hands stayed folded, the posture stayed easy—but the air pressure of the person shifted, the way a hull shifts when a distant door closes somewhere in its depths. Ila had spent nine years learning to read archives the way other people read faces, and she read this one now: *he knows something is coming.*

"The commissioning file," Casper repeated.

"Vesperine-class survey vessel, hull number 77-Kestrel. Standard registry requires a commissioning package: crew manifest, mission charter, beacon authorization, sponsor signatures. I queried it four days ago from Meridian Station, cross-referencing decommissioned navigation beacons for my—" she caught herself before saying *cover story*, and hated how smoothly the lie came even now, "—for my research. The file returned a standard header and then deleted itself. Mid-query. While I was watching."

"Files corrupt," Casper said. "Old ships, old formats—"

"It didn't corrupt. Corruption leaves debris. Fragments. Partial strings you can reconstruct." She pulled up the query log and set the slate flat on the table between them, turning it so he could read it if he chose. She hoped he wouldn't. She hoped he would look at her instead, and give her something true to hold onto. "This erased cleanly. Every field. Every checksum. And then—and this is the part I need you to hear—it reached back up the chain and deleted itself from the station master archive too. From *every* archive. Meridian, Ceres Deep, the Halvard repository, three private collections I have access to through my fellowship. Eleven archives, Casper. I queried all of them independently. All eleven copies of your ship's birth certificate died within the same four seconds."

The silence that followed had texture. It came off the walls of the old chapel like heat off stone.

Casper looked down at the slate. Read it. Looked up again, and the face was still, so carefully still, that Ila understood she was looking at effort—not at calm, but at the performance of calm by someone who had practiced it alone for a very long time.

"I don't know anything about that," Casper said.

"I know."

"I mean it. I wound the beacon. I kept the seals. Nobody ever told me anything about commissioning files or archives or—" A pause, half a breath too long. "Or sponsors."

"And yet." Ila leaned forward. The amber light caught the dust motes between them, turning the air into something almost solid, a medium you could drown in. "And yet when I told you last night that I was researching decommissioned beacons, you didn't ask me a single question. Do you know how strange that is? I've been doing this work six years. Every keeper, every lighthouse tender, every retired nav-tech I've ever interviewed has asked me questions. Who sent you. What do the records say about my beacon. Is there compensation. Is there trouble. You asked me whether I'd eaten, and then you showed me where the water recycler was, and then you went back to winding your clock."

"The beacon needs winding."

"The beacon doesn't need winding!" The words came out louder than she intended, and they rang in the chapel-space, and she let them ring. "Beacons don't need *winding*, Casper. That's not a thing. Beacon cores are closed-loop fusion taps; they run for centuries without maintenance. What you're doing every morning—that ritual, that beautiful, insane ritual of yours—isn't maintenance. It's *ceremony*. You just don't know what it's a ceremony *for*."

He flinched. It was small—the barest contraction around the eyes—but she saw it, and seeing it hurt her more than any denial could have, because it meant she was right, and being right had never felt so much like cruelty.

"Ila." Her name in his mouth. She had forgotten, in four years of absence, what it did to her—how it seemed to place her somewhere, to fix her coordinates in a system where she had always felt adrift. He said it gently, the way he must have said everything to himself for eleven years, and she had to grip the edge of the table to keep her composure. "I'm the last person aboard a dying ship. Whatever you think I know, whatever you think I'm hiding—I promise you, if there were secrets in my head, I'd have found them by now. There's a lot of time out here. Time is very thorough."

"That's exactly what worries me." She softened despite herself. She couldn't help it; that was her flaw, her fatal architecture, the reason she'd been chosen for this work. "Time is thorough, Casper. So are archives. And yours is the cleanest blank I've ever seen. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Files don't erase themselves. *People* erase files. Someone spent enormous resources—resources that make my fellowship look like pocket change—making sure that no one in human space could ever answer the question of who built your beacon and why. And whoever they were, they were good enough to leave the ship itself intact. They left *you* intact. Which means either you're not worth erasing—"

"—or I'm part of what they're protecting." He finished it quietly.

The admission hung between them like frost forming.

Ila exhaled slowly. "You said it, not me."

"I said what you were thinking. There's a difference." But his hands had come apart on the table now, and one of them drifted to his own wrist—an unconscious gesture, thumb pressing the pulse point, as if checking that he was still running. She recognized the gesture. She had seen the original Casper Crane do it a hundred times, before exams, before launches, before the night he'd told her he'd signed onto a long-haul contract and wouldn't be able to send messages. *Checking that he was still real.* The gesture survived the man, apparently. Or the man survived as gesture.

"Casper." She made her voice gentle now, gentler than she felt, because the interrogation was over and something else had begun, something with higher stakes. "Four seconds ago, I watched a file kill itself across eleven archives because I asked about your ship. Yesterday, a salvage skiff followed me into this system on the same query trail, and I don't think they're researchers. Somebody wants what's on this vessel badly enough to burn records and chase archivists. You are sitting on top of something enormous, and you genuinely don't know what it is, and that innocence is the most dangerous thing about you."

"You sound frightened."

"I am frightened." She laughed, short and unsteady. "I've been frightened since the moment I docked and saw your face. But that's not—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Some doors, once opened, could not be politely rehung. "That's not the point. The point is that the deletion wasn't defensive. Defensive deletions are messy, panicked, partial. This was scheduled. Elegant. It waited for my query like a trap waits for weight. Which means somewhere, something is still *watching* your records, Casper. Something is still minding the store. And if it's watching the records, it may be watching the ship. And if it's watching the ship—"

The deck shuddered.

It was slight—less than a tremor, more than imagination, a single deep pulse through the Vesperine's bones like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Both of them went still. Above them, faint through the deck plating, came the sound Ila had been hearing in her sleep since she arrived: the beacon's hum, that low patient tone Casper wound into existence every dawn.

Except the hum had changed key.

She saw Casper hear it too—saw the head tilt, the fractional narrowing of focus, the body responding to the shift before the mind could frame a denial. For one unguarded instant, the careful face fell away entirely, and what was underneath was not ignorance at all. It was recognition. Old, worn-smooth recognition, the look of a person hearing a word from a language they'd been told they never learned.

"That happens sometimes," Casper said. But the voice was thinner now, stretched over something.

"How often?"

"Sometimes." A breath. "More lately."

"Define lately."

Casper stood. Walked to the slatted window, where the amber light lay in bars across the floor like the shadow of a lattice neither of them could see. When he spoke again, he spoke to the light, not to her, and that was its own kind of confession.

"Eleven years, it held one note. One note, Ila. I could set my days by it. Then—" the shoulders rose, fell, "—then things started answering it. Little shifts. Harmonics. Like someone on the other end of a very long wire, clearing their throat. I logged them as drift. Negligible drift. Because the alternative was logging them as *conversation*, and I didn't—" The thumb found the wrist again. Pressed. "I didn't want to be the only person in the universe talking to my own lighthouse."

Ila rose and crossed the space between them. She stopped an arm's length away—close enough to touch, far enough to choose not to—and studied the profile she had loved across four years and one impossible distance. The scar at the hairline. The eyebrow. The pulse ticking visibly in the throat, quick now, quicker than a machine's should be.

"Your file erased itself today," she said softly, "because I asked who you were. Think about that. Not what the beacon does. Not who built it. *Who you are.* The question wasn't about the ship, Casper. The question was about the keeper. And something out there decided the answer was too dangerous to exist on paper."

Casper turned. And for the first time since she'd boarded, the careful ignorance cracked—not broke, cracked, the way ice cracks before it gives—and behind it she saw not guilt, not deception, but fear wearing devotion's clothing. A person who had kept faith with something they couldn't name, and was only now beginning to wonder what the faith had cost.

"Then I need to know what's true," Casper said. "And there's only one place aboard I've never had the courage to look."

Ila's stomach dropped. "Which is?"

"The hold." His eyes went past her, down, toward the lower decks, toward the humming she had always assumed was machinery. "Forty pods. I've checked their seals for eleven years. I've never once opened one. I told myself it was protocol." The thumb pressed the wrist. "I'm starting to think it was obedience."

Above them, the beacon shifted key again—and this time, unmistakably, the new note was one Ila knew. It was the first bar of an old colony hymn, the kind sung on founding-day recordings, the kind archived under names like *Calloway's Reach*.

Neither of them said it aloud. Neither of them had to.

Casper was already moving toward the hatch, and the dark below decks seemed, for the first time, to be listening.
