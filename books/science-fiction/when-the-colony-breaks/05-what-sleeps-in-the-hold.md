## 5. What Sleeps in the Hold

The hatch had never been locked.

That was the thing Casper could not stop turning over, in the hours after Ila Voss's questions had gone quiet and the skiff's engines had faded into the dark beyond the docking collar. Eleven years of walking past that hatch in the aft corridor, past the faded stencil—*HOLD 3 — ENVIRONMENTAL SPARES*—and never once, not in the whole long geography of his routine, had he opened it. Not because it was sealed. Not because the ship forbade it. The latch worked. He'd tested it once, early on, the way you test a door in a house you've just moved into, and it had given with the soft hydraulic sigh of a thing maintained, oiled, *expected* to be opened.

He had closed it again and told himself he'd check the contents tomorrow.

He had told himself that for eleven years.

Casper stood in front of it now, at the end of the night-cycle, when the Vesperine's corridors went the color of weak tea and the only sound was the beacon's pulse coming up through the deck plates like a second heartbeat that wasn't his. The gravity was steady again. Nine seconds of stutter, and then the system had caught itself like a sleeper jerking awake, and everything was normal, everything was fine, except that the beacon had *answered* something, and Casper had spent two days pretending he hadn't heard the frequency shift the way you'd pretend not to hear your name called from a dark room.

And now the archivist was asleep in the guest berth he'd offered because turning her away had felt, absurdly, like a moral failure. Ila Voss, who looked at him like he was a page she'd read before. Ila Voss, who had said, *your face, I've seen your face somewhere,* and then gone pale as frost and refused to say where.

He put his hand on the latch.

The metal was warm. That was wrong too—everything aft of the reactor should have been cooling toward the ambient chill of a ship running on habit rather than purpose—but the hatch was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and when he pulled it, the seal broke with a breath of air that smelled like nothing he had a name for. Not machine oil. Not recycled atmosphere with its faint onion tang. Something older and sweeter, like the inside of a violin case, like paper kept in a dry room for a very long time.

The stairwell beyond descended in a spiral, and the lights came on as he went down—not flickering on, not stuttering, but *arriving*, one after another, in a sequence so smooth it felt less like automation than like welcome.

"Oh," Casper said, to no one, and kept descending.

* * *

The hold was larger than the ship.

He understood, standing at the bottom of the stairs, that this was impossible. He had measured the Vesperine during his first year—out of boredom, out of the particular madness of a man alone with a maintenance schedule—and he knew her dimensions the way he knew his own hands. She was a survey vessel, mid-century hull, two hundred and six meters stem to stern. The volume beneath his feet could not possibly contain the room he was standing in.

And yet.

The bay stretched away from him in long dim aisles, its ceiling lost in a haze of amber light that seemed to generate itself, sourceless, the way light behaves in dreams. The deck was not deck-plating but something grown—ribbed and pale, like the inside of a shell—and it gave slightly under his boots, resilient, warm through the soles. Rows of pods stood in the aisles. He counted without meaning to, the way he counted everything, the way the beacon had taught him to count: intervals, repetitions, the arithmetic of keeping things alive.

Forty.

Forty pods, each one a smooth oval of dark glass canted at a gentle angle, each one cradled in a frame of that same shell-pale material, and each one occupied.

Casper's breath went thin.

The faces were visible through the glass—frost-clear, close, intimate in a way that felt like trespass. A man with a beard going gray at the chin, his mouth slightly open, mid-dream or mid-sentence. A woman whose hair floated around her head in the suspension fluid like ink poured into water, frozen there. A child. His stomach dropped at the child—a boy, maybe ten, fists curled near his chin in the universal posture of the deeply asleep—and Casper had to stand very still for a moment and breathe through the animal horror of it, the cold certainty that he was looking at something he was not permitted to see.

But the pods were labeled. That was the first mercy. Small plates at the base of each frame, etched in a font he recognized—*recognized*, that was the wrong word, *had always known*, the way you know the shape of your own signature—and each plate carried a name and a string of characters that might have been a date or a designation or a prayer.

*MERIT OKONKWO-VASHTI. SEED 11.*

*PRIYA LUND. SEED 12.*

*TOMAS LUND. SEED 13.*

Husband and wife, he guessed, from the shared name. He looked at their faces in their separate pods, floating a meter apart, and thought: someone put them next to each other. Someone decided that even in this, they should not be separated.

He walked the aisles. His footsteps made no sound on the grown deck, or rather they made a sound like footsteps on a beach at a distance, muffled and wrong, and the amber light followed him down each row and dimmed behind him like a courtesy. He read every name. He made himself read every name, because it seemed suddenly like the most important work he had ever done, more important than winding the beacon, more important than the eleven years of filters and bearings and coolant flushes—because these people had names, and names were the smallest unit of keeping, and keeping was the only thing Casper Crane knew how to do.

A woman with a scar through one eyebrow. A man so old his skin had gone translucent at the temples. Two pods that held what he first took for twins and then understood were simply sisters, their resemblance a family resemblance, not a copy's. A young man with a cleft chin who had, Casper noticed with a strange lurch, a small tattoo at his wrist, a line of script too fine to read through the glass.

And then, in the fourth row, near the back, where the light pooled deepest and warmest—

Casper stopped.

The pod was labeled like the others. *C. CRANE. SEED 4.* And the face behind the glass was his own.

Not similar. Not the uncanny near-match of a sibling or a cousin. His own. The same asymmetry in the brow, the left one a fraction heavier, that he had seen ten thousand times in the washroom mirror and hated in a way he'd never said aloud to anyone because there had never been anyone to say it to. The same small notch in the earlobe, from an earring he did not remember ever wearing. The same crease between the eyebrows that he'd been told, once, by someone he could not now place, made him look permanently like a man doing arithmetic.

He was looking at a man doing arithmetic.

Casper raised his hand. The face behind the glass did not move—of course it did not move—but he raised his hand anyway, slowly, and pressed his palm flat to the pod's surface, and the glass was warm, body-warm, and somewhere under his hand, faint as a rumor, he felt the pulse.

* * *

He did not know how long he stood there. The light did not change. The beacon's pulse came up through the shell-deck at its familiar interval—four seconds on, two off, the rhythm he had set his own sleep to for a decade without ever asking himself why—and Casper stood with his palm on the glass of his own sleeping face and felt the floor of his identity tilt beneath him, gently, the way a ship lists when a compartment floods somewhere below the waterline.

*Backup,* Ila had said. She hadn't said it about him, not exactly. She'd said it about the hold, about the manifest she'd tried to pull, about the word that had self-deleted out of every archive the moment she touched it. But he had heard the shape of the thought behind her words. He had seen her look at him and do arithmetic of her own.

There were explanations. He made himself list them, because listing was what he did, because procedure was the raft he had built his whole self on. Survey crews carried genetic archives; that was standard. Redundant personnel records; standard. Cryo-stored crew for long transits; standard, standard, all of it standard, and none of it explained why the archive held a living, breathing, *dreaming* man with his exact face and his exact tired crease between the brows, and none of it explained why Casper had walked past this hatch for eleven years with a latch that worked and a tomorrow that never came.

Because that was the part that made his hands shake. Not the pod. The *not opening*.

He tried to remember his first day aboard. He tried, and the memory came—and this was its own horror—too smooth, too complete, a memory like a promotional video: arriving on the shuttle, the Vesperine's hull scarred and patient against the stars, the beacon already pulsing, and himself, Casper Crane, navigator, walking aboard with a toolkit and a maintenance schedule and no history at all. No departure port. No goodbye. No face of anyone who had sent him. He had never once, in eleven years, thought about that absence, and now it opened in him like a wound that had been there all along, kept clean by his own refusal to look.

*You didn't forget to open the hatch,* he thought. *You were not opening it. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole size of you.*

The pod's readout, a thin line of characters glowing along its base, shifted as he watched. He leaned closer. The characters were not a temperature or a date. They were a sentence, cycling slowly, in that font he had always known:

*YOU ARE SAFE. YOU ARE NOT ALONE. HOLD.*

He read it three times. His eyes kept snagging on the last word. *Hold.* Hold the position. Hold the pattern. Hold on. And also, simply: this room. This warm, breathing, impossible room. The word doing all its work at once, the way words did in the old prayers Ila had probably catalogued somewhere, in an archive that kept its records better than the Vesperine kept its people.

"I don't understand," he said aloud, and his voice sounded young in the vast amber dark. "I don't understand what I am."

The pod did not answer. But as he stood there, the readout cycled again, and this time the sentence was different:

*WIND THE BEACON.*

Casper went cold from the scalp down.

Because he had not told anyone. He had never logged it, never written it into the maintenance schedule, never said it to Ila in the two days she had been aboard, though she had watched him do it and asked what he was doing and he had said *calibration* the way he always said *calibration*, the word that closed doors. The ritual was his alone: the descent to the beacon housing at the same hour each cycle, the three-turn key, the pressure of his thumb on the plate for exactly as long as it took to say, silently, the words he had never examined—*still here, still here, still here.*

Nobody knew about the words.

The readout held its sentence. *WIND THE BEACON.* And beneath it, after a pause long enough that he understood it was deliberate, that the thing in the pod was choosing its words the way he chose his:

*IT IS ALMOST TIME. FOUR.*

The word landed in him like a verdict read in a language he almost spoke. *Four.* Not his name. Not *Casper*. A number, a place in a sequence, and the sequence had a one in it, and a two, and a three, and every one of those positions was a person, and the person in the fourth position was standing in the amber dark with his hand on the glass of his own face, learning that he had been counted.

Behind him, at the top of the spiral stair, the hatch sighed.

Footsteps on the grown deck—hesitant, unpracticed, someone who had never walked this floor before. He knew the rhythm already. Two days was enough. Ila Voss came down the stairs with her hand light on the rail and her eyes wide, and the amber light rose to meet her like water finding its level, and she stopped when she saw him, and then she saw the pod, and Casper watched her face do the thing that his had done: the tilt, the flood below the waterline.

"I followed the warmth," she said, uselessly, and then, more quietly: "Casper. That's—"

"I know what it is," he said. His hand was still on the glass. He could not make himself take it away. It felt, obscenely, like the only warm thing in his life that he had not personally maintained. "It's me, Ila. It's *me*, and it's been down here the whole time, and I never opened the door."

She came closer. In the glass of the pod, the three of them were reflected together—Casper standing, Casper sleeping, Ila between them like a hinge—and her reflection was trembling.

"There's something you should know," she said. "About where I've seen your face before."

* * *

The readout pulsed once, softly, and cycled to a new line as she spoke, and Casper read it over her shoulder without meaning to, the way you read your own name on a document passed across a table:

*SHE KNOWS. LET HER TELL YOU.*
