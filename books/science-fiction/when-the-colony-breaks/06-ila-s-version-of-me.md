## 6. Ila's Version of Me

The question hung between them for three full minutes before Ila answered it, and in those three minutes she catalogued every escape route in the room the way she'd once catalogued citations—methodically, desperately, knowing none of them would hold.

Casper had backed her against the galley bulkhead without touching her, which was somehow worse than if they had. They stood close enough that she could see the fine grain of exhaustion around their eyes, the faint asymmetry of a face that had been repaired at least once by someone in a hurry. The ship's recycled air smelled of coolant and old tea. Somewhere below them, forty hearts beat in suspension, and above them the beacon sang its one note into the dark, and between those two facts Ila Voss had run out of true things to say that were also safe things to say.

"You knew my face," Casper said. "The first second. You looked at me like someone had shown you a photograph of a house you used to live in."

"That's not—"

"Don't." Casper's voice wasn't angry. It was something more frightening than angry. It was patient. "I've spent eleven years alone on this ship, Archivist. I know what it sounds like when someone is being careful with the truth around me. I've had a lot of practice being the person people are careful around."

"Because you don't remember anything," Ila said. It came out before she could stop it, and she watched the words land, watched Casper's jaw tighten.

"Because I don't remember anything," Casper agreed quietly. "So tell me what you remember. Tell me why you froze in the docking bay like you'd seen a ghost wearing my coat."

* * *

She had rehearsed this conversation for six years, in the way that people rehearse things they never believe will actually happen. In the rehearsal, she was composed. She was an archivist, and archivists presented evidence in order, with provenance, with dates. She had imagined beginning with the station manifest from Meridian, the transit logs, the small verifiable facts that would let her approach the large unverifiable one sideways, the way you approach a wounded animal.

But Casper was standing in front of her with their hands flat against the bulkhead on either side of her shoulders—not trapping her, she understood that, bracing themselves—and all of her careful provenance had dissolved.

"His name was Casper Crane," she said.

The ship hummed. The beacon sang. Casper didn't move.

"Meridian Station, outer arm, eleven years ago. I was twenty-six. I'd just taken my certification in archival recovery, and the station was a waypoint—nobody's destination, everybody's layover. He was there for nine days waiting on a transfer to a survey posting. He told me the ship was called the Vesperine and that he'd been told it was a lighthouse tender, and that he didn't entirely believe it, and that he'd signed on anyway because—" Ila stopped. Her throat had closed around the next part.

"Because," Casper said. Not a question. A door held open.

"Because he said someone had to keep the light on for people who weren't awake yet to see it." Her voice cracked on *awake* and she hated that, hated the precision of her own grief finding the exact seam in the sentence. "He said it like a joke. He said everything like a joke. That was the thing about him. He made enormous things sound small so he could carry them."

Casper's hands slid an inch down the bulkhead. "What did he look like."

"You know what he looked like."

"I know what *I* look like. I want to know what *he* looked like. To you."

Ila closed her eyes. It was a mistake, because with her eyes closed he was there, fully there, in the memory she had rationed like water for six years—standing at the observation rail on Meridian's third ring with a cup of terrible station coffee, watching the traffic lanes, laughing at something she'd said about cataloguing systems. He'd had the same face. That was the unbearable part. Not similar. The same. The same crooked incisor on the left side when he smiled. The same scar through the right eyebrow that he claimed was from a cargo accident and that she'd later suspected was from something he wouldn't name. The same way of listening—head tilted, entirely still, as if the person speaking were a signal he was trying not to lose.

"He looked like you," she said. "That's the whole horror of it. He looked exactly like you. He was you. Or you're him. Or neither of those things is true and I'm standing here talking to a stranger with a dead man's face, and I don't know which possibility is worse, and I have spent six years not knowing, and I got on that skiff anyway because I couldn't *not* know anymore—"

She was shaking. She made herself stop talking, because she could feel the confession accelerating past the part she'd intended to give and toward the part she hadn't.

Casper stepped back. Just one step, but it opened a corridor of cold air between them, and Ila realized with a strange, distant clarity that she had wanted the distance and dreaded it in the same breath.

"You loved him," Casper said.

It wasn't a question either, but it deserved one, and she gave it the only honest answer that existed.

"I don't know what tense to use."

* * *

Afterward they sat in the galley, because the galley had two chairs and the rest of the Vesperine had almost none. Casper made tea with the automatic economy of someone who had performed the ritual ten thousand times, and Ila watched their hands and tried to reconcile them with the memory of other hands—the same hands, younger, turning a cup the same way, tapping the same rhythm against the ceramic, three taps, pause, three taps, some private metronome he'd probably never known he kept.

Casper was doing it now. Three taps. Pause. Three taps.

She must have made a sound, because Casper looked down at their own fingers and went still, as if catching themselves in an act of theft.

"Tell me about the nine days," Casper said.

"You want the whole thing?"

"I want everything. I've had eleven years of nothing, Ila. I have a service history that starts eleven years ago with a medical entry and a blank. I have a face I can't place in any mirror from before. I have a ship full of sleepers and a beacon I know how to maintain the way you know how to breathe, and I have never once asked *why* because asking why felt like opening an airlock." Casper set the tea down in front of her, and their hand was not quite steady. "And now there's a person sitting in my galley who knew me. Who knew *him*. And I'm supposed to what—pretend that doesn't matter? Let you leave without giving me back even the smallest piece?"

"I'm not leaving yet."

"You came on a skiff with a forty-day range and a research cover story that fell apart the moment you looked at me. You're not leaving. You're deciding." Casper sat down across from her. "So while you decide, talk. Please. Talk to me about him."

And so she did.

She told him about the observation rail, and how he'd asked her what an archivist actually *did* all day, and how she'd explained that most of her work was deciding what to keep, and he'd gone quiet in that way he had—head tilted, entirely still—and said, *that's the most important job there is, then. Someone has to choose what survives.* She'd thought he was flirting. She'd spent years afterward wondering if he'd been confessing.

She told him about the second night, when the station's gravity lurchers hiccupped during a docking accident and every loose cup on Meridian floated for four seconds, and he'd caught her tea out of the air without looking at it, without even breaking the sentence he was in, and she'd said *you've done zero-g work* and he'd said *everyone's done zero-g work* in a tone that closed the subject like a door. She'd filed it. Archivists filed everything. It was only years later, in the wreckage of every database she'd torn through looking for him, that the file had surfaced again and meant something.

She told him about the ninth day, the transfer arriving, the shuttle that would take him out to a ship whose name she'd had to dig through three registries to confirm even existed. She told him that he'd kissed her at the airlock and said, *if I'm ever lost, check the records. I'll leave myself somewhere findable.* And she told him—her voice flat now, stripped of everything but the fact—that she had checked the records. For six years. Station archives, guild registries, shipping manifests, the deep indexes of four different systems. And Casper Crane had not been in any of them. Not in the crew lists of the Vesperine. Not in the medical databases. Not in the death registries. He had been erased so thoroughly that the erasure itself was the loudest record she'd ever found.

"You don't erase a man," she said. "You erase a *file*. Someone looked at Casper Crane and saw a document that needed redacting."

Casper was staring at the table. Three taps against the ceramic. Pause. Three taps. Then their hand flattened, pinning the rhythm down, killing it.

"The medical entry," Casper said slowly. "The one at the start of my service history. It's dated eleven years and about two months ago. I always assumed it was an injury. Some accident I'd been patched up from." They looked up. "Meridian was eleven years and three months ago."

Ila felt the arithmetic land in her own chest like a dropped tool. "A month," she whispered. "A month after the airlock."

"A month after he kissed you and told you to check the records." Casper laughed—a short, terrible sound with no humor in it. "And there's no record. Because I *am* the record. Whatever happened in that medical bay, whatever they did—I'm what came out of it. I'm the redaction walking around maintaining a lighthouse."

"You don't know that."

"I know that you looked at me like a ghost, and I know that I have no past, and I know that somewhere in the hold there are forty pods and I have never once been able to make myself open one." Casper's voice was very quiet now. "I've had eleven years to open one. Eleven years of maintenance walks right past the bay door. And every time, something in me says *not yet, not you, you're not ready to see what's in there.* And I always thought that was cowardice." They looked at her, and their eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "What if it's not cowardice? What if it's the last thing *he* left in me? What if he knew what I'd find, and he couldn't stop me from finding it, so he just—he made sure I'd wait until I had a reason?"

Ila couldn't answer. Because she had spent six years building theories in the dark, and every one of them was worse than that, and none of them were kinder.

"I loved him," she said at last, because he had asked her to talk and because the truth was the only cargo she had left. "Nine days, and I loved him, and I have hated myself for six years for loving someone in nine days, because it felt like proof that I hadn't really known him, that I'd loved a surface. And then I walked into your docking bay and saw his face on a stranger, and I understood that I hadn't loved a surface at all. I'd loved the only nine days of him that anyone got to keep." Her voice broke and she let it. "And now I'm sitting across from you, and you're not him—you move differently, you're angrier, you're *lonelier* in a way he never was—and I don't know what to do with the fact that I already—"

She stopped.

Casper waited. The beacon sang. Forty hearts beat below them in the dark.

"You already what," Casper said, so softly it was almost not a question.

"Nothing." She wrapped her hands around the tea, which had gone cold. "It doesn't matter what I feel. It can't matter. Because if you're him, then I'm betraying him by loving someone new, and if you're not him, then I'm betraying *you* by loving a memory that happens to have your face. Either way I'm unfaithful to somebody. That's the trap. That's what I've been sitting in since the docking bay."

"That's a terrible way to live."

"It's the only way I've got." She looked up at them, and made herself hold their gaze, because he had deserved honesty and so did they, whoever they were. "You asked me what tense to use. Here's my answer: I loved Casper Crane. Past tense. He's dead—I believe that now, I have to believe it, because the alternative is that he walked away from Meridian and chose to forget me, and I can survive a dead man better than that. But you—" she set the cup down, "—you I met four days ago, and you wound that beacon like a man saying prayers, and you asked me to talk about *him* instead of pretending you *are* him, and that—"

She stopped again. This time because she heard what she was about to say and it was too large for the room.

"That what," Casper said. Not letting her off.

"That's a different person," Ila finished, barely above a whisper. "That's a person I might—"

The ship interrupted her.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing she'd remember afterward—how undramatic it was. A soft chime from the overhead, the kind of sound a kitchen timer makes, and Casper's whole body changed. Every soft thing in their face shut like shutters.

"What is it?" Ila asked.

"Proximity alert." Casper was already moving, tea forgotten, crossing the galley in three strides. "Long-range. Something just entered the system's outer marker." They paused at the hatch, and for one moment the shutters opened again, and Ila saw the fear underneath—not fear of the contact itself, but fear of the interruption, fear of the sentence she hadn't finished hanging in the air between them like an unsecured load.

"Ila. When we're through whatever this is—finish the sentence."

Then they were gone, boots ringing up the corridor toward the bridge, and Ila sat alone in the galley with two cups of cold tea and the shape of everything she'd almost said.

On the display by the galley door, unattended, the proximity feed updated itself with a small, patient line of text: *CONTACT BEARING 114 MARK 3. DESIGNATION: HLC-771. CLASS: HEAVY CRUISER. BROADCAST IDENT: HALCYON LEDGER — SALVAGE & RECOVERY.*
