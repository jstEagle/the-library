## 8. Cargo of Minds

The manifest arrived in pieces.

That was the first thing Ila understood about it, hunched over the Vesperine's archive terminal at what the ship insisted was 0300 hours: whatever this document had been, it had been *broken* on purpose. Not deleted—Casper's beacon file had been deleted, scrubbed clean with the surgical arrogance of someone who owned the erasure—but shattered. Shredded into fragments and scattered through the ship's data architecture like a message folded into a thousand paper boats and set adrift in the dark.

She'd found the first fragment by accident, three days after the gravity stuttered. She'd been running a routine integrity sweep of the hold's environmental logs—archivist's habit, the compulsive cataloguing of other people's neglect—and the sweep had returned a checksum error so old it predated Casper's tenure by decades. Buried inside the error, nested like a seed in stone, was a header: **CALL-TRN/01**, followed by forty-one characters of ciphertext that made no sense until she realized they weren't encrypted at all. They were *compressed*. Compressed the way you compress something you intend to reconstruct later, when it's safe, when the war is over.

Except the war, whatever it had been, had never bothered to end.

* * *

"You're going to want to sit down," Ila said.

Casper was already standing in the archive doorway, which meant they'd been there long enough to hear her talking to herself, which meant they'd been listening to her mutter at fragments for some time now. Eleven years alone aboard a dying ship will do that to a person—Ila had watched it happen in the way Casper moved through rooms, narrating their own actions under their breath, *checking the couplings, checking the couplings*, as if the sound of a voice were proof of being crewed.

"I don't sleep much anymore," Casper said. "Since the nine seconds." They crossed to the terminal, and Ila watched them read over her shoulder—their breath stirring the fine hair at her temple, close enough that she could feel the warmth of them and had to make herself not lean into it, because leaning into Casper Crane felt like leaning into a photograph of someone she used to know.

"Forty pods," she said. "You told me forty pods in the hold. You've been winding their environmentals for eleven years."

"Forty-one." Casper said it quietly, automatically, the way a person corrects a detail they've corrected a thousand times to no one. "There's one pod that runs cold. I've never opened it."

Ila turned in the chair. "You've maintained a pod for eleven years without ever opening it?"

"It didn't ask to be opened." Casper's face did something complicated then—a flicker of expression that moved too fast to name, there and gone like a fish under ice. "None of them have. That's not—" They stopped. Started again, slower, as if feeling their way along a wall in the dark. "Ila, why are we doing this at three in the morning? What did you find?"

She turned back to the screen because it was easier than looking at them. "I found the manifest. Or the skeleton of one. It's been distributed across the ship's maintenance architecture—hidden inside coolant schedules, structural load calculations, the beacon's own timing tables. Whoever built this hiding scheme was very good, and whoever tried to destroy it was better, but destruction leaves scars, and scars can be read." She pulled up the reconstruction she'd been building all night: a lattice of recovered fragments, each one tagged with its hiding place, gaps glowing red where the data was simply gone. "It's a shipping manifest, Casper. A cargo declaration. And the cargo is listed by mass, volume, power draw—and consciousness signature."

The word hung in the recycled air between them.

"Cargo doesn't have a consciousness signature," Casper said.

"No. People do." Ila pulled up the first decoded block. "The Vesperine wasn't a survey ship. Not originally. The survey designation is a skin stretched over something older—it's been re-registered four times, each registration eating the last, and underneath all of them is a colonial transport charter out of a system called Calloway's Reach. Does that mean anything to you?"

She felt rather than saw Casper go still behind her. "No," they said, and then, honestly, terribly: "Should it?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She keyed the next fragment open. "Calloway's Reach appears in exactly eleven documents in every public archive I can access from this ship, and all eleven are the same document copied eleven times: a census annex noting the colony's founding population at thirty-eight thousand and its current status as—listen to this phrasing—*'administratively dormant.'* No dissolution record. No disaster report. No evacuation log. Thirty-eight thousand people don't become administratively dormant, Casper. Colonies get purged, or they get abandoned, or they get erased, and the difference between those words is who's writing the history."

"And the manifest says—"

"The manifest says the Vesperine left Calloway's Reach carrying forty-one passengers in suspension. Not crew. Passengers. Look at the fields." She highlighted them one by one, her finger tracing the columns on the screen. "Neural compression ratio. Memory integrity index. Continuity assurance rating. These aren't life-support metrics—they're *fidelity* metrics. Whoever loaded these pods wasn't keeping bodies alive. They were keeping *minds* intact. Whole personalities, compressed down and stored, waiting to be poured back into something that could hold them."

The terminal hummed. Somewhere below them, through four decks of corroded plating, the hold breathed its slow mechanical breath—forty-one hearts beating at one beat per hour, or however you counted a heartbeat that had been paused mid-sentence.

Casper sat down slowly in the second chair, the one that groaned on its mountings, the one Ila had privately named the Confession Chair during her first week aboard because everyone who sat in it ended up telling her something true. "A colony's minds," they said. "Smuggled out."

"Smuggled is the right word. Look at the routing." She brought up the navigation fragments—not many survived, but enough. "The Vesperine's original flight plan isn't a survey loop. It's an evasion pattern. Sixteen course changes in the first seventy-two hours, each one designed to break sensor contact, each one timed to a window when the system's own traffic control was—here's the phrase—*'attending elsewhere.'* Someone with access to Calloway's Reach's infrastructure looked at what was coming for that colony and decided that thirty-eight thousand people weren't all going to fit on the ships that were leaving. So they took the next best thing. They took the people themselves, folded up small, and ran."

"Forty-one out of thirty-eight thousand."

"Forty-one out of thirty-eight thousand." Ila heard her own voice go flat, which was what it did when the numbers got too large to carry any other way. "And the manifest has a priority column. These forty-one weren't random. They're flagged by role—geneticists, engineers, teachers, a composer, two people whose entries just say *witness*—and every single flag is annotated with the same note. Let me find it." She scrolled, and there it was, the phrase she'd read a dozen times tonight and still hadn't gotten used to: **RETENTION PRIORITY: CIVILIZATION-CRITICAL.** "Whoever chose these forty-one wasn't saving people. They were saving *enough people to be a people*. A seed bank of minds. Enough to restart everything, if anyone ever came back for them."

"And nobody came back."

"Nobody came back." She said it gently, though gentleness felt obscene applied to arithmetic like this. "Eleven years, Casper. Your beacon has been burning for eleven years, and the colony that built it has been administratively dormant the whole time, and these forty-one minds have been sleeping in your hold the whole time, and the war that emptied their world—" she gestured at the red-glowing gaps in the reconstruction, the places where the manifest's final pages had been deliberately burned away, including, notably, the field that should have named the enemy— "that war's name was the first thing they cut out. Before the coordinates, before the sender's identity, before anything else. Somebody made sure that even if you found this manifest, you couldn't find out *who they were running from*."

"Because whoever it was might still be looking."

"Because whoever it was might still be listening."

* * *

They sat with it for a long time. The ship creaked around them in its old-bone way, thermal expansion ticking through the hull like a clock counting down nothing in particular. Ila watched Casper's face in the terminal light and saw the moment the moral weight landed—not as a blow but as a settling, the way ballast settles in a hull, shifting everything a few degrees off true.

"I wound their environmentals," Casper said finally. Their voice had gone somewhere small. "Every day. For eleven years. I checked nitrogen levels and adjusted humidity and logged pulse indicators, and I thought—" A breath. "I thought I was maintaining equipment. I thought it was a lighthouse and a storeroom and a job that somebody had to do, and I did it because nobody else would, and I never once asked myself what kind of cargo needs its atmosphere tuned to within two percent of baseline for a decade."

"You couldn't have known."

"Couldn't I?" Casper laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a strange raw edge. "Ila, I knew the beacon wasn't a lighthouse before you ever docked. I've known since the nine seconds. And now you're telling me the hold full of pods I've been singing to—because yes, I talk to them, don't look at me like that—is a nation in exile, and my first instinct isn't horror. My first instinct is *relief*. Do you understand that? Some part of me has been waiting eleven years for this cargo to mean something, and now it does, and the meaning is that I've been the janitor for the last survivors of a genocide, and my relief is that I wasn't wasting my time."

"I don't think that's what you feel."

"Don't you?" Casper looked at her then, really looked, and Ila held the gaze because she owed them that much and more. "What do I feel, Archivist? You're the one who reads people for a living."

Ila considered lying. She was, she admitted to herself, quite good at it, and there were versions of the truth she wasn't ready to hand over yet—the version where she'd known a Casper Crane before this one, the version where her arrival here was not the accident she'd let them believe. But the Confession Chair worked both ways, and Casper had asked.

"You feel *chosen*," she said. "And you feel guilty about feeling chosen. Because forty-one minds in suspension means forty-one minds that trusted someone enough to be switched off, and that someone left them with you—or left you with them, I genuinely don't know which direction that sentence goes anymore—and being trusted like that is the heaviest thing a person can carry. Heavier than grief. Grief you can put down. Trust you have to keep holding, because the moment you set it down, it stops being trust."

Casper stared at her. "How do you do that?"

"Do what?"

"Say the thing I've been circling for eleven years like I've been saying it my whole life." There was something almost frightened in it. "I've never told anyone about the pods. Not the singing, not the schedule, none of it. And you walk in and describe the inside of my head back to me like you're reading from a file."

*I have read files about you,* she almost said, and bit it back, and hated how easy the lie was becoming, how smoothly it wore the shape of discretion. "Archivists spend their lives learning what preservation costs," she said instead. "You learn to recognize it in others. Devotion has a shape, Casper. Yours is very legible."

Something passed over Casper's face—hurt, maybe, or its cousin—and Ila realized too late that *legible* could land as *predictable*, and predictable could land as *not special*, and she wanted to take it back and couldn't, and the silence that followed had a new texture, thinner, watchful.

* * *

It was Casper who broke it, by standing and walking to the viewport.

From the archive, the view faced aft—back along the Vesperine's scarred spine toward the hold module, a blunt cylinder studded with radiator fins, unremarkable as a grain silo. Forty-one minds. Thirty-eight thousand dead or dormant or worse. One beacon burning its patient frequency into the dark, and Ila suddenly understood that frequency differently now: not a lighthouse's come-hither, not a lock's keep-out, but a *roll call*. A voice saying, once per cycle, into a void that had stopped answering: still here, still here, still here.

"My grandmother kept seeds," Ila said, to Casper's back. "On Meridian. Not professionally—just in jars, on a shelf in the utility room. Beans and peppers and one variety of tomato that her mother had carried from a place that doesn't exist anymore. Every year she planted a few and saved the rest, and when I asked her why she bothered, when the station hydroponics sold better stock for less effort, she said—" Ila smiled at the memory, and the smile hurt. "She said, *because someday someone will come asking, and I want to have an answer.*"

Casper didn't turn around. "Is that why you became an archivist?"

"Partly. Partly because I wanted to be the person who comes asking." She rose and joined them at the viewport, standing close enough that their reflections overlapped in the glass. "Casper. If the manifest is right—if those pods are Calloway's Reach—then this ship isn't derelict. It's an embassy. It's the entire surviving territory of a nation, and its borders are the walls of your hold, and you're not its janitor. You're its government."

"That's worse."

"It's worse," she agreed. "But it's also true, and true things have a way of getting heavier whether you pick them up or not."

Below them, the hold hummed. Ila found herself thinking about the cold pod—the forty-first, the one Casper had never opened—and about the manifest's passenger list, which she'd reconstructed only partially, most names lost to the deliberate burns. But one entry had survived, tucked in a timing table like a coin under a floorboard, and she had turned it over and over all night:

**PASSENGER 00 — RETENTION PRIORITY: ABSOLUTE — ROLE: NAVIGATOR/GUARDIAN — NOTE: *stays awake***

One mind, out of forty-one, designated not to sleep. One person chosen to remain conscious through the whole dark crossing—to wind the beacon, tune the atmosphere, sing to the pods, and wait. And the Vesperine's current navigator had no memory of volunteering, no memory of the choice, no memory of anything before waking alone at a console with a wrench in their hand and a job that needed doing.

She didn't say it. Not yet. The weight in the room was already more than two people could stand upright beneath, and she was a coward in exactly one respect: she could not bear to be the one who told Casper Crane who they used to be. Not while she still wasn't sure which of them she'd be telling.

Instead she said, "There's a gap in the manifest. The destination field. The original flight plan ends at a set of coordinates that were burned out with everything else—but the beacon's timing tables reference a relay. Something at the edge of dead space. Something the ship was supposed to reach."

Casper's reflection turned toward hers. "You think the beacon's been pointing at it all along."

"I think the beacon's been *counting* toward it all along. And I think—" She stopped, because the terminal chimed.

Not the archive chime. The comm chime. Long-range, unscheduled, incoming.

They both looked at it. On the screen, a transmission header assembled itself letter by letter, and Ila read it twice before her throat would work:

**INBOUND VESSEL: HALCYON LEDGER (SALVAGE CLASS) — CLAIM TYPE: DERELICT RECOVERY — ESTIMATED ARRIVAL: 61 HOURS**

"They found us," Casper said quietly.

"No," Ila said, watching the claim type scroll beneath the header, watching the word *derelict* attach itself to a ship that was, as of tonight, a nation. "Someone told them we're here. And salvage law is very clear about what happens to cargo when a derelict gets claimed."

Behind them, down four decks, forty-one hearts beat once per hour apiece, patient as seeds in jars, waiting for someone to come asking.

Sixty-one hours.
