## 10. Terms of Surrender

The message came in on the low band, the one nobody used anymore because nobody was left to use it. Ila found it at 0400, ship's time, in the archive alcove she had slowly colonized over the past weeks—her portable terminal wired into the Vesperine's ancient data spine, her coat draped over the back of a chair that had been bolted down before either of them was born.

*Vessel Vesperine. This is the salvage cruiser Halcyon Ledger, registry Meridian Free Port. We hold a prior claim. You have forty hours to surrender the vessel and cargo. Noncompliance will be treated as piracy.*

The words sat on her screen like a diagnosis. Ila read them three times, waiting for the part where it stopped being real, and the part never came.

She should have woken Casper. He slept badly these days—she could hear him through the bulkhead, the particular restlessness of a man whose body had started telling him things his mind refused to say aloud. Instead she sat in the blue glow of the terminal and did what archivists do with terrible documents: she read them again, looking for the seams.

There was a seam. Beneath the formal demand, a second channel, encrypted lightly—lightly enough that it was meant to be found. A private frequency. A courtesy, or a hook.

She looked at it for a long time before she opened it.

*Ms. Voss,* the message said. *We know who you are. We know what you're carrying. Come to the table and we can talk about how you walk away from this.*

*—O. Marsh, Master, Halcyon Ledger*

Ila closed her eyes. The hold hummed through the deck plating, that low patient sound she had stopped noticing weeks ago and now heard constantly, the way you hear your own heartbeat when you're afraid. Forty pods. Forty sleeping minds. A colony's worth of someone, packed in gel and cold, and a syndicate cruiser sitting out past the shoal like a creditor who had finally found the debtor.

She thought about Casper's face in the hold bay, the day he'd found the pod that wore his own features. She thought about the way he'd said *I don't understand* in a voice that understood perfectly.

She keyed the channel.

*This is Voss. What do you want to talk about?*

The reply came back in under a minute, which meant Marsh had been waiting up too. That was somehow the most frightening detail yet.

*Everything,* Marsh wrote. *But not in writing. Docking collar, port side, 0600. Come alone. Bring nothing you'd miss.*

* * *

The docking collar between the Vesperine and the Ledger's skiff was a steel throat six meters long, and Ila walked down it feeling like a letter being delivered to someone who intended to burn the envelope.

She had dressed carefully—not her best, because her best said *I have somewhere better to be*, but her second-best, which said *I am a professional and professionals are expensive to kill*. She had told Casper she was running a spectral analysis on the beacon's carrier wave. The lie had come out smooth and that frightened her more than the message had. Lies were supposed to cost something. Hers were getting cheaper.

The skiff's airlock opened into warmth and light, and that was the first shock: the Ledger was *clean*. After weeks aboard the Vesperine's soft decay—bulkheads furred with condensation, corridors that smelled of ozone and old tea—the cruiser's interior was bright as a hospital. White composite walls. Air that tasted filtered and faintly of citrus. Somewhere, a real gravity plating hummed at a frequency just different enough from the Vesperine's to make her stomach notice.

Ondine Marsh was waiting in a small wardroom with a table set for two. Not a negotiation room—a *breakfast* room. There was a pot of actual coffee, which Ila hadn't smelled in three years, and two cups, and a plate of something that might once have been bread.

"You're younger than your file," Marsh said. She was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the compact build of a woman who had spent her life in ships built for smaller people. Her hands were still. That was the thing Ila noticed most—hands that never fidgeted, never reached, never apologized. "Sit. Eat. The bread's synthetic but it's honest synthetic."

"I'm not hungry."

"Everyone's hungry. They just forget." Marsh poured the coffee herself, both cups, and pushed one across. "Ila Voss. Formerly senior archivist, Meridian Station Central Records. Dismissed—resigned, the file says, but I've read enough resignations to know a defenestration when I see one. You spent two years pulling decommissioned beacon records for a private client who paid you in access instead of money. And then you booked passage on a survey ship that officially doesn't exist, crewed by a navigator who officially doesn't exist." She smiled without warmth. "I do my homework. It's the only homework worth doing."

Ila didn't touch the coffee. "You said you wanted to talk about how I walk away."

"I did. I do." Marsh sat, folded her still hands on the table, and for a moment the syndicate captain was gone and something older and tireder looked out through her face. "Here's what's going to happen, Ms. Voss, and I'd rather you hear it from me than from the hull breach. The Ledger holds a salvage claim on this vessel. It's a good claim—clean chain of title, filed before your navigator was born. In thirty-six hours I file the enforcement order, my people board, and the Vesperine becomes mine. The cargo becomes mine. And the cargo, Ms. Voss, is worth more than every ship I've ever taken combined."

"Forty people in suspension."

"Forty *assets* in suspension." Marsh said it without cruelty, which was worse. "I've read the manifests you pulled. Partial, encrypted, but I have better cryptographers than you have patience. Stored minds. Calloway's Reach, if the fragments are right." Something flickered across her face then—there and gone, like a fish under ice. "You know what happened to Calloway's Reach?"

"No one does. That's the point. The war erased the name."

"The war erased the name," Marsh agreed. "The war didn't erase the debt. There are parties who paid a great deal of money to make sure that colony stayed asleep and stayed *lost*, and there are other parties who will pay a great deal more to have it delivered." She leaned back. "I'm the delivery mechanism. It's not a noble job. It's a job."

"And me?"

"You're the interesting part." Marsh turned her cup a quarter-rotation on the table, precise as a lock mechanism. "You're not crew. You're not cargo. You're a passenger with a documented research purpose and a clean legal identity. When I take this ship, you have three options. One: you resist, and you're detained as an accessory, and your clean legal identity becomes a very expensive problem. Two: you stay aboard out of sentiment, and you go wherever the ship goes, and where the ship goes is a syndicate auction block. Or three—"

She slid a data chit across the table. Ila looked at it without touching it.

"—you deliver the vessel. You walk onto my deck, you transmit the surrender code from the Vesperine's own authentication—your navigator will give it to you, or you'll find it, or you'll convince him it's the merciful thing—and in exchange, the Ledger logs you as cooperating civilian witness. Passage out. Any port in charted space. A stipend. A clean record, scrubbed of Meridian, scrubbed of this whole sad errand." Marsh's voice softened by a degree, and the softness was the most dangerous instrument in the room. "You go back to being an archivist, Ms. Voss. A good one. With a career. You're what, thirty-one? You could have thirty years of work that matters. Archives. Memory. The things you actually love."

"And the forty sleepers?"

"Delivered to their buyers. Which is more than the war was going to give them."

Ila finally picked up the coffee, mostly to have something to do with her hands, and found it was good—real, bitter, hot enough to hurt. Mercy, she thought, tasting it. This is what mercy tastes like when it's priced.

* * *

Marsh let her talk. That was the trap inside the trap.

Ila had prepared arguments—legal ones about the claim's provenance, ethical ones about sentience in suspension, practical ones about the beacon and whatever it was guarding—but Marsh deflected none of them and answered all of them, gently, the way you talk someone down off a ledge by agreeing the view is lovely.

"You think I don't know what he is to you," Marsh said, at one point. "The navigator. I've watched the traffic between your terminals. Two people alone on a dying ship learn each other fast. It's not love yet. It's the *shape* love makes when it's running out of time." She refilled Ila's cup unasked. "That's exactly why you should take the offer. Because in thirty-six hours, that man is going to be cargo or collateral, and you will watch it happen, and it will break something in you that thirty years of good work won't fix. I'm not offering you a bribe, Ms. Voss. I'm offering you an exit before the building comes down."

"He's not cargo."

"No. He's worse." For the first time, Marsh looked away, out the wardroom's viewport, at the Vesperine hanging in the black with its one lit window. "He's a *key*, and he doesn't know what he opens. Do you understand what that means? It means every hour he stays ignorant is an hour someone else decides what he's for. The people who built that beacon didn't leave a man aboard. They left a lock with a heartbeat. And locks—" her still hands tightened, just once, around her cup, "—locks get broken by whoever gets there first. I'd rather it be me. At least I'll keep them cold. The buyers might not."

It was a good argument. That was the horror of it. Ila sat in the warm light with real coffee in her hands and Marsh's mercy laid out like a bed made up for a guest, and she could feel the arithmetic working on her the way it must have worked on a hundred people before her: *one life saved against forty lives frozen, and the forty aren't even awake to lose anything.*

"You lost someone," Ila said.

Marsh's face did something very small. "Everyone lost someone. That's not a question."

"That's why the bread. That's why the coffee. You feed them first."

Marsh was quiet for a moment. "My family signed with a colony ship when I was nine. Sleep-out contract. Whole generation, packed away to skip over a war. The war ended. The ship was never found." She turned her cup again, that quarter-rotation. "So yes, Ms. Voss. I know exactly what's in your hold. I've spent forty years wondering if my mother is in a hold like it, somewhere, waiting for someone to press the right button. And I'm telling you, as someone who has imagined that button every night of her life—" she looked up, and her eyes were dry and very old, "—sometimes the button doesn't wake anyone. Sometimes it just opens the door for the thing that's been listening."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a *forecast*." Marsh stood, and the audience was over; Ila could feel the room reclassify her from guest to deadline. "Thirty-four hours. The chit's good until then. You don't have to decide now. You have to decide before I stop being able to be merciful." She walked Ila to the airlock herself, and at the threshold, in the steel throat of the collar, she said the thing Ila would carry back down the corridor like a splinter:

"He'll forgive you, you know. That's the worst part. Whatever you choose—he'll find a way to make it your duty, and forgive you for it, and mean it. I've read his file. People like that always do."

* * *

The Vesperine received her in cold and shadow and the smell of old tea, and it had never felt so much like home, and that frightened her most of all.

Casper was awake. Of course he was awake; the beacon's rhythm had shifted in the night, some fractional change in its carrier wave that only he could hear, and he was at the main console with his sleeves rolled up, doing the thing he did—winding it, tuning it, keeping the impossible promise of a machine nobody had commissioned.

He looked up when she came in, and his face did the thing it always did now, the small involuntary brightening he probably thought he was hiding.

"You were gone a while."

"Marsh wanted to talk."

His hands went still on the console. "I know. I saw the skiff dock. I've been sitting here for two hours deciding whether to ask you what she said." He turned in the chair, and in the console's amber light his face was open in a way that made the splinter go deeper. "I decided to ask. Whatever it is. I'd rather hear it from you."

Ila stood in the doorway with Marsh's chit in her pocket and the coffee's ghost still bitter on her tongue, and she heard herself say, "She offered me passage out. In exchange for the ship."

Something moved behind Casper's eyes—hurt, and then, immediately, terribly, the forgiveness Marsh had promised, arriving on schedule, unasked. "And you came back to tell me."

"You sound surprised."

"I sound—" He stopped. Looked at his hands. "I don't know what I sound like anymore. Eleven years on this ship and in the last month I've found out I might be a copy, that my ship is a prison, and that the only person I've talked to in a decade had a ticket out the whole time." He laughed, short and unamused. "I'm not surprised. I'm just—trying to find the version of this where I don't lose you."

The beacon hummed. Through the viewport, out past the shoal, the Halcyon Ledger sat in the dark with its running lights burning, patient as a deadline.

And then the console chirped—a sound Ila had never heard it make, a bright insistent triple tone, and Casper was on his feet before it finished, because his body knew it before his mind did.

"That's the beacon," he said. "It's never—that tone isn't in the maintenance manual. It's not in *any* manual." His fingers moved across the keys, decoding, and Ila watched his face as the final transmission assembled itself out of eleven years of faithful, mindless winding—coordinates, she could see them forming, a point in the empty chart-space past the system's edge.

Casper went very still.

"It's not a lighthouse," he said quietly. "It never was. It's an *address*."
