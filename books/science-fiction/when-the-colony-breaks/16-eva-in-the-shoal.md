## 16. EVA in the Shoal

The tethers came aboard at 0400 ship's time, which was a fiction Casper had invented years ago and now could not stop believing in.

They heard it first through the hull—a sound like knuckles dragging along the outside of the world, four magnetic clamps biting into the Vesperine's flank one after another, each impact a small earthquake translated through eleven years of corrosion and patchwork plating. Casper was already awake. They had been awake since the debris shoal began to sing against the forward sensors, that granular hiss of rock grinding on rock, and so they felt the boarding latches in their teeth before their hands found the overhead rail.

"They're on," Ila said from the nav alcove, her voice pitched low, as if the hull might carry it outward. "Four lines. Aft port quadrant."

Casper pulled up the external feed and watched the camera's grainy eye confirm it: four umbilical tethers, thick as a man's leg, running from the Vesperine's hull back into the dark where the *Halcyon Ledger* hung like a held breath. The tethers were not just mooring lines. They were conduits—power draw, data siphons, and worst of all, a hard connection the syndicate could use to force an airlock cycle whenever Marsh grew tired of waiting. Every minute the lines stayed attached, the Ledger read the Vesperine's pulse: life support margins, pod telemetry, the forty small heartbeats sleeping in the hold.

And somewhere below decks, in a maintenance crawl no salvage crew had bothered to map, Sef's pod ticked softly, half-awake, whispering fragments of a name.

"Cutting them isn't the problem," Ila said. She had pulled up the schematic without being asked, because she always did, because she had spent three weeks learning this ship the way other people learned lovers. "The problem is you'll be doing it inside a rock garden moving at nine kilometers a second relative drift."

"The shoal's thinned on the starboard side." Casper traced the plot with two fingers. The debris field wrapped around them like a slow-motion storm—shattered hull plate, regolith, the fossilized bones of some mining operation dead a century—all of it tumbling on currents of residual momentum, none of it predictable past about ninety seconds. "There's a lane. Narrow. But if I go out through the dorsal hatch and traverse the spine, I can reach the aft junction clamps without ever crossing open space."

"Without ever crossing open space," Ila repeated. "Except for the part where you cross open space."

"Ila."

"Don't 'Ila' me." She turned in the alcove chair, and the low emergency lighting caught her face in amber, and for a moment Casper forgot entirely about the four tethers chewing on their hull. She looked tired in the way that made her more herself, not less—the archivist's stubbornness worn openly now, no longer disguised as professional interest. Three weeks ago she had been a stranger with a forged research permit. Now she was the only voice Casper trusted more than their own, and they were both still pretending that fact didn't terrify them.

"If the Ledger pulls those tethers taut while you're outside," she said, "they don't just board us. They reel you in with the rest of the cargo."

"I know."

"And if a shard comes through your suit—"

"I know."

"You keep saying *I know* like knowing is the same thing as coming back."

Casper looked at her. Outside, something scraped along the dorsal plating—the boarding party walking the hull, checking their anchors, patient as tide. Inside, the hold hummed its constant note, forty pods breathing their refrigerated breath, and Casper thought: *this is what the original must have felt every day.* Not fear. Arithmetic. The cold, continuous arithmetic of one person standing between a thing that sleeps and everything that wants it.

"Talk me home," Casper said. "That's all I'm asking. Keep the channel open and talk me home."

Ila held their gaze for three full seconds. Then she reached over and keyed the suit charger, and the indicator light went green, and that was her answer.

* * *

The airlock cycled at 0417.

Casper had done EVAs before—eleven years of maintaining a beacon meant eleven years of crawling along its struts with a wrench and a prayer—but never like this, never with the ship under way through a debris shoal, never with a syndicate crew anchored to the same hull they were climbing. The moment the outer door cracked, the sound of the shoal arrived whole: a vast dry whispering, millions of fragments hissing past the suit's microphones, the acoustic signature of a graveyard settling.

The Vesperine's dorsal spine stretched ahead, a ridge of handrails and conduit runs running sixty meters aft toward the junction where two of the four tethers met the hull. Below and to port, the other two. The suit's heads-up painted the hazards in patient orange: drift vectors, fragment tracks, the Ledger's distant mass hanging off the bow like a knife someone had left balanced on the edge of the world.

"Casper." Ila's voice in the earpiece, close enough to be breath. "Spine is clear for the first twenty meters. After that there's a shadow zone where my sensors lose resolution behind the comm tower. You'll be blind to me and I'll be blind to you."

"So talk louder."

A pause. Then, softer: "I will."

Casper clipped the tether reel to the first handrail and started moving. Hand over hand, boots magnetized, the old rhythm taking over—the body remembering what the mind had been denied, again, always. Eleven years of winding a beacon had built something into Casper's muscles deeper than memory: a patience with machinery, a feel for load and torque, a way of listening to metal. They moved down the spine the way water moves downhill, without deciding to.

At thirty meters, the comm tower's shadow swallowed them.

The universe contracted to the width of a handrail. No stars—the tower blocked them. No Ila—her signal stuttered into static hash. Just the hiss of the shoal and the orange ghost-lines of predicted fragment paths drifting across the visor like weather forecasts for hell. Casper kept moving. Counted rails. Twelve. Fifteen. Twenty.

Something pinged off the tower above them—a pebble of ancient slag, no bigger than a thumbnail, striking with the crack of a rifle shot—and Casper's whole body flinched, and the flinch cost them their grip.

For one full second they hung by the safety leash alone, swinging out over the port side, the hull falling away beneath their boots, and the shoal opened beneath them like a mouth. Fragments tumbled past at closing speeds that turned gravel into shrapnel. And far below, catching the light of the Ledger's floodlamps, Casper saw it: the aft port junction clamp, and beside it, a figure in syndicate gray, working a cutting torch along the Vesperine's sensor trunk.

They weren't just holding on. They were harvesting.

"Casper?" Ila's voice, broken by static. "Your biometrics just spiked. Talk to me."

"I slipped." Their own voice sounded foreign inside the helmet. "I'm fine. I'm—" They got a boot back onto the rail. Pressed down until the magnet caught with a click they felt in their shin. "Ila, there's a tech on the hull. Port side, aft junction. He's cutting the sensor trunk. If he gets through it, we're blind to the shoal for the rest of the transit."

"Then the tethers aren't the mission anymore."

"They were never the mission," Casper said, and pushed off down the spine.

* * *

The last ten meters were the longest of Casper's life, and they would spend the rest of it, whatever length that turned out to be, unable to say why exactly. Nothing went wrong. That was the thing. The shadows parted, the rails held, the shoal hissed its indifferent hiss, and Casper arrived at the aft junction with the tech still bent over his torch, unaware, absorbed in his theft of another person's eyes.

Casper settled behind the junction housing, drew the line cutter from the thigh kit, and keyed the radio. "Ila. I'm at the clamps. Two lines here, two on the far side. Once I cut, the Ledger will know within seconds. There won't be time for the second pair unless—"

"Unless you cut both pairs yourself." Static chewed the edges of her words. "Traverse under the hull. Forty meters of open space between junctions, Casper. In this drift."

"I did the math on the way out."

"Do the math again. Out loud. I want to hear you do it."

So Casper did it, out loud, into the dark: closing velocities, gap timing, the window between fragment swarms that the sensors showed opening in ninety seconds and closing in maybe forty after that. Forty meters of nothing between them and the next handhold. If the math was right, they'd cross during the lull. If the math was wrong—

"Casper." Ila's voice changed. It dropped all its argument, all its fear, and became something level and warm, a rope thrown across the gap before Casper asked for one. "I'm going to be talking the whole time. You don't have to answer. Just move when I tell you the sky's empty, and hold when I tell you it isn't. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"Then trust me faster than you're scared. Ready?"

Casper looked up at the black between the junctions—at the tumbling glitter of the shoal, at the Ledger's lights burning beyond it like a city seen from underwater—and understood, with a clarity that felt almost gentle, that they had been afraid since the gravity first stuttered nine months ago, and that every single day since then, Ila had been the reason the fear hadn't finished the job.

"Ready."

"Sky's empty. Go."

Casper let go.

The universe rotated. For a heartbeat there was nothing beneath them at all—no ship, no rail, no beacon, no name—just Casper Crane, fourth of their name or no version of it at all, adrift in a river of dead stone with forty sleeping hearts at their back. Then the thruster puff, calibrated by muscle memory they didn't remember earning, and the far junction swelling in the visor, closer, closer—

"Fragment cluster, two o'clock high, hold position—"

Casper froze mid-drift. Above and ahead, a knot of shattered plating tumbled through the space they would have occupied, close enough that its shadow crossed their visor like the wing of something enormous. Gone. Three seconds, five.

"Clear. Move."

Another puff. Another rotation. The junction rail swung up to meet their gloves and Casper caught it, both hands, the magnet slamming home, and they were laughing—actually laughing, breath fogging the inside of the helmet—before they remembered where they were.

"There it is," Ila said quietly. "There's the sound I wanted."

* * *

The second pair of tethers took ninety seconds to cut. The first pair had taken forty; these were thicker, better anchored, and Casper's hands were shaking now in the delayed arithmetic way, the body presenting its bill after the transaction cleared. They set the cutter against the first line and pressed.

The tether parted with a groan Casper felt through the hull rather than heard, whipping away into the dark like something released from a long captivity. Alarm klaxons bloomed silently across the Ledger's superstructure—lights strobing behind vacuum, a silent fury at this distance. On the second line, Casper didn't wait to admire the effect. Cut. Both severed ends drifted apart, and the Vesperine shrugged, minutely, like a sleeper shifting a shoulder.

Then the floodlamps found them.

A cone of white light snapped across the junction, pinning Casper to the hull like a specimen. Through the glare, the tech from the port side stood at the edge of visibility, torch in one hand, and behind him—farther out, riding a maneuvering pack above the shoal's glitter—two more figures in syndicate gray, rising toward the Vesperine's spine.

"Casper." Ila's voice, sharp now. "Movement on the Ledger's deck. They're launching riders. You have maybe two minutes before they reach the hull."

"Working on it." Casper jammed the cutter back into the kit and grabbed the rail. "Dorsal hatch is forty meters forward. If I go spine-side, I run straight into them."

"Then don't go spine-side." A pause, and Casper could hear her thinking, could almost see her in the nav alcove with the schematics reflected in her eyes, the archivist hunting through data the way she hunted through everything—relentlessly, sideways, finding the door nobody had catalogued. "Underneath. There's a maintenance channel along the keel—an inspection crawlway with external hatches, pre-refit. It's on the original schematics, the ones from before the records scrubbed themselves. Nobody who boarded this ship in the last decade would even know it exists."

"Because nobody remembers this ship," Casper said, already moving, dropping over the junction's edge into the shadow of the keel.

"Because nobody stayed," Ila said. "You stayed. That's the whole trick, Casper. That's always been the whole trick."

Casper moved along the underside of the world, hand over hand, the shoal whispering past inches from their spine, the Ledger's searchlight slicing uselessly above them. Behind, the riders reached the empty junction and found cut cables and no one. Ahead, the inspection hatch waited, small and unglamorous and utterly forgotten, exactly the kind of thing that survived only because one stubborn person had kept oiling its seal for eleven years without knowing why.

The hatch accepted Casper's code. The lock, older than the scrubbing, older than the lie, turned like it had been waiting.

Inside, as the outer door sealed and pressure crept back into the crawlway, Casper stood in the dark with their helmet in their hands, breathing recycled air, alive, and listened to Ila's voice come through the suit radio still clipped to their collar, still talking, telling them the drift vectors, the rider positions, the state of the sky—as if she hadn't yet noticed the mission was over, or as if she had noticed and simply wasn't ready to stop.

"Ila," Casper said. "I'm inside. I'm safe."

Static. Then a laugh, shaky and unguarded, the first unguarded sound Casper had ever heard her make. "Copy that, Navigator. Welcome home."

But the celebration lasted exactly as long as it took Casper to climb down into the hold and check the pod telemetry—because Sef's pod was no longer ticking. Its readout climbed steadily, patiently, toward waking, and on the small screen beside it, in characters that had not been there an hour ago, a single line of text repeated itself:

**THEY KNOW WHERE FOUR IS.**
