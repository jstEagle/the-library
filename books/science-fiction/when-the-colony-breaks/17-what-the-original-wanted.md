## 17. What the Original Wanted

Sef woke in fragments.

That was the only way Ila could describe it, sitting cross-legged on the deck beside the open pod with a diagnostic slate in her hands and no idea what any of the numbers meant. The pod had been cycling its revival sequence for six hours—slowly, carefully, the way Casper had coaxed it, feeding the sleeper's systems one gentle increment at a time instead of the hard boot the relay protocols demanded. And what had come out of that patience was not a woman but a weather system: Sef surfaced and submerged, lucid for ninety seconds, then gone somewhere deep and gray for twenty minutes, her lips moving around words that belonged to no conversation in the room.

"She's dreaming the old memories," Casper said. They were standing at the pod's foot, arms folded, wearing the expression Ila had come to think of as their watchman's face—steady, unreadable, a little too still. "The suspension fluid preserves the hippocampus better than the cortex. Short-term comes back last. She's going to keep sliding for a day or two."

"You've done this before," Ila said. It wasn't a question. She'd stopped framing them as questions three days ago, when she'd finally admitted to herself that every instinct Casper had about the pods—their tolerances, their rhythms, the exact pitch of hum that meant contentment versus distress—was knowledge with no legitimate origin. Casper Crane, last navigator, keeper of a beacon nobody had commissioned, knew the sleepers in the hold the way a shepherd knows individual sheep.

"I've maintained them," Casper said. "That's different."

"Is it?"

Casper didn't answer. Out in the hold, forty pods breathed their slow amber breath, and the beacon overhead turned its patient circle, and somewhere beyond the hull the Halcyon Ledger was still out there in the dark, patient as arithmetic.

Ila turned back to Sef. The woman was perhaps fifty, though suspension had frozen her somewhere in her forties—brown skin gone gray-gold in the pod light, close-cropped hair, a faded tattoo on her collarbone that might once have been a constellation. Her eyes moved under their lids. Her mouth shaped syllables. Ila had angled the slate's pickup toward her an hour ago, on a hunch she hadn't shared with Casper, and the recorder had been drinking in every half-word since.

She was an archivist. Fragmentary testimony was her native country. She knew how to wait for a record to declare itself.

* * *

It came in pieces across the long watch.

"—third rotation, the vote was—no. No, the *vote*—"

"—told them. I told them the purge ships wouldn't wait for a second ballot—"

"—Casper said. Casper always said—"

Ila's chest tightened at that one. She glanced up and found Casper watching her, not Sef, and there was something in their face she couldn't parse—wariness, maybe, or the particular exhaustion of a person waiting for a floor to open.

"Casper said what?" Ila asked gently, though the question wasn't really for Sef.

Sef surfaced a little, her eyes half-opening, unfocused, finding the pod light and nothing else. "Casper said," she murmured, "somebody has to stay *outside* the sleeping. That's the whole design. A lock needs a hand. You can't put the key inside the lock." A breath, wet and slow. "He volunteered before the vote finished. Before anyone could argue. He said—" Her face crumpled, and for a moment she was forty years old and terrified and awake, wherever she was. "He said he'd rather be the one who remembers alone than make anyone else do it."

Then she slid under again, and the pod hummed, and the hold was very quiet.

Ila sat with it. She was good at sitting with things; it was most of her training. But this piece of testimony wouldn't sit still. It kept rearranging itself around the person beside her, this Casper, this *fourth*, who had spent eleven years winding a beacon they couldn't explain and tending pods they couldn't account for, and who had learned this morning—three days ago now, at the relay's archive, in a room grown rather than built—that they were a copy. That the original was eleven years dead. That the hands winding the beacon had been the original's hands all along, or as close as flesh could come.

And now Sef, dreaming backward through her own preserved years, had handed them the missing middle of the story: the original Casper Crane had not been assigned to the Vesperine. He had *chosen* it. Chosen to be the first backup—chosen, Ila understood with a slow cold settling, to be copied before the sleep, so that a version of him could stand outside the suspension and keep the watch while the original slept with his people, or died with them, or both.

"He volunteered to be first," Ila said quietly. Not to Casper, exactly. To the record.

"Yes," Casper said.

"You knew."

"I suspected. The relay's archive confirmed most of it." Casper crouched beside the pod, forearms on knees, and looked at Sef's sleeping face with an expression Ila had no name for. "But hearing it from her is different. The archive has dates and designations. She has—" A small, helpless gesture. "She has the room where it happened. The vote. The argument I apparently ended before it started."

"You ended it by out-nobling everyone," Ila said. It came out sharper than she intended.

Casper looked at her. "Does it bother you? That he was like that?"

*He.* The pronoun sat in the air between them like a coin balanced on its edge. Ila had been careful with pronouns for days now—*the original, the first, the one on the record*—and Casper had begun using *he* for the dead man and *I* for themselves with a clean, deliberate separation that Ila suspected was costing them something every time they did it.

"It doesn't bother me," she said. "It frightens me. It's different."

"Tell me."

She thought about how to say it. The slate hummed in her lap. Out in the hold, pod twelve's amber light cycled, and pod thirty-one, and the whole slow constellation of them, forty hearts beating at one beat per minute, a colony folded up small enough to fit inside a dying survey ship.

"When I was training," Ila said, "they taught us about archival ethics. The first rule is that a record is not a person. The second rule is that you will be tempted to treat a record as a person, because records are *easier*. They don't argue. They don't need things. They just mean what they mean, forever." She turned the slate over in her hands. "The original Casper is a record to me. A magnificent one. A man who volunteered to be copied and left behind so his people would have a key outside the lock. And the terrible thing—the thing that frightens me—is that the better I understand him, the more I understand that I never knew him at all. Because a man who does that, who *gives himself up* like that, so completely, so early, before anyone could even offer to share the burden—" She shook her head. "That's not a person I loved on Meridian Station. That's a monument. And I have been grieving a monument for six years while the person standing in front of me is—"

"Is a copy," Casper said. Flat. "Fourth iteration. No legal self. No original memories past the point of the first scan."

"Is *mine*," Ila said, before she could stop herself.

The word landed in the hold and echoed off forty pods. Casper went very still. Sef, mercifully, stayed under.

"I didn't mean—" Ila began.

"You did," Casper said. Not unkindly. "You meant it exactly. And I need you to know that I've been doing the same arithmetic from the other side." They stood, slowly, and walked to the pod's edge, and looked down at Sef as if the answer might be written on her sleeping face. "Every memory I have of the beacon's commissioning, of the vote, of *her*"—a nod at Sef—"is secondhand. Muscle memory. Reflex. I know how she takes her tea and I don't know her name until someone says it. I loved—Ila, *he* loved you, and what's left of that in me is a groove worn into a surface by a wheel that isn't turning anymore. I feel the shape of it. I don't have the wheel."

Ila's throat ached. "And yet you stayed on the course he set. Eleven years."

"Because the course was *right*," Casper said, with a sudden fierceness that startled them both. "That's what I keep coming back to. I can't verify a single feeling he had. I can't confirm that he loved you, or that he loved them—" another gesture, at the hold, at the colony in suspension, "—or that the duty meant what she says it meant. But I can verify the work. The work held. Forty people are alive right now because somebody wound a clock every day for eleven years in a ship that everyone else had written off. Whatever he was, whatever I am—the work was *true*. And I don't know how to be anything except the person who keeps it true."

* * *

Sef surfaced again near the end of the watch, and this time she came all the way up—eyes open, gaze tracking, the gray receding from her face like water from a shore.

Ila was ready. She'd spent the quiet hours assembling the recorder's fragments into order, and when Sef's eyes cleared, Ila leaned in and said, softly, "Sef. My name is Ila Voss. You're aboard the Vesperine. You're safe. Do you know where you are?"

Sef's gaze crawled across the ceiling, the pod frame, the beacon light wheeling overhead. Then it found Casper, and something moved through her face—relief, grief, recognition, in that order.

"Four," she said.

"I'm here," Casper said.

"Not—" Sef's hand twitched against the pod's rim, too weak to lift. "Not you. I mean—" Her brow furrowed, the effort of precision almost more than her waking mind could fund. "The first. Our Casper. He stood right there. Right where you're standing. The day we went under. He said—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He made us a promise. He said: *when you wake, someone will be here to tell you it's over, and you're safe, and the war lost.* He said that's what the beacon was. Not a lock. A voice. He said a lock only keeps people out, but a promise keeps people *together*." Her eyes filled, and she blinked the suspension fluid's residue out of them impatiently, angry at her own body. "He gave up waking whole so we'd have that. He told the council: *copy me first, and let the copy keep the promise, because I can't promise and sleep at the same time, and I won't ask anyone else to be split in half.*"

The hold hummed. The beacon turned.

"And then he went under," Sef whispered, "and the copy stayed, and we slept, and that was the last bargain of Calloway's Reach." Her gaze sharpened suddenly, found Casper's face again, and held it with the terrible clarity of the newly lucid. "You're not him. I can see it. You hold yourself different. He carried the promise like a weight. You carry it like—" She searched. "Like a question you haven't finished asking."

Casper's jaw tightened. "And which is better?"

Sef almost smiled. "Ask me when I've been awake longer than an hour."

She slid under again—not as far this time, Ila could tell, the gray shallower now, the tide turning—and the pod resumed its breathing hum, and the two of them were alone with forty sleepers and the wreckage of a dead man's nobility.

* * *

Later, in the narrow galley, Ila found Casper staring at a cup of tea they hadn't drunk.

"He split himself in half," Casper said, without turning. "That's what she said. *I won't ask anyone else to be split in half, so I'll do it to myself first.* And the relay's archive says the split worked exactly as designed. The first backup kept the beacon. The original slept. And when the original's pod failed—eleven years ago, quietly, in the dark, while I was winding the clock above him—" Casper's voice caught, just barely, a hairline fracture in the steadiness. "I was maintaining the machine that was killing the man I'm a copy of. And I didn't know. I *wound the clock* over his grave."

"Casper—"

"And the worst part." They turned, and their eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "The worst part is that I understand him completely. Every choice. Every step. I can feel the logic of it in my own hands, the way I can feel the beacon's winding pattern. And I don't know if that's because I'm *him*—four copies deep, worn smooth—or because anyone would have done it, or because duty like that isn't nobility at all but just a very beautiful way of—" They stopped. Set the cup down. "Ila. If the choice comes. If it comes to restoring his final memories, becoming the lawful key, being the original again in every way that counts—"

"Then you'd lose the eleven years," Ila said. "The years that made *you*."

"I'd lose you," Casper said simply. "He didn't choose you. He chose them. And he was right to. And I don't know how to be a man who was right to." A breath. "I only know how to be the one who stayed."

Ila crossed the galley and did the thing she had been not-doing for six years and three weeks: she took their hand. Casper's fingers were cold from the hold's chill, and they closed around hers with a grip like a man holding a tether in open space, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left to say that the holding didn't say better.

It was Ila's slate that broke the silence—a soft chime, the long-range receiver waking. She almost ignored it. Then she saw the header, and her stomach dropped.

The Halcyon Ledger had changed its broadcast. No longer a salvage claim, dry and legal. Now it was a demand, repeating on every frequency, addressed by name:

*VESPERINE. YOU HAVE TWELVE HOURS TO HEAVE TO AND PREPARE FOR BOARDING. CAPTAIN O. MARSH. THIS IS NOT A NEGOTIATION.*

And beneath it, in the clear, a second line that made Ila's blood run cold:

*WE KNOW THE CARGO IS WORTH MORE AWAKE. DELIVER THE SHIP INTACT OR WE TAKE IT IN PIECES.*

Casper read it over her shoulder. When they spoke, their voice had gone quiet and level, the watchman's voice, the voice of a person who had already begun moving toward the decision.

"Then we'd better make sure there's nothing left aboard worth taking in pieces."
