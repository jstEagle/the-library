## 23. The Relay's Door

The dead space did not announce itself. That was the first thing Casper noticed, and the thing they would remember longest: no threshold, no shimmer, no line drawn across the viewport where the living universe ended. One moment the stars were stars—distant, indifferent, plentiful—and the next they were fewer, as if someone had gone through the sky with a damp cloth and wiped away the ones that mattered least.

Casper stood at the helm of the Vesperine with both hands flat on the console, feeling the ship breathe beneath their palms. Eleven years of maintenance had taught them the Vesperine's moods the way a spouse learns breathing in the dark: the arrhythmic shudder of the port thruster when it ran cold, the way the environmental system sighed every forty minutes like an old man settling into a chair, the faint harmonic that lived in the deck plating and rose into the soles of the feet. All of it was still there. But around it, the silence had changed texture. Comms were dead—not broken, *absent*, the way sound is absent underwater. The long-range array returned nothing but a hiss so uniform it felt manufactured, a wall of static built by something patient.

Behind them, Ila sat cross-legged on the deck with her archive slate balanced on her knees, though she hadn't read anything from it in over an hour. She was watching the viewport too. Watching the sky get thinner.

"It's not empty," she said quietly. "Dead space. Everyone calls it empty."

"I know," Casper said.

"But it isn't. Look at the distribution." She lifted the slate and turned it toward them without looking at it herself; her eyes stayed on the glass. "The stars that are left aren't random. They're spaced. Evenly. Like—" She stopped, and Casper watched her arrive at the word the way you watch someone step onto ice and feel it hold. "Like markers."

"Like a road," Casper said.

Neither of them said the rest of it aloud. They didn't need to. The beacon's final transmission had decoded to coordinates, and those coordinates pointed here, into the wiped-clean dark, at a relay that every chart in eleven databases insisted did not exist. And now the dark itself was arranged like a corridor, and they were walking down it.

Casper engaged the approach burn. The Vesperine answered sluggishly—the old girl had never been quick, but out here even her sluggishness felt deliberate, like a person lowering their voice in a church. The hold hummed behind them, forty pods, forty suspended hearts, and Casper found themselves listening for it the way they listened for the beacon: not because it needed checking, but because the sound was proof. *Still here. Still sleeping. Still mine to carry.*

Sef's pod was different now. It rode in the cargo cradle they'd moved to the aft compartment, its cycle light burning amber instead of blue, waking and sleeping in shallow intervals since the debris shoal. Sometimes she spoke. Fragments, mostly—names, a word that might have been a child's nickname, once a full sentence in a language Casper didn't recognize and then, horribly, half-recognized. But sometimes she said *Four*, in a voice gone soft with age or grief or both, and each time it landed in Casper's chest like a coin dropped down a well.

They flew for six hours down the road of sparse stars. Then the relay appeared.

* * *

It did not appear so much as it became apparent—one moment a smudge against the last usable star, the next a shape with edges, and then all at once *there*, filling the forward third of the viewport, and Casper heard Ila make a small involuntary sound that she would later deny making.

Every station Casper had ever seen—and they had seen hundreds, docked at dozens, serviced three—had been built. Welded. Riveted. Assembled by hands or machines following plans, every joint a decision, every seam a place where two things that were not originally one had been persuaded to coexist. Human construction had a grammar, and Casper knew it fluently.

The relay had no seams.

It grew out of the dark the way coral grows out of rock, or the way a tree grows out of a hillside—as if it had always been there and the dark had simply agreed to make room. Its structure branched. It forked and rejoined itself in sweeping organic arcs, spines of some material that was neither metal nor stone but caught the starlight and gave it back slowly, reluctantly, like something being asked to remember. The whole station was perhaps four kilometers across at its widest span, and it turned—no, it didn't turn, it *breathed*, the branches flexing by fractions of degrees on a rhythm Casper's body recognized before their mind caught up.

Forty seconds in, sixty seconds out. The same rhythm as the backup pods in the hold.

"Oh," Ila whispered. "Oh, it's synchronized to them."

"It's synchronized to everything," Casper said, and heard their own voice come out strange.

Because it was true, and the truth kept arriving in pieces. The relay's outer surface pulsed with light—not running lights, not beacons, but a diffuse luminescence rising and falling along the branches like sap moving under bark. And the frequency of that light, the actual wavelength of it, was one Casper knew better than their own heartbeat. Eleven years of winding the beacon had burned it into them: the particular violet-white of the Vesperine's signal, the frequency whose maintenance manual listed no manufacturer, whose power draw matched nothing in the ship's schematics, whose wind-down ritual Casper performed with hands that remembered steps their mind had never learned.

The relay was lit by the beacon's frequency. Every branch, every arc, every hollow of it, glowing in the exact color of the light Casper had tended alone for a third of their life.

"That's impossible," Ila said. Her voice had gone thin. "That frequency is registered to your beacon. It's in the commissioning file—or it *was*, before it deleted itself. It's yours. It's specific."

"Yes," Casper said.

"So either the beacon is broadcasting this station's light across half a system for eleven years—"

"—or the beacon was never broadcasting anything of its own." Casper finished it because someone had to, and saying it made it worse, made it solid, made it real in the way words are real. "It was relaying. It was always relaying. I wasn't keeping a lighthouse lit. I was keeping a window open."

Ila looked at them then, and Casper saw her doing the arithmetic she'd been doing since the salvage skiff, since the moment she'd frozen at the sight of their face: adding up everything Casper didn't know about themselves and finding the sum kept growing. Pity was in her expression, and love, and something fiercer than either—a kind of anger on their behalf, at a universe that had used a person as a component and called it duty.

"Casper," she started.

"Approach vector's holding," Casper said, because if they let her speak they would have to stop flying, and the Vesperine still needed flying. "There's a docking throat on the primary trunk. It's open."

"How do you know it's open?"

Casper opened their mouth to say *the sensors* and then actually looked at what the sensors were showing, which was nothing—which was a smooth unbroken surface with no aperture anywhere—and yet Casper's hands had already entered the approach pattern, throttle and trim set to numbers that had arrived fully formed in their fingers. "I know it's open," they said. "The way I know how to wind the beacon."

* * *

The docking throat opened for them at four hundred meters. There was no other way to describe it: a section of the trunk's surface dilated outward, petals of that slow-glowing material peeling back like an iris, revealing a cavity lined with the same violet-white light. No guidance beams. No docking clamps extended. Just an opening, held patient, exactly the size and shape of a survey ship that had been designed—Casper understood now—for exactly this.

"They built the door to fit us," Ila said. "Or the door has been waiting to fit us."

"The Vesperine is a survey ship. Standard hull." But even as Casper said it, they knew it wasn't true, hadn't been true for longer than the eleven years. A standard survey ship didn't have a hold rated for forty suspension pods. A standard survey ship didn't have a navigator's station wired to accept a beacon frequency that matched no known manufacturer. A standard survey ship didn't have a captain who volunteered to be the first backup of a colony that officially, in every record that survived, had never existed.

The Vesperine slid into the throat. The petals closed behind them with a sound they felt rather than heard—a deep, structural exhalation, the station accepting them the way a body accepts breath—and the external feed went violet.

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Casper released the harness and stood, and their legs nearly failed them, and it wasn't fear. It was recognition. Standing on the deck of the Vesperine inside the relay, Casper felt what a word feels when it finally finds its definition: *so that's where I've been all along.*

"Suit up anyway," they said, voice steadier than they felt. "We don't know the atmosphere. We don't know anything."

"We know it's lit," Ila said, pulling her suit jacket from the hook. "Eleven years of the same frequency. If it wanted to hurt us, it's had a decade and a half of notice."

"If it wanted to hurt us," Casper said, "it wouldn't have needed to wait. It could have let the Ledger catch us in the shoal." They sealed their collar and checked Ila's seals without asking, an intimacy of buckles and gaskets that had become, somewhere over the past weeks, the most honest conversation they had. "Whatever this place is, it's been waiting for the ship. Maybe for longer than the ship has existed."

The airlock cycled. The inner door opened onto the docking throat's floor—a surface warm through their boot soles, faintly yielding, like packed earth after rain—and beyond it, a corridor.

* * *

The corridors were grown.

Casper had expected it and was still unprepared for it. The passage arched overhead in smooth ribs that branched and merged like the interior of a heart, and the walls gave off that same violet-white luminescence, brighter here, pulsing gently with the forty-second rhythm. Underfoot the floor was seamless and slightly springy, and every few meters a thin line of brighter light ran along its center, leading deeper, the way a path leads through a garden when the gardener wants you to follow.

No dust. That detail arrived late and hit hard. Eleven years the Vesperine had fought dust and decay and entropy in every seam; this station, ancient beyond guessing, was immaculate. Either it maintained itself, or—

"Or time works differently here," Ila murmured, running a gloved hand along the wall. Her slate was out, sampling passively. "Casper. The material is reading as *alive*. Not biochemically active—alive. Cell walls. Nucleic analogues. It's not a station with biology in it. It's an organism we're walking through."

"Is it awake?"

Ila was quiet for a moment. "I think," she said slowly, "we're inside its sleep. Or its dream. The activity patterns are..." She shook her head. "They match the pods. They match Sef's pod exactly. Whatever state those people are in, the station is in it too. Everything here is holding its breath."

"Waiting for what?"

"You already know the answer to that."

Casper did. Waiting for the key. Waiting for the lock to be brought to the door. Forty minds asleep in the hold of a dying ship, one navigator who didn't know what they were, and a beacon that had spent eleven years whispering this station's frequency into a system that had forgotten why it mattered. The whole architecture of Casper's life—the loneliness, the rituals, the winding, the devotion disguised as habit—rearranged itself around this moment and suddenly had a shape, and the shape was a keyhole.

They walked. The light-lines led them inward, branching when the corridor branched, always choosing the direction Casper would have chosen anyway, which stopped feeling like coincidence after the third junction and started feeling like courtesy. Or like the station knew the way Casper thought. Which raised questions Casper deliberately did not ask, because the answers were walking beside them in the form of a woman who loved someone with their face.

The corridor opened without warning into a chamber.

It was vast—easily a hundred meters across and tall enough that the ceiling vanished into soft darkness above the reach of the light. The walls curved away in tiers, and the tiers were lined with niches. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each niche was empty, each one the size and shape of a suspension pod, each one rimmed with the violet-white glow, each one dark and waiting.

Casper stood at the threshold and felt awe arrive, enormous and clean, the pure animal wonder of standing inside something older and larger and more patient than yourself.

And then the awe curdled.

Because the niches weren't random. They were arranged in rows, and the rows were grouped, and the groupings repeated in a pattern Casper's eyes traced involuntarily—clusters of forty. Rows upon rows upon rows of clusters of forty, thousands of them, stretching up into the dark. This chamber was not built to hold one colony. It was built to hold colonies beyond counting. Whole populations, sleeping in their forties, stacked to the ceiling of a station in dead space, waiting for ships that would bring them home.

"This isn't a relay," Casper said. Their voice came out very small in the huge room. "This is an ark. This is where everyone goes when the colony breaks."

Ila had gone pale inside her suit collar. Her slate was recording everything, and her hand was shaking, and she didn't try to hide it. "Casper. The niche nearest us. Look at the designation."

Casper looked. Above the nearest empty pod-shaped hollow, etched into the glowing material in characters that predated every alphabet Ila's archive contained and yet resolved, effortlessly, into words Casper could read:

**VESPERINE — MANIFEST 1 OF 1 — KEYHOLDER: CRANE, C.**

Not *Captain*. Not *Navigator*. Keyholder. And beneath it, smaller, in the same impossible script:

**ITERATIONS AUTHORIZED: FOUR. ITERATIONS EXPENDED: THREE.**

The chamber's light pulsed once—all of it, every tier, every niche, the whole organism flaring violet-white in a single heartbeat—and in the pulse's wake, the far wall began to change. Light gathered there, flowing like water finding its level, forming shapes, forming figures, forming a doorway where no doorway had been.

Through it, approaching at an unhurried walk, came a figure in a navigator's uniform eleven years out of date.

Casper knew the walk. Knew the set of the shoulders, the slight favoring of the left knee, the habit of tilting the head to listen to machinery. It was the walk they saw in mirrors every morning.

The figure stopped at the edge of the light, and its face resolved, and it was Casper Crane's face, worn older, worn tired, worn kind—and it looked at them with an expression of such vast, gentle sorrow that Casper's whole body went cold.

"Hello, Four," the original Casper Crane said. "You took the long way. Come sit down. We don't have much time, and there's something you need to decide."
