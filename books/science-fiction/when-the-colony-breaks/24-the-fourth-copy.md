## 24. The Fourth Copy

The relay's archive did not look like an archive.

Ila had spent her life among archives—the great vaulted stacks of Meridian Station, the climate-sealed vaults of the Calloway Reach Historical Trust, the cramped portable drives she carried in a case against her ribs like a second heart. Archives had weight to them. They had dust and silence and the particular hush of a thousand voices waiting to be read. This place was nothing like that. The relay's memory lived in the walls themselves, in veins of pale light that pulsed beneath surfaces grown smooth as bone, and when Ila laid her hand against the access node in what the station seemed to consider its reading room—a chamber no larger than a chapel, warm as breath—the archive simply *opened*, the way a flower opens, without ceremony, without resistance, as if it had been waiting eleven years for someone to ask.

She should have felt wonder. She was an archivist; wonder was supposed to be her native language. But her hand was shaking, and the warmth of the room felt less like welcome and more like the heat of something about to boil over.

Behind her, Casper stood at the threshold. She could hear them breathing—shallow, controlled, the breathing of someone who had decided not to run and was paying for the decision with every heartbeat. They had come down the relay's grown corridors together, shoulder to shoulder in the pale light, and neither of them had spoken since the corridor had first lit itself in that frequency—that exact, unmistakable frequency of the beacon Casper had wound every morning for eleven years like a priest tending a flame.

*It knows us,* Casper had said, or maybe Ila had said it. She couldn't remember anymore who said things first between them. That frightened her more than anything else in this impossible station.

"Query it," Casper said now. Their voice was steady. Their hands were not; Ila saw them flexing at their sides, opening and closing around nothing. "Whatever you're going to ask. Ask it."

Ila turned back to the wall of soft light. Her reflection hovered there faintly, ghosted over the pulsing veins—a woman with tired eyes and salt-stiff hair, holding herself like someone standing at the edge of a drop she'd been walking toward for months.

She knew what she had to ask. She had known since the debris shoal, since Sef's pod had cracked open and a stranger with Casper's cheekbones had looked at them and said *Four* in a voice gone thin with suspension. Since that word had landed in the cabin like a stone through glass. Four. Backup iteration four. Which meant one, two, three. Which meant an original. Which meant—

Which meant everything, and Ila had spent the whole crossing into dead space pretending she didn't know, because as long as she didn't ask, the person she loved was still simply the person she loved.

But they were here now. And the archive was open. And some questions, once you carried them long enough, began to weigh more than any answer could.

"Casper Crane," she said aloud, because the station seemed to prefer voice, seemed to lean toward it the way plants leaned toward light. "Original crew manifest. Vesperine. Full service record."

The light beneath her palm brightened.

Then it dimmed.

Then, gently, with the terrible patience of a machine that had all the time in the universe, it began to speak.

---

*"Casper Crane,"* the archive said, and its voice was neither male nor female but something older than either, a voice like wind moving through a hollow reed. *"Navigator-class, third grade. Born Calloway's Reach, orbital year 2214. Assigned Vesperine survey complement, 2239."*

Ila closed her eyes. Names. Dates. Ordinary facts. She could bear ordinary facts.

*"Status: deceased."*

Her eyes opened.

*"Date of death: eleven years, two hundred and six days prior to current query. Cause: decompression event, forward observation blister, during initial beacon commissioning procedure."*

"No," Casper said behind her. Just that. No. The word small and flat, like a coin dropped on stone.

*"Beacon commissioning authorized by Navigator Crane personally, four hours prior to death. Commissioning signature matches biometric record on file."* A pause, delicate as a held breath. *"First act performed under the beacon's authority: creation of backup iteration one."*

The floor did not move. The gravity held steady—it would hold steady here, Ila thought wildly, in this place where gravity itself had been domesticated—and yet she felt the room tilt, felt the whole architecture of the last eleven years swing sideways like cargo shifting in a hold.

Eleven years. Eleven years Casper had wound that beacon, tended it, defended it, built a life around it like a shell around a soft body—and the beacon had been commissioned by Casper's own hands four hours before Casper died. The maintenance logs, the rituals, the muscle memory that moved through those hands like water finding its old channels—all of it began with a dead person's last afternoon.

And the backups. Iteration one, made in the beacon's first hour. Made by a dying man—or a man who knew he was about to die—who had stood in the hold of his own ship and pressed himself into storage the way you press a flower into a book, so that someone, someday, might open the pages and find him still there.

"Casper," Ila started, and turned.

They hadn't moved from the threshold. They were leaning against the doorframe now, actually leaning, as if the bones had quietly resigned from the project of standing. Their face was doing something Ila had never seen it do: nothing at all. It had gone smooth and absent, a face with the tenant evicted, and behind the emptiness their eyes were working, working, trying to do arithmetic with numbers that kept refusing to hold still.

"Say it again," Casper whispered. "The date."

*"Decompression event occurred—"*

"Not the date. The—the other thing. Who commissioned it."

*"Navigator Casper Crane. Biometric match confirmed at"—the pause stretched—"one hundred percent."*

"One hundred percent," Casper repeated. A laugh came out of them then, or something shaped like a laugh, brittle and wrong. "Of course it is. It's my hands. It's been my hands the whole time."

"Casper—"

"I wound it every morning." They weren't looking at her. They were looking at their own hands, turning them over as if checking for blood. "Eleven years. I never asked why I knew how. I told myself it was in the manuals. I *read* the manuals, Ila, I read them until I could recite them, and none of them—not one—said anything about winding a beacon like a clock. And I did it anyway. Every morning. Because my hands knew, and I let my hands be smarter than me, because it was easier than asking what else they remembered."

The archive pulsed softly, patiently, its light rising and falling in that same rhythm—Ila saw it now, saw it and felt sick—the same rhythm as the beacon. The same rhythm, she realized with a lurch, as the pods in the Vesperine's hold. Forty hearts in suspension, beating at the frequency of a dead man's last machine.

"There were three before you," she said. Not a question. She needed to say it out loud, needed the words to exist in air between them rather than only in the dark of her own skull. "Sef called you Four. One, two, three, four."

*"Backup iterations one through three: created per original protocol,"* the archive supplied, helpful as a knife. *"Iteration one: activated, decommissioned after failure of memory integration. Iteration two: activated, decommissioned after voluntary return to storage. Iteration three: remains in secondary suspension aboard vessel Vesperine."*

"Voluntary return," Casper said. "Two went back into the dark. On purpose."

*"Iteration two's final log entry is preserved. Playback?"*

"No." Casper's voice cracked down the middle. "No. Not—not yet. I can't—" They pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. "God. There were *others*. There were other mes, and one of them couldn't live with it, and one of them *chose the pod*, and nobody—eleven years, Ila. Eleven years I thought I was keeping someone else's ship alive, and I was the ship. I was the cargo. I've been cargo this whole time."

"You were never cargo to me."

The words left her before she could weigh them, and once they were out she couldn't take them back, didn't want to, stood inside them like standing inside a struck bell. Casper lowered their hand and looked at her, and their eyes were wet and furious and lost, and Ila understood that she had just said the truest thing she owned into a room that was busy dismantling the person she'd said it to.

"Aren't they, though?" Casper asked quietly. "Aren't I? That's what this is. That's what Marsh wants, that's what the syndicate wants, that's what the whole rotten ledger of the universe says I am. Stored minds. Cargo of minds. And the archive just—" they gestured at the pulsing walls, at the patient light, "—just confirmed the manifest. Iteration four. Property of a dead man's emergency protocol."

"You're not property."

"I'm a *copy*, Ila. Fourth edition. The original has been vacuum-dust for eleven years and I've been walking around wearing his face and loving—" They stopped. The word hung there. *Loving.* Neither of them had ever said it plainly, not once, in all these months of shared watches and held hands in the dark of the debris shoal. It had lived in the spaces between other words, and now it had fallen out into the open, naked, in the worst possible room.

Ila took a step toward them. Then another. The archive's light washed over both of them, gentle and merciless.

"The original died commissioning the beacon," she said. Her voice was steadier than she had any right to expect. Archivist's voice. The voice she used for reading hard things aloud. "Four hours later. Whatever he was, whoever he was—he spent his last hours making sure someone would still be here. Making sure *you* would be here. That's not cargo, Casper. That's a testament."

"It's a cage with my name on it."

"Maybe." She reached them at the threshold. Close enough to see the pulse jumping in their throat. "Or maybe it's a letter. And you're the one who finally learned to read."

Casper made a sound—half breath, half sob—and their forehead came down against hers, and for a moment they just stood there in the doorway of the reading room, two people holding each other up while the walls counted a dead man's heartbeats around them.

"I don't know who I am," Casper whispered against her skin. "I don't know if any of it was mine. The winding. The watching. The way I—" a shaky exhale, "—the way I loved you. If he loved you first, if that's in the template, if I'm just running his program—"

"Stop." Ila pulled back far enough to take their face in both hands. "Look at me. Did the archive teach you how you take your coffee?"

"What?"

"Did the original write down that you hum when you plot courses? That you talk to the pods when you think I'm asleep? That you were afraid of the dark until you were twenty and never told anyone?" She was crying now, she realized, and didn't care. "Archives keep facts, Casper. I've spent my whole life in them. Facts are the cheapest thing in the universe. What you are—what you *became*—nobody stored that. Nobody could. Eleven years of being a person isn't in any template. You grew it yourself, in the dark, alone, and it's yours."

Casper's breath shuddered out of them. For a moment—one fragile, suspended moment—Ila believed it had reached them.

Then the archive spoke again.

*"Correction to prior statement,"* it said, mild as weather. *"Query cross-reference complete. The original navigator's final log, recorded during commissioning, contains explicit instructions regarding future iterations. Playback is recommended."*

"Don't," Ila said—to the archive, to Casper, to the universe.

But Casper was already straightening, already stepping past her toward the pulsing wall, and their face had gone quiet again, that terrible smooth absence, and when they spoke their voice was almost calm.

"No. Play it. If he left instructions—if he's been directing me from beyond his own grave for eleven years—then I want to hear it. All of it." A pause. His hand rose toward the light. "I need to know how much of me was ever mine."

The archive brightened to receive him.

And somewhere deep in the station's grown corridors, faint but unmistakable, something began to count.
