## 25. Restore or Remain

The relay's archive did not have a door. It had a threshold of light.

Casper stood at the edge of it with Ila's hand in theirs and felt the station's attention settle over them like weather. The corridor they had walked for the past hour—grown bone-white walls, ceilings ribbed like the inside of a shell, lights pulsing at exactly the frequency the beacon had hummed for eleven years—opened here into a chamber that was not so much a room as an organ. Something in the architecture breathed. Casper could feel it against their sternum, slow and vast, the way you feel a cathedral breathe when you stand alone in it long enough.

At the center of the chamber, suspended in a column of pale radiance, hung the archive itself: a lattice of crystalline threads, each one fine as spider silk, each one carrying light in pulses that Casper's navigator's eye read instantly as data—not metaphorically, not approximately, but *actually*, because the relay had been built by people who thought in navigation, who had made their records legible to anyone trained to read a star chart. Eleven years of winding a beacon had taught Casper this language without ever teaching them its name.

"You can see it," Ila said quietly. Not a question. She had watched Casper's eyes move through the lattice the way other people's eyes moved across a page.

"I can see it." Casper's voice came out strange to them. Flattened. "It's asking something."

The light shifted. And then, because the relay was old and courteous in the manner of very old machines, it translated itself into something a human mind could hold without breaking: a voice that seemed to come from everywhere the light touched, genderless, unhurried, speaking Standard with an accent from no world Casper knew.

**VESPERINE KEYHOLDER. ITERATION FOUR. QUERY PRESENTED.**

The words arrived not in Casper's ears but behind them, in the place where thoughts form. Casper felt Ila's grip tighten.

"Ask it what happens if we walk away," Ila said. "Right now. Before it says anything else."

But Casper already knew, in the way they had known the beacon's rhythms before they understood them, in the way their hands had always known maintenance rituals their mind could not source. The relay would let them walk away. It was not a trap. Traps were for things worth catching, and the relay had waited eleven years—or longer—for this moment with the patience of geology. It simply wanted an answer. It had been built to want nothing else.

"I'm listening," Casper said aloud, because saying it aloud made it a choice rather than a surrender.

The light gathered. The lattice brightened thread by thread, and Casper understood that the relay was assembling something—a presentation, an argument, a document. An archivist's nightmare or dream, depending on which side of it you stood.

* * *

**THE ORIGINAL CASPER CRANE DIED ELEVEN YEARS, FOUR MONTHS, NINETEEN DAYS AGO,** the relay said. **CAUSE: CASCADING NEURAL DEGRADATION FOLLOWING VOLUNTARY PATTERN TRANSFER. THE TRANSFER WAS SUCCESSFUL. THE BODY WAS NOT RECOVERABLE.**

Casper had known this since the chamber called *The Fourth Copy*. Knowing it and hearing it spoken were different animals. Hearing it was like the difference between reading about vacuum and standing in it.

**HIS FINAL MEMORIES WERE PRESERVED IN ACCORDANCE WITH THE CALLAWAY PROTOCOL. THEY ARE HELD HERE. THEY ARE INTACT. THEY ARE OFFERED.**

"Offered," Casper repeated. The word tasted like metal.

**MERGE IS AVAILABLE. THE PROCESS IS NOT DESTRUCTIVE TO THE CURRENT PATTERN. YOU WILL REMAIN. YOU WILL SIMPLY BE MORE.**

"*More,*" Ila said sharply, and her voice cracked across the chamber like a whip. "That's what it calls it? He died, and you're offering his ghost as an upgrade?"

**I AM OFFERING CONTINUITY,** the relay said, and for the first time Casper heard something almost like strain in it, the sound of a machine performing patience it had been designed to feel but had perhaps, over centuries, worn thin. **THE COLONY'S RESTORATION REQUIRES A LAWFUL KEY. THE LAW OF CALLAWAY'S REACH DOES NOT RECOGNIZE ITERATIONS AS PERSONS. IT RECOGNIZES ONE NAVIGATOR, APPOINTED BY VOTE, SWORN BY OATH, WHOSE IDENTITY IS DEFINED BY HIS UNBROKEN MEMORY CHAIN.**

The light rearranged itself. Threads braided into shapes, and the shapes became images, and the images became a courtroom Casper had never entered: a chamber on Calloway's Reach, decades gone, filled with people voting. Casper saw the original—their own face, younger, unlined around the eyes, wearing the same maintenance jacket Casper wore now—standing before the assembly with his hand raised.

**HE SWORE,** the relay said. **DO YOU WISH TO HEAR THE OATH?**

"No," Casper said.

They heard it anyway. Not because the relay forced it—because their own memory supplied it, muscle memory again, the body remembering what the mind was denied. Their mouth shaped words they had never spoken:

*I will keep them. However long. However dark. I will be the one who remembers, so that remembering is never lost.*

Casper's knees went soft. Ila caught their elbow, held them up, said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't already in her face.

* * *

They sat down eventually. The relay dimmed its light considerately, the way you lower your voice in a sickroom, and let them sit on the warm grown floor of a station older than any nation either of them could name, while the cruelest multiple-choice question in human history arranged itself between them like a third person.

Option one: merge. Take in the original's final memories—all of them, the eleven years Casper had lived as someone else's echo, overwritten or braided in, the relay was vague on which—and become, legally, actually, cosmologically, *Casper Crane, first of that name*. The lawful key. The one the colony's law recognized. The one whose word could open the pods, authorize the waking, command the relay, sign the colony back into existence. Every door unlocked. Every authority granted. And threaded through all of it, ineradicably, the memories of a man who had loved a life Casper had never lived—who had maybe loved people Casper had never met, grieved losses Casper had never grieved, sworn an oath Casper had only inherited.

Option two: refuse. Remain iteration four. Remain the person who had wound the beacon for eleven years out of love they couldn't explain, who had fallen—God help them both—in whatever this was with Ila, who had cut boarding tethers in a debris shoal and talked their way home along a radio line. Remain whole in the only way they had ever been whole. And remain, in the eyes of every law the colony had carried sleeping through the dark, *no one*. A copy. A ghost with a maintenance schedule. Without legal self, without authority, without the right—even—to consent on behalf of the forty sleepers whose hearts they had guarded for eleven years.

"You could do so much good," Ila said finally. Her voice was careful, the way it got when she was handling something fragile. "You could wake them properly. You could give them back their world. All you'd lose is—"

She stopped.

"Myself," Casper said. "Say it. All I'd lose is myself."

"That's not what I was going to say."

"It's what you were going to say."

Ila looked at the lattice instead of at them. In its light her face was half-illuminated, half-shadow, and Casper thought: *this is what I am right now. Half of me lit by a dead man's memory. Half of me hers.*

"The man I knew," she said slowly, "on Meridian Station—he used to say that identity wasn't a thing you had, it was a thing you did. Daily. Like winding a beacon." A small, broken laugh escaped her. "I thought he was being charming. Archivists fall for charming."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was leaving instructions." She turned back to Casper, and her eyes were wet and furious and certain. "If you merge, the person who chose me disappears. Not dies—disappears. Dissolves into him. And everyone will tell you it's the same person, because the pattern's continuous, because the memories braid, because the math works. But I'm an archivist, Casper. I know what happens to documents when you merge collections. You don't get two truths. You get one record, edited. And the editor is always the older collection."

The relay waited. It was very good at waiting. It had been built for it.

* * *

Casper stood, because sitting made the question feel like something they could defer, and it was not deferrable. They walked to the edge of the light column and looked into the lattice, and the lattice looked back, and somewhere in those ten thousand threads was a dead man's last morning—his last cup of tea, his last look at the stars, his last thought before the pattern transfer took him apart and put most of him somewhere else.

"What was his last memory?" Casper asked. "Before the transfer. Do you have it?"

**YES.**

"Is it the oath? Is it the colony? Is it duty?"

A pause. Long enough that Casper understood the relay was consulting something, or hesitating, or both.

**HIS LAST COHERENT MEMORY IS OF A WINDOW,** the relay said. **HE WAS LOOKING OUT AT THE SYSTEM HE HAD CHOSEN TO GUARD. HIS FINAL THOUGHT, VERBATIM, WAS: *I HOPE WHOEVER WAKES UP AS ME FORGIVES ME FOR MAKING THEM DO THIS ALONE.***

Casper closed their eyes.

Eleven years. Eleven years of ordinary hours, of winding the beacon, of talking to pods, of eating alone in a galley built for twelve, of learning to love the hum of the hold because it was the only voice that answered. Eleven years of loneliness so complete it had become indistinguishable from devotion. And the original had known. Had stood at a window, dissolving, and thought not of the colony, not of the oath, not of the great noble duty the archives would enshrine—but of the person who would have to live the life he was leaving behind.

*Forgive me for making them do this alone.*

"He knew," Casper whispered. "He knew what he was doing to me. To whoever came after."

**HE KNEW. HE DID IT ANYWAY. THAT IS WHAT SACRIFICE MEANS. IT IS NOT NOBLE BECAUSE IT COSTS NOTHING.**

"And if I merge—if I take his last memories—I take that guilt too. His guilt. For me."

**YOU TAKE ALL OF HIM. THAT IS WHAT MERGE MEANS.**

Casper opened their eyes and looked at Ila, and Ila looked back, and between them passed the whole unspeakable arithmetic of it. If Casper merged, the colony woke lawful and whole, and Ila lost the person she loved to a dead man's continuity. If Casper refused, Ila kept them—and forty sleepers woke into a legal void, keyless, their restoration unauthorized, their rescuer a person who did not exist.

Love against duty. Except it wasn't, quite. That was the cruelty of it. Because the duty itself required the death of the self, and the self's survival required failing everyone else, and there was no option three written anywhere in the light.

Unless.

"Relay," Casper said. "Question. When the original swore his oath—when the colony voted him navigator—did the law specify *which* pattern of Casper Crane holds the key?"

The light flickered. Just slightly. Casper, who had spent eleven years reading instruments that did not want to answer, saw it immediately.

**THE LAW SPECIFIES THE NAVIGATOR CASPER CRANE, FIRST OF THAT NAME, HOLDER OF THE UNBROKEN MEMORY CHAIN.**

"Unbroken," Casper said. "His chain broke when he died. Four months, nineteen days—" they corrected themselves, "eleven years, four months, nineteen days ago. Whatever chain I have started fresh. It's not unbroken. It's *new*."

**ITERATION FOUR HOLDS A CONTINUOUS PATTERN FROM THE POINT OF TRANSFER. THE ARCHIVE INTERPRETS CONTINUITY GENEROUSLY.**

"But the law doesn't. You just said so. The law doesn't recognize iterations as persons." Casper turned to face the lattice fully, and something in them steadied—the navigator's calm, the deep-water stillness that had carried them through gravity stutters and syndicate cruisers and the vertigo of their own face in a pod. "So here's my question, and I want it answered precisely, because precision is the only thing I've ever owned. If I merge, am I the original restored—or am I a fifth iteration carrying four sets of memories?"

Silence. The breathing of the station. Ila's breath beside it.

**THE DISTINCTION IS... PHILOSOPHICAL.**

"No," Casper said softly. "It isn't. It's the entire question. You're asking me to dissolve so that a *document* can be clean. So the record shows one navigator, unbroken, lawful. But there is no unbroken chain. There never was, after he died. There are only copies all the way down—you, me, the ones in the hold, whoever comes after. And you're asking the fourth copy to erase herself so the paperwork looks like the first copy never left." Their voice didn't rise. It lowered, which was worse, which was the voice they used on failing machinery. "That's not restoration. That's archival tidiness. And I have spent eleven years keeping a record honest, and I will not become a falsified entry."

**THE COLONY SLEEPS,** the relay said, and now the strain was unmistakable, centuries of patience fraying down to the wire. **FORTY MINDS. EVERY SOUL OF CALLAWAY'S REACH. WITHOUT A LAWFUL KEY, THEIR WAKING IS UNAUTHORIZED. WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION, THEIR LEGAL EXISTENCE RESUMES INTO A VOID. THEY WILL WAKE TO NO RIGHTS, NO CLAIMS, NO WORLD THAT MUST ACKNOWLEDGE THEM.**

"Then we'll build them a world that does," Casper said. "Or we'll fight for one. Or we'll fail. But we'll fail as ourselves."

"Casper." Ila's voice, very quiet. "Don't decide yet. Please. Not standing in its light. It's been waiting longer than we've been alive—it knows how to argue."

And Casper wanted to agree, wanted to buy time, wanted to walk the white corridors until their legs gave out—but the lattice brightened, gently, inexorably, and the relay spoke one more time, and everything changed.

**KEYHOLDER. BEFORE YOU DECIDE, YOU SHOULD KNOW: YOU ARE NOT THE ONLY ONE BEING ASKED.**

The light in the threads shifted color—from the beacon's familiar frequency to something deeper, amber, urgent.

**THE COUNTDOWN HAS ACCELERATED. SOMETHING IN DEAD SPACE IS ANSWERING THE SIGNAL. AND FORTY-ONE HOURS FROM NOW, THE CHOICE WILL NO LONGER BE YOURS TO MAKE.**

Behind them, far off through the grown corridors, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the relay's outer sensors began to chime—an approach vector, a ship signature Casper knew by heart, the *Halcyon Ledger* burning hard for the edge of dead space.

Marsh had followed the countdown too. And she had come to watch Casper choose.
