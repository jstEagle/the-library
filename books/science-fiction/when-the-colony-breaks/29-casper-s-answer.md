## 29. Casper's Answer

The relay's chamber had no chairs.

Casper noticed this the way they had learned to notice everything aboard the station—slowly, and with a suspicion that nothing here was accidental. The walls curved in on themselves like the inside of a throat, grown rather than built, ribbed with pale cartilage that glowed faintly at the same frequency as the beacon they had carried across dead space like a lantern through a storm. The light pulsed once every nine seconds. Casper had stopped counting how many times they'd counted.

They stood alone in the center of the chamber. The relay had asked for that much: *stand where the archive can see you.* Ila was somewhere behind them, beyond the membrane-door that sealed like a healing wound when it closed. Sef was awake now, three days awake, moving through the Vesperine's corridors on legs that remembered gravity differently than her body did. And forty pods still slept in the hold, forty hearts keeping their long slow time, waiting for an answer to a question Casper hadn't known they were carrying until this room asked it aloud.

*The original Casper Crane died eleven years ago,* the archive had said, in a voice like water finding its level. *You are the fourth iteration. The first act of the original, upon waking as navigator-guardian, was to commission the beacon and then to begin the sequence of backups that would outlast him. You are not his ghost. You are his plan.*

*There is one thing left to plan,* it had said. *Merge with his final memories. Become whole. Become lawful. Become the key the colony intended.*

And now the room waited, glowing its patient nine-second pulse, and Casper stood in the middle of it with their hands empty and their heart doing something the archive could not parse: beating too fast for someone who was about to say no.

They thought about the word *lawful*. It sat strangely in the chest, like a coin from a country that no longer existed. Lawful to whom? The colony had voted itself into sleep to escape a purge whose architects were, according to Marsh, still listening. The law that made Casper a key was written by people who had chosen never to wake up and see what their law cost. Eleven years Casper had wound that beacon without knowing why, and every turn of the mechanism had been a kind of faith—a faith that whatever they were tending deserved the tending. They would not trade that faith now for a certificate of authenticity stamped by a dead man's good intentions.

But it wasn't only defiance. That was what surprised them, standing there. It wasn't the hot refusal they might have flung at Ondine Marsh, or the bitter refusal they'd aimed at the mirror in the weeks after learning what they were. This was quieter. This felt almost like tenderness.

Because Casper had spent eleven years remembering wrong—or so the archive implied—and the remembering had built something the original never got to have.

* * *

They had asked the archive, before answering, for one thing. Not the original's final memories—the merge offer hung over them like a held breath—but the shape of him. A description. An inventory of the man whose face they wore.

The relay obliged in its bloodless way. It projected the original into the chamber as a lattice of light, standing where Casper stood, and for nine seconds Casper looked at themselves from the outside and felt nothing so simple as recognition.

He was taller in the way memory makes people taller. He held his hands differently—loose at his sides, a spacer's habit, always ready to catch himself against a wall that might stop being a wall. His face was younger than Casper's felt. Thirty-four, maybe thirty-five, though he had lived thirty-nine years by the calendar. He had a scar Casper didn't have, a thin white line along the jaw, and Casper found themselves grieving it absurdly, the way you grieve a stranger's photograph.

*State your name,* the archive prompted the lattice, running some ceremonial protocol, and the light-Casper said, in a voice that was Casper's voice worn smoother, "Casper Crane. Navigator, Vesperine. Guardian of Calloway's Reach."

"And your duty?"

"To remember them," the lattice said. "All of them. Until they can remember themselves."

The words went through Casper like cold water through cloth. Because that was the duty, laid bare—not to be the colony's key, not really. Keys open things and are forgotten in doors. The duty was to be the one who stayed behind in the burning house of time and kept saying the names. The beacon, the winding, the eleven years of ordinary hours—all of it had been a mnemonic device with a human being attached.

And here was the thing Casper understood then, standing in front of their own unfinished portrait:

The original had designed the backups to remember *him*. To carry his memories forward intact, uncorrupted, lawful, so that when the colony woke there would be one continuous thread from the vote to the morning after. One man, serialized against death. It was elegant. It was devoted. It was also, Casper thought, a little arrogant—the assumption that his way of remembering was the only true one, that any deviation was corruption, that a copy who loved different people and feared different things and wound the beacon with different hands was a *failure state* rather than a continuation.

The archive had told them, in its flat voice, that Casper's memories diverged from the master record by a measurable percentage. Drift, it called it. Error accumulated across four iterations.

Casper had another word for it.

* * *

When the membrane-door opened, Ila came through the way she came through everything—sideways, cataloguing, her eyes doing that quick archival sweep of the chamber before they settled on Casper and softened in spite of herself. She had been crying, or close to it; the skin under her eyes had the shine of held-back weather. She had spent the last hours in the relay's outer galleries reading the full history of Calloway's Reach, and she carried it now the way she carried everything, folded small against the sternum.

"They're waiting," she said. "Marsh's people are holding at the docking ring. Sef is asking for you. And the archive—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully. "The archive wants your answer."

"I know."

"You don't have to give it alone."

"That's just it." Casper turned to face her fully, and saw her do the thing she always did when she was frightened: square her shoulders like a woman bracing a door. "Ila, if I take the merge, I won't be the person who chose you. I'll be the person who planned to choose you. There's a difference, and it's the width of eleven years."

Ila's breath caught. She had heard versions of this argument from Casper for days—for weeks, since the relay confirmed the truth—but this was the first time Casper had said it plainly, without wrapping it in navigation jargon or jokes about paperwork. She crossed the chamber slowly, her boots silent on the grown floor, and stopped close enough that Casper could see the pulse in her throat.

"Tell me what you're going to do," she said. "Not because I get a vote. Because I want to hear you say it while I'm looking at you."

So Casper said it.

* * *

"I refuse the merge."

The archive received the words without reaction, which was somehow worse than argument. The light pulsed. Nine seconds. Nine seconds.

"Clarify," it said.

"I decline integration with the original's terminal memory state. I decline reclassification as the lawful key of Calloway's Reach. I remain—" and here Casper paused, because the next part mattered, mattered more than anything they'd ever said into a machine, "—I remain what I have become."

"The original's design intent specifies continuity of self as the highest priority. Your divergence constitutes—"

"Say *drift*," Casper said. "You already did. Say error. Say corruption. I've read the file."

"—constitutes a deviation from the guardian standard," the archive finished, with the faintest reluctance, like a librarian forced to use slang.

"Then let me tell you about my deviation." Casper's voice was steady. They were surprised by that. They had expected to shake. "Your original remembered a colony. Forty thousand names in suspension, a war, a vote, a promise. He remembered it perfectly, because he was built to, and he died doing it, and I honor that—I do. But I remember other things too. I remember the sound the hold makes at third shift, when the pods cycle and the whole ship hums like it's breathing. I remember that the number seven access panel sticks and you have to lift-then-push, and I know that because I fixed it four hundred times, not because anyone told me. I remember a woman named Ila Voss falling asleep in the archives with her glasses crooked, and I remember deciding—deciding, not being programmed—that I would rather lose every memory I have than let her walk off this ship believing she imagined me."

Silence. Even the pulse seemed to lean in.

"The original's duty was to remember," Casper said. "I remember differently. I remember *forward* instead of back. And that is enough. That has to be enough, because it's what exists, and existence doesn't ask permission to diverge."

"The colony will require a key to wake," the archive said. "Without the merged guardian, authorization protocols—"

"Will fail," Casper agreed. "I know. I've read those files too. Which means we do it another way. My way. The way nobody authorized."

For the first time, the relay's tone changed—not in substance but in texture, a fractional softening, like a machine discovering hesitation. "The original anticipated refusal," it said. "Iteration one refused. Iteration two refused. Iteration three deferred the question and returned to the ship." A pause of exactly nine seconds. "You are the first to refuse and remain coherent afterward."

"What happened to the others?"

"They became the backups you carry," the archive said. "Refusal, in the original's design, was always the first step toward sleep. He believed no copy should bear the whole weight while awake. He believed the waking one would eventually choose wholeness or rest." Another pause. "He did not design for a copy who would choose neither."

"Then he didn't imagine me well enough," Casper said, and found, to their own astonishment, that they were smiling. "Which proves my point better than anything else I could say."

* * *

Afterward—they walked.

Not anywhere in particular. The relay's galleries branched like coral, and Casper took the branch that sloped upward toward a viewport the station grew for reasons no one had ever recorded, and Ila followed, and for a long time neither of them spoke. Below them, through layers of translucent tissue-wall, the Vesperine hung at her moorings, old and patched and stubborn, her hull catching the relay's pulse-light so that she seemed to breathe in time with the station. Somewhere in her belly, forty pods. Somewhere in her belly, a second Casper sleeping off the confusion of waking, dreaming—who knew?—dreams that belonged to iteration three.

"He would have taken the merge," Ila said at last. "The original. Wouldn't he? If he'd lived to stand in that room."

"Yes," Casper said. "In a heartbeat. He'd have considered it his duty. He volunteered to be first backup, Ila. He volunteered to die serially so the colony wouldn't have to. A man like that doesn't refuse the last piece of himself."

"And you're not sorry? That you're not him?"

Casper leaned against the viewport's cool lip. Out past the station, dead space went on forever, black and absolute, and somewhere in it the thing that had answered the countdown was still coming, vast and patient, drawn by the signal they had not yet managed to silence. Fear lived in Casper's stomach like swallowed stone. It had lived there since the countdown started. It would live there until either they masked the signal or the dark arrived to collect them.

"No," they said. "I'm not sorry. Listen—I spent eleven years thinking I was a lighthouse keeper. Keeping a light for ships I'd never see, for reasons nobody could tell me. And when I found out the truth, I thought: fine, I'm not a keeper, I'm a lock, I'm cargo, I'm a copy, I'm a legal fiction wearing a dead man's face. Every answer I got made me smaller." They turned to look at her. "But the merge was just one more smaller. It would have made me *his*, complete and finished and correct, and there's nothing smaller than correct. What I am instead is unfinished. I'm the fourth draft. And drafts are allowed to change the ending."

Ila was quiet for a moment. Then she laughed—a short, wet, disbelieving sound—and pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes.

"Do you know what I wrote in my field notes, the night I docked with the Vesperine?" she said. "Before I met you. Before any of it. I wrote: *Subject vessel appears derelict; beacon anomalous; recommend observation.* Observation. Like you were a weather pattern." She lowered her hand. Her eyes were bright and fierce. "I have spent my whole life believing that records are the only honest form of love. That if you write it down precisely enough, it stays. And then I met a man who was nothing but a record—a perfect, lawful, archived continuity—and he was gone, Casper. He was eleven years gone. And the person in front of me, the drifted, corrupted, unauthorized version—" her voice cracked and she let it, "—the person in front of me is the one who stayed."

"So you're saying I'm a clerical error worth keeping."

"I'm saying," Ila said, taking their hand, lacing her fingers through theirs with the deliberateness of someone entering data into a permanent ledger, "that some things are kept not by locks but by people who stay. And you stayed. Four times. In four bodies. Whatever else you are, you are the stayingest person I have ever met."

Casper laughed despite everything—the countdown, the dark, the stone in their stomach—and pulled her close, and for nine seconds, or ninety, or some uncounted stretch that belonged to no archive, they simply stood together at the window while the dead space stared back.

* * *

It was Sef who broke the moment, her voice arriving thin and urgent over the relay's internal channel, routed through systems older than the colony itself:

"Casper. Come down to the ship. Now." A pause, filled with the sound of her breathing hard. "The pods. Three of them are cycling early. And the beacon—" 

Static. Then, worse than static, clarity:

"The beacon just changed its frequency on its own. It's not asking the relay for permission anymore. It's asking *you*."

Casper looked at Ila. Ila looked at Casper. And in the space of a single pulse of light, the fourth copy of Casper Crane understood that the choice they had just made was not an ending at all—it was the first turn of a new mechanism, and it was time to go wind it.
