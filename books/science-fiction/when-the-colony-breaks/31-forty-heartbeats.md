## 31. Forty Heartbeats

The first sound was not a voice. It was a breath.

Ila heard it through the hold's open hatch—a single, wet, human inhalation that cut through the Vesperine's ambient hum like a knife through silk—and every hair on her arms rose at once. She had spent the last hour standing in the corridor outside the backup bay with her palm flat against the bulkhead, feeling the ship shudder as Casper rewrote the beacon's final function somewhere above her, and she had told herself she was ready for whatever came next.

She had not been ready for a breath.

Forty pods lined the hold in two long rows, their surfaces fogged with eleven years of condensation and dust, and now—pod by pod, in no order she could discern—the fog began to clear from the inside. Not all at once. That was the terrible part, or the beautiful part; she couldn't decide which. The pods woke the way people wake: reluctantly, unevenly, some snapping to alertness while others lingered at the threshold, murmuring names of streets and children and dead men into the dark of their own skulls.

Ila stepped inside and the smell hit her first—sterile gel and old air and, beneath it, something organic and unmistakably alive. Forty bodies beginning to remember they were bodies. The environmental system groaned, recalibrating for a load it hadn't carried since before Casper Crane had ever wound a beacon, and the temperature climbed by half a degree, then another, and Ila felt those degrees like a tide coming in around her ankles.

*You're awake,* she wanted to say. *You're safe.* The words the beacon had been holding for eleven years, the promise Casper had finally spoken aloud when they'd rewritten its purpose together. But her throat closed around them, because the first pod to fully open was third from the aisle, and the man inside sat up too fast, gasping, and his eyes found hers with the wild, uncomprehending terror of someone who has just learned that time is real.

"Where—" he started, and coughed, and coughed again, gel flecking his lips. "Where is the war? Did we—did we miss it?"

"You missed it," Ila said, and knelt beside him because her legs simply decided to stop holding her up. "Both of them, I think. You're on a survey ship called the Vesperine. You've been asleep for eleven years."

He stared at her. He was perhaps fifty, gray threading his temples, a faded tattoo of a lighthouse on his forearm—Calloway's Reach's old harbor beacon, she realized, the one from the archive images, the one the colony had named itself after. His hands shook as he pressed them flat against the pod's rim.

"Eleven years," he repeated. Then, quieter, in the voice of a man doing arithmetic he already dreaded: "My daughter would be twenty-three."

Ila had no answer for that. She reached out and took his hand instead, and he gripped hers hard enough to hurt, and she let it hurt, because there was no other honest thing to offer him.

Behind her, another pod hissed open. Then another. The hold filled with the small sounds of returning life—coughing, weeping, a woman laughing in a way that was mostly sobbing, someone asking over and over *is it over, is it over, is it over*—and Ila moved among them with water and blankets from the emergency lockers, saying the same things again and again until the words wore smooth: *the Vesperine, eleven years, you're safe, one at a time.*

She counted them without meaning to. Archivist's habit. Thirty-one awake by the time the hold's lights cycled to full brightness. Thirty-four. She kept a running tally in her head the way she'd once kept catalog numbers, each waking person a record recovered from a fire, and she understood suddenly, viscerally, why Casper had spent eleven years winding a machine nobody remembered commissioning. This was what devotion looked like when it had nothing left to hold but a number.

Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine.

The fortieth pod stood at the end of the row, apart from the others, its casing marked with a small stenciled numeral she had noticed days ago and not understood: **III**.

It opened last. Of course it did. Even the machines, it seemed, understood hierarchy.

* * *

Ila was helping an elderly woman sit upright when she heard the pod's seal break behind her—a soft pneumatic sigh, almost gentle—and she turned, and the world went narrow and ringing, the way it does in the moment before a fall.

He sat up slowly, more controlled than the others, as if waking were a procedure he had rehearsed. He was Casper's height. Casper's build, though leaner, harder, the body of someone who had not spent eleven years alone with maintenance routines and rationed meals. Casper's face—but weathered differently, a scar bisecting his left eyebrow that Casper didn't have, a stillness around his mouth that Casper's face had never quite achieved. Casper's eyes, exactly, terribly, the gray-green of shallow sea water over slate.

He looked at the hold. At the waking colonists. At the stenciled III on his own pod's rim. And then he looked at Ila, and Ila watched recognition move across his features—not confusion, not curiosity, but *recognition*, sharp and immediate and wrong, because he should not know her, he had never seen her, he had been asleep for—

"You," he said. His voice was rougher than Casper's. Lower. "You're the archivist."

The words landed in Ila's chest like dropped stones. "How do you—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm Ila Voss. I came aboard three weeks ago. You've never met me."

"No," he agreed, and swung his legs over the pod's edge, and stood, unsteady but refusing the wall's help, and even his stubbornness was Casper's stubbornness wearing different clothes. "But Casper described you. In the messages. Before the sleep took." He looked at her the way one looks at a photograph of a place one has dreamed about—hungering, grieving, certain and uncertain at once. "Eleven years of letters to a woman who'd never read them. He wrote you anyway. Every week. Said it kept him honest."

The hold tilted. Or Ila did. She put a hand against the cold rim of the nearest pod and breathed.

*Casper described me.* Which meant the original—the first Casper, the dead Casper, the navigator who had volunteered to be backup number one—had known about her before she had ever boarded this ship. Had known her name, her work, her face. Had written to her across the gap between his life and her arrival, letters she had never received, addressed to a version of herself that existed only in his hope.

And this man—iteration three, the stencil said, third of four—had read every one.

"I don't understand," she said, and hated how small her voice came out.

"Neither do I," he said, and for a moment something flickered across his face that was so purely, painfully Casper—wry, self-deprecating, kind—that Ila's eyes burned. "Most mornings I didn't understand anything. But he talked about you like weather. Like something inevitable. 'There'll be an archivist,' he said. 'She'll come asking about the beacon, and she'll stay.' I used to think it was sentiment." He paused. "Now you're standing here, and I can't decide if he was a prophet or if I'm still dreaming."

Around them, the hold continued its slow resurrection. A young man vomited gel into a recovery basin and apologized to no one in particular. The lighthouse-tattooed man had fallen asleep again sitting up, exhaustion claiming him mid-grief, and Ila's heart clenched at the mercy of it—that the mind could grant even the newly returned a few hours' leave. Somewhere near the far wall, two women who had clearly known each other before the sleep held each other and rocked, wordless, an entire conversation conducted in pressure and breath.

But Ila could only look at the man from pod three.

"What's your name?" she asked. It seemed important. It seemed like the only question left.

He considered it longer than the question deserved, and she realized he wasn't deciding what to tell her—he was deciding what was true.

"Casper," he said finally. "They called us all Casper, after the first. Four backups, four navigators, four keepers of the same promise. I was third. I went under six years after him." His jaw tightened. "Six years awake in this hold, keeping the watch while the original slept in the captain's quarters and the beacon sang. Then my turn came, and I lay down in this box, and the last thing I remember is him telling me—" His voice caught. He mastered it. "Him telling me that whoever woke next should find the archivist. That she'd know what to do."

"He never told me any of this," Ila whispered.

"No," the third Casper said gently. "He never told *you*. He told *me*. There's a difference, isn't there?" And he smiled—not unkindly, but with an edge of something that might have been sorrow or might have been challenge—and Ila felt the smile land somewhere beneath her ribs and lodge there. "You loved him. The first one. On Meridian Station. He mentioned that too."

* * *

Later—Ila could not have said how much later; time in the hold had gone soft and unreliable—she found herself in the galley, boiling water on the ancient heating coil because the wakers needed something warm and the ship offered little else. The corridor outside echoed with footsteps, voices, the tentative relearning of bodies: someone testing their legs against gravity that had flickered twice in recent memory, someone else laughing at the taste of recycled water as if it were wine.

Casper appeared in the doorway. Her Casper. The fourth. They had barely spoken since the beacon's rewrite—since Casper had stood at the console with shaking hands and chosen, deliberately, to become a key instead of a lock—and there was a lightness in their frame now that frightened Ila more than any dread had, the lightness of a person who has set down something heavy and doesn't yet know what will fill the space.

"They're drinking the water," Casper said, watching the galley's small traffic of ghosts. "All forty. Sef says the filtration tastes like Meridian Station's. She cried about it. Cried about *water*."

"People cry about strange mercies," Ila said.

"Mm." Casper leaned against the bulkhead. Their eyes moved over her face with the particular attention they'd developed in recent weeks—the attention of someone reading a document for signs of tampering, or perhaps for signs of truth. "You've met him."

Ila's hands went still on the kettle. "Yes."

"And?"

"And he's you," she said. "Or close enough that I keep forgetting which room I left you in."

Something crossed Casper's face—hurt, quickly folded away, folded with a skill that eleven years of solitude teaches. "That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." She set the kettle down and turned to face them fully. "Casper. He knew about me. Before I ever came here. The original wrote to me—for years—and this man read every letter, and when he woke up, he looked at me like—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Like I was a letter he'd been waiting to deliver."

Casper was quiet for a long moment. Beyond the galley's viewport, dead space stretched away in every direction, black and absolute, and the relay station hung in it like a held breath.

"The original knew about you," Casper said at last, slowly, working it through. "Before Meridian. Before your archive query tripped the deletion protocols. Which means—" Their breath caught audibly. "Which means I was sent for you. All of this. The beacon answering, the coordinates, the timing. Someone designed it so that you would find this ship. So that *we* would find each other."

"Does that change it?" Ila asked. "What we—" She gestured vaguely between them, at everything unspoken that had accumulated in the dark of the debris shoal and the long approach and the confession at the airlock. "Does it matter how it was arranged?"

Casper looked at her for a long time. "I don't know yet," they admitted. "Ask me tomorrow."

From the hold, a bell tone sounded—the ship's old muster chime, unused for a decade—and Sef's voice carried down the corridor, hoarse but carrying: "Everyone to the bay. All forty. There's something you need to see, and someone you need to meet."

* * *

The colonists gathered in the hold in various states of repair—wrapped in emergency blankets, leaning on each other, thirty-nine faces turned toward the fortieth pod and the man still standing beside it. Ila stood at the edge of the crowd with Casper, and the geometry of the moment was not lost on her: two Caspers in one room, separated by fifteen meters and six years of suspended time, and forty strangers beginning to sense that something in the room's arithmetic did not balance.

Sef stood between them. She looked steadier than she had during her brief waking in the transit—her color better, her voice anchored—and she addressed the colonists with the authority of someone who had, apparently, appointed herself their herald.

"You slept through a purge," she said. "Calloway's Reach voted to hide rather than burn, and one navigator stayed behind to keep the promise that you'd be woken safe. That navigator was Casper Crane." She gestured to the man by pod three—or began to gesture—and then her hand hesitated, drifted, and settled instead toward the fourth Casper at the hold's edge. "And this is also Casper Crane. The fourth copy. The one who broke the lock."

Forty faces turned. Ila felt the weight of their attention pass over her companion like a physical thing—curiosity, gratitude, suspicion, awe—and she watched Casper receive it the way they received everything: quietly, shoulders braced, as if applause and accusation weighed the same.

The third Casper stepped forward. "The one who broke the lock," he repeated. His voice was pleasant. It was not warm. "Interesting phrase. Because from where I stand, the lock was the only thing keeping forty people alive, and the man who built it died believing it would hold." He looked at the fourth Casper across the gulf of themselves. "So before anyone thanks anyone—I'd like to hear, from my own face, why the fourth copy decided eleven years of protocol was his to overturn."

The hold went very quiet. Even the newly woken, even the grieving, felt the shape of what was coming.

Casper lifted their chin. "Because the protocol was killing them," they said. "And because I'm not him."

"No," the third Casper agreed softly. "You're certainly not."

And Ila, standing between them with her back against the cold hull of a pod that had held a stranger with her beloved's face, understood that the hardest part of the waking had only just begun.
