## 1. The Manifest That Never Was

The *Long Patience* was eleven days out from Ceres Gate with nothing but ballast water, spare turbine housings, and the particular silence of a ship too big for its crew. Sebastian Montrose had the night shift, which on a hauler with a crew of four meant she had the whole dark side of the ship to herself: the observation blister, the cold coffee, the hum of the drive at a frequency her body had learned to mistake for calm.

She was three hours into a decoding job she'd taken for money and was continuing for spite.

The file had come bundled in a data-salvage lot out of the Belt — corrupted shipping records, mostly junk, the kind of thing salvage brokers sold by the kilo to linguists and hobbyists who liked puzzles. Sebastian liked puzzles. It was either that or sleep, and sleep had been unreliable lately. The dreams were back.

She had them tagged in her own private taxonomy: *water dreams*, *door dreams*, and the worst kind, the ones she privately called *language dreams* — dreams that arrived not in images but in grammar. Whole sentences in a tongue she didn't know, spoken by voices she almost recognized, structured with a logic that felt less like a human language and more like something built to be *understood by machines*. She'd recorded fragments of them for twenty years, transcribed what she could, and never once found a match in any corpus on any world. A xenolinguist haunted by a language that didn't exist. Her dissertation committee had found it charming. Her therapist had found it concerning.

The manifest file was a different kind of ghost.

It was fragmentary — forty percent intact at best, checksums shattered, headers rewritten so many times the original formatting had scarred over like healed burns. But the underlying structure was standard Meridian Terraforming shipping protocol, third generation, and Meridian hadn't changed its manifest schema in sixty years because Meridian didn't believe in changing things that worked. Sebastian had spent two years of her early career auditing Meridian freight contracts. She knew their bureaucratic skeleton the way she knew her own hands.

By hour two she had the schema reconstructed. By hour three, she had a vessel name.

**HALCYON REACH** — colony-class transport, hull registry MT-4471, departure port Meridian Hollow Station, destination field: *unlisted*.

She sat back and looked at it for a long moment.

There was no Halcyon Reach. She was almost sure of that, the way you're sure of the color of the sky — not because you check it every morning, but because it has simply always been so. Meridian Terraforming had launched eleven colony ships in the Expansion decades. She could name them the way other people named the planets of a childhood solar system: *Prosperine, Amity, Long Meadow, Second Harvest* — the whole litany, drilled into every schoolchild in Meridian space, the founding myth of the corporate commonwealth. Eleven ships. Eleven new worlds. The Halcyon Reach was not among them. It had never been among them.

Which meant either the file was a forgery — someone's elaborate fiction, a hoax planted in a salvage lot — or it was real, and everything else was the forgery.

She pulled the registry against the open Meridian database through the ship's anemic relay. Hull MT-4471: *not assigned*. Meridian Hollow Station: *decommissioned, records sealed, no public manifest history*. The destination field, she noticed, hadn't been corrupted. It had been *edited* — overwritten with the digital equivalent of a shrug, a placeholder string that read, in Meridian's bland corporate dialect: `VALUE WITHDRAWN FROM RECORD`.

Not *unknown*. *Withdrawn*. Someone had known, and then someone had decided no one else should.

Sebastian had spent her whole professional life around erasure. Dead languages were erasure with a long fuse — a tongue dies, and within three generations the people who spoke it become a different people, and the grief gets inherited without the memory attached. She had built a career on reading the shapes that vanished things left behind. She knew what a hole in the record looked like.

This was a hole with edges. This was a hole that had been *cut*.

She should have closed the file. She told herself that later, in the version of the night she retold to herself on the long dark stretches of the route: *I should have closed it, billed the salvage broker for the decode, and gone to sleep.* But her hands were already pulling the passenger annex, because manifests of colony-class transports carry passenger annexes, and the annex was the least corrupted section of all — as if whoever had done the scrubbing had cared about hull numbers and destinations and had not thought, or had not been able to bring themselves, to touch the names.

The names came up in a clean column. Four hundred and six of them. Families, mostly — the annex grouped by household, the way Meridian did, the way her mother had always said was barbaric, *as if you emigrate as a filing unit and not as a person*. Sebastian had heard that a hundred times at their kitchen table on Callisto Verde, her mother waving a teacup, her mother who had grown up somewhere she never named, in a household that spoke a language she claimed not to remember.

Sebastian scrolled. She wasn't looking for anything. She was a professional; she was cataloguing; she was doing her job.

She reached the two hundred and sixtieth household and stopped.

**MONTOREA, ILSE V.** — age 34 — occupation listed as *linguistic technician, neural interface systems* — status: *embarked*.

The name was spelled wrong. Or rather, it was spelled the old way, the way it had been spelled before some clerk on some world had flattened it into MONTROSE, the way her mother had spelled it on Sebastian's birth registration before softening it again, the way it appeared in exactly one place in Sebastian's life: on the inside cover of her mother's dictionary, in faded ink, in a language Sebastian had never been able to read.

*Ilse Montorea.* Her mother's name. Her mother's real name, the one she'd kept like a pressed flower, the one she'd said — laughing, deflecting — came from "somebody's grandmother, doesn't matter, sweetheart."

Age 34. Her mother would have been 34 in the year the Halcyon Reach supposedly didn't launch.

The observation blister was cold. Sebastian realized she'd been sitting without moving for a long time — long enough that her coffee had gone from cold to ceremonial, long enough that the ship's clock had rolled past the shift change and nobody had come looking for her, because on the *Long Patience* nobody came looking for anybody. That was the deal out here. You hauled your cargo and your ghosts and everyone pretended not to hear the second one rattling.

*Embarked.* The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Her mother had died twenty-six years ago in a station accident — that was the story, the whole story, the story Sebastian had been given at eleven years old by a Meridian claims officer with kind eyes and a form to sign. A pressure breach at a logistics hub whose name Sebastian had never been able to find on a map afterward. No body recovered — common enough in vacuum. A settlement payout, generous, with a clause she'd been too young to read. Her father had signed it and never talked about it again, and had died himself before Sebastian was old enough to ask the questions that were forming in her even then, the questions that had eventually pushed her into linguistics, into dead languages, into the professional study of things that stop being said.

*Embarked* meant her mother had boarded a colony ship that never flew, bound for a destination withdrawn from record, out of a station whose history was sealed.

Sebastian did what she always did when the ground moved. She worked.

She ran the annex against every public and semi-public database the relay could reach. Four hundred and six names. She got hits on eleven — all dead ends, all names belonging to other people, common names, or so the databases insisted. She ran the household groupings and found that the annex had a second column she'd missed, a narrow one, mostly stripped: a classification code. Of the four hundred and six, three hundred and ninety-one carried the standard colonist code. Fifteen carried a different one — a prefix she recognized from her contract-audit days, because it appeared in exactly one class of Meridian document.

Personnel. Not passengers. *Assets*.

Her mother was one of the fifteen. *Linguistic technician, neural interface systems.* Her mother, who had taught her the alphabet with cereal pieces on the kitchen table. Her mother, who had hummed melodies in a language she said she'd forgotten. Her mother, who had been *property*, filed under a code, on a ship that did not exist, from a station that had never happened, toward a destination that had been withdrawn from the record by someone with clean hands and a mandate.

The dreams chose that moment — or maybe it was just the hour, the dead watch, the brain's own graveyard shift — to surface. Not a full dream, just a fragment, a piece of grammar rising like silt: a word that meant, she had always been certain, *the ones who remember on our behalf*, and a second word that meant *burning*, and a name.

She had never been able to make out the name. It had always dissolved on waking.

Now she sat in the observation blister of a cargo hauler with a decoded manifest glowing on her screen, and the name was right there in the two hundred and sixtieth household, spelled the old way, and the dissolution was over. The dream hadn't been a dream. It had been a message in a bottle, twenty years crossing a dark sea, and it had finally washed up on the one shore with a reader.

Sebastian Montrose pressed her palm flat against the cold of the blister glass and watched the stars hold still, and felt the first crack run through the load-bearing wall of her life — the quiet, unexamined certainty that her mother had simply been unlucky, that grief had simply been weather, that there was nothing underneath it to find.

The manifest said otherwise. The manifest said: *there is a room, and it is locked, and your mother is inside it.*

She copied the file to three drives, encrypted two, and swallowed the third in a data-chit against her skin, the way you carry something you're not ready to admit you're protecting. Then she pulled up the *Long Patience*'s route chart, found the one scheduled waypoint she could bend without the crew noticing for six days, and laid a course correction toward a set of coordinates she'd never had a reason to look at before.

Meridian Hollow Station. Departure port. *Decommissioned, records sealed, no public manifest history.*

The drive hummed. The stars held still. Somewhere behind her, down the spine of the ship, the coffee machine clicked off, and Sebastian Montrose — who had spent her whole life translating the dead for a living — finally understood that she had been learning one language her entire career without knowing it.

Her mother's.

The course locked in. Eleven days to a station that had never existed, and a massacre that, every archive insisted, had never happened either.
