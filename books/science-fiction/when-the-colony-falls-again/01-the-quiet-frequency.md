## 1. The Quiet Frequency

The station woke Paloma the way it always did: not with an alarm, but with a sound. A low harmonic thrum from the coolant loop, pitched just below hearing, felt more than heard through the frame of his bunk. Six years aboard Vesper-9 and he had never once needed the clock. The station had its own time, and it kept him the way a body keeps a heart — without asking, without mercy.

He lay still for the count of ten, listening. Coolant: steady. Air handlers: steady. The long slow respiration of the solar array's tracking motors as they turned to meet a sun that wouldn't crest for another forty minutes, local reckoning. All of it steady. All of it the same as yesterday, and the day before that, and the four hundred days before that which had blurred into a single gray season of sameness.

"Good morning, Vesper," he said, to no one, to everyone, to the station itself. His voice came out rough with sleep and disuse. He'd have to be careful about that. Some days he went eleven, twelve hours without speaking, and when he finally did, the sound startled him — a stranger living in his throat.

He swung out of the bunk and into the cold. The habitat ring ran at seventeen degrees at night to save power, and Paloma had stopped correcting it years ago. Cold was a kind of company. It kept you honest. It reminded you that you were a body, that bodies had needs, that needs were proof you were still here.

He dressed in the dark by touch — thermal layer, work layer, the patched knees of a man who spent half his life kneeling in crawlways. In the galley he ran water through the recycler's last good filter and drank it warm because heating it cost watts he'd rather spend on the transmitter. Breakfast was a nutrient bar with the flavor profile of damp cardboard and a cup of tea grown from the last living cutting of a plant that had, in another life, belonged to someone else.

The someone else had been named Odile Ferreira. Paloma knew this because the cutting had come to the station in a supply crate labeled with her name, back when there were still supply crates, back when there was still a colony to supply. He kept the plant alive on the sill of the comms bay, where it got six hours of grow-light a day, and he thought of it sometimes as the last colonist. It had more right to the title than he did. He'd never set foot on the world he served.

He carried his tea to the comms bay and began the ritual.

The ritual had a shape, and the shape had not changed in six years, and Paloma had come to understand that this was the point of it — not efficiency, not even maintenance, but shape. A shape you could hold onto when everything else had gone soft.

First: systems check. He ran it manually even though the station could do it itself, because the station had been wrong twice and he had never forgiven it. Power distribution, nominal. Antenna alignment, two-tenths of a degree off, corrected. Signal buffer, empty, as it had been empty every morning for six years. He looked at the empty buffer anyway. He looked at it the way a fisherman looks at still water.

Second: the relay queue. Vesper-9 existed, officially, to catch and forward the colony's transmissions — telemetry, civil traffic, personal messages, the whole chattering stream of a living world forty light-minutes out from the station's quiet corner. The colony had gone silent in the seventh month of Paloma's second year. Not gradually. Not with a distress call, not with a final broadcast, not with the dignified fade of a dying signal. It had simply stopped, between one packet and the next, mid-stream, the way a sentence stops when the speaker's mouth is covered.

Officially, per the last advisory from Core Relay Authority, the silence was a technical fault. A planetary network failure. Awaiting resolution.

Paloma had stopped believing that in the third month. He had never stopped transmitting.

Third: the morning send. He queued the day's handshake packet — station ID, timestamp, health telemetry, the dry bureaucratic heartbeat of a relay that wanted, more than anything, to be needed — and addressed it to the colony's primary receiver. The packet went out at 0614, as it did every day, riding a carrier wave across forty light-minutes of nothing, toward a receiver that had not answered in six years.

"Good morning," Paloma told the microphone, because the packet included a thirty-second voice header, and because he had long ago decided that if anyone ever did hear these, they should hear a human voice and not a machine's chime. "This is Vesper-9, day two thousand one hundred and eighty-eight. All systems nominal. Weather here is vacuum, as always. The tea is terrible. The plant is alive. If anyone's listening — I'm still here. I'm still here."

He let the recorder catch the echo of his own words in the small room. Then he sent it, and watched the signal indicator climb and fall, climb and fall, and felt the particular ache that lived in that motion — hope and habit fused so thoroughly he could no longer tell them apart.

* * *

The rest of the morning was work, and work was the other thing that kept him.

Vesper-9 was old — older than the colony it served, older than the Consortium charter that had built both, old enough that its bones creaked in a language of stress fractures and thermal expansion that Paloma had learned to read the way sailors once read weather. The station had been designed for a crew of eleven. There were eleven bunks. There was a mess hall with eleven chairs, and a rec room with a table tennis setup that hadn't been powered in a decade, and a hydroponics bay built to feed eleven mouths that now fed one man and one tea plant.

He spent the morning in the antenna trunk, chasing a harmonic vibration that had shown up in Tuesday's diagnostics. The trunk was a ribbed throat three meters across, running the spine of the station, and inside it the air was cold and dead and smelled of ozone and old insulation. He crawled it with a sensor wand and a headlamp, tapping panels, listening with his whole body. The vibration was nothing — a loose damping collar at junction four, a twenty-minute fix. But he did the whole trunk anyway, junction by junction, because the trunk was where the station's voice went out, and he liked to be able to say he had put his hands on every meter of it.

Around him, the station hummed its one note. Paloma had come to think of that note as the station breathing. He knew it was just machinery. He knew it was coolant pumps and gyros and the endless patient work of keeping a pocket of warm air alive in a place where warm air had no business existing. But knowing didn't change what it felt like at 0900 on an ordinary morning, alone in the antenna trunk with his tea going cold in a wall clip, listening to a building breathe.

His mother had been a lighthouse keeper's daughter, back on the coast of a country that no longer existed under that name. She used to tell him about her father's rituals: the lamp polished every dawn, the log written every night, the fog signal tested every week whether there was fog or not. *The sea doesn't care if you're diligent,* she'd say. *You're diligent because you care.* Paloma had thought, as a boy, that this was backwards. Now he understood it was the only order that made sense. Diligence wasn't a response to need. It was a response to meaning, and meaning was something you made with your hands when the universe declined to provide it.

He finished the trunk at 1130 and logged the fix in the maintenance record, in his own handwriting, on paper, because the station's digital logs had begun to worry him. That was a thought he kept folded up small and didn't look at directly. The logs had begun to worry him. He'd examine that later, or he wouldn't. Some days the not-looking was a survival skill.

* * *

Lunch, and then the part of the day that had no official designation, no line in any manual, no justification he could have offered a supervisor if supervisors still existed.

He called it the listening hour.

He took the secondary console — the one wired to the deep-field array, the big ear, the dish that could hear a whisper from the edge of the system — and he swept the colony's frequencies. All of them. The civil bands, the emergency bands, the old amateur allocations that hobbyist farmers had used to talk about weather and weddings, the tight-beam channels the hospitals had kept for patient records, the orphaned frequencies of a world that had once been loud.

He swept them the way you'd walk a beach after a storm, looking for anything the water had left behind.

Static. Static. Static. A ghost carrier on band 7, the old market channel — but he'd traced that months ago to a resonance artifact in his own receiver, a scar the hardware had developed from six years of listening to nothing. Static. A burst of something on the deep emergency band that made his heart slam before the decoder resolved it: a pulsar, natural, indifferent, its lighthouse sweep passing through his dish on its way to nowhere.

He sat with it. He always sat with it, the full hour, even after the sweep came back empty, because the hour was not for finding things. The hour was for witnessing. Somebody had to be pointed at that world, listening, and Paloma had appointed himself, and the appointment had no term limit and no successor and no salary, and it was, as far as he could determine, the most important job in the universe.

Because here was the thing he did not say out loud, the thing he kept in the folded-up place with the worry about the logs:

The colony had not gone silent the way networks go silent. Networks fail messily. They degrade, they stutter, they send error cascades and partial packets and last gasps of automated diagnostics. He had the recordings of a dozen network deaths from other relays in the sector, archived in the station's training files. Networks die like animals, thrashing.

The colony had gone silent like a held breath. Clean. Instant. Total. Between one packet and the next, mid-stream — and Paloma had the packet. He had the last packet, timestamped, checksummed, sitting in his private archive: a routine agricultural telemetry burst, soil moisture readings from a district called Verdance, signed off by a system administrator with a name that meant nothing and now meant everything. And then nothing. Not a corrupted packet. Not a retry. Nothing, as if the entire planet had been asked a question and had decided, all at once, in every district, on every band, to stop answering.

He had filed a report. He had filed eleven reports. The responses had come back — eventually, from addresses that changed with each reply — each one more bland than the last: *Fault confirmed. Resolution pending. No action required at this time. Thank you for your vigilance.* The last one, fourteen months ago, had thanked him for his vigilance and informed him that the colony's relay license was under administrative review, and that Vesper-9's forwarding queue should be repurposed to general sector traffic.

He had not repurposed the queue. The queue was empty and it would stay empty and it would stay pointed at the colony until someone came to Vesper-9 and made him stop, and he did not think anyone was coming, and he did not think anyone would make him.

* * *

Evening. He ate alone in the mess hall, at the chair nearest the window, because the other ten chairs were for people and he had decided he was not going to become a person who sat at the head of an empty table like a king of nothing. The window looked out on the dark and the dark looked back, and somewhere out there, forty light-minutes along an invisible line, a world turned with no one on it. Or with someone on it. Or with graves on it. He had stopped trying to make the not-knowing bearable and had instead made it structural, load-bearing, a wall he leaned against every day.

After dinner: the log.

He kept it in the comms bay, on the old recorder, the analog one with the tape spools, the one piece of equipment on the station too obsolete to be networked, too obsolete to be touched by whatever touched the digital archives. He had bought it at a junk auction before deployment, a sentimental indulgence, and it had turned out to be the most important object in his life. He pressed record, and the spools began to turn, and the red light came on, and Paloma Sterling, the last technician of Vesper-9, told the tape about the world.

"Day two thousand one hundred and eighty-eight," he said. "Things that used to be.

"Verdance had a river festival. I never saw it — I only ever heard it, secondhand, in the traffic. But for three days every year the civil bands filled up with music, actual live music, people playing instruments and singing badly, and the relay traffic would spike four hundred percent because everyone was calling everyone to say *come to the river, we're at the north bank, bring the kids.* The kids. You could hear the kids in the background of half those messages. There was one — I kept a copy — a woman named Ilsa, I think, Ilsa something, calling her sister, and her daughter was shouting in the background about a paper boat, a race, she'd made a paper boat and it was winning. You could hear her laughing. You could hear the river.

"I think about that paper boat. It's been six years and I think about it more than I think about most living people. Somewhere on that world there's a riverbank, and maybe there's still a piece of paper in the reeds, and there's nobody left to know it was ever a boat.

"Other things. The terraforming towers at the equator — you could see their heat signature from here, five bright threads on the thermal band, the planet's new spine. They went dark the same minute everything else did. The night market on band 7, the one I told you about, with the auctioneer who sang his prices. The school broadcasts, Thursday afternoons, children reading their essays out loud over the open channel because the teacher believed — this was in her header, I remember it — *the airwaves belong to everyone, especially the small ones.* The airwaves belong to everyone. I've been thinking about that a lot lately. I've been thinking about who they belong to now.

"I don't have anything new today. Sweep came back empty. Systems nominal. The plant grew a new leaf, which I'm counting as news, because I've decided it is.

"Here's what I know, tape, and I want you to hold onto it, because I think someday someone's going to ask, and I think the asking is going to be dangerous. I know what a network death sounds like. This wasn't one. I know what a quarantine looks like in the traffic — I've got those archives too, three sector quarantines on file, and every one of them announced itself, loudly, legally, with codes and committees and a paper trail a kilometer long. This had no paper trail. This had the opposite of a paper trail. Things have been *removed* — not failed, not corrupted, removed, cleanly, the way you'd remove a name from a ledger. And I have started to notice that the things being removed are the things I remember most clearly, and that the record and my memory are drifting apart, and that my memory is the only copy left of certain days.

"So I'm making copies. That's what this is. That's what it's always been. I'm the redundancy, tape. I'm the backup of last resort for a world of forty thousand people, and my storage medium is a middle-aged man with a bad knee and a tea plant, and I know how that sounds. But somebody has to be the backup. Somebody has to keep the frequency.

"Ilsa's daughter's boat was winning. I'm going to say that into every recorder I own until I die, and then it'll be on the tape, and the tape will outlast me, because tape is stubborn, tape doesn't update, tape doesn't get revised. Tape just keeps saying the same true thing until someone presses play.

"Goodnight, Vesper. Goodnight, colony. Goodnight, paper boat. I'm still here. I'm still here."

He stopped the recording. The spools wound down to silence.

And in the silence, in the hum of the breathing station, in the cold and the dark and the enormous patience of the empty queue — the console chirped.

Paloma froze.

The chirp came again. Not the handshake timer. Not the maintenance scheduler. The deep-field array. The big ear. Something had entered its field of view, out past the colony's orbit, out in the black where nothing should be, and it was broadcasting.

He sat down slowly, his heart going like a child's fist on a door, and pulled up the signal, and saw the registry code, and felt the floor of the world tilt beneath him.

The code was seven years retired. The code belonged to a ship that had burned with everything else.

The ship *Meridian* was coming home.
