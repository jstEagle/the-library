## 1. The Garden for the Unborn

The moon was called Vesperine, and it smelled like rain that had never fallen anywhere Yara Nystrom had ever lived.

He noticed it most at the end of his shift, when he stepped out of the cargo hauler's cab and stood in the long violet dusk with his boots still ringing from the deck plates. The atmosphere processors had been running for sixty years, and somewhere in their vast chemical patience they had learned to make an evening. Not a real evening — nothing on Vesperine was real in the way Old Earth evenings had been real, if the archive-films were to be believed — but a close cousin of one, a damp and mineral-scented hush that settled over the crater rim like a hand laid gently over a sleeping mouth.

Yara unloaded the last crate by hand, though the exo-frame would have taken it in half the time. He liked the weight. Twenty years of hauling cargo taught you that weight was honest; it told you exactly what it was and asked exactly what it needed. Everything else on this moon lied a little, or hoped, or waited.

The crate held seed-stock: barley from the Meridian vaults, cold-germinated, each grain tagged with a lineage code older than the colony itself. He carried it through the airlock of the Seed Garden and set it down among the other crates in the staging bay, where the smell changed — wet loam and chlorophyll and the faint sweet rot of compost turning itself into tomorrow. His shoulders dropped an inch. They always did. The garden was the only place on Vesperine where his body relaxed without being asked.

"Evening," he said, to no one.

That was not entirely true. It was evening to forty thousand sleepers.

Above the garden, stacked into the cliff face in silent terraces, hung the cryo-bays — the Frozen Half, the fleet called them, though nobody used the word *half* out loud anymore, not since the truce. Forty thousand colonists in frosted glass caskets, dreaming whatever cryo-dreams were made of, waiting for a moon to become worth waking up for. The garden existed for them. Every row Yara planted was addressed to someone he had never met. Every harvest went into the vaults against the day they opened their eyes and needed bread.

He had been twenty-two when the fleet assigned him here, a cargo-hand with strong arms and no prospects, and he had resented it the way young men resent anything that smells of being forgotten. Twenty years later he could not remember what else he had wanted to be. The garden had gotten into him the way soil gets under fingernails — quietly, permanently, past all washing out.

He washed his hands anyway, at the long steel basin by the potting benches, because his mother had raised him to wash his hands before touching living things. The water was recycled eleven times over and tasted faintly of the sea they'd engineered in the southern hemisphere, but it ran warm, and he let it run over his scarred knuckles longer than cleanliness required.

Then he went to see her tree.

* * *

It grew in the third conservatory, in the corner farthest from the door, where the light came down filtered and slow through the polarized canopy. A rowan. On Earth it would have been an ordinary thing, a hedgerow tree, a berry-bearer for birds. Here it was the only one of its kind, grown from cuttings his mother had carried across nine light-years in a gel-pack strapped against her ribs, and it stood about as tall as Yara himself, slender and pale-barked, holding its leaves up like small green questions.

His mother's name had been Signe. She had died eleven years ago, in the second winter, when the fungal blight came through the hydroponics levels and the medics ran out of the right antifungals before they ran out of patients. She had spent her last lucid week dictating recipes to the household archive — flatbread, nettle soup, a fish stew she'd invented herself because Vesperine's seas gave up their protein reluctantly — and her last coherent hour asking Yara to check on her cuttings.

He checked on them every day. Eleven years. The cuttings had become a sapling, the sapling had become this tree, and the checking had become a conversation he was no longer willing to admit was one-sided.

"You're leaning again," he told it, setting a stake and tying off the new leader with soft cloth strips. "I know. Everyone leans toward the light. It's not a character flaw."

The leaves moved in the ventilation draft. He chose to take that as agreement.

He worked as he talked, the way he always did — pruning the deadwood, turning the soil, testing moisture with two fingers pushed deep into the dark. His hands knew this work better than they knew anything, better than they knew his own face in the mirror. There was a callus map on his palms that read like a history of the garden: the burn scar from the nutrient-line rupture in year six, the split-thumb scar from the pruner slip in year twelve, the smooth places where the skin had simply worn itself into something useful.

"My brother came by the depot today," he said. "Stood there in the loading bay with his faction pin on his collar like I wouldn't notice. Didn't say much. Dov never says much anymore. Just looked at the crates going up to the bays and said, 'You know what those are costing us.'"

He tied off another branch. The rowan said nothing, which was its way.

"'Costing us,'" Yara repeated. "Like they're a debt. Like they're not people." He wiped his hands on his thighs. "Mom, he's got four hundred people ready to march on the spire, and the fleet's counting days, and I'm in here talking to a tree."

But that wasn't fair, and he knew it, and the tree knew it, or he pretended it did. Because he talked to the tree the way he talked to the bays — and he did talk to the bays, had done for twenty years, standing at the garden's upper gate on clear nights when the frost-glass terraces caught the light of the gas giant and glowed like a city of lanterns sunk in ice.

* * *

He climbed to the upper gate after closing, as he did most nights. It was a habit the other caretakers had stopped teasing him about years ago, the way you stop teasing a man about praying.

From the gate terrace, the cryo-bays rose in their thousands — rows upon rows of pods set into the cliff like cells of honeycomb, each one lit from within by the pale amber of its status ring. Amber meant stable. Amber meant alive, more or less, in the way the fleet had agreed to define it. Forty thousand small warm lights, and between them and Yara only a kilometer of rock and a treaty that everyone knew was dying.

"Ninety days," he said aloud. Everyone said it now. Ninety days until the truce expired, ninety days until the power arithmetic became unavoidable — enough reserve to revive the Waking Half or the Frozen Half, and not both, not both, never both. The fleet's own auditors had said so in a report that leaked in the spring, and since then Vesperine had been a moon holding its breath.

He pressed his palm against the gate's cold rail.

"I planted the barley today," he told them. Forty thousand sleepers, and not one of them heard him, and he told them anyway. "Meridian stock. Good yield, if the fungus stays polite. And your garden's coming along. The rowan's taller than me now. Mom would've liked that — she always said she wanted a tree her sons could stand under and feel short."

He smiled, and the smile hurt, the way old smiles do.

Somewhere up there was a woman who would have been his neighbor. Somewhere up there was a girl who might have married Dov instead of hating him. Somewhere up there were children who would wake into a world already built, already planted, already tended by strangers' hands — his hands — and never know his name unless someone thought to carve it somewhere.

That was the job. That was the whole job. You planted for people who could not thank you, in soil you would not live to see exhausted, and you spoke to the dark because the alternative was silence, and silence in a garden is how things die.

"Sleep well," he said, the way he said it every night, and turned back down the path.

* * *

He heard the footsteps before he saw anyone — small footsteps, quick ones, on the gravel path below the terrace. Nobody came to the garden at night. The gates closed at nineteen hundred, the caretakers went home to the habitat rings, and the only visitors after dark were the wind and the irrigation drones.

A child stood at the lower gate.

She was perhaps eight years old, in a school coat too big for her, breath fogging in the cooling air. Yara knew her distantly — Mirei Osei's class, one of the born-colonist children, a face from the harvest festivals. She should have been home hours ago. She should have been afraid, alone in the dark at the edge of the wild-planted acres.

She was not afraid. She was looking straight at him with the terrible directness of a child who has come a very long way for a reason she cannot explain.

"You shouldn't be out here," Yara said, kneeling at the gate. "It's late. Where's your guardian?"

The girl didn't answer. Instead she tilted her head, the way a singer does before finding the first note, and when she spoke, her voice was not quite her own — older, softer, shaped around words with a lilt in them that Yara had not heard spoken aloud in eleven years.

"*Kveldssol kvæði, blundar björt,*" the child sang, low and clear, in a language dead everywhere but inside him. "*Sofðu nú, sonur minn.*"

Evening sun sings, bright eyes drowse. Sleep now, my son.

Yara's mother's voice. Word for word. Note for note. The lullaby she had sung only to him, in the cramped quarters of the transport, the one he had never written down, never recorded, never spoken of to anyone — not to Dov, not to the archive, not to the tree.

The song ended. The girl blinked, and her own voice returned, small and puzzled and frightened:

"Mister? Why do I know that song?"

Above them both, forty thousand amber lights burned on in the dark, and Yara Nystrom stood very still in his garden, feeling the ground tilt beneath everything he had believed was solid.
