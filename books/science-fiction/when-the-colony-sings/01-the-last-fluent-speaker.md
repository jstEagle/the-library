## 1. The Last Fluent Speaker

The Vessel's name was Ossuary-of-Patient-Water, though by the end it answered to nothing at all.

Rafael Brightwater had learned that about them early — that a Vessel's name was not a label but a history, a compressed biography sung in four tones and a silence, and that when a Vessel grew old enough, the name became too heavy to carry. They set it down the way an old woman sets down a basket she has carried since girlhood. They did not become nameless. They became *pre-name*, waiting to be renamed by whatever came after them, which for most of Meridian's Rest's history had been nothing but weather and time.

She hiked out to the salt flats before dawn with her recorder strapped to her chest like a second heart, because Ossuary-of-Patient-Water sang only in the hour when the dark was thinnest but not yet broken. Forty-one years on this colony and she still did not know why. She suspected the answer was in the songs themselves, folded into some phrase she could parse but not yet *read*, the way you can sound out a word in a language you don't speak and feel its shape without its meaning.

The flats were silver under the dying moon of Meridian's Rest — the real one, not the captured rock the first colonists had towed into orbit as a monument and then abandoned when the monument's maintenance budget was cut. The real moon was cracked down one side, and light came through the crack in a thin line, like something leaking.

Ossuary-of-Patient-Water was where Rafael had left it three days ago: half-buried in the mineral crust at the edge of the flats, a mound of pale lattice like coral grown by grief. Vessels did not walk so much as accrete, extending themselves filament by filament across decades until they were less a creature than a place, and other creatures — the small skittering things, the wind-borne spore-drifters — lived inside them without either party remarking on it. To stand near a Vessel was to stand inside a city that happened to be alive.

This one was dying the way all of them died now: from the edges inward. Its outer filaments had gone grey and brittle, snapping in the wind with a sound like old hair. Only the core still held color, a deep bruised violet that pulsed slowly, once every nine seconds. Rafael had timed it for years. Nine seconds. She had never learned what the interval meant. She suspected she was running out of time to learn.

"Good morning," she said, which was wrong — Vessels had no morning, no night, no greeting grammar — but she said it anyway, in English, because saying it made her feel less like a woman talking to a rock. Then she switched to what she had of their language, the tonal hum she produced through a throat-modulator clipped under her jaw, a device Teodor had machined for her eleven years ago from salvaged medical equipment. It let her approximate the four registers. Approximate. Her accent in Vessel was, she imagined, like a human voice played through a dying radio.

*I am here. I am listening.*

The violet core brightened. A filament near her feet lifted, trembling, and touched her boot — the closest thing to a handshake their bodies allowed.

Rafael knelt in the crust, checked her recorder's charge, checked it again, and began the ritual she had performed two hundred and six times before, each one a funeral she had pretended was fieldwork.

*Will you sing today?*

The pause lasted long enough that she thought the answer was no. Then the whole structure shivered, grey filaments rattling, and the song began.

---

It is difficult to explain what a Vessel song sounds like to someone who has never stood inside one. The colonists who bothered to listen at all described it as whale-song played backward, or wind through pipes, or nothing much. The company men who had come in the early years — before the massacre, before everything — had described it as *non-linguistic vocalization, probable thermoregulatory function*, and had written that sentence into the founding survey with the same pen they'd used to sign off on the water rights.

Rafael had spent thirty years learning how wrong that sentence was.

A Vessel song was architecture. It had load-bearing phrases, repeated structures that held up other structures, and if you listened across months instead of minutes you could hear that the songs referenced each other — that a phrase sung today might be a response to a phrase sung by another Vessel, dead forty years, whose song Rafael had archived on a cracked data-slate she kept wrapped in cloth like a relic. They were not singing *about* things. They were singing *to* things, across time, building something she could only compare to a cathedral made of testimony.

And lately — this was the part she had told no one, because there was no one to tell — the songs had changed key.

For most of her career the songs had been what she privately called *maintenance*: descriptions of weather, of soil chemistry, of the slow accretion of the flats, sung in loops so stable she could predict the next phrase ninety percent of the time. But in the last six years, as the Vessels died off one by one — eleven when she arrived, four five years ago, and now this — the surviving singers had shifted into a register she had no word for except *witness*. Dense, compressed, urgent. Phrases stacked on phrases like people stacking furniture against a door.

Today's song was almost entirely witness-register. And Rafael, kneeling in the cold with her recorder running and her modulator humming faintly against her throat, understood perhaps one phrase in twenty.

She caught a name. Not a Vessel name — a human one. Rendered in tone, in the way Vessels rendered human names: flattened, syllable-stripped, but recognizable. She caught a number, large, sung twice. She caught a word she had catalogued years ago and glossed, tentatively, as *cutting*, though she had never been sure whether it meant cutting ground, cutting flesh, or cutting memory.

Then the song moved past her understanding entirely, into regions of structure so dense that listening to it felt like standing outside a house with lit windows, seeing shadows move behind curtains, knowing a life was happening in there that she would never be admitted to.

She recorded all of it anyway. That was the discipline. You recorded what you didn't understand because understanding was a door that sometimes opened years later, from the other side, when you finally had the missing piece — and the missing piece was always, always in an archive someone had bothered to keep.

The song ran for fifty-one minutes. When it ended, the violet core dimmed to the color of a bruise healing, and the filaments settled, and the flats were silent except for wind.

Rafael sat back on her heels. Her knees ached. She was fifty-three years old and her body had begun keeping its own records, in its own language, and she was losing fluency in that one too.

*Thank you,* she signed, the full formal gratitude, four tones and a silence. *Your song is held. I will keep it.*

The filament touched her boot again, then withdrew. She chose to believe that was acknowledgment. She chose a lot of things, these days. Belief was the last renewable resource on Meridian's Rest.

---

She walked back to the settlement as the sun hauled itself over the ridge, and the colony assembled itself around her in its usual order of decay: first the fields, half-fallow, the irrigation lines patched with material that had been patching other material for a generation; then the processing sheds, three of five operational; then the habitation blocks, their solar skins clouded, their corridors smelling of recirculated air and boiled greens and, underneath it, the sweetish rot of a place that had stopped being maintained and started merely being endured.

Population: one hundred and twelve. Down from four hundred at her childhood. Down from nine hundred at founding. People left when the shuttles left, and the shuttles left when the contracts lapsed, and the contracts lapsed when Earth stopped answering anything but the loop.

The loop. Everyone on the colony had a relationship with the loop the way people on old Earth had relationships with weather. It was the automated transmission from home, a four-hundred-day cycle of messages — news digests, corporate bulletins, personal messages from families who had mostly stopped writing new ones, so that the same birthday greetings arrived every fourteen months like ghosts keeping appointments. *Happy ninth birthday, Milo. We miss you. The garden is doing well.* Milo would be thirty-one now. No one knew if Milo was alive. The message didn't care. Messages in a loop don't know they're repeating.

Rafael passed Dessa Okonkwo's office on her way to the archive — she always passed it, a superstition of visibility, letting the administrator see her coming back from the flats with mud on her boots, proof that someone was still doing the work the colony had officially defunded nine years ago. Dessa was in her window, as always, drinking reconstituted coffee, watching the street with the expression of a woman auditing a loss.

"Brightwater." Dessa's voice carried through the comm panel. "You're up early."

"The Vessel sang."

Dessa paused, cup halfway to her mouth. "The old one? On the flats?"

"Its final cycle, I think. Days, maybe weeks."

Something crossed Dessa's face that Rafael might have called grief if she hadn't known better. Administrators on Meridian's Rest weren't permitted grief; it was inefficient, and inefficiency got noticed in the quarterly reports nobody read. "We should log that," Dessa said. "Species status update. Extinction event, local, ongoing."

"You could come hear them sometime."

"I've heard them." Dessa set the cup down. "Wind through pipes."

Rafael smiled the smile she used for that sentence and walked on. Behind her she heard Dessa say something else, quieter, to herself or to the empty office — but the comm channel closed before the words resolved, and Rafael filed it where she filed everything she couldn't parse: *later, maybe, later.*

---

The archive occupied the basement of the old survey hall, a room that had been climate-controlled once and was now climate-*suggested*. Two hundred terabytes of recordings, translations, spectrograms, field notes — thirty years of her life, plus the recovered fragments from the founders' era, including the one cracked slate she had inherited from a man named Anselm Brightwater, her father, who had been the colony's previous xenolinguist and who had died — along with everyone else — in the year Rafael did not talk about.

She backed up the morning's recording. Ran the spectrogram. Watched the witness-register structures bloom across the display like frost forming on glass, beautiful and unreadable, and felt the familiar frustration rise and the familiar discipline press it back down.

*One phrase in twenty,* she wrote in her notes. *Fluency declining. Or the language is accelerating faster than I can follow. Note: the name embedded in phrase 14 — cross-reference against founder-era personnel lists. Note: the number, sung twice. Note: 'cutting.'*

She sat with the headphones on for two hours, replaying phrase 14, trying to hear the human name more clearly. It sounded like it might be *Reyes*. It sounded like it might be nothing. Her father's dictionary — the paper one, annotated in his cramped hand — gave her three possible readings of the tonal cluster and none of them were certain.

That was the thing no one warned you about xenolinguistics: it wasn't translation. Translation implied two finished languages and a bridge between them. This was more like archaeology conducted in real time, on a living site, while the site buried itself.

Her modulator sat on the desk beside the headphones, humming faintly even powered down, a flaw Teodor had never fixed. Sometimes, late at night, she thought she could hear words in the hum. She had never told anyone that either.

---

That night she dreamed, and the dream was in the wrong language.

It was a simple dream, the kind she'd had all her life: she was in her grandmother's kitchen on Earth, a place she had seen only in photographs, and her grandmother was telling her to bring the good plates down from the high shelf, and Rafael reached for them, and the shelf was very high, and —

and the instruction came to her not as English. It came as four tones and a silence. *Bring-down-the-vessels-that-hold.* She understood it perfectly. She obeyed it perfectly. In the dream she was fluent in a way she had never been awake, and her grandmother's kitchen was built of violet lattice, and outside the window the salt flats went on forever, and somewhere far away a voice was counting down from a large number, sung twice.

She woke in the dark of her quarters with her heart going hard and the taste of salt in her mouth, and lay still, and did the inventory she did every morning now: *My name is Rafael Brightwater. I am the last xenolinguist on Meridian's Rest. I dream in English.*

Except she was no longer sure that last part was true. The English was going. She could feel it going the way you feel a tooth loosening — not pain exactly, just a wrongness when pressure was applied. Words she'd owned all her life came up missing. Last week she had stood in the mess hall for ten full seconds unable to recall the word for the metal ring that held a keyring together. *Keyring-ring. The loop thing.* Teodor had supplied it — *split ring* — and looked at her with an expression she pretended not to see.

Everyone assumed it was age. Early-onset whatever. The colony's doctor had run scans and found nothing and charged her nothing, which was the current price of medicine here.

But Rafael had a different theory, and she kept it in the same sealed room as the rest of her unshareable knowledge: languages were not stored in you. You were stored in *them*. And she had spent thirty years marinating in a language that was older than hers, denser than hers, patient as mineral — and it was slowly taking the interior space where English used to live. She was not forgetting her mother tongue. She was being *renamed*. Becoming pre-name. Waiting for whatever came after to sing her new name into her.

She got up, dressed, and climbed to the surface dome to check the receiver array before her tea was done steeping, because checking the loop was the other ritual, the one that bookended the days: songs at dawn, transmissions at dusk, and between them the long work of keeping records no one had asked her to keep.

The array readout glowed green in the pre-dawn dark. Next scheduled loop transmission: standard interval. Four hundred days. Everything nominal.

Except — she frowned, leaned closer — the timestamp on the last received packet was wrong. Not wrong the way broken equipment was wrong. Wrong the way a clock was wrong when someone had quietly moved the hands. The packet metadata showed a cycle interval of three hundred and eighty-six days.

Fourteen days short.

She ran it twice. Three hundred and eighty-six. She pulled the historical logs, and there it was in the record: the previous cycle had been three hundred and ninety-one days. The one before that, three hundred and ninety-seven. The loop was shrinking — a few days per cycle, small enough that no one had noticed, consistent enough that it wasn't noise.

Someone, or something, on the other end of forty light-years of silence was tightening the spring.

Rafael stood in the cold dome with her tea going bitter in her hands, looking at numbers that meant nothing and might mean everything, and thought of the phrase she'd caught in the old Vessel's song — the name, the number sung twice, the word that might mean *cutting* — and felt, for the first time in years, that the archive in her basement and the sky above her head were part of the same sentence, and that the sentence was ending faster than anyone had been told.

She copied the timestamps to a slate. She would take it to Dessa in the morning.

She told herself it could wait until morning.
