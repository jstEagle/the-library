## 1. The Long Route

The trick to the Meridian Route, Freya Galloway had learned in her first year of running it alone, was to stop thinking of the silence as empty.

Eleven days out from Verdance with a hold full of seed-stock and medical resin, she had gotten very good at this particular trick. The silence between the two colonies wasn't nothing. It was a thing with texture and weight, like felt, like the inside of a coat pocket you kept your hand in because there was nowhere else for it to go. It had its own weather — solar gusts off the Kestrel Reach corona, dust lanes that hissed against the hull like static through an old radio. If you listened right, the silence almost talked back.

Almost was the operative word. Almost was the whole problem.

"Day nine," she said to the cabin recorder, because day nine was when the talking started whether she liked it or not. "Cargo nominal. Pilot's sanity: pending review." She turned the little brass dial on the console — a real dial, salvaged, satisfyingly clicky — and watched the red light stay lit. Recording. Not sending. There was no one to send to. That was rather the point of the exercise, and also the whole reason she did it at all.

The *Marrow's Gift* — her ship, her name for her ship, since the registry still listed it as *Freighter 4471-C* and registries had no poetry in them whatsoever — hummed along at a patient three-tenths light, threading the corridor between two worlds that had stopped speaking to each other a decade ago and had not, in all that time, found anything worth breaking the silence for.

Except cargo. Cargo they still spoke about, in the language of manifests and escrow accounts and neutral couriers who belonged to neither side. Which meant, in practice, that the only conversation Verdance and Kestrel's Reach had left ran through one woman and a freighter held together partly by duct tape and partly by spite.

"I'm the conversation," Freya told the recorder. "That's either very flattering or very depressing, and I've decided to schedule my feelings about it for when I'm docked and can drink something."

She checked the nav board without looking, which was its own small pride. Third finger, second panel, the drift readout that always ran hot on the port side. Fine. Everything fine. The *Marrow's Gift* flew like a well-trained animal: you didn't have to watch it every second, but it appreciated being acknowledged.

She poured coffee from the bulb that lived permanently welded to the galley rail — the handle had broken in year two and she'd decided the weld was now part of the ship's character — and settled into the pilot's chair with her boots tucked under her, looking out at the long dark.

Here is what most people got wrong about clones, she thought, in the way she thought about it maybe once a week, usually around day eight or nine of a run: they assumed the strange part was the copying. It wasn't. The strange part was the inheritance.

Elena Galloway had been a transit-surveyor, good at her work, better at keeping secrets, and dead eleven years before Freya drew her first breath in a Verdance creche vat. Murdered — that word was official, stamped into the case file, though *unsolved* was stamped right next to it in equally official ink. And so Freya had come into the world owing a debt to a woman she'd never met, wearing her face, carrying her name, expected by everyone who'd known Elena to be, in some ineffable way, her continuation.

"You're so like her," people said at the Verdance docks, with that soft, wondering look, as if resemblance were a compliment instead of a sentence.

Freya drank her coffee and let the thought pass through her like weather. Day nine thoughts were allowed to be heavy. That was the system. You gave them their hour, you recorded them so they existed somewhere outside your own skull, and then you put them away until the next run.

* * *

The routine carried her through the afternoon shift, if you could call it an afternoon when there was no sun to have opinions about time. Pre-flight checks she didn't need. A walk-through of the hold, where the seed crates sat in their foam cradles like sleeping animals — Verdance-grown drought wheat bound for Kestrel's Reach, because whatever else the two colonies couldn't agree on, Kestrel's soil had gone bitter and Verdance's agronomists still shipped, money being the last treaty standing.

She talked to the crates. She'd deny this under oath.

"All right, you beautiful little genomes," she told them, tapping crate seven, which had developed a rattle she distrusted. "You behave, and in six days some Kestrel farmer I'll never meet is going to plant you and think fond, vague thoughts about logistics."

The rattle, when she opened the crate, turned out to be a loose sensor clamp. She fixed it in four minutes flat, hands moving with the unthinking fluency of someone who had grown up taking things apart — another inheritance, though nobody ever said *you're so like her* about the mechanical aptitude. They noticed the face. They never noticed the hands.

Back in the cockpit, she ran the long-range scan out of habit more than hope. The Meridian corridor was empty the way a church is empty — full of presence, absent of people. Verdance sat behind her, a green-white jewel she hadn't been able to look at directly on departure. Kestrel's Reach waited ahead, a rust-orange ember she'd never been allowed to land on without three kinds of permit and a chaperone's escort to the exchange bay. Neutral courier status. Neutral meaning *trusted by both sides and welcome at neither's table.*

Her mother's route. Her mother's neutrality, too, as it happened — Elena Galloway had run survey work for both colonies back when they still pretended to be one project, and there were those who muttered that her murder had something to do with exactly what she'd seen while straddling the border. Freya had grown up on those mutterings the way other children grew up on lullabies.

She keyed the recorder again.

"Message forty-one," she said. "Unsent. Obviously." A breath. "I passed the halfway marker today. There's a rock out here — survey designation KR-118, everybody just calls it the Waystone — and I always salute it, which is ridiculous, it's a rock, it doesn't know. But Elena logged it first. Her handwriting is in the survey plaque, this little sideways scrawl, and I've stood in front of it maybe thirty times trying to see if my handwriting matches hers."

A pause. The ship hummed. Somewhere far behind them, Verdance turned without them; somewhere far ahead, Kestrel's Reach did the same.

"It does match," she said. "That's the worst part. I never practiced it. It just came out that way."

She stopped the recording. Forty-one messages in the queue, going back three years, none of them addressed to anyone in particular and all of them addressed to the same person: the woman whose death had made Freya's life possible and whose absence made it unlivable at odd intervals. She kept them the way you keep letters to the dead — not because they'll be read, but because writing them proves you're still here.

* * *

Evening watch, such as it was. She ate rehydrated noodles with dried Verdance peppers floating in them like tiny red lifeboats, and she played chess against the ship's ancient tutorial program, and she lost, because the program cheated and she let it, because losing to a cheating computer was still a game played against something.

It was during the third loss that the loneliness arrived on schedule, punctual as tide.

This was the part she never recorded. Some things you don't say out loud even to a machine that will never play them back for anyone. The loneliness didn't feel like sadness, exactly. It felt like competence with nowhere to pour. She could fly anything with an engine and half the things without. She could fix a coolant loop blindfolded. She could read two colonies' worth of customs law and find the loopholes both sides pretended not to notice she noticed. And every skill, every day, went into the hold of a ship crossing a gap between people who would each have taken her in only as a copy of a dead woman, never as herself.

Belonging, Freya suspected, was mostly a matter of being needed as yourself instead of as an echo. She wouldn't swear to it. Her sample size was terrible.

"Your move," said the tutorial program, smugly, in a voice like a disappointed schoolmaster.

"You're a cheater and a coward," Freya informed it, and moved her knight anyway, and lost in eleven more moves, and felt marginally better, which was the entire function of the ritual.

* * *

On day ten, the dreams started, and the dreams were always the same, and she had long ago stopped pretending they weren't memories.

In the dream she was small — four, five, creche-sized — and standing in the archive corridor of the Verdance administrative hall, where the portraits hung. Elena's portrait was there among the honored dead: transit-surveyor, lost in service, case unresolved. In the dream, adult Wren — Councilman Aldous Wren, silver at the temples, voice like warm oil — crouched down to her height and pointed at the portrait with the tenderness of a man showing a child a family album.

"That's you," he'd say in the dream. "That's who you are. Isn't that lovely? You already know who you are. Most people spend their whole lives searching."

And dream-Freya, small and solemn, would look up at her own face rendered in oils, and feel the trap close so gently she couldn't even call it a trap, and wake up in the pilot's chair with her heart going like a reactor on a bad day.

She woke that way on the morning of day ten, blinked at the nav board, and laughed at herself — short, humorless, effective.

"He's not a villain," she told the empty cockpit. "He raised me. He paid for my schooling. He cried at my graduation, or produced tears with excellent production values." She rubbed her face. "You're doing the day-ten spiral. Drink water. Fly the ship."

But the dream stayed with her through the morning checklists, sitting in the back of her skull like a passenger who'd paid for the whole cabin. Because here was the thing she'd never said aloud, not even to the recorder: Wren had overseen everything. The licensing of her clone sequence. Her placement in the creche. Her apprenticeship on the freight routes — *Elena's* routes, chosen by him, he'd said, because continuity mattered. Even the case file access he'd arranged for her eighteenth birthday, sealed documents handed over with ceremony: *Your original's history is your history, Freya. You deserve to know.*

She'd thought it was kindness for twenty-six years.

Lately — and she couldn't say when lately had started — it had begun to feel like a leash measured out one generous length at a time.

"Stop," she ordered herself, and obeyed, because self-command was the one inheritance she was fairly sure was hers alone.

* * *

Day eleven. Approach.

Kestrel's Reach swelled in the forward viewport, and Freya did what she always did: she slowed the ship early, took the approach wide, and gave herself ten minutes of just looking. The colony clung to its orange world like lichen on a hearthstone — dome clusters, smelter glow, the thin bright stitch of the orbital tether. From this distance it was beautiful. From this distance, so was Verdance. From this distance, the whole stupid severed family of humanity looked like it might still work.

The comm board chimed. Kestrel traffic control, automated handshake, the same dry protocol exchange they'd run a hundred times.

"*Freighter 4471-C*, you are cleared to Bay Nine, exchange protocol standard, escort will meet you at the airlock. Welcome to Kestrel's Reach."

*Welcome.* The word did its small, dishonest work on her every single time. She cleared her throat.

"*Freighter 4471-C*, acknowledging. Good to be back," she said, knowing the automation didn't care, saying it anyway.

Then the board chimed again — different tone, manual transmission, text-only relay bounced through a dispatcher's console. She pulled it up expecting customs boilerplate.

It wasn't customs boilerplate.

**PRIORITY — ALL COURIERS ON THE MERIDIAN CORRIDOR. REPEAT TO VERDANCE RELAY IMMEDIATELY. DEEP-SPACE CONTACT CONFIRMED. VESSEL IDENT: HALCYON DAWN. LOST FORTY YEARS. RETURNING UNDER OWN POWER. REQUEST IMMEDIATE COURIER RELAY TO BOTH GOVERNMENTS. THIS IS NOT A DRILL.**

Freya read it twice. Three times. The *Halcyon Dawn*. Every colonial kid knew the name the way Earth kids had once known the *Mary Celeste* — the generation ship-turned-deep-survey vessel that had gone out past the heliopause with two hundred souls aboard and simply stopped answering, forty years ago, back when the colonies were still one colony, back when her mother had been alive and surveying those same approaches.

Elena had worked the Halcyon Dawn's launch corridor. Freya knew that the way she knew her own heartbeat — one more fact filed under *inheritance*, one more thread leading back into the dark.

Her hands were already moving before her mind finished deciding: course correction, relay burst toward Verdance, priority flags stacked three deep. The silence between the worlds — eleven years of it — cracked open like ice underfoot, and suddenly everyone on both sides of the gap was shouting into it at once, and the shouting all had to pass through ships like hers.

Through her.

"Well," Freya said to the cockpit, to the recorder, to the ghost in the portrait and the ghost in the mirror, who were the same ghost and also, stubbornly, not. "So much for a quiet run."

She fed power to the engines and turned the *Marrow's Gift* toward the dark where a dead ship was coming home.
