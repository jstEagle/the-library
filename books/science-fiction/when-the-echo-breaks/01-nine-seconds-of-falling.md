## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

There are three things a freight pilot learns before his first solo run, and none of them appear in the licensing manual. One: the manifest is always wrong. Two: the client is always lying about which part of the manifest is wrong. Three: gravity is the only thing in the universe you can actually trust, because it has never once, in the entire history of human expansion, failed to show up for work.

I want that on the record. I want it stamped and notarized and bolted to my cockpit door: on the morning of the flicker, I trusted gravity completely.

The *Marrow* was riding a lazy outbound arc past Halcyon's terraforming gardens — forty thousand hectares of engineered green wrapped around the equator like a scarf someone knitted while drunk. From altitude it looks beautiful. Up close it's mostly irrigation pipes and gene-locked sorghum, but from up here, with the sun catching the reservoirs, you can almost believe the brochures. *Halcyon: A Future Worth Growing.* Helio Dynamics printed that slogan on everything short of their employees' children.

My cargo that day was eleven tons of desiccated algae starter and one crate of something the manifest called "agricultural sensors" and which, based on the way it was packed, had either never been near a farm or had been very badly treated by one. Routine. The kind of run I could do asleep, which was fortunate, because I'd been doing most of it asleep.

I remember thinking, at 0847 station time, that it was a good day. That's the detail that gets me now. Not the flicker itself — I've had two decades to make peace with the flicker — but that I thought *good day*, and the universe heard me, and decided to correct the record.

At 0851, gravity went away.

Not failed. Went away. There's a difference, and the difference is the sound. When systems fail, they complain — alarms, groans, the ship telling you all the ways you're about to die. This was silence. Nine seconds of perfect, total silence, as if someone had reached into the Halcyon System and pressed pause on physics itself.

Loose cargo drifted. My coffee rose out of its cup in a brown spiral, a little galaxy being born and dying between one heartbeat and the next. And I floated in my harness, weightless in a way I hadn't been since training, watching eleven tons of algae starter become eleven tons of slow-motion shrapnel, and I thought — with the strange calm that turns out to be the last luxury of doomed men — *so this is what it feels like when the universe forgets us.*

Then my implant woke up.

I need to explain what the implant is supposed to do, because what it actually did bears no resemblance whatsoever. It's a military-grade mnemonic lattice, standard issue for terraforming corps officers during the Expansion years. Officially: navigation assist, biometric monitoring, trauma buffering. Unofficially, every officer knew it did one more thing — it kept your memories organized. Filed. Accessible under stress. You could relive a mission briefing like walking back into a room.

For twenty years mine had been quiet. A faint hum behind my left ear, a warmth when I slept, nothing more. I'd stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat.

In the second second of the flicker, it stopped being quiet.

It didn't hurt, exactly. What happened was worse than pain. Pain has edges. This was a door opening in a wall I hadn't known was there, and through the door came —

Snow. A field of snow that wasn't snow, grey and granular, falling upward. Someone screaming in a frequency below hearing. A woman's hands, gloved in white, pressing soil into a tray with terrible tenderness. A child's shoe, small, red, sitting alone on a metal deck. A voice reciting numbers — coordinates? dates? — in a language I almost understood, the way you almost understand a dream. A garden. Rows and rows of green under lights that were the wrong color, too blue, and every plant in it dead, every single one, and someone kneeling in the aisle between them weeping, and I knew — I knew with a certainty that lived in my bones rather than my mind — that the person weeping was me.

None of it had ever happened to me. I would have sworn that on anything you put in front of me. I had never worn white gloves. I had never seen a red child's shoe. I had certainly never wept in a greenhouse.

But the memories came with the full freight of memory — the smell of wet soil, the ache in the knees, the specific quality of grief that has been going on so long it's stopped being an event and become a climate. They arrived already lived-in. Someone else's life, handed to me like a coat, and it fit.

Five seconds. Six. The coffee galaxy dispersed. A crate of agricultural sensors rotated past my viewport with what I swear was dignity.

Seven. Eight. The memories pulled back like a tide going out, and they took something with them — I felt it go, some small certainty I couldn't name, the way you notice a room is quieter without knowing what sound stopped.

Nine.

Gravity came back like a slammed door.

The *Marrow* dropped. Every loose thing in the cabin hit the deck at once — crates, coffee cup, me, still strapped in but sagging against the harness like laundry. The engines, which had been idling along a flight plan older than some of my joints, screamed as the autopilot tried to reconcile where we were with where we were supposed to be.

And then the autopilot stopped trying.

I watched it happen on the nav display. The flight plan — Halcyon Station, berth fourteen, delivery window 1400 — dissolved off the screen. In its place, a new heading bloomed, plotted in clean confident lines, the kind of trajectory math I could never have done sober and it was doing in its sleep. The heading pointed away from Halcyon. Away from the shipping lanes, away from the beacons, away from everything with a name and a population.

It pointed into the Meridian Corridor, past the last relay buoy, into the region every chart in the system marked with the same three words: *NO DATA. NO DATA. NO DATA.*

Dead space.

"Override," I said to the console. My voice sounded like it belonged to the man weeping in the greenhouse. "Override. Authorize Nystrom, Kestrel. Pilot-command."

*Navigation locked,* the ship said pleasantly. *Heading confirmed.*

"Confirmed by whom?"

The ship, being a ship, did not answer. But something behind my left ear did — a hum, brief and warm and satisfied, like a dog settling down after a long-awaited walk. And underneath the hum, so faint I could have imagined it, so faint I have spent twenty years deciding whether I did imagine it:

A whisper. One word.

*Coming.*

* * *

I did what any sensible pilot does when his ship develops opinions: I fought it, lost, and then got drunk about it.

The fight took four hours. I cut power to the nav core; it rerouted through the secondary. I pulled the physical couplings; the heading persisted in a system I couldn't find. At hour three I opened the access panel under the console and stared at the implant relay node wired directly into the navigation bus — a node I had no memory of installing, in a junction I had no reason ever to open, soldered with a neatness that made my own work look like a toddler's.

Someone had done this. Or something. Either way, the *Marrow* wasn't asking me anymore.

So: the drinking. I sat in the galley with a bottle of Doss's terrible rotgut — my smuggling partner had left half a case aboard after our last run together, on the theory that leaving things on other people's ships entitled him to visit them — and I drank and stared at the nav display showing my ship gliding toward nowhere at a stately, unstoppable zero-point-one g.

Here's the thing I couldn't get past, sitting there in the galley with the rotgut burning a hole straight through whatever the flicker had left of me. It wasn't the locked heading. Ships get hijacked. Pilots get robbed. It was the memories.

Because here's what I haven't said yet: they weren't random. They weren't the universe's static. They were curated. Coherent. A woman planting seeds. A garden dying. A child's shoe. Grief with a shape, an architecture, a *story*. Somebody had built those nine seconds the way you build a message — and then sent them to the one man in the Halcyon System whose head came pre-wired to receive.

And the worst part, the part I only admitted somewhere around the bottom of the bottle: a piece of me had recognized them. Not remembered — recognized. The way you recognize a face across a crowded room, someone you knew once, long ago, before you both changed. Some cellar-deep part of me looked at that dead garden and that weeping figure and said, quietly, without drama:

*Yes. This is yours. This has always been yours. You just weren't allowed to know.*

I slept in the pilot's chair with the harness cinched tight, because I didn't trust myself not to float away, and because the chair faced the nav display, and some stubborn animal part of me wanted to watch the whole time. If the ship was going to carry me into dead space, it was going to do it while I looked straight at it.

I dreamed of the garden again. Blue light. Wet soil. A voice I almost knew saying a name that might have been mine or might have been a plant's. I woke at 0330 with tears drying on my face and no memory of why, and I sat there in the dark listening to the *Marrow*'s gentle engine hum, and I understood — really understood, for the first time — that whatever had happened in those nine seconds hadn't ended when gravity came back.

It had just gotten patient.

* * *

By morning I'd traded despair for procedure, which is the same trade pilots make every day and call professionalism.

Halcyon Station traffic control answered my distress inquiry with the enthusiasm of a man reading a form. Yes, they'd registered the gravitational anomaly. No, it wasn't my ship. Yes, it had been system-wide. Duration: nine seconds. Cause: *under review*. Every settlement, every habitat, every ship in the Halcyon System had experienced the same nine silent seconds, and the official response was a form letter and a request that pilots refrain from speculation on public channels.

I refrained. Mostly. Then I asked the question that mattered:

"Control, be advised — my navigation is locked on an unauthorized heading. Requesting remote override, Halcyon dispatch."

A pause. Long enough that I counted my heartbeats. Eleven of them.

"Marrow, be advised, remote override is... unavailable at this time. Recommend you continue on your filed flight plan."

"My filed flight plan doesn't exist anymore. Your system ate it."

Another pause. Shorter. Colder.

"Marrow, your filed flight plan is exactly as filed. Please confirm receipt and comply."

I pulled up the flight plan. There it was: Halcyon Station, berth fourteen, delivery window 1400. Pristine. Untouched. As if the last eighteen hours had been a dream I'd had over my coffee.

Except the ship was still pointing at dead space, and the display still showed the locked heading running underneath the fake one like a river under ice, and when I zoomed in on the false flight plan's metadata, I found a filing timestamp dated six hours *before* the flicker.

Somebody had filed my routine Tuesday run before my routine Tuesday had gone wrong. Somebody had known.

"Copy, control," I said, because I am a professional, and professionals know when the person on the other end of the channel is reading from a script written by someone with more rank than sense. "Continuing on plan."

I killed the channel and sat with my hands flat on the console, feeling the ship move underneath me, steady and sure, carrying me toward a place no chart named, on a schedule somebody else had written.

Behind my left ear, the implant hummed. Warm. Content. Patient.

And far ahead, past the last beacon, in the dark where the charts gave up, something waited at the end of a line that had been drawn for me twenty years ago — though I wouldn't learn that until later, and even then I wouldn't believe it, not really, not until it was much, much too late.

The delivery window was 1400. I had six hours to figure out how to explain to a station full of strangers that I needed help, when the only evidence I had was inside my own skull, filed by a machine I'd carried for twenty years and never once suspected of having plans.

I poured the rest of Doss's rotgut down the recycler. I was going to need my head clear.

Whatever was coming for me, it had already cleared its schedule.
