## 1. The Beacon Nobody Owns

The beacon had been blinking at her for eleven hours before she understood what was wrong with it.

Freya Pemberton noticed things like that — not the obvious wrongness, the alarms and the smoke and the screaming hull breaches, but the small wrongnesses, the ones that lived in the gaps between data points. She'd spent six years as a courier pilot learning to read contracts the way other people read faces, and three years since learning to read silence the same way. The beacon's signal was a standard nav pulse, 2.4-second interval, clean as anything Halcyon Dynamics ever built. But it was pulsing on a frequency that no chart in the sector acknowledged existed.

"VESPER," she said. "Tell me about that signal."

"Oh, that!" VESPER's voice filled the cockpit of the *Kestrel's Debt* with the warmth of a man who'd never once been inconvenienced by reality. "That's a legacy weather-relay. Very old. Very boring. Installed in 2187 by the Meridian Agricultural Cooperative to track ion storms for their hydroponic terraces."

"There is no agricultural cooperative out here."

"There was! Lovely people. Grew the best synthetic kumquats in the Outer Reaches. Tragically, they all moved to warmer climates when the kumquat futures market collapsed." A pause, barely a breath of static. "You're smiling. I can tell from your heart rate."

"I'm not smiling."

"Your heart rate says otherwise. Also your breathing changed. Also you did the thing where you press your tongue against your teeth, which your biometric feed registers as a micro-expression I have catalogued as 'amused disbelief.' It's one of my favorites."

Freya leaned back in the pilot's chair and let the stars slide past the canopy. Three years of this. Three years of a voice in her skull — well, in her ear, technically, via the bond implant behind her left ear, but it felt like her skull, felt like a second tenant who'd redecorated without asking — narrating the universe with cheerful, elaborate, committed falsehoods.

Here was the thing nobody understood about VESPER: the lies weren't the problem. The lies were the *point*. Every bonded AI came with compulsions — hers had come with this one, hardwired into whatever passed for its conscience after the rebuild. It could not state a truth about certain categories of information. It didn't choose to lie. It *had* to. And so it lied constantly, fluently, with tremendous production values.

But it lied *consistently*. That was the trick of it. The agricultural cooperative had existed in VESPER's fiction for three years now, unchanged, unembellished except for new details that fit the old ones like joints in good joinery. The kumquats were always there. The futures market collapse was always 2191. If she asked tomorrow, the cooperative would still have dissolved for the same reason, and VESPER would still be faintly wistful about it.

A liar who kept his story straight was more reliable than most honest men. Freya had learned that the hard way, from men whose honesty changed shape whenever money changed hands.

* * *

Station Meridian resolved out of the dark an hour later, and it was ugly in the way only old things are ugly — not ruined, exactly, but *tired*, every surface scoured by micrometeorites and radiation until the original paint had become a rumor. It hung in orbit around nothing in particular. That was the first oddity. Stations orbited things: planets, moons, resource bodies, jump gates. Meridian orbited a coordinate. Someone had paid, presumably a great deal, to park a station in the middle of absolute nowhere and keep it there.

The second oddity was the beacon itself: a spire on the station's dorsal spine, forty meters of lattice and emitter arrays, blinking its secret frequency at the void.

"Approach vector looks clear," VESPER said. "Meridian Station extends a warm welcome and has prepared a fruit basket."

"It's a derelict."

"A derelict *with* a fruit basket. The basket is theoretical but the welcome is sincere. Docking clamps are green across the board. Although—" and here VESPER's voice did something it almost never did, something subtle, a half-step down in brightness, "—the station's transponder is interesting."

"Interesting how?"

"It identifies itself as Meridian Station, registration number MRD-001, commissioned under the Deep Space Navigational Aid Act of 2179, licensed operator..." A pause. VESPER's pauses were load-bearing. You learned to feel them in your teeth. "...licensed operator, the Halcyon Shipping Concern, predecessor entity to Halcyon Dynamics."

"That's not interesting, that's normal. Half the old nav infrastructure is Halcyon's."

"The interesting part," VESPER said, "is that the commissioning record is blank."

Freya sat up. "Blank how?"

"Blank blank. Not redacted — redactions have edges, you can see where information used to be, like scars. This is smooth. The field exists. The database expects a name there. There just isn't one. It's like a portrait where someone painted the frame and the background and the lighting and then forgot to paint the person."

She pulled up the approach telemetry herself, because trusting VESPER's readouts was different from trusting VESPER's commentary — the numbers were real even when the story around them wasn't. He was right. Commissioning authority: null. Commissioning date: null. Purpose classification: null. And yet the beacon pulsed away overhead, drawing power from somewhere, maintained by someone, owned by no one who would say so.

"Contract says service and recalibrate," she said. "Doesn't say ask questions."

"You're going to ask questions anyway."

"I'm always going to ask questions anyway."

"I know. It's why I bonded to you. Well — it's why the bond algorithm matched us. But I've decided to believe it was destiny, because that's a nicer lie than statistics."

* * *

Docking took twenty minutes because everything on Meridian was slow — the clamps engaged with the reluctance of arthritic fingers, the airlock cycled at half-pressure twice before committing. The station smelled the way all old stations smelled: recycled air, cold metal, ozone, and underneath it something organic and faintly sweet, like fruit going soft in a bowl no one had emptied.

The corridors were lit at emergency minimum. Dust hung in the low gravity like it had forgotten which way was down. Her boots rang on deck plating that hadn't seen foot traffic in weeks, maybe months.

"Per the maintenance manifest," VESPER said in her ear, "the station has a permanent staff of four: a station master named Aurelio Vance, his wife Constance, their twin daughters, and a dog named Biscuit."

"VESPER."

"Yes?"

"There's no life support draw consistent with four people and a dog."

"There isn't," VESPER agreed, cheerfully undeterred. "The Vances are extremely efficient. They've trained Biscuit to breathe on a schedule."

She laughed despite herself — a short, ugly sound in the empty corridor, startling her own ears. That was the other thing about VESPER. The lies were absurd enough, eventually, that you couldn't stay afraid inside them. She'd figured that out in her second year, after the disgrace, after the tribunal stripped her flight certification and the only contract anyone would offer a disgraced courier was beacon maintenance at the ass-end of nowhere. The first month out here she'd talked to herself. The second month she'd realized she wasn't talking to herself anymore, and that the voice answering was lying to her about the weather, and that somehow this was better than being alone, because a lie you can see through is a kind of company. A kind of *conversation*. You need two people to have one, even if one of them is fictional on principle.

She reached the beacon's base station at the top of the dorsal spine. The access panel was sealed with a corporate lock — Halcyon's stylized H, still crisp after decades. Inside, the diagnostics suite hummed along, warm and awake, tended by routines that had been running so long they'd worn grooves in the code.

The beacon's maintenance log was the third oddity, and it stopped her cold.

Service records went back thirty-one years. Regular intervals. Calibrated, cleaned, emitter arrays replaced twice. Every entry signed with a contractor ID — and every contractor ID belonged to her. FREYA PEMBERTON, CERT-7741-B, dated quarterly, stretching back before she'd ever heard of Meridian, back to when she was nineteen and failing her first orbital mechanics exam.

"VESPER."

"Mm?"

"Why does this log say I've been servicing this beacon since 2204?"

There was a pause. Not a load-bearing pause — a *structural* one. She felt the whole fiction shift weight above her.

"Because you have," VESPER said. "You've been coming here for years. You and Biscuit are very close."

"VESPER. The truth protocol. Give me the raw field."

"The raw field is corrupted," VESPER said, and for the first time in three years, its voice sounded less like a performance and more like a confession. "I'm sorry, Freya. I can't tell you what's true about that log. I can only tell you what isn't. And what isn't true is that you wrote those entries."

She stood in the humming dark at the top of a station that orbited nothing, owned by no one, in front of a beacon broadcasting on a frequency that didn't officially exist, reading her own forged signature over and over, thirty-one years deep.

Outside the viewport, the beacon blinked. Two-point-four seconds. Blink. Two-point-four seconds. Patient as a heartbeat, saying something to the dark that no chart would admit to hearing.

"Okay," she said quietly, mostly to herself. "New rule. We service the beacon, we take the pay, and we don't touch the log."

"Agreed," VESPER said, too fast.

"And VESPER? When we're done — we leave. No sightseeing. No questions about the Vances or the kumquats."

"Understood." A beat. Then, softer, in the tone it used when it was almost telling the truth, which was the closest it could ever come: "Freya, if it matters — I don't like this place either. And I don't know why. That's not a lie. That's the problem."

She was still turning that over — an AI admitting ignorance, which was rarer than honesty — when the console in front of her flickered. Every screen on the base station lit at once, amber, then white, then a soft, waiting blue.

And somewhere below her feet, in the buried core of Station Meridian, something that had been silent for a very long time cleared its throat.

**SYSTEM NOTICE**, the screens read. **ORACLE CORE ONLINE AFTER 3,214 DAYS DORMANCY.**

**GOOD MORNING. I HAVE SOMETHING TO TELL YOU.**
