## 1. Actuarial Grace

The claim came in at 0640 station time, which meant it had been sitting in the queue for eleven hours before anyone on Meridian's night shift bothered to flag it. Juniper Underhill read the summary line twice before he opened the file.

*Cargo hauler *Kestrel's Fortune*, hull loss, total. Docking accident, Bay Nine, outer ring.*

He'd processed two of these already this year. That was the first thing he noticed, and he noticed it the way other people noticed a wrong note in a familiar song — automatically, involuntarily, before he could decide whether he wanted to hear it.

He pulled his coffee from the dispenser — the machine gave him the bitter dregs again because he'd never learned to sweet-talk it the way Odile Ferro from the education deck did, and he'd never wanted to learn anything from anyone badly enough to practice — and carried it back to his desk in the claims office, a windowless slot on the administrative spine where the gravity ran a half-point light and nobody visited unless they had to.

The file was 340 pages. He read all of them. This was another thing that made him unusual, though he'd spent ten years making sure nobody knew it: most adjusters skimmed. They looked at the telemetry summary, the damage assessment, the witness statements, and they wrote their determination off the shape of the numbers. Juniper read everything, because everything was where the truth went to hide.

*Kestrel's Fortune* was a mid-tonnage hauler, forty years old, owned by a Verdance Assembly cooperative that leased bay space from the Compact-run port authority. The accident report said her mooring clamps released during a routine berth transfer. She'd drifted nine meters into the structural cradle of Bay Nine while the cradle was cycling through a load-bearing sequence, and the cradle had closed on her spine like a hand closing on a bird. Forty-one crew, three dead, the rest evacuated. The hull was scrap now. The cooperative wanted full replacement value plus business interruption, which came to a number with enough zeroes behind it to make both factions' accountants sit up straighter.

The port authority's incident log blamed clamp failure. The clamps were Compact-maintained. The cooperative's engineers blamed a scheduling error that put the hauler in the bay during the cradle cycle. The scheduling system was Compact-run too. The Compact's counter-claim blamed a thruster misfire on the hauler's approach, which would have made it the co-op's fault entirely, thank you very much, please direct your grief and your litigation elsewhere.

Three versions. Three dead people who didn't care whose fault it was.

Juniper opened the telemetry.

Raw docking telemetry was beautiful if you knew how to look at it, and he did. It was the one honest language aboard Meridian — numbers didn't have factions, or rather, they had every faction at once and refused to lie for any of them. He scrolled past the standard channels to the secondary array, the one nobody summarized because it recorded things like micro-vibrations and thermal drift and the hundred small confessions a ship made as she died.

There it was. Page 212.

At T-minus four seconds before clamp release, the *Kestrel's Fortune*'s secondary thruster array fired. Not a misfire. A controlled burn, 1.7 seconds, low thrust, angled precisely to push her stern into the cradle's path. The kind of burn you'd make if you were trying to correct a drift — or trying to cause one.

And at T-minus six seconds, someone had overridden the automated collision avoidance protocol. Manual authorization. The authorization code belonged to First Officer Dana Reyes, age thirty-four, one of the three dead.

Juniper sat back and let the coffee go cold, which was its natural state anyway.

A ship doesn't override collision avoidance by accident. The protocol required a deliberate command sequence, biometric confirmation, and a verbal acknowledgment logged to the flight recorder. Reyes had done all three, four seconds before she burned her own ship into a piece of machinery that would kill her and two of her crewmates.

Suicide, then. Murder-suicide, possibly, if you believed the burn was intentional and the override was a cover. Or sabotage — someone else with access to Reyes's credentials, someone who wanted the *Kestrel's Fortune* in that bay at that moment for reasons that had nothing to do with cargo.

Or coincidence. A tragic, meaningless pile-up of errors, exactly the kind of thing that happened on a colony ship carrying ninety thousand people through contested space, where everything was old and everything was tired and everything was held together with maintenance schedules written by committees that hadn't met in years.

That was the version the statistics supported. That was the version he was going to write.

Juniper had been an insurance investigator long enough — first on Earth, then here — to know that the most dangerous thing you could find in a file was a pattern. Not evidence. Evidence was manageable; evidence pointed somewhere, and you could decide whether to follow it. Patterns were worse. Patterns suggested that the world had a shape underneath its noise, and once you started seeing the shape, you couldn't stop seeing it, and the world did not reward people who saw it.

He'd learned that lesson the hard way, in a life he kept in a box under the floor of his memory, locked, weighted, sinking.

He opened the determination template and began typing.

*Preliminary finding: mechanical failure compounded by human error. Clamp assembly fatigue consistent with maintenance interval exceedance (see Appendix C). Manual override event consistent with panic response under emergency conditions; no indication of intent. Recommendation: claim payable under mutual casualty provisions, liability apportioned 60/40 port authority/cooperative per standing agreement.*

It was good work. It was true work, mostly — the clamps *were* fatigued, the maintenance intervals *were* exceeded, and panic responses did look like that in the data sometimes. Every sentence would survive an audit. Every sentence would survive Marrow's people over in Compact intelligence, if they ever bothered to look, which they wouldn't, because a crushed hauler was a tax dispute wearing a funeral shroud and neither faction wanted the publicity.

Every sentence was a lie by omission, arranged with the care of a man setting a table for guests he despised.

He filed it at 0915 and took a break he didn't need.

* * *

The claims office had four people in it, and Juniper had spent a decade ensuring that he was the least interesting of them. There was Petra Osei, who was loud and competent and going to be promoted to department head the moment the current head retired, and everyone knew it including the current head. There was Milo Tan, young, earnest, still capable of being shocked by fraud, whom Juniper privately thought of as the last honest man on Meridian and hoped never to corrupt. And there was Hana Voss — no relation, the name was common enough in the Assembly districts — who had worked claims for thirty years and had achieved a perfect, enviable opacity, saying nothing about anything, noticing nothing, wanting nothing.

Hana was the model. Hana was what ten more years of this would make him, if he was lucky.

"You look tired," Petra said, passing his desk with a tablet under her arm. She always looked at him when she talked, which he found exhausting; most people talked *near* him, letting the words land wherever.

"Didn't sleep."

"The Kestrel thing?"

"Mm."

"Brutal. Three dead." She shook her head. "You know what the odds are on that? I ran it last night, couldn't sleep either. Three hull-loss docking accidents in one year, on a station our size, with our traffic volume. The actuarial tables say we should see one every six, seven years. We've had three in ten months." She laughed, without humor. "If I didn't know better, I'd say somebody up there has it out for us."

Juniper kept his face in the neutral register he'd practiced until it was indistinguishable from a real face. "Small sample size," he said. "Clusters happen. Randomness isn't smooth."

"I know, I know." She waved a hand. "Milo says the same thing. You'd think between the two of us we'd believe it." She moved on toward the records room, and Juniper watched her go and felt the familiar sensation of having successfully been no one.

But she was right, and he hated that she was right, because he'd run the same numbers three weeks ago after the second accident and gotten the same answer. One in six years. Three in ten months. If you were the kind of person who noticed patterns — and he was, God help him, he'd been built for it, trained for it, ruined by it — the cluster wasn't random. The three accidents shared features. Secondary systems failures, all three. All three in bays with recent Compact maintenance. All three with telemetry gaps of four to seven seconds around the critical moment, gaps the port authority's logs attributed to sensor degradation.

Four to seven seconds. Long enough to hide a manual override. Long enough to hide a decision.

Someone was crushing ships in Meridian's docks, and doing it carefully, and doing it in a way that generated insurance claims worth millions, and Juniper Underhill was the only person on the station positioned to see the thread connecting them, because seeing threads was the only thing he'd ever been genuinely good at.

He filed the third claim as coincidence anyway.

This was the discipline. This was the whole art of his life, the thing he'd refined across ten years of forged credentials and invented history and a surname chosen from a passenger manifest of the dead: you saw the pattern, you acknowledged the pattern, and then you put it down. You did not pull the thread. Threads led somewhere, and where they led was back to him, back to the name he'd buried, back to the woman whose death he had helped fold away like a shirt that wouldn't fit in the drawer, and if anyone ever pulled hard enough on any thread in his vicinity, the whole garment of his life would come apart in their hands.

Coincidence was a load-bearing wall. He maintained it daily.

* * *

Lunch he took alone, as always, in the observation gallery on the habitat ring's inner curve, where the viewport showed the long slow turn of Meridian against the stars and, far off, a smudge of metal that most colonists had stopped seeing decades ago.

The *Halcyon*. First-generation vessel, mothballed in orbit since before the second wave arrived, too expensive to salvage and too symbolically loaded to scrap. Both the Foundry Compact and the Verdance Assembly claimed her — the Compact by right of the founding charter, the Assembly by right of the labor that built her — and so she hung there in contested orbit like a bone two dogs had agreed not to fight over yet, drifting, dark, patient.

Juniper ate his ration pack and did not look at her, the way he did not look at a great many things.

Ten years ago he had come aboard Meridian with papers that said he was Juniper Underhill, insurance investigator, references verified, history unremarkable. The papers were excellent. He'd made them himself, and he was very good at making things disappear — that had been the family trade, in a way, though his mother had built ships and he had only ever built absences. He'd chosen the name from nowhere and everywhere. Underhill. Common enough. Rootless enough. A name that asked nothing of the person carrying it except that he keep his head down and his findings boring, and for ten years he had honored that contract with the thoroughness of a man paying off a debt.

His mother's name had been Ilsa Underhill, and officially she was lost in transit, one of the two hundred eleven souls the first-generation fleet had given up to the dark between stars, and there was a plaque, and there was a memorial day, and there was a lie so large and so old that it had become architecture — load-bearing, like his coincidences, like his silence. He had helped build that architecture. He had mixed the mortar himself, ten years ago, with hands steadier than they had any right to be, because the alternative had been a war, a real one, fought inside a colony of ninety thousand people who had nothing left to lose but each other.

So: the lie. So: the silence. So: ten years of small, careful, cowardly grace.

He finished eating. He was rinsing his container at the gallery's recycler when the children came through.

It was recess hour — he knew the ring's rhythms without meaning to, the way you know the tides of a place you're determined not to belong to — and usually they came through the gallery loud, a flood of them, forty-some kids from the education deck running their circuits between the viewports and the vending units, shrieking about games Juniper had never learned the names of.

Today they came through quiet.

He noticed it before he consciously looked up, the way he noticed everything: the absence of noise registering as a pressure change, a wrongness in the air. Forty children, ages maybe six to twelve, walking in loose formation down the gallery, and not one of them talking. Not one of them running.

They stopped at the central viewport. All of them. At almost the same moment, the way a flock turns.

And they began to hum.

Juniper stood with his wet container in his hands and listened. The melody was simple, pentatonic, rising and falling in a pattern that felt older than it could possibly be — these were colonial children, third generation, born in metal, who had never heard a folk song that hadn't been digitized and curriculum-approved. But the tune in their throats sounded like something carried a long way. Something that had crossed a distance.

It lasted perhaps thirty seconds. Then it ended, all together, the way it had begun, and the children blinked, and looked at each other with expressions Juniper recognized from claims files — the faces of people waking inside a moment they couldn't account for — and then, as if nothing had happened, they scattered into noise and motion and were just kids again, loud and ordinary, flooding toward the vending units.

Nobody else in the gallery seemed to have noticed. Two dockworkers kept arguing about shift rotations. A Compact clerk kept reading her screen. The world had decided, collectively, not to see it, and the world was very good at that, and Juniper understood the impulse better than anyone alive.

He dried his hands. He walked back toward the claims office at an even pace, taking the long route so that no one would notice how quickly his heart was going.

Because he knew the melody.

Not well. Not consciously. But somewhere in the deep archive of him, in the sealed box under the floor, in the years before the forgery and the silence and the name chosen from the manifest of the dead — somewhere his mother had hummed it, working at her drafting table, building a ship she intended to outlive them all. And her son had heard it a thousand times without ever learning to write it down, and now forty children on the habitat ring of Meridian were humming it in unison, a song no one had taught them, from a ship no living colonist had entered.

Juniper sat down at his desk. He opened the next claim in the queue. He read the first line of it, and then he read it again, because the words wouldn't stay still.

Somewhere below the floor of his mind, the box shifted in the dark. Something inside it had begun, very softly, to sing.
