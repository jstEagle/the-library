## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The freighter *Long Patience* ran the Silent Corridor the way a monk walks a vow: same speed, same lane, same silence, every run.

Zephyrine Quintana had made the crossing forty-one times. They knew the route the way other people knew their own hands—by feel, by habit, by the particular hum the drive made when it passed the midpoint beacon, a note just below hearing that old spacers swore was the ship dreaming. Routine was not a cage. Routine was armor. On a route where two worlds had stopped speaking to each other eleven years ago, the safest thing a person could be was boring.

They were boring. They were excellent at it.

The manifest said agricultural seed-stock and medical polymers, Calla to Vesper, the usual quiet trade that kept both worlds fed while their governments pretended the other didn't exist. The *Long Patience* flew neutral registry, neutral colors, a neutral name painted in flaking letters on the hull. Its captain filed flight plans in triplicate and never deviated. Its captain had a face you forgot the moment you looked away, which was a skill Zephyrine had practiced in mirrors for years until it stopped being a skill and became a fact.

They were in the galley, halfway through rehydrating a pouch of Marisol's black bean stew, when gravity broke.

There was no warning. No shudder, no alarm, no flicker in the lights. One moment the stew was pooling heavy and familiar in the bottom of the pouch; the next, the pouch was drifting, and so was the spoon, and so was Zephyrine's stomach, lifting out of them like a bird startled off a wire.

Free fall.

The *Long Patience* didn't do free fall. The spin-ring kept a steady point-nine-two g, tuned by Dov every run to the decimal, because Dov believed gravity was a promise you made to the cargo and breaking it was a kind of lying. Zephyrine had never once felt weightless on this ship. Not in forty-one runs.

Their first thought was not *malfunction*. Their first thought was a single, absurd word that arrived from somewhere below thought, from some floor of the mind they didn't own:

*Nine.*

They didn't know why. They didn't have time to.

The stew pouch bounced off the ceiling—except there was no ceiling anymore, there was only *up*, and *up* was a lie—and Zephyrine pushed off from the galley table toward the corridor, toward the cargo hold, because the manifest was ninety percent sealed crates of seed-stock but ten percent live cultures in suspension tanks, and if the tanks lost their orientation for long, the cultures would stratify and die, and a dead cargo was a dead run, and a dead run was the end of everything.

The corridor was a river of floating debris. A wrench. A data-slate. Marisol's good ladle, spinning end over end like a slow silver propeller. Zephyrine swam through it all, hand over hand along the guide rail, the cold of the metal biting through their gloves, their breath loud in their own ears.

The ship's comm crackled. Dov's voice, stripped of all its usual warmth: "Grav ring's not down. Grav ring's *gone*. I'm reading full field collapse across every deck. This isn't us. Repeat, this is not us."

"Ash?" Zephyrine said. "Tell me it's not us."

"Navigation's clean," Ash said from the cockpit, their voice thin and fast. "We're not in anything. No anomaly, no shear, no—Zeph, there's nothing *out here*. There's nothing out here at all."

The comm channel was suddenly full of voices. That was the thing Zephyrine would remember afterward, more than the falling, more than the cold: the way the whole system opened up at once, like a house where every door slams open in the same instant. Every ship on the Corridor, every hauler and courier and customs skiff, all of them keying their mics at the same moment because for nine seconds nobody in the space between Calla and Vesper had weight, and nobody knew why, and the silence that the two worlds had maintained for eleven years shattered into a hundred panicked voices all talking over each other.

*"—lost grav on all decks, requesting—*"*

*"—is anyone else reading this, is anyone else—"*"

*"—Calla Traffic, do you copy, Calla Traffic, our ring is fine, our ring is FINE, but the—"*"

*"—same here, same timestamp, same—"*"

Every ship. Every deck. The same instant. Zephyrine's mind, trained by eleven years of careful invisibility to notice patterns and then immediately look away from them, snagged on the fact and could not let go. Gravity did not fail system-wide. Gravity was not a thing that *happened* to a system. A gravity lattice was local, mechanical, owned. For every lattice in the Corridor to stutter at the same moment was like every clock in a city stopping—not an accident. A *statement*.

*Nine,* said the voice in the floor of their mind again.

They reached the cargo hold and the sight stopped them for one full second of the nine.

The hold was an ocean. Ninety tons of sealed crates hung in the dark like icebergs, drifting in slow, majestic, indifferent spirals. The suspension tanks—there were six, each the size of a coffin—had broken loose from their cradles and were turning lazily in the middle air, trailing their umbilicals like kelp. And Zephyrine was directly in the path of Tank Four, which weighed, fully loaded, four hundred kilograms, and was rotating toward them with the patience of a planet.

They dove. Not a decision—a reflex older than thought, some animal arithmetic of mass and angle that lived in the spine. The tank's corner passed through the space where their head had been close enough to stir their hair. It clipped their shoulder instead, a glancing blow that spun them end over end into the drifting cloud of crates, and they had one bright, clear moment of understanding: *I am going to die in my own cargo hold, and it will be an accident, and it will not even be the interesting kind.*

Then their hand closed on the guide rail bolted along the hold's spine, and the spin stopped, and their arm nearly came out of its socket, and they hung there in the dark among the icebergs of their boring, invisible life, breathing.

"Zeph?" Marisol's voice on the comm, sharp with fear. "Zeph, your vitals just spiked, talk to me."

"I'm fine," they said, and it was almost true. "Tanks are loose. I've got the rail. Somebody get down here before the cultures—"

"I'm coming," Dov said. "Hold on."

*Hold on.* Zephyrine almost laughed. They were holding on to the only fixed thing in a hold full of drifting everything, and the fixed thing was a rail bolted to a ship that was itself falling around a star that was itself falling, and the only reason *hold on* meant anything at all was that gravity had, for the moment, decided to keep its promise again.

They counted. They didn't decide to count; the counting was happening to them, the way breathing happens. *Four. Five. Six.* The voices on the open channel were still tumbling over each other, a system-wide chorus of confusion, and underneath it, faint as a pulse, Zephyrine could hear the drive humming its below-hearing note, and the note had changed. It had changed by a fraction, a half-step, and no one else on the ship had noticed because no one else had spent eleven years listening to this ship the way a monk listens to a vow.

*Seven. Eight.*

The gravity came back.

It didn't fade in. It didn't surge. It arrived, complete and exact, like a word finishing a sentence, and the whole hold fell at once—ninety tons of cargo dropping in the same instant, crates slamming into cradles, tanks swinging on their umbilicals, the ladle striking the deck with a small bright *tink*. Zephyrine dropped too, their boots hitting the deck plates, their knees buckling, and they went down hard on one knee with their hand still wrapped around the rail, and the pain in their shoulder flared white, and they stayed there, kneeling in the sudden restored weight of the world, shaking.

On the comm, the voices were still going. But they were changing. The panic was curdling into something else—questions, and then the careful, procedural tone of people being told what to say.

*"—all vessels, this is Calla Traffic Control, be advised the event is under review, maintain current positions and—"*"

*"—Vesper Authority concurs, all vessels maintain—"*"

And then, threaded through it, one transmission on a channel that should have been empty, a channel so old most ships didn't even carry the hardware for it anymore. A woman's voice. Calm. Measured. Speaking with the cadence of someone reading coordinates.

*"Nine seconds. Check your logs. Nine seconds missing from every one."*

Then static. Then nothing. Zephyrine knelt on the deck of the *Long Patience* with their heart slamming and their shoulder screaming, and the voice moved through them like cold water, because they knew that voice.

They had never heard it in their life. They had heard it every day of their life. It was the voice that lived behind their eyes, in the memory imprint they'd been grown from, the voice that narrated Vela Quintana's last recorded morning the way a ghost narrates a house: *Coffee first. Then the long corridor. Then the numbers.*

"Zeph." Marisol was in the hold doorway now, one hand on the frame, her face pale. Behind her, Dov, still in his harness, wrench in hand, staring at the restored weight of the world like a man who'd watched a miracle and didn't trust it. "You're bleeding. And you've got that look."

"What look," Zephyrine said.

"The one where you know something," Marisol said. "The one I hate."

Zephyrine got up. Their knee ached. Their shoulder throbbed. Around them the hold had returned to its proper order, crates seated, tanks swaying gently in their cradles like sleeping animals, everything exactly where it belonged, as if the last nine seconds had been a dream the ship had agreed to forget.

But Zephyrine had counted. Nine seconds of falling, and then the voice on the dead channel, saying *nine seconds missing from every log*, and Zephyrine already knew, with the cold certainty of inherited instinct, that when they pulled the *Long Patience*'s telemetry, the event would not be there. Not truncated. Not corrupted. *Missing.* Erased so cleanly it would look like it had never happened—except that their shoulder hurt, and the ladle was on the floor, and forty-one runs of routine lay cracked down the middle like river ice.

"Log the incident," they heard themselves say. Their voice was steady. They had practiced that too. "Standard report. Gravity fluctuation, brief, resolved, no casualties, no damage beyond loose restraints. File it and forget it."

Dov frowned. "That's not standard. That's the opposite of—"

"File it," Zephyrine said, "and forget it."

They looked at the hold one more time—the restored order, the sleeping tanks, the ladle someone should pick up—and felt the first hairline fracture run through the armor of their careful, invisible, boring life. Because the voice had said *check your logs*, and it had said it in Vela's voice, and Vela Quintana had been dead for eleven years, murdered somewhere along this exact route, and dead women did not broadcast on dead channels.

Unless, Zephyrine thought, climbing the corridor toward the cockpit with gravity pulling honest and steady at their boots, they had left something behind that could.

Unless the nine seconds hadn't been a malfunction at all.

Unless they had been a message—and Zephyrine, of all the souls adrift in the Silent Corridor, was the only one who could read it.

* * *

In the cockpit, Ash had the telemetry up on the main display, and the display was lying.

"Here's the event window," Ash said, tapping the timeline. "Every system on the ship recorded a gap. Nine point zero seconds. Not corrupted—*absent*. The data just... isn't. Like someone reached into the log and took a clean bite out of it."

"Every system?"

"Every system. Life support, drive, nav, comms. Same gap, same timestamps, down to the millisecond." Ash looked up, and their eyes had the flat shine they got when the numbers scared them. "Zeph, logs don't do this. Logs *fail* messy. They don't fail politely. They don't fail *identically*."

"Pull the external feeds," Zephyrine said. "Traffic control, the relay echoes, everything we passively recorded."

Ash worked in silence for a minute. The display filled with channels, and channel after channel showed the same clean absence, the same surgical nine seconds, and then Ash stopped scrolling and went very still.

"Zeph," they said quietly. "The Corridor relay echo. The one at the edge of dead space. It's not missing."

Zephyrine leaned in. There it was: nine seconds of telemetry, intact, unedited, timestamped to the millisecond. The only copy in the entire system that nobody had gotten to yet—or nobody had *bothered* to erase, which was worse, because it meant whoever was curating the record hadn't needed to. It sat out there at the edge of everything, in the dark past the last beacon, holding nine seconds of the truth like a stone under its tongue.

"Route's not due out that far for six months," Ash said.

"I know," Zephyrine said.

The drive hummed its changed note below hearing. Behind their eyes, in the quiet rooms of an inherited mind, Vela Quintana's voice said something it had never said before, in all the years of the imprint, in all the forty-one crossings:

*Come and get it.*

Zephyrine stood in the cockpit of the *Long Patience*, in the honest restored weight of a gravity that had broken its promise for nine seconds and then pretended it hadn't, and understood that the life they had built—the routine, the armor, the deliberate invisibility—was over.

Somewhere out at the edge of dead space, a dead woman was waiting.
