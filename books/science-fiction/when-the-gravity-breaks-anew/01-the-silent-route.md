## 1. The Silent Route

The cargo hauler *Patient Arithmetic* had a name Edmund Marchetti had never chosen and had long since stopped resenting. It suited the work. Nine years of running freight along the outer relay lines, from colony to colony that no longer answered hails, and every day was arithmetic: mass, burn time, oxygen margins, the slow subtraction of everything human from the dark between stations.

He was three days out of Ceres Verge with a hold full of desalination membranes and seed stock bound for Thessaly Reach, and he was dreaming again in a language that no longer existed.

That was the thing about his dreams. They had gone quiet in every tongue he'd been born to—English first, then Portuguese, then the trade creole of the Belt, each one draining out of his sleep like water through cupped hands until his nights were wordless, gray, a theater after closing. But the dead languages still came to him. Old Meridian settler dialects he'd studied at university. The tonal whistles of the Kestrel-adjacent mining families on Halcyon's rim. The sign-speech of the deaf community that had once thrived on Port Anselm before the station closed its airlocks for good.

In sleep, he was fluent in extinction.

He woke in the pilot's chair with the taste of it still in his mouth—a lullaby fragment, something in the low-vowel cadence of the old Thessaly fishing clans, a language whose last native speaker had died, by his own cataloguing, four years and two months ago. He kept the records himself. Nobody else did. A private ledger, forty-one entries now, each one a language he could parse but no longer dream *in*—no, that wasn't right either. He dreamed *in* them. He simply couldn't dream *of* them anymore. The living languages had abandoned him; only the dead ones stayed.

He logged the dream anyway. Habit. Discipline. The small rituals of a man keeping vigil over a graveyard the size of a star system.

*Thessaly fishing-clan register, subdialect "harbor-low." Last fluent speaker: Maren Oduya, d. 4 years 2 months prior. Current status: remembered only.*

The *Arithmetic* hummed around him, patient as her name. Outside the viewport, the relay beacon at Junction Nine pulsed its automated greeting into the void—*this node operational, this node listening*—and received, as always, nothing back. The colonies beyond had stopped transmitting years ago. Not all at once. That was the strange part, the part nobody on Earth wanted to talk about: they hadn't gone silent in a catastrophe. They had gone quiet the way a house goes quiet when everyone has left the room. One by one. Politely. With their lights left on.

Edmund ran cargo to them anyway. Someone had to bring the membranes, the seed stock, the replacement parts for systems that might or might not still have hands to install them. And someone had to knock on doors that didn't answer, just to confirm the silence, to timestamp it, to say *we came, we called, you did not reply.* It was grim work dressed in bureaucratic language—his manifests said ROUTINE DELIVERY, SCHEDULED CONTACT ATTEMPT—and he had come to think of himself less as a courier than as a coroner making rounds.

Thessaly Reach appeared on the third day as a smudge of light against the dark, then resolved into its familiar geometry: the spinning habitat ring, the dock arms folded like sleeping insects, the agricultural domes glinting green-gold under floodlights that burned whether anyone tended the crops or not. Life support readings, when he pinged them, were nominal. Power draw suggested habitation. The comms channels offered him the standard automated courtesy—*vessel identified, docking clearance granted, welcome to Thessaly Reach*—in a voice that had been recorded by a woman named Odile Ferrant, who had died, according to his ledger, six years ago, and whose voice now greeted every ship forever without knowing anyone had gone.

He docked. He waited the customary hour for a dockmaster who would not come. Then he cycled the airlock himself and walked the arrival concourse with his cargo manifest under his arm, calling out the old greeting in harbor-low, the fishing-clan dialect, because protocol said to attempt contact in any registered local language and because some stubborn ember in him refused to let the words go unspoken even to empty corridors.

*"Sela vun tora,"* he said to the concourse. *We see your boats come home.*

The concourse said nothing. The lights came on ahead of him, sensor-triggered, courteous, automatic. Somewhere below, hydroponics pumps churned. The colony breathed around him like a body in a coma, alive in every measurable way except the one that mattered.

He made his deliveries. Seed stock to bay four, membranes to the water reclamation level, all signed for by a terminal that stamped his manifest with an authorization code belonging to a man who had not drawn breath in three years. On his way back through the concourse, he paused at the community board—the physical one, cork and pushpins, because the settlers here had been old-fashioned about it—and read the notices one more time. A lost cat, five years stale. A harvest festival announcement dated eleven months ago. A child's drawing of a boat, sun-faded almost to white.

He took nothing. He left a note, as he always did: *Cargo delivered. Route remains open. —E.M.* It was a lie of a kind, the route open, but he wrote it every time, on every dead colony, because the alternative was to write *nobody is coming* and he could not yet make his hand form those words.

Back aboard, he filed his contact report—*colony nonresponsive, infrastructure functional, no signs of distress*—and pulled up his next assignment from the relay queue. Routine. Another run. He scrolled the manifest with the numb efficiency of long practice, checking weights, volumes, hazard codes.

And then he stopped scrolling.

There, in the passenger section—a field so rarely used on these runs that he'd nearly forgotten it existed—one line item. One name.

**PASSENGER MANIFEST ADDENDUM — VESSEL KESTREL — 1 SEAT CONFIRMED**

He read it twice. Three times. The *Arithmetic*'s cabin lights buzzed faintly overhead, and somewhere in the hold, a cargo strap settled with a soft creak, and Edmund Marchetti sat very still in his chair while the words rearranged themselves in his mind like a sentence in a language he almost knew.

*Kestrel.* There was no vessel registered anywhere in the colonial fleet by that name. He knew this the way he knew phoneme inventories and case structures—not as data looked up, but as data lived in. He had spent nine years memorizing the registries of dying routes, the hull numbers and call signs of every ship still flying, every ship recently grounded, every ship scrapped. A man cataloguing extinctions learns the names of everything that might yet be saved. There was no *Kestrel*. There had never been a *Kestrel*. The registry databases would confirm it if he asked, and he found his hand hovering over the query console and not pressing it, because some older instinct, some linguist's ear tuned to the false note, told him that the confirmation would be worse than the question.

A name that shouldn't exist. Sitting on his manifest like a word from a language with no speakers, no corpus, no history—except that his ear, his treacherous perfect ear, insisted it *did* have a history. It sounded constructed. Deliberate. The way cover names are constructed, the way false fronts are built: plausible, modest, forgettable. A bird name. A safe name. The kind of name you give a thing you want no one to look at twice.

One seat confirmed. For whom? For what? Passengers didn't ride the dead routes. In nine years, he had carried exactly three: a surveyor, a grieving widow visiting a grave that was also officially empty, and a courier whose papers had been too clean. No one bought passage to colonies that had stopped speaking.

He queried the origin point of the addendum instead of the name itself. Safer, he told himself. Just logistics. The routing stamp came back: **MERIDIAN STATION — TRANSFER AUTHORITY**.

Meridian. He knew the name the way he knew all of them—from his ledger, from his university years, from the linguistic surveys he'd helped compile as a younger man with a grant and a belief that documentation could save things. Meridian Station, one of the great settlement successes of the second wave, home to perhaps eleven thousand people speaking a rich braided creole of settler dialects that he had once, briefly, dreamed in. Officially decommissioned eleven years ago. Officially uninhabited. Its last transmissions had ended not with distress but with a tidy administrative closure, the bureaucratic equivalent of a door closed gently by someone careful not to wake the house.

Eleven thousand people, and the file said *decommissioned*, and the decommissioning paperwork was flawless—he had read it once, early in his career, and marveled at how clean it was. No appeals. No diaspora records. Eleven thousand people had scattered so completely into the human population that not one of them had ever filed a claim, sent a message, or died with Meridian listed as their birthplace. He had noticed that, once, and then he had done what careful men do with observations that don't fit: he had shelved them, labeled them *anomaly*, and moved on.

Now the anomaly had bought a ticket on his ship.

He sat with it for a long time while Thessaly Reach wheeled slowly past the viewport, its green domes turning in the dark like a thought someone couldn't finish. The beacon at Junction Nine pulsed its patient lie—*this node listening*—and Edmund understood, with the quiet vertigo of a man hearing his own mother tongue spoken with a wrongness he can't locate, that the silence he had been cataloguing for nine years might not be death at all.

Death leaves traces. Grief leaves records. Even extinction, in his experience, left bones—corpora, recordings, a child's drawing of a boat fading on a corkboard. What the colonies had left was smoother than that. What they had left was *nothing*, and nothing, he knew now, staring at the single confirmed seat on a ship that had never been built, nothing was the hardest text there was to read. Nothing had grammar. Nothing had an author.

His hand hovered over the console again. This time he pressed it.

**REGISTRY QUERY: VESSEL "KESTREL."**

The response came back fast, clean, untroubled:

**NO MATCHES FOUND. NO VESSEL BY THIS NAME HAS EVER BEEN REGISTERED.**

*Ever been registered.* He noted the phrasing the way he noted everything, helplessly, professionally. Not *is not currently registered*. Not *registration expired*. *Has ever been.* A denial not of the present but of the entire past tense. Somewhere, a database had been taught to lie about history, and it lied the way all good lies are told—in the grammar.

Edmund Marchetti leaned back in his chair, and for the first time in nine years on the silent route, he set a course for a colony that was not on his delivery schedule. Meridian Station. Population zero. Life support, according to the routing authority, long since shut down.

He entered the jump coordinates with steady hands, and if they were not entirely steady, well—the dead languages had taught him that trembling is just another kind of speech.

Behind him, Thessaly Reach shrank to a spark and went out. Ahead, the dark route opened, and somewhere down it, a name that had never flown was waiting to take him somewhere he had already been in his dreams, in a language he alone still heard, spoken by people the universe insisted had never existed.

He engaged the drive.
