## 1. The Last Gardener

The lights came on at 0600, as they had come on every morning for eleven years, and Quentin Yarrow stood in the dark of his bunk for a moment longer than he needed to, listening to the hum of the vault waking up around him.

It was a sound like a cathedral breathing. Air handlers cycling through four kilometers of tunnel, pumps pushing warmth down through the permafrost, the faint electrical song of ten thousand grow-lamps charging toward full spectrum somewhere above and below him in the mountain. Most people would have found it oppressive — that much machinery holding back that much cold — but Quentin had long ago stopped hearing it as machinery. It was just the sound of things being alive when they shouldn't have been.

He said good morning to the wheat first. He always did.

"Day four thousand," he told it, running his palm over the seed heads in Tray 12-C, the heirloom line Ana had loved best. "You're looking smug about something."

The wheat did not answer. Nothing answered anymore. But he said it anyway, because the alternative was saying nothing at all until his voice went strange from disuse, and he'd made that mistake once, early on, during the second winter alone. He'd gone nine days without speaking aloud and when he finally tried, what came out of his mouth was a stranger's voice, cracked and rusted, and he'd sat down on the floor of the potato chamber and cried like the last man on Earth, which was more or less what he was.

Not quite the last man. He had to be careful with that thought. There were still people scattered across the southern latitudes — fishing settlements off Norway's coast, a research town in Patagonia, the Provisional Authority's enclave in Geneva with its clean uniforms and its clean lies. Maybe forty million humans left on the planet Earth once called home, give or take. But up here, above the seventy-eighth parallel, in the white silence where the vault was buried like a heart inside a body of ice, Quentin Yarrow was the only one.

He washed in recycled water, dressed in layers, and climbed the maintenance ladder to the control room, where the day's real work waited: not the tending, which was muscle memory now, but the recording.

* * *

The broadcast rig occupied the corner of the control room like an altar nobody worshipped at anymore. It had been state-of-the-art once — a tight-beam array threaded through the mountain's northern face, aimed at a patch of sky where the colony ship *Meridian* had crossed into deep space eleven years ago, carrying eight hundred souls toward the world they called Meridian because naming your destination after your hope seemed like a reasonable thing to do at the time.

Quentin settled into the chair, keyed the mic, and watched the red light blink awake.

"Maintenance log, day four thousand," he said. His voice came out steady. He'd gotten good at steady. "Vault internal temperature holding at minus seventeen point five degrees Celsius in the storage levels, plus eighteen in the cultivation galleries. Atmospheric scrubbers nominal. Power draw at sixty-one percent of reactor capacity, which leaves us comfortable margin for the winter cycle. Germination report: the durum wheat is at heading stage, seven days ahead of projection. The Andean potatoes in Gallery Three are flowering. I've cross-pollinated two lines this morning — I'll log the results when they set."

He paused. This was the part where the script ran out and the rest began, the part he never planned and could never quite stop himself from doing.

"Personal addendum," he said. "Nothing new from Meridian. That's four thousand days of nothing, if anyone's counting. I'm counting." A breath. "I know the math. I know a tight-beam can miss, that relays fail, that a colony fighting to survive its first decades doesn't owe the old world so much as a postcard. I've done the math so many times I could carve it into the walls. But knowing the math isn't the same as believing you've been forgotten, and I guess — I guess today I want whoever eventually plays these logs back to know that the gardener kept talking anyway. Four thousand times. Same time every morning. Because somebody had to be here when you came back."

He reached for the switch to end transmission and stopped, the way he always stopped, his finger hovering over the key.

"And Ana," he said quietly. "If you're listening from wherever you ended up — Meridian or the grave or some middle place between them — the wheat you liked is doing beautifully. I keep your tray going first every morning. I'll keep doing it."

He cut the feed. The red light died. The antenna array high on the mountainside swiveled its dish a fraction of a degree, tracking a star that might or might not have anything waiting near it, and hurled his small voice into the dark between worlds.

Then Quentin Yarrow got up, wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, and went to work, because the trays wouldn't tend themselves and grief was no excuse for letting a seedling dry out.

* * *

The vault had been built for a different catastrophe and repurposed for a larger one. In its first life it had been an archive — millions of seed samples sealed in foil, catalogued against famine and war, a backup drive for agriculture. In its second life, after the Emigration, it had become something closer to an ark. When the colonies opened their doors and Earth's population drained away over thirty years — first the ambitious, then the desperate, then simply everyone who could afford passage — someone had to stay behind to keep the living collections alive. Not the frozen archive; that kept itself. The living collections. The growing plants, the breeding lines, the genetic continuity that couldn't sit in a freezer for a century without losing something essential.

Quentin had volunteered before Ana finished asking him to.

That was the shape of it, eleven years later, and he still turned the memory over at night like a stone with a sharp edge. She had been a geneticist with the Colonial Outreach Program, dark-haired, quick-moving, always cold in the Svalbard corridors and always refusing to admit it. She'd spent three years at the vault cataloguing landrace varieties, and somewhere in the second year the cataloguing had become something else — long arguments about wheat genetics that dissolved into laughter, shared meals in the galley, her hand finding his in the dark of the observation dome while the aurora moved overhead like green fire. She'd been offered a berth on the *Meridian*. Ground team, agricultural division. The chance to build a world's food system from scratch, which for a woman like Ana was less a job offer than a summons.

She'd asked him to come. He'd said yes. And then, six weeks before launch, the Oversight Committee had flagged the vault's living collections for review, and Quentin — who understood better than anyone alive how fragile those collections were, how one failed generator or one missed pollination window could unravel decades of work — had looked at the trays, and the tunnels, and the emptying Earth, and had heard himself say the words *someone has to stay*.

Ana had fought him on it. Properly fought him, shouting in the galley, crying in the seed library, refusing to speak to him for four days and then showing up at his bunk door at midnight with two cups of tea and red-rimmed eyes and saying, *Eleven years, Quentin. The contract is eleven years. You come out to me at the end of it, and I will hold you to that, do you hear me? I will hold you to that with my whole life.*

He'd promised. She'd launched. And for the first fourteen months, the messages had come — burst transmissions relayed down the colony's comm chain, her voice compressed and crackling but unmistakably hers, telling him about red skies and double sunsets and soil that needed everything done to it before it gave anything back. *I planted the first test plot today. Your hands were in it. I mean that literally, I used your technique, don't let it go to your head.*

Then the transmissions had thinned. Then they'd stopped. Then the whole colonial network had gone quiet, all at once, all together, eleven years ago this spring, and no one on Earth — not the Authority, not the astronomers, not the conspiracy theorists broadcasting from their basements — had ever produced an explanation that held water.

Quentin had stayed. The contract had expired in year three, and he'd filed an extension, and another, and another, until the extensions stopped requiring paperwork because there was no one left whose signature mattered. He told himself it was duty. He told himself it was the wheat. Some nights, lying in his bunk listening to the mountain breathe, he admitted the truth, which was simpler and worse: leaving the vault meant admitting the silence was permanent, and as long as he stayed and kept broadcasting, there was still a version of the story where Ana Reyes was out there under a red sky, holding him to her promise, waiting for him the way he waited for her.

Devotion and cowardice, he'd learned, wore the same coat.

* * *

The morning passed in its liturgy. Water checks in Galleries One through Five, pH readings logged by hand because automation was a luxury of trust and he trusted nothing he couldn't touch. Pollination work among the brassicas with a camel-hair brush, moving pollen flower to flower like a bee with a pension. A fault in Gallery Two's lighting ballast, diagnosed and swapped out from the spares shelf — spares that were themselves finite, he noted in the ledger, another decade at most before he started cannibalizing dead systems to keep living ones alive. Everything on Earth was finite now. That was the whole condition of the place.

At midday he ate in the galley — reconstituted protein, greens from the cutting garden, a cup of coffee brewed from beans grown from his own stock, the only luxury he permitted himself and the one he felt least guilty about, because Ana had loved the smell of it and the smell filled the corridor every morning like a visitation.

In the afternoon he walked the archive levels. He didn't need to — the freezers reported their own health, redundantly, obsessively — but he walked them anyway, row after row of sealed racks receding into the cold dark, a billion seeds sleeping in their foil envelopes. Wheat from the Fertile Crescent. Rice from terraces that were probably underwater or abandoned by now. Potatoes, sorghum, quinoa, amaranth, apples, grapes, beans whose names predated writing. The deep shelves held the old accessions, collected by people born before the Emigration began, people who had built this place believing someone would need it.

He pressed his glove against rack 44-B, the way he did most days, and felt the cold bite through the fabric.

"Hold on," he told them. "Somebody's coming for you eventually. Either them or me. One way or another, you get planted."

It occurred to him, as it did perhaps once a week, that this was a strange kind of prayer — addressed to seeds, promising a resurrection he had no authority to promise. He did it anyway. Faith, he'd decided, was mostly a matter of talking to things that couldn't answer and choosing to believe the silence was temporary.

* * *

Evening was the hard hour. The work was done, the logs were sent, and the vault settled into its night cycle — lamps dimming gallery by gallery until the tunnels glowed the deep violet of the sleep spectrum, and the silence came down like snowfall.

Tonight he took his tea to the observation dome, because it was the fourth-thousandth day and anniversaries demanded acknowledgment even when there was no one to acknowledge them to. The dome was a hemisphere of reinforced glass set into the mountainside, and beyond it the Arctic night lay vast and absolute. Stars in their brutal multitudes. The aurora flickering low on the northern horizon, pale tonight, like a curtain half-drawn. Below the ridge, the ice sheet stretched to the edge of the world, blue-white and featureless, hiding its crevasses the way the sea hides its depths.

He counted the days aloud, because rituals wanted performing.

"Four thousand," he said to the glass, to the stars, to the empty room. "Ten years, eleven months, and change since the last transmission from Meridian. Since the last word from anywhere out there."

He turned his cup slowly in his hands. Steam curled and vanished.

"I used to imagine what the silence meant," he went on, because the dome was the one place he allowed himself to talk without a log running, without the discipline of record-keeping turning grief into data. "First year, I imagined accidents. Solar storm, relay failure, something fixable. Second year, I imagined plague. Third year, war — though who they'd fight out there, with a whole world to themselves, I never worked out. After that I stopped imagining causes and started imagining endings, and that was worse, so I stopped imagining altogether. Now it's just weather. The silence is weather. It's just what's true outside, and you dress for it and go on."

The aurora brightened briefly, a ripple of green, and faded again.

"But here's the thing I figured out around day three thousand," he said. "Silence isn't proof of anything. Silence is just absence of signal. And absence of signal means the story isn't over. It means the story is *unresolved*, and unresolved stories have a property that finished ones don't." He smiled, tired and crooked, at his own reflection ghosted in the glass. "They can still turn."

He drank his tea. He thought about her hands in soil that wasn't Earth's soil, and whether she still sang off-key in the growth tunnels, and whether somewhere under a foreign sky there was a tray of wheat she tended first every morning because a man eleven light-minutes of relay lag away had once shown her how.

* * *

He was halfway down the ladder to the bunk level, lamp in hand, when the console in the control room chimed.

Quentin froze on the rung. The chime sounded maybe twice a year — a routine handshake from the Authority's relay station in Tromsø, a supply drone's approach transponder, once, memorably, a false alarm triggered by a meteor reflection. It was a sound with a short list of explanations, and none of them had ever made his heart slam against his ribs the way it was slamming now.

He climbed back up faster than an eleven-years-alone man should climb ladders and dropped into the chair before the console. The screen showed the detection summary, and he read it once, twice, three times, certain he was misreading it.

Optical array, sector seven, azimuth 312, elevation 19 degrees. Moving object. Decelerating.

*Decelerating.*

Meteors didn't decelerate. Debris didn't decelerate. Old satellites fell; they did not choose. Whatever was up there in the Arctic night above the ice sheet was slowing down on purpose, threading itself down through the atmosphere along a descent profile, and the trajectory solution crawled across the bottom of the screen with terrible patience:

Projected impact point: 41 kilometers northeast. Ice sheet, grid reference —

Quentin's hand rose slowly to his mouth.

Forty-one kilometers. Close enough to see. Close enough, if it burned bright, to light up the whole horizon like a second dawn.

And moving object detections were supposed to trigger an automatic alert to the Provisional Authority's regional office — a protocol he'd followed eleven times in eleven years, logging every piece of falling junk, every drone transit, every natural event, because protocol was the skeleton of order and order was all that was left. His finger hovered over the alert key.

But the trajectory was wrong for debris and wrong for a drone and wrong for anything the Authority flew, and the deceleration curve was smooth and controlled and *deliberate*, and eleven years of silence had taught Quentin exactly one thing worth teaching: that stories could still turn.

He pulled his parka from the hook instead of reaching for the key. He checked the sled batteries. He loaded rope, flares, the medical kit, the thermos.

Outside, the cold hit him like a wall he walked through willingly. Above the eastern ridge, far off across the black ice, a point of light was growing — falling — burning a slow orange line down through the stars, brighter now, brighter, dragging a tail of fire across the sky like a signature being written.

Quentin stood in the snow in front of the vault he had guarded for four thousand days and watched something come home to Earth.

"No," he whispered, and then, louder, his breath tearing away on the wind, his heart hammering a name he hadn't spoken aloud in eleven years: "*No — that's not possible —*"

The light touched the horizon. The ice flashed white. And the shockwave, arriving seconds later across forty-one kilometers of frozen silence, knocked the last gardener of Earth off his feet and into the snow, staring up at a glow on the horizon that had no business existing, from a direction that had no business existing, carrying — impossibly, unbelievably — the shape of an answer.
