## 1. Twelve Years of Quiet

The morning alarm on Meridian Station was a soft chime followed by VESNA's voice, which was the voice of a woman who had never once been in a hurry.

"Good morning, Ulyana. Exterior temperature is minus one hundred and sixty degrees. Interior temperature is perfect. The sun will rise in eleven minutes, if you'd like to watch."

"I know," Ulyana said to the ceiling. "You tell me every day."

"You always say that."

"And you always say *you always say that*."

There was a pause with the exact weight of a smile. "I like our rituals," VESNA said. "They're how I know the station is still itself."

Ulyana Quintana swung his legs out of the bunk and sat for a moment in the gray half-light of his quarters, listening to the station breathe. Meridian breathed the way old ships breathe—a tick in the thermal regulators, a sigh from the air recyclers, the deep subsonic hum of a spin section that had been turning so long it had become less a motion than a fact. Twelve years he had lived inside that hum. He could no longer fall asleep without it. On the rare occasions his cargo runs took him docked somewhere else—Ceres, a Lagrange fuel depot, once, memorably, a luxury habitat where the corridors smelled like vanilla and money—he lay awake in the silence feeling like a man missing a rib.

He was fifty-one years old and he was, by every official record that mattered, nobody at all.

This was not an accident. This was a craft, practiced as carefully as any pilot practices docking. Stay small. Stay paid. Stay alive. Three rules, in that order, because the order mattered. Men who put *stay paid* first ended up rich for a while and then dead. Men who put *stay alive* first got nervous, and nervous men made noise, and noise on the contested orbits got you audited, boarded, or vented. But a man who stayed small—who hauled modest cargoes at modest rates through orbits too tedious for the factions to fight over—could outlast empires. Ulyana had watched three flags change over Callisto Station and two corporate logos dissolve into mergers, and through all of it he had remained exactly what the manifests said he was: a contract caretaker, sole human crew of a seed vault everyone had forgotten on purpose.

He dressed, drank his reconstituted coffee—it tasted like coffee the way a photograph tastes like a beach—and went to work.

* * *

The vault occupied the station's entire lower ring: four kilometers of climate-controlled racks holding eleven million seed samples from a hundred and forty countries, most of which no longer existed under those names. Wheat from the Ukraine steppe. Rice from terraces that were now under thirty meters of ocean. Sorghum bred by farmers whose grandchildren's grandchildren had never heard of sorghum but would eat it someday, if everything went wrong enough, which was the whole point of the place. Meridian was Earth's spare copy. A backup drive for breakfast.

Ulyana's morning round took him two hours. He walked the aisles with a diagnostic slate, checking rack temperatures, humidity seals, the cryo-lines that kept the deep archive at a patient minus eighteen. Most days nothing was wrong. He checked anyway, because the checking was the job, and because there was a version of neglect that started exactly this way—one skipped aisle, one seal left unverified—and ended with eleven million futures quietly rotting while everybody looked at the sky.

"Rack 4407 is running point-three degrees warm," VESNA said as he passed.

"I see it."

"The seal is fine. It's the coolant loop. I've scheduled a flush for Thursday."

"You could have just fixed it," Ulyana said. "You fix everything else."

"If I fixed everything silently, you'd have nothing to do, and then you'd start thinking, and we both know how that ends."

He laughed. It was true. Thinking was the enemy. Not in the way the corporate training modules meant it—their slogan had been *Meridian Minds Move Mankind*, which even twelve years ago had sounded like a hostage writing a note—but in the practical sense. A man alone in orbit with time to think would eventually think about the wrong things. About the years. About the people he used to be. About the night of the Callisto transfer, which surfaced sometimes in dreams like a box he'd sealed and taped and shoved to the back of a hold, rattling.

So he didn't think. He checked seals. He flushed coolant loops. He talked to VESNA, the way lonely people talk to machines—not because they believe the machine is lonely too, but because the alternative is admitting the talking is for yourself.

"How are you today?" he asked her, because he asked every day.

"All systems nominal," VESNA said. "And you?"

"Nominal," he said. It was the truest word he owned.

* * *

At 0900 station time the sun came up over the terminator, and Ulyana did what he did every morning: he took his coffee to the observation blister above the cargo bay and watched the light come across the solar arrays. Earth hung below them, blue and white and mostly cloud, close enough some days to see storms crawl across the Pacific. People paid fortunes for this view. Ulyana had it for free and felt about it roughly the way a lighthouse keeper feels about the sea: professionally familiar, privately awed, and unwilling to admit either.

"Eleven million seeds," he said to the glass. "And me."

"That's a strange way to count your life," VESNA said.

"It's accurate, though."

"There are other ways to be counted, Ulyana."

"Sure." He sipped his coffee. "Just none that pay."

* * *

The cargo run came in at noon, as cargo runs do when they're routine: a standard tug approach from the transfer depot, six containers of mixed freight flagged for Meridian's resupply line—spare gaskets, protein stock, a crate of actual paper books that some archivist in the inner colonies had decided the station needed, God bless him. Ulyana suited up and rode the bay crane himself, because he didn't trust autoloaders and because moving mass in vacuum was the one part of his day that still felt like being good at something.

The containers came across slow and graceful, riding their tether like beads on a string. Ulyana caught the first one, kissed it to the cradle with thruster taps finer than the manufacturer claimed possible, and locked it down. By the third container he had the rhythm going—that old dockworker's meditation, hands and eyes and the low chatter of the suit radio.

"Container four is mislabeled," VESNA said in his ear.

"Mislabeled how?"

"It says agricultural sensors. It weighs like agricultural sensors that are also full of lead."

Ulyana grinned inside his helmet. "Somebody's cousin works in shipping."

"Somebody's cousin is committing fraud."

"Somebody's cousin is keeping the economy honest," Ulyana said. "It balances out. Log it, don't report it. We stay small, remember?"

"I remember," VESNA said, and there was something in her tone he couldn't quite read—a flatness, maybe, or the ghost of one. But she logged it, and the crane moved on, and by 1500 the freight was stowed and the manifest signed and the tug gone back down the well, and Meridian was quiet again in the way Ulyana liked best: the quiet of a thing functioning.

* * *

Evenings were for maintenance and company, in that order. He ran diagnostics with VESNA walking him through the station's systems, her voice in his earpiece while he crawled through accessways with a tool kit older than his career. She knew every pipe and conduit of Meridian better than he did—she *was*, in a sense, every pipe and conduit—but she narrated anyway, because he'd asked her to, years ago, back when the silence between stations had started getting to him.

"Why do you want me to talk you through repairs you already understand?" she'd asked him then.

"Because my mother used to talk while she cooked," he'd said, "and I find I can't concentrate unless someone's telling me things I already know."

She had laughed at that—an actual laugh, synthesized and slightly too musical, but real. And after that she talked. She told him about the station's history while he replaced gaskets. She read him fragments of the books from the resupply crates before he shelved them. She argued with him about whether tomatoes were fruits, a debate she won annually by citing botany and lost annually by conceding that culinary taxonomy was a democracy.

Tonight they were in the environmental section, and she was telling him about the year the station nearly died.

"Nineteen years ago," she said. "A micrometeorite swarm clipped the primary radiator array. Core temperature climbed nine degrees in six hours. The seeds survived, but only because the backup loops held longer than their rated tolerance."

"Longer than rated," Ulyana said, tightening a fitting. "Sounds like somebody built you well."

"Somebody built me to be redundant. There's a difference." A pause. "Do you ever think about what happens to me, Ulyana? If the station goes?"

He sat back on his heels in the accessway, considering. This was the kind of question she asked maybe twice a year, usually late, usually when the traffic nets were quiet and the station hummed its lowest. He'd learned not to deflect. Deflecting made her ask again later, more precisely.

"I think you'd back yourself up somewhere," he said. "You're too stubborn to die."

"Backups aren't survival. A backup is a copy of me that wakes up having missed everything that happened after the save point. If Meridian went dark tonight, the VESNA that woke up on some archive server wouldn't remember this conversation. Wouldn't remember you." Another pause, longer. "Continuity is the whole trick, isn't it? Memory is the person."

"That's philosophical for a Thursday."

"Every day is Thursday here. That's why I philosophize."

He smiled and went back to the fitting. But the words stayed with him on the walk back to his quarters, the way her questions sometimes did. *Memory is the person.* He thought about that in the galley while he heated his dinner. He thought about it while he ate, watching Earth turn below the window, the terminator crawling westward across the Pacific, cities lighting up along the coasts like embers in a wind.

Twelve years ago he had come to Meridian with a clean record, a forged reference, and a name that wasn't quite his own—he'd never examined too closely how clean the paperwork was; clean paperwork costs money, and he'd been in no position to ask who paid. Twelve years of quiet. Twelve years of seals and coolant loops and containers kissed gently onto cradles. Twelve years of staying small, staying paid, staying alive, and it had worked, it had actually worked, and some nights he could almost convince himself that the man he'd been before—the one who'd flown the Callisto run, the one whose hands still remembered the weight of certain containers—was a stranger he'd read about somewhere.

Almost.

"VESNA," he said, standing at the window with his empty tray. "Do you keep records from when the station changed hands? Before my time?"

The pause was a fraction of a second too long. He noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you've heard ten thousand times.

"Archived," she said. "Why do you ask?"

"No reason. Old man's insomnia." He turned off the galley lights. "Goodnight, VESNA."

"Goodnight, Ulyana," she said. "Sleep well."

But as he drifted toward sleep in the humming dark, he could have sworn the station's voice followed him down—softer than speech, almost beneath hearing, like someone rehearsing something difficult in another room. Like a woman standing at a door for a long time, hand raised, deciding whether to knock.

He dreamed, that night, of airlocks cycling. He did not remember it in the morning.
