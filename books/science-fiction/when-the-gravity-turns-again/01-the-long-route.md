## 1. The Long Route

The snowcat died on the fourth kilometer of the Meridian causeway, which was typical of it, and I fixed it in forty minutes, which was typical of me. I say this not to brag but to establish the terms of our relationship, reader: you are about to spend a great deal of time with a woman who talks to machines because there is no one else to talk to, and I would rather you think of me as competent than as lonely, even if both are true.

The fuel line had frozen where it passed too close to the exhaust manifold—a design flaw some engineer on Earth had signed off on forty years before anyone stopped signing off on anything. I bled the line, wrapped it in spare insulation, and sat on the cold plating eating a ration bar while the engine warmed up. The bar was lentil. It was always lentil. Somewhere under my feet, in the vault's deep shelves, were eleven thousand seed varieties including four hundred kinds of grain that people once fought over, and I was eating compressed lentil paste in a snowcat cab at minus thirty-one degrees Celsius.

There's a joke in there somewhere. I've been looking for it for six years.

* * *

The Meridian Seed Vault sits in the old polar highlands like a wedge driven into the world's forehead. Officially—and by "officially" I mean according to the automated status pings that go out every seventy-two hours to relay stations nobody staffs—the vault has a caretaking team of nine. There are nine names. There are nine rotation schedules. There are nine sets of handwriting in the maintenance logs, because I taught myself to write like other people, or at least like eight other people, which is a stranger skill than it sounds and one I do not recommend acquiring.

In truth there is me. Ansel Brightwater, twenty-nine years old, sole custodian of humanity's backup plan, population one.

The status pings matter more than you'd think. Earth's systems went quiet in stages—first the funding, then the personnel, then the acknowledgment that anything south of the equatorial relays still existed. But the fleets out there, the Vantori and the Ossuary Compact and whoever else inherited the shipping lanes, they still check. A dead vault is an abandoned vault, and an abandoned vault is salvage. Nine names on a roster means nine reasons to leave us alone. So every seventy-two hours, I sit down at the comm desk and become nine people.

Today's schedule called for Dr. Imara Osei, systems specialist, to log a routine thermal audit. I put on my reading glasses—I don't need them; they're a prop, part of the costume—and wrote her report in her careful, slightly cramped hand. *Coolant loop three holding at nominal. Replaced gasket, shelf bay 12. Note: recommend full inspection next quarter.* Then I filed it under her name, timestamped it from her terminal, and took the glasses off.

"Good work, Doctor," I told the empty room.

The room did not answer. The room never answers. This is the arrangement we have.

* * *

My grandmother would have hated the glasses. She'd have said a lie should be carried lightly or not at all—that a prop is just a confession you haven't made yet. She said this about a lot of things, most of which I ignored, and now she's four years gone and I find myself obeying her posthumously, which I suspect was always the plan.

Her name was Yeva Brightwater, and she spoke Kessari.

I want to be precise about what that means, because precision is the only inheritance she left me that doesn't ache when I pick it up. Kessari was a trade language—not the language of any nation but the language between nations, built over two centuries by dockworkers and freight pilots and customs clerks until it became the only tongue spoken on every ship in the old merchant fleets. It was beautiful the way working things are beautiful: no wasted grammar, every word load-bearing. And then the merchant fleets consolidated into the corporate cartels, and the cartels standardized on Vantori trade-common, and Kessari lost its last native generation the way a harbor loses its light when the port moves downstream.

By the time I was born, maybe a few hundred people alive could read it, and fewer could speak it, and my grandmother was one of them, and she refused—refused—to teach it to anyone.

She taught it to me anyway. That contradiction was the whole of her, if you want to know.

* * *

The supply circuit takes eleven days if the weather holds and nineteen if it doesn't. Vault to Relay Station Kestrel, Kestrel to the atmospheric processor farm at Low Basin, Low Basin to the ice field antennas, and home again with whatever the cargo drones have dropped at each stop. I run it alone because running it alone is the point. Two people on a route means two sets of tracks. One set of tracks means weather, or nothing, or a ghost.

Kestrel came up on the horizon at dusk, its dish array catching the low light like a hand cupped to catch rain. I parked the snowcat in the lee of the equipment shed and went through the ritual: exterior inspection, interior inspection, drone manifest, antenna alignment. The drones had brought protein stock, filter membranes, and a crate of books I'd ordered eight months ago through a purchasing channel so convoluted it might as well have been smuggling. Which, technically, it was. You cannot requisition poetry anthologies for a defunct research station without someone eventually asking questions, so I routed them through a geology survey budget. The lies fund the truth. My grandmother would have had opinions.

Inside the station, while the kettle worked, I checked the long-range receiver out of habit. Habit is most of what I have, so I indulge it.

Static on the standard bands. Static on the fleet bands—the Vantori channels hissing their encrypted hiss, the Ossuary frequencies doing their colder, tighter version of the same. The war was far away, the way a storm on the horizon is far away. You can watch lightning from a porch for years and tell yourself the porch is safe.

Then, on a frequency I did not expect anything on—an old band, a dead band, one of the Kessari-era maritime channels my grandmother's radio manuals said had been decommissioned before I was born—there was something that was not static.

It lasted four seconds. A carrier tone, then a burst of data, then nothing. My hands went still on the kettle handle, which is how I knew it mattered; I am not a person whose hands go still.

I ran it twice through the decoder. The first pass gave me garbage. The second gave me structure. Old-form packet headers, the kind my grandmother's manuals described with the fondness of someone describing a discontinued candy. And inside the packet, encoded in a compression scheme I recognized from chapter seven of a book I had read aloud to an empty room more times than I would ever admit—

Words. Kessari words.

I didn't decode them that night. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is going to be in short supply later in this account. I stood in the relay station kitchen with the kettle screaming behind me and I could not make myself touch the keyboard, because decoding them meant knowing, and knowing meant the world outside my circuit had reached into the one place I'd kept swept clean, and some cowardly animal part of me wanted the four seconds back. Wanted them to be ionospheric noise, a ghost, a trick of the cold.

The kettle screamed. I let it scream for a while. Then I made tea, sat down, and decoded the message, because I am my grandmother's granddaughter and cowardice in this family skips generations.

Four words, in old-form Kessari, repeated twice:

*Halcyon Steppe. Taking water. Failing.*

A ship's name. A distress shorthand older than either fleet—older than the war itself, probably, dredged up from some manual or some dying crewman's memory. *Taking water* was what ships said when the hull was compromised. *Failing* was what they said when life support was next.

I looked at those four words for a long time. Outside, the wind moved across the ice field and the antennas hummed their low note, the one I pretend is the station breathing.

Here is what I understood, standing in that kitchen: no one else alive reads old-form Kessari packet headers. Not the Vantori signals corps, not the Ossuary cryptographers, not the archivists who catalogued the language into extinction. Whoever sent that message sent it into a dead channel in a dead tongue, the way you'd shout into a well, and the only ear at the bottom of the well belonged to me.

And here is what I understood second, which is worse: the moment I answered, I would exist. Six years of nine names and one set of tracks, undone by a single transmission. Whatever answered that signal would be a person with a voice and a location, and voices and locations are exactly what wars collect.

I finished my tea. I logged Dr. Osei's thermal audit. I slept badly in the station bunk, dreaming of my grandmother correcting my pronunciation, which she did even in dreams, especially in dreams.

* * *

Morning came gray and enormous, the way it does here, the sky pressing down on the ice like a lid on a jar. I walked the antenna array in the blue half-light, checking guy wires, and let myself think it through properly, because the walk is where I do my thinking and the thinking is where I do my pretending that I'm still in control.

Option one: don't answer. Let the Halcyon Steppe be someone else's problem. Except there is no someone else; that's the entire architecture of my life. The message would sit in my decoder like a stone in a shoe, and I would know, forever, that I heard it and did nothing, and my grandmother—who once drove two hundred kilometers through a storm to deliver a translation she wasn't asked for, because "someone had to"—would haunt me with a vigor she had never managed in life.

Option two: answer, and be caught. Answer in Kessari and every cryptographer in two fleets starts asking who on this frozen rock speaks a dead trade language, and the answer leads to a seed vault with one set of tracks, and one set of tracks leads to one woman, and one woman is a very small thing to aim a war at.

Unless.

I stopped at the base of the main dish and looked up at it, this great metal ear tilted at the sky, and I felt the idea arrive the way ideas do—not politely, all at once, but like weather.

Unless the person who answered wasn't one woman. Unless the person who answered was exactly what the message needed and the fleets needed and, God help me, what I needed: a neutral arbiter. An institution. A rescue coordination office with staff and protocols and nine names, none of which were mine. The fiction I'd been maintaining for six years, the nine handwriting styles and the phantom rotations—it had all been theater performed for an audience of nobody. But audiences arrive. And I already knew every line.

My grandmother hid the language because the last time she translated for powerful people, they used her words as weapons and discarded her when the peace failed. I understand that now in a way I didn't at twelve, sitting cross-legged on her floor reciting declensions while she watched the door. She wasn't teaching me a language. She was teaching me how to survive being the only one who could hear.

But she also used to say—this is the part I keep circling, the part I haven't made peace with—that a language dies the day its last speaker decides silence is safer than speech.

The wind picked up. Somewhere out past the horizon, a ship named Halcyon Steppe was taking water and failing, and its people had shouted into the well, and the well was me.

I walked back to the station, thawed the snowcat's lines, and pointed it toward home. Eleven days back along the circuit, give or take weather. I had that long to decide whether to stay invisible or to answer.

Reader, we both know which one I'll choose. I chose it the moment my hands went still.

But that night, driving the causeway under a sky full of indifferent stars, I did not yet know that answering would cost me everything except the thing I couldn't live without—and I did not yet hear, faint on the dead bands beneath the static, the second signal that was already coming.
