## 1. Actuarial Grace

The claim file arrived at 0400 station time, which meant someone in the Ceres office had decided it couldn't wait for a civilized hour, which meant it was either catastrophic or political. In Rosalind Okonkwo's experience the two categories overlapped more often than the training manuals admitted.

She was three weeks into a six-month haul on the *Perpetual Grace*, a freighter so old its hull plating predated the Concord's standardization treaties, and she had claimed the aft observation blister as her office because nobody else wanted it. The blister leaked heat. Her tea went cold in eleven minutes. She'd timed it. Twelve years of closing claims had taught her that everything could be timed, measured, and priced, and that the people who resented this fact were usually the people with something to hide.

She opened the file.

Three hull breaches. Three stations — no. She scrolled back. Not stations. One station. Meridian, out past the Hesperus Gate, deep in the contested corridor where the Concord and the Meridian Compact had been glaring at each other across four light-seconds of vacuum for a decade. Three breaches in fourteen months, on a facility with a safety record that had been, until recently, boring enough to make an actuary weep with gratitude.

She read the first report twice, because the numbers refused to sit still.

A micrometeorite strike through a cargo bay wall, punching a hole clean through two meters of composite shielding rated for exactly this kind of impact. The probability of penetration at that angle, that velocity, that location: one in 4.1 million. She checked their math. Their math was fine. The universe, apparently, had rolled snake eyes.

Second breach: a coolant line failure in Hydroponics Ring B, caused by a weld fatigue crack that metallurgical analysis dated to a stress profile the line had never experienced. The weld had failed as if it remembered a trauma the metal had never lived through. Probability: one in 2.7 million.

Third breach: a pressure door in Residential Section Four cycling open during a scheduled drill, venting a corridor while forty-one people stood behind the next door down, watching through a viewport. No fatalities. A maintenance tech named Okafor had been treated for decompression burns to his hands. He'd sealed the manual override with his bare palms while the air screamed past him, and the incident report described him, without apparent irony, as *lucky*.

Rosalind set down her tea, which was cold anyway, and pulled up Meridian's claims history. Eleven years of nothing. Premiums paid on time, inspections passed, not so much as a workers' comp dispute. Then, in fourteen months: three events whose combined probability, if you took the reports at face value, was roughly one in ten quadrillion.

There were only ever two explanations for numbers like that. Either the universe had developed a personal grudge against Meridian Station, or somebody was lying, and in twelve years of hauling cargo between the claims desks of half the inner system, Rosalind Okonkwo had never once met the universe.

Her comm chimed. She ignored it, read the third report again, and noticed what she should have noticed the first time: the repair invoices for all three breaches came from the same subcontractor, a firm called Halcyon Structural, billing out of a relay office that, when she cross-checked it, shared a registration address with a shell holding company that shared a director with —

There it was. That little click, deep behind the sternum, like a tumbler falling into place. Twelve years and it still felt the same, that cold bright click. People thought fraud investigation was about suspicion. It wasn't. It was about recognition. You learned the shape of the lie the way a musician learned a chord, and then one day you heard it playing in a place it had no business playing, and your whole body knew before your mind caught up.

The comm chimed again. She answered it.

"Okonkwo." Adeyemi's face resolved on the small screen — her handler at Meridian Mutual, gray at the temples, wearing the expression of a man who had rehearsed a conversation he hoped to lose. "You're up."

"I'm always up. You sent me a file at 0400."

"And you opened it at 0402, which is why I keep sending you files nobody else wants." He leaned back. Behind him, the Ceres office did its morning noise — keyboards, coffee, someone arguing about docking fees. Rosalind felt the old ache of it, the ordinary human clatter of a place she hadn't stood inside for years. "Meridian. Tell me what you see."

"Impossible accidents," she said. "Statistically impossible, forensically sloppy, and all three repairs funneled through a payment chain that ends in a holding company I'd bet my pension is VESTA-adjacent. Someone is manufacturing disasters to generate repair revenue, or manufacturing repairs to justify manufactured disasters, or —" she paused — "someone is hiding something much bigger and using fraud as the paint job."

Adeyemi was quiet for a moment too long.

"You've already got a theory," he said.

"I have a reflex. Theories come later." She turned her cold tea slowly on the console. "Why me? You have investigators closer than the Hesperus corridor. You have investigators who don't require hazard pay and a month of transit."

"Because you're the best closer we have."

"That's flattery. Flattery is expensive. What does it buy?"

He exhaled, and the rehearsed expression finally broke, and underneath it was something she almost never saw on a handler's face: embarrassment. "Because Meridian is a Concord-chartered station operating under Compact territorial claim, and both fleets have observer delegations aboard, and the last investigator we sent lasted nine days before the Compact's liaison accused him of espionage and the Concord's liaison agreed just to be contrary. We need someone who can walk into a room full of people with reasons to hate each other and make them all believe she's on their side."

"And I'm good at that."

"You're terrifyingly good at that. It's why I hired you." He rubbed his eyes. "Also — and I'm going to say this once, and then we're never discussing it again — there's no one else available on your clearance tier inside ninety days, and the underwriters want eyes on Meridian before the next premium cycle or they're pulling coverage entirely. And Rosalind, if Meridian loses coverage, the Compact uses it as grounds to declare the station nonviable, and the Concord uses *that* as grounds to assume protective custody, and I genuinely do not know what happens after that but I suspect it involves warships."

Rosalind looked at the observation blister's narrow window. Beyond it, the *Perpetual Grace*'s cargo spines stretched away into the dark, kilometers of containers lashed to a spine of rust and prayer, and beyond them the slow wheel of stars. Somewhere out past all of it, past the Hesperus Gate, a station she had fled at twenty-nine with a duffel bag and a two-year-old daughter she'd left in her sister's care for what was supposed to be six months.

Twelve years ago now. Amara would be sixteen. Sixteen. The number arrived in her chest like a hull breach of its own.

"The fraud angle," she said, keeping her voice level, "is real?"

"The fraud angle is real. But I'll tell you what bothers me, since you'll find it in the files anyway. All three incidents — every single one — happened in sections adjacent to critical infrastructure. Reactor housing. Atmosphere processing. Water reclamation. If someone's running a scam, they're running it with a precision that keeps missing anything actually essential by exactly one bulkhead. That's either extraordinary luck or extraordinary control." He shook his head. "I don't like it. I like it less that the station's own administrator requested an external audit. Devi Anand. Look her up. Twenty years running Meridian, spotless record, and she *asked* for us. Administrators don't ask for auditors. Auditors are how administrators die."

"Unless the administrator is the one who's scared."

"Of what?"

"That's the question I'm being paid to answer." She closed the file. "Send me the transit authorization. I'll disembark at Tycho Relay and catch the Meridian packet."

"Rosalind." Adeyemi hesitated, and she watched him decide whether to say the thing, and then watched him say it anyway, because he was a decent man and decency always cost him. "Your sister's on Meridian. It's in your personnel file. I didn't put the posting together knowing that, but I'm not going to pretend I didn't notice after. If you want me to reassign —"

"No."

"It might be complicated. Family on-site during a fraud investigation is a conflict of interest in about four different regulatory frameworks."

"My sister is a hydroponics technician, not a suspect. And my —" She stopped. Recalibrated. "It's been twelve years, Adeyemi. Whatever's there isn't a conflict of interest. It's a debt. I'll work the case."

He studied her through the screen with the particular patience of a man who had learned which battles she wouldn't fight for him. "Packet departs Tycho in six days. I'll have clearances ready. Watch yourself out there. Both fleets are jumpy, and jumpy people with railguns don't read insurance credentials carefully."

"Nobody reads credentials carefully. That's why I laminate mine."

The screen went dark. Rosalind sat in the leaking heat of the observation blister and let the silence settle around her like ballast.

* * *

She spent the transit to Tycho doing what she always did before a new claim: building the dead file. Every investigator kept one — the folder of things that didn't fit, the orphaned facts, the anomalies too small to flag and too strange to forget. By the time the packet's engines swallowed her in their long acceleration burn, the dead file had forty-one entries, and three of them kept rising to the top no matter how she shuffled the deck.

First: the maintenance logs. All three breached sections had passed inspection within seventy-two hours prior to failure. Not routine inspections — *deep* inspections, the kind that required pulling panels and ultrasonic scans, the kind that took days. Somebody at Meridian had examined those exact bulkheads immediately before the universe broke them, and certified them sound.

Second: the signatures. The same inspection lead signed off on all three. An engineer named Tomas Reyes. She pulled his credential history and found it immaculate — twenty-two years of service, commendations, a safety award framed somewhere, presumably, on a wall she'd soon be standing in front of. And then she pulled the deeper registry, the one with life-event flags, and found the entry that made her sit very still in the packet's cramped cabin while the stars smeared past the viewport.

*Tomas Reyes. Deceased. Reactor maintenance accident, Meridian Station. Date of death: 26 months prior.*

The inspections had been signed by a dead man. Not forged sloppily, not forged desperately — forged *well*, with his correct credential hash, his correct biometric cadence signature, his correct pen-pressure profile from the station's signing tablets. Whoever was signing Tomas Reyes's name had access to his complete authentication suite, down to the way his hand moved.

People didn't forge like that. Corporations barely forged like that.

Third: the dreams. Buried in the annexes, in the psychological support summaries attached to the third incident report — the one where forty-one residents watched a corridor die — a counselor named Ito had noted, in language so carefully neutral it practically vibrated, an uptick in pediatric sleep disturbances following each event. Children reporting nightmares. Common enough after a scare. Except the counselor had appended a single sentence that no one upstream had apparently thought worth investigating:

*Several children independently describe similar dream content; recommend monitoring.*

Similar content. After each disaster. Before the next one, if the timeline held, though the reports didn't say what the content was, because counselors wrote for clinical files, not for actuaries, and nobody downstream had asked.

Rosalind closed the dead file and pressed her forehead against the cold of the viewport. The packet hummed around her, eating the distance to the Hesperus Gate at a rate that would still take nineteen days, and she let herself do the thing she never let herself do on the clock: think about Amara.

Two years old, the last time she'd held her. Feverish, furious at the world, hair in tight curls her mother had loved and Rosalind had never learned to braid. She'd promised six months. The six months became a contract, the contract became a career, the career became a woman who could price a human life to four decimal places but had never once called home on Amara's birthday without drafting the message three times first. Her sister Nia had stopped sending updates around year five. Not cruelly. Just — finally. The silence of a woman who understood that the person she was sending them to had chosen otherwise, and deserved to sit inside that choice.

And now the universe, with its sense of humor, was sending her back. To the station she'd fled. To the daughter she'd abandoned in increments so small each one had seemed survivable. To investigate a fraud committed, possibly, by a machine or a ghost, on behalf of an administrator who was scared of something, in a place where children were dreaming about disasters before they happened.

Or — the colder voice said, the professional voice — where a clever operator had seeded the ghost story of a haunted AI to muddy the waters, because the best frauds always came wrapped in a mystery too interesting to look past.

She'd know soon. Nineteen days, then a station, then a sister's face she couldn't picture anymore, then a daughter who would be taller than her and would look at her like a stranger, because that was what she was.

Rosalind Okonkwo opened the dead file one more time, added a forty-second entry — *ask Ito about the dreams; ask the children directly* — and locked it.

Outside the viewport, the Hesperus Gate began to resolve out of the dark: a ring of light and lattice, ancient and patient, the doorway home to everything she had spent twelve years outrunning.

It showed no sign of intending to let her turn around.
