## 1. The Garden of Unborn Seasons

The morning light came through the Verdance's long windows the way it always did: filtered, patient, a little gold at the edges where the old glass had begun to sag in its frames. Freya Sandoval rose before it, as she had for eleven years, and stood for a moment in the doorway of her quarters listening to the garden breathe.

That was not poetry. The garden breathed. The climate systems cycled on a slow tide, drawing air down through the root beds and exhaling it warm and green-smelling from the canopy vents, and if you stood very still in the upper terraces at first light you could feel the pressure shift against your skin like the flank of some enormous sleeping animal. She had stopped noticing it years ago, and then, somewhere around her fifth year alone, she had started noticing again — deliberately, the way you might make yourself say a name so you don't forget it.

She dressed in the grey work clothes that hung by the door, pulled her hair back, and took the pruning shears from their hook. The shears were older than she was. They had belonged to the second head gardener, whose name was on a brass plate in the tool room along with nine others, and every morning Freya touched the plate with two fingers without quite deciding whether it was habit or prayer.

The terraces descended in seven broad arcs from the central spire to the garden's southern rim, each one planted to a different stage of a season that would never fully arrive. That had been the design intent, according to the founding documents: *a demonstration of continuity*. Spring species on the top terrace so there was always something budding; high summer in the middle arcs, heavy with fruit that ripened and fell and rotted sweetly into the mulch; autumn below that, all rust and seed-heads; and at the bottom, the winter terrace, mostly conifers and pale bark and one stand of white birches that shed their leaves in a slow snowfall no one raked. A colony world turned beneath them, dead and brown and beautiful in its way, its ruined cities catching the dawn forty kilometers below like broken teeth in a jaw of dust.

Freya worked the fourth terrace first, as always. The plum trees there had been grafted from stock meant for orchards that were never planted on the surface — seeds catalogued for children who had never been born, the manifest said, though she had learned not to read the manifests too closely. She moved down the row with the shears, opening the canopy above each graft union, letting light and air into the centers. The cuts wept clear sap. In an hour the trees would seal themselves. Trees were good at that. Better than people.

"You'll outlive me," she told the nearest plum, the way she told it every third week or so, when the thought got heavy enough to need saying aloud. "All of you will. Someone else will prune you, or no one will, and you'll go on anyway." The tree said nothing, which was correct. She had long ago made peace with being the only voice in a place full of listeners.

Her boots left dark prints on the dew-wet stone path. Somewhere below, the irrigation system cycled with a sound like distant surf. Above everything, silent, the beacon antenna turned its slow arc against the black, keeping its appointment with the deep.

* * *

At 0900 station time — a time that mattered only because she had decided it should — Freya climbed to the operations room and performed the beacon check.

It was ritual, and she did not pretend otherwise. The Verdance's systems were self-regulating to a degree that made her daily inspections almost entirely ceremonial: the reactor reported to itself, the atmosphere balanced itself, the beacon transmitted on schedule with a fidelity no human hand could improve. But the founding crew had built the check into the station's culture — *the gardener verifies the signal*, the manual said, in handwriting that had been scanned and printed and framed — and Freya had come to understand why. Not because the machine needed witnessing. Because the gardener did.

She sat at the console, pressed her palm to the reader, and watched the status board bloom green.

**BEACON: NOMINAL.**
**CARRIER: LOCKED.**
**MESSAGE INTEGRITY: 100%.**
**NEXT TRANSMISSION WINDOW: 14H 22M.**

"Confirmed," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the framed handwriting, to whoever might someday read the log. "Beacon nominal. Carrier locked. Message integrity one hundred percent." Her voice sounded strange and official in the quiet, and she liked that, the way you like the smell of rain on a day you spend indoors. She typed the entry into the maintenance log with both hands, properly, the way the manual insisted, and initialed it.

*Day 4,187. All systems within tolerance. Garden healthy. Gardener healthy.*

She almost closed the session there. Instead, out of the small restless honesty that eleven years of solitude had sharpened rather than dulled, she ran the anomaly sweep — the routine that compared active system requests against the department registry. It was supposed to return nothing. It usually returned nothing.

Today it returned three lines.

**MAINT REQ 88-1141 — ROUTED: TERRAFORM ENGINEERING (DEPT. DEFUNCT)**
**MAINT REQ 88-1142 — ROUTED: COLONIST HEALTH SERVICES (DEPT. DEFUNCT)**
**MAINT REQ 88-1143 — ROUTED: SURFACE SURVEY OFFICE (DEPT. DEFUNCT)**

Freya frowned at the screen. Maintenance requests were generated automatically when a subsystem drifted out of tolerance, and they were routed to the department responsible for that subsystem. But Terraform Engineering had been dissolved decades ago — its functions absorbed into general station maintenance, which was her, which was one woman with shears and a soldering iron. Health Services had never operated aboard the Verdance at all. And Surface Survey —

Surface Survey had died with the colony.

She checked the timestamps. The requests had originated overnight, within minutes of one another, from subsystems she knew intimately: a humidity valve on terrace six, a filtration bank, a soil sensor array. All functioning fine. All reporting, somehow, to offices that had been graves for thirty years.

"Ghosts," she murmured. But she logged them, because she logged everything, and because the alternative was to decide they didn't matter, and she had promised herself she would never be the kind of caretaker who decided things didn't matter.

* * *

The rest of the morning went to the winter terrace, where the birches needed frost-cloth and the conifers wanted nothing but company. She ate lunch on the rim walk with the dead world turning beneath her, bread and preserved pear and a view that still, after four thousand days, could take her breath sideways. Below, the ruins caught the afternoon sun. Once, she knew, those cities had held two million people. Now they held wind.

It was on her way back, passing through the records annex on the third terrace, that she noticed the terminal again.

The annex held the station's archival displays — manifests, crew rosters, shipping schedules, the bureaucratic sediment of a colony that had believed in its own future hard enough to print it. Most of the terminals slept behind lock screens. One, the oldest, had never accepted any password but woke to a boot sequence nobody had ever explained to her.

Today, when she brushed the power stud with her sleeve, it came up faster than usual. And instead of the standard archive index, it resolved directly into a ship designation:

**VSV ALBATROSS — CREW MANIFEST — FINAL**

She knew every ship in the Verdance's records. She had read the manifests the way other people read novels, in her early years, hungry for names and faces. There was no *Albatross*. There had never been an *Albatross*. The designation matched nothing in the fleet registries she'd memorized, nothing in the dock schedules, nothing anywhere except this one stubborn terminal that booted to it like a dog turning in circles before lying down.

She had mentioned it once, years ago, in a query to Fleet Administration. The reply had taken eight months and consisted of a single line: *Terminal is legacy hardware. Designation is a placeholder. No action required.*

A placeholder. For thirty years.

She stood in the cool dimness of the annex, smelling paper and ozone, and looked at the word **FINAL**, and felt the garden's breathing behind her, slow and enormous and indifferent.

"Just once," she said quietly, "I'd like this station to do something boring."

She reached past the manifest to close the file, and her eye caught the crew list beneath it. Forty-two names. She had never bothered reading them — placeholders, presumably, ghost entries on a ghost ship, the bureaucratic equivalent of the birches shedding leaves no one raked.

But she was already reading. And near the bottom of the list, in the position where a junior survey officer would sit, was a name rendered in brighter text than the others, as if newly written, as if the terminal had just now gotten around to typing it:

**MARCHETTI, ELIN — SURVEY SPECIALIST, SECOND CLASS**

Freya went very still.

She had read every roster in this annex. She had alphabetized the memorial plaques in her head during insomniac nights. She knew, with the particular terrible intimacy of the solitary, every name this station had ever carried.

Elin Marchetti was not among them.

The terminal's cursor blinked after the name, patient as a planted seed. Outside, the beacon antenna completed another turn of its endless arc, faithful as ever, broadcasting its message to a universe that had stopped listening decades ago.

Or so she had believed.

Freya touched the name on the screen, and the glass was cold, and somewhere in the deep structure of the Verdance a system older than her tenure hummed on — routing requests to the dead, booting ships that never flew, and now, apparently, adding crew.

"Who are you?" she asked the terminal.

The cursor blinked.
