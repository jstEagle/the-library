## 1. Fifteen Years of Quiet

The cargo hauler *Kestrel's Debt* rode the dark between Kepler Station and the outer terraforming platforms like a woman who had learned not to be noticed, which is to say: quietly, and on a schedule so predictable it had become a kind of camouflage.

Lior Sandoval had been running the same route for fifteen years. Tuesday, she loaded atmospheric scrubbers and nitrogen stock from Bay Fourteen. Wednesday, she made the slow burn out to Platform Ceres-9, where the atmosphere processors chewed the thin air of Kepler-442c and exhaled something a human could breathe without tasting metal. Thursday, she came home. Friday, she slept until the station's artificial dawn had been up for three hours, and no one said a word, because no one expected anything else from her.

Predictability was the point. In the contested orbits—the shipping lanes where Halcyon Combine patrol cutters drifted like patient sharks, where the registry checks came without warning and the penalties for irregularity were written in years—you did not want to be interesting. Interesting got you boarded. Interesting got your manifests audited down to the serial numbers on your rivets. Interesting, in the worst years of the border disputes, got you a name on a list.

Lior had spent fifteen years becoming the least interesting woman on Kepler Station, and she was proud of the work in the way other people were proud of medals.

"Debt to Ceres traffic control," she said, thumbing the comm as the platform's docking arms swelled out of the black ahead of her, ringed in running lights the color of old bruises. "Incoming, one hauler, standard load, standard approach."

"Copy, *Debt*. You're cleared for Arm Three. Same as always, Sandoval."

"Same as always," she agreed, and felt the small, worn comfort of the phrase settle over her like a coat.

The platform grew in her viewport as she made her final corrections—thirty kilometers of scaffolding and habitat modules bolted around the great processor spines, breathing machines the size of cities. Somewhere below her, on the surface of the planet they were all slowly teaching to live, the first terraformed valleys were turning green. Not green the way Earth was green, the archive footage showed—nothing that extravagant—but green the way a promise is green. Lichen. Moss. Grass engineered to survive a sky still full of ammonia.

She would never walk in those valleys. That was the deal she had made with herself, the one that let her sleep: she hauled the machinery of the future and stayed out of the frame. The colonists who would actually live down there—colonists not yet born, most of them, children of children still in school—would inherit valleys she had fed with her cargo and never seen.

It was enough. She told herself it was enough, and after fifteen years, the telling had mostly become true.

* * *

Kepler Station smelled like recycled air and hydroponic fertilizer, and to Lior it smelled like safety.

She docked the *Debt* at 1940 station time, signed her manifests with the biometric stamp that made her just another line in the Combine's logistics ledgers, and took the tram from the cargo ring down to the habitat levels, past the market stalls where vendors sold protein cakes and bootleg music chips and, on the corner near the tram stop, a rack of cheap printed flags for the upcoming Founding Day celebrations. The flags showed the seven founding families' crests arranged in a circle around a stylized sun. Wren's ship-and-spear sat at the top, as it always did.

She didn't look at them. Fifteen years of practice went into not looking at things.

The garden was on Level Six, in what had once been a water reclamation bay—two hundred meters of curved ceiling and old plumbing that the station engineers had decommissioned a generation ago and never quite gotten around to demolishing. Lior had found it her first year on Kepler, when she'd been looking for somewhere to be alone, and she had never really left.

The garden was not hers, exactly. That was the first thing she'd learned, from the old woman who had kept it before her—a botanist named Ama Sefu, dead now eleven years, who had explained it the way you'd explain gravity.

"It's not a garden, girl. It's a *loan*. Everything in it belongs to people who haven't been born yet. We're just the hands it passes through."

Lior pushed through the pressure door at 2015, and the smell hit her the way it always did—wet soil, growing things, the faint green sweetness of chlorophyll working under grow-lights tuned to a spectrum that made everything look the color of memory. Rows of planting beds curved away into the dark ends of the bay. Fruit vines climbed the old reclamation pipes. A stand of dwarf wheat hissed softly in the ventilation current, and beyond it, the nursery trays: thousands of seedlings in their gel pods, each one labeled in a small, careful hand.

*Kepler-442c Valley 3, batch 12. Kepler-442c Valley 7, batch 4. Reserve stock. Reserve stock. Reserve stock.*

She walked the rows the way she did every evening, checking moisture sensors, pruning where the vines had gotten ambitious, talking to the plants in a low voice because Ama Sefu had sworn they grew better for it and Lior had never found a reason to argue with a dead woman's methods.

"Evening," she told the wheat. "Evening, all of you."

The garden was the generational project—officially, the Kepler Foundational Flora Initiative, funded by a Combine grant that had been renewed every five years mostly because nobody senior enough to kill it remembered it existed. Its purpose, per the grant language, was to maintain viable seed stock and cultivated specimens for the future surface colonies. Which was true. It was also, and Lior knew this better than anyone, something else. A place where the future was kept alive in the present tense. A promise you could put your hands in.

She stopped at the nursery trays and lifted one of the gel pods to the light. Inside, a seedling of old-Earth apple—*Malus domestica*, a variety called Ashmead's Kernel that had gone extinct on Earth itself two hundred years ago—unfurled its first true leaves. Someday, decades from now, a child born in a valley that didn't exist yet would bite into one of its fruit and never know there had been a time when the whole species had hung by a thread in a dark room, tended by strangers.

"You'll make it," she told the seedling. "That's the whole arrangement. You make it, and you don't have to know my name."

She said it lightly. She always said it lightly. But her chest did the thing it did, the small tightening, the old ache with the old shape, and she put the pod back in its cradle and moved on down the row before it could become anything she'd have to think about.

* * *

The guilt had a schedule too, like everything in her life.

It came in the quiet hours—after the garden, after the cheap meal in the crew mess, after Dev had gone to his bunk with his tablet and his conspiracy feeds and his cheerful, oblivious humming. It came when the station's night cycle dimmed the corridors to amber and the only sound was the ventilation, breathing its endless artificial breath.

*You could have stayed. You could have learned it properly. You could have kept it alive instead of letting it die in your mouth.*

She lay in her bunk and looked at the ceiling and let the thought arrive, because fighting it had never worked, and after it had said its piece it would leave, the way it always did, and she would sleep.

It was about her grandmother. It was always about her grandmother.

Mirela Sandoval had died fourteen years ago, in a hospice module on the inner colony of Thessaly, two hundred thousand kilometers and a whole lifetime away from Kepler Station. Lior had been twenty-three, newly licensed, hungry for a hauler contract that would get her off Thessaly and out into the black where nobody asked questions. She had visited the hospice twice in the last year. Twice. She could still see the room—the narrow bed, the window looking out on the colony's dome-lit fake dusk, her grandmother's hands folded on the blanket, hands that had kneaded bread and braided hair and once, in a kitchen on Thessaly with the door locked, had taught a small girl a song in a language the girl was never allowed to speak anywhere else.

*"Sing it only in this room, Liorika. Only here. Promise me."*

She had promised. And then she had grown up, and the promises of children are made of different material than the promises of adults, and somewhere in the years of licenses and cargo runs and careful, careful anonymity, she had stopped singing it even in her own head. The language had gone quiet in her the way a stream goes quiet when the winter freezes it—not gone, never gone, just locked under the surface, waiting.

She had meant to go back. She had meant to visit a third time, and a fourth, and to sit by that bed and ask all the questions she had never asked—*who were the Veyans really, Grandmother, what really happened at the massacre, why do you flinch when the Founding Day flags go up*—and then the contract offer had come, the good one, the Kepler run, and she had told herself she'd visit after the first year. After she was established. After things were settled.

Mirela Sandoval had died in the spring of that first year, and Lior had found out by message, eleven days late, because she'd been out on a haul and hadn't checked her personal queue.

She had not gone home for the funeral. She had told herself it was the schedule. The *Kestrel's Debt* had a Tuesday loading, and the route was her armor, and armor cannot be taken off for funerals.

Fifteen years later, she knew the truth, in the way you know things at three in the morning: she hadn't gone because going home meant the questions, and the questions meant the past, and the past was the one cargo she had spent her whole adult life refusing to carry.

The thought finished its rounds and left, as it always did. Lior closed her eyes.

Outside her porthole, Kepler-442c turned below, half its face still raw rock and poison sky, the other half veined with the green of the first valleys. The future, arriving one day at a time.

She slept.

* * *

Thursday was the *Debt*'s maintenance day, which meant Lior spent it in the cargo bay with an inspection checklist and Dev Achterberg, her loadmaster of nine years, who was currently lying on his back under the number two cargo clamp with his legs sticking out into the aisle and his opinions sticking out even further.

"I'm just saying," Dev said, voice echoing off the deck plates, "the Combine doesn't run patrol cutters on the Ceres lane because of *pirates*. There's no pirates. There's never been pirates. There's nothing out here to steal except fertilizer and air scrubbers. They're watching for something else."

"They're watching because watching is what they do," Lior said, running a diagnostic on the clamp's hydraulic line. "It's a big orbit. Somebody has to be bored in it."

"Bored. Sure. Or they're looking for smugglers. Or dissidents. Or—" he rolled out from under the clamp, upside down, grinning at her, a wiry man of fifty with a beard he trimmed into deliberate disrepute, "—or they don't even know what they're looking for, which is worse. You ever notice how the Founding Day flags went up two weeks early this year? Two weeks. Somebody at Combine HQ wants everybody in a patriotic mood before the anniversary."

"The anniversary of the Veyan Massacre," Lior said, flat, not looking up from her diagnostic. "Yes. I noticed."

She hadn't meant to say it that way. Two hundred years since the Veyan transports went down—every schoolchild learned the story, the terrorist fleet, the brave founding families, the terrible necessity. Every schoolchild learned to say *massacre* with the right somber tone and the right approved facts. But something in the way she'd said it just now had a different weight, and Dev heard it. She saw him hear it. His grin faded a degree, and he looked at her a half-second too long, and she felt the old reflex rise in her throat like bile—the reflex to smooth it over, to laugh, to be exactly as interesting as a loadmaster's employer required and no more.

"Anyway," she said, before he could ask, "clamp two's seals are good. Check three and we can sign off."

"Right." He rolled back under the clamp. The moment passed, the way moments always passed, because Dev was a good man and not a curious one, and Lior exhaled slowly and told herself the armor had held.

It had held for fifteen years. It would hold for fifteen more. That was the plan. That was the whole plan: keep the route, keep the garden, keep the silence, grow things for people who would never know her name, and take the past with her into the ground unopened, the way her grandmother had, the way everyone she had ever loved had, because some doors were better left locked and some songs were better left unsung.

* * *

Friday morning, station time, the *Kestrel's Debt* sat in Bay Fourteen with an empty hold, waiting for the Tuesday cycle to begin again. Lior came down to do her pre-load walk-through at 0700, coffee in hand, and found the bay quiet in the way she loved—just the hum of the dock systems and the slow blink of status lights and, far off, the station's morning announcements murmuring through the hull about Founding Day events and Combine shipping advisories.

She was halfway up the ramp when the ship's comm panel chimed.

Not the dock channel. Not the Combine traffic net. The panel chimed on the open emergency-receive frequency—the one every ship monitored by law, the one that was supposed to carry distress calls, maydays, the raw voices of people in trouble.

Lior set down her coffee and thumbed the receiver.

Static. Long, ugly static, the kind that meant the transmission had come from far away or from something with a dying transmitter. And underneath the static, a voice.

She almost dismissed it as noise. Almost. But there was a rhythm to it, a rise and fall, and the rhythm snagged on something buried so deep in her that she felt it in her sternum before her brain caught up—a cadence, a music, the shape of words she had not heard spoken aloud in fourteen years, not since a locked kitchen on Thessaly, not since a woman's hands folded on a hospice blanket, not since a lullaby sung only in one room, only here, *promise me*—

Old Veyan.

The voice on the frequency was weak and repeating, a distress pattern, and it was speaking a language that had been extinct for two hundred years, a language with perhaps a dozen living speakers left in all the colonies, and one of them was standing alone in a cargo bay at seven in the morning with her coffee going cold and her armor cracked clean down the middle.

Lior stood very still. The status lights blinked. The station murmured its announcements. And somewhere out in the dark beyond the contested orbits, something that should not exist was calling in her grandmother's voice, over and over, and waiting to be answered.
