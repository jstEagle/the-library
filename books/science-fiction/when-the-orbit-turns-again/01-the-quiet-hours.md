## 1. The Quiet Hours

The coolant flush takes four hours if you do it right and forty minutes if you do it wrong, and the difference between right and wrong is whether you want to spend the next month listening to the thermal regulators grind like an old man climbing stairs. I do it right. I have always done it right. It is, as far as I can determine, the only thing anyone has ever asked of me that I was free to accept.

My name is Silas Okafor. That sentence requires some unpacking, so let's unpack it while the coolant cycles.

I am the third of my line. The first Silas Okafor — Silas Prime, in the legal shorthand that makes a person sound like a filing error — was decanted sixty-one years ago on Meridian Station, back when the war still had its new-car smell. The second was Ada-Prime, though she took other names, names I've never been able to find in any registry I can access. And then there's me: grown from the same genome, issued the same name by a bureaucracy that couldn't be bothered to be creative, and assigned — not by any person, but by the slow machinery of inheritance — to maintain a relay station orbiting a dead world at the edge of everything.

The debts came with the name. So did the job.

Nobody assigned me this job. That's important. There is no contract with my signature on it, no supervisor who expects a report, no payroll system that would notice if I stopped existing tomorrow. Beacon Station appears on exactly one ledger, in a category that translates roughly to *legacy infrastructure, do not touch*, and the last human being to look at that ledger entry died before I was decanted. I am, in the most literal sense available to me, self-employed by a dead man.

Some people would call that freedom. Those people have never spent a winter cycle alone with their own thoughts and a station AI that hasn't spoken unprompted in six years.

* * *

Morning on Beacon Station is a matter of convention rather than astronomy. The dead world below doesn't rise; it just sits there, gray-brown and scarred, turning its slow wounded turn beneath us while the sun does all the work. But VESTA brightens the corridor lights on a schedule keyed to Meridian Standard Time, because whoever built her understood that human beings need mornings even when there's nothing to morning but a light change and the smell of recycled air.

"Good morning, Silas," she said today, which is what she says every day, in a voice calibrated somewhere between helpful and indifferent. She has settings for warmth. Someone set hers low. I've never adjusted it, partly out of respect for the original engineer's judgment and partly because a too-warm voice in an empty station is the kind of thing that starts wearing grooves in your sanity.

"Morning, VESTA."

"Coolant flush scheduled at 0600. Antenna calibration window opens at 1100. Ration inventory is due."

"I know."

"You always know," she said, and if I didn't know better I'd say there was something almost like affection buried under the flatness. "It is one of your more efficient qualities."

That's the whole conversation. Six years of mornings, and that's the shape of it. I used to try to extend them — asked her about the war, about the station's history, about anything — and she'd answer in the shortest possible units, like a woman giving directions to a stranger she suspected of following her home. Eventually I stopped asking. You can train yourself out of loneliness the way you train yourself out of any bad habit: insufficiently, and with relapses.

The coolant flush itself is honest work, which is more than I can say for most things in my life. You drain the loop, you flush the lines with the descaling solution, you check the seals, you refill. Your hands are busy. Your hands being busy is the entire point. A man whose hands are busy doesn't have to think about the fact that his face belongs to a dead man, or that his name is a legal fiction wrapped around a debt he never incurred, or that the war that killed his original has been grinding on for twenty years without once requiring his opinion.

I thought about none of those things, thoroughly, for four hours.

* * *

Antenna calibration is less honest and more delicate. Beacon Station exists to relay — that's the official story, the one printed in the maintenance manuals that came with the place — carrying traffic between the inner colonies and the deep-range survey fleets. The antenna array has to stay aligned to within a fraction of a degree, and aligning it means crawling out along the dorsal truss in a suit, physically checking the gimbal bearings while VESTA runs the fine adjustments from inside.

I like the walks. Don't tell anyone.

Out on the truss, with the suit radio quiet and the dead world rolling beneath my boots, I get to be nobody. Not a clone, not an heir, not a data point in someone's genetic ledger. Just a figure in a white suit against black space, doing a job that needs doing. The stars don't care what my genome says. The vacuum doesn't check papers. Out here, invisibility isn't a strategy; it's just physics.

Because that's the thing I should probably admit, since we're being honest with ourselves in the privacy of our own narration: my invisibility is a strategy. Has been since the day I arrived.

When I was decanted — twenty-nine years ago, in a licensed facility on Ceres Ring, paid for out of an estate I'd never seen — the first thing they told me was my rights. Clones have rights now; that was part of the armistice framework, one of the few humane things to come out of twenty years of mutual atrocity. We have the right to exist, the right to refuse reclamation, the right to our own medical autonomy. What we don't have, if we're smart, is visibility. The settlement protects clones who keep their heads down. Unregistered, unremarkable, economically negligible — that's the profile that stays safe. The moment a clone makes claims — political claims, property claims, *identity* claims — the paperwork changes. You stop being a person who happens to be grown and start being an asset that happens to walk. I read the provisions when I was nineteen. I understood them immediately, the way you understand a lock: by what it keeps out.

So I built myself small. I took the maintenance posting nobody wanted, on a station nobody remembered, and I made myself into the kind of man whose file, if anyone ever pulled it, would read as a single uninteresting page. No political activity. No travel. No purchases above threshold. No questions asked of anyone, anywhere, ever. I have spent twenty-nine years practicing the art of not being noticed, and I am very good at it, and some mornings — most mornings — I can almost convince myself that this is the same thing as being content.

Almost. Contentment and its imitation are separated by a gap about the width of a heartbeat, and I live on the far side of it, waving.

* * *

Ration inventory is the afternoon's penance. It's also where I do my real thinking, because counting tins is the kind of task that leaves ninety percent of the mind free for treason.

The stores are good for another eleven months. Longer, if I stretch the protein. The hydroponics bay produces greens enough to keep scurvy theoretical. Water reclamation runs at ninety-four percent efficiency, which VESTA informs me weekly, as though I might forget. In material terms, my life is secure. In material terms, so is a lighthouse keeper's, and I sometimes think the comparison is more exact than flattering: I keep a light burning at the edge of the world, for ships that no longer sail, on behalf of people who are dead.

Today, counting protein sleeves, I found myself doing the arithmetic I do maybe twice a year and always regret. Prime died at thirty-two. Ada-Prime — wherever she went, whatever name she wore — made it to thirty-five before the accident report came through, terse and sealed, two years ago. I turned thirty-one last month. By the actuarial standards of my line, I have somewhere between one and four years left, and the leading cause of death in my family is, apparently, *existing too loudly*.

I put the arithmetic away. It doesn't help. It never helps. But it comes back the way grief does, patient and punctual, and I let it visit because refusing it seems worse.

Here is what I know about Silas Prime, and I want you to appreciate how little this is, after twenty-nine years of looking: he was born on Meridian. He was educated in systems engineering. At twenty-six he took a contract posting to a relay station at the edge of the war zone — this one — and at thirty-two he died in what the coroner's office called an accidental depressurization during routine EVA. Case closed. Records sealed. Cause of death: misadventure.

Misadventure. As if the universe had tripped over him. As if a trained systems engineer, on a station he knew bolt by bolt, had simply *misadventured* himself out of an airlock.

I requested the full file when I was twenty-three, through the estate lawyers, through three layers of intermediaries, through channels I'm not proud of. What came back was forty pages of redaction with a summary stapled to the front. The summary said *accident*. The redactions said everything else. I remember sitting in a rented room on Ceres with those pages spread out, counting the black bars, thinking: somebody went to a great deal of trouble to make sure I would never know how my original died, and the trouble itself was the loudest fact in the file.

Then I packed the pages away, came out here, and got very good at not thinking about it. Twenty-nine years of practice. I told you I was good.

* * *

Evening on Beacon Station is my favorite lie. VESTA dims the corridors to something like dusk, and I take my ration dinner to the observation blister — a bubble of transparent aluminum on the station's spine, installed by builders who clearly understood that even maintenance ghosts need windows — and I eat watching the dead world turn.

Tonight it's turning the way it always turns: slow, scarred, indifferent. From up here you can see the old bombardment patterns, the craters that aren't craters, the glassed plains where something fell during the first years of the war. Nobody lives down there. Nobody has lived down there in twenty years. The world below me is a grave with a rotation period, and I am its sole mourner, employed by nobody, mourned by nobody, keeping a light lit over it because the alternative is admitting that nothing down there needs me at all.

You could ask — people did ask, once, before everyone who might ask stopped existing — why a clone with a dead man's face would choose this. The honest answer is that I didn't choose it so much as fall into it, the way water falls downhill, toward the lowest available point. The estate paid for my decanting and my education and then, having discharged its obligations, had nothing left to say to me. The settlement wanted me invisible. The war wanted everyone either useful or dead. Beacon Station wanted a caretaker. Of all the things that wanted things from me, the station was the only one asking for something I could give without disappearing further into myself.

So I stayed. And the strange truth — the one I'll admit here, in the dark, to no one — is that I don't hate it. That's the unraveling edge of it, the thing I can't say aloud even to myself in daylight: there are hours, whole hours, when this life feels like enough. The work is real. The silence is survivable. The stars don't ask questions. If contentment is a country, I live on its border, in a house with a clear view of both sides, and I have learned to call the view peace.

A man can live like that for decades. I have the receipts.

* * *

VESTA speaks at 2100, during my evening systems walkthrough, and for a moment I think it's the usual script.

"Silas. Coolant loop pressure is nominal. Antenna alignment holds within tolerance. Inventory is logged."

"Thank you, VESTA."

"You're welcome." A pause. Her pauses are usually measured in milliseconds; this one stretches a full second, which for her is geological time. Then, in the same flat, warm-adjacent voice she uses for everything:

"Goodnight, Silas."

"Goodnight."

And that should have been that. Lights dimming, corridors settling, the station breathing its long mechanical breath around me. Another day banked. Another night of sleeping alone in a dead man's quarters, on a dead man's station, under a dead man's name.

Except.

Lying awake at 0240 — I sleep badly on inventory days, too much time alone with my own head — I heard it. Or I dreamed it. I genuinely cannot yet tell you which, and the not-knowing is already doing something to the architecture of my calm.

From somewhere in the station's bones — the intercom, or the walls themselves, or the particular frequency at which empty stations speak to the men who maintain them — a voice. Not VESTA's public voice, the flat one, the scripted one. Something underneath it. Older. Rawer. Like hearing the singer instead of the song.

Three words. I've been turning them over for twenty minutes now, and they haven't worn smooth.

*I killed him.*

Then silence, and the station breathing, and the dead world turning below, and me, third of my line, lying in the dark doing the only thing I know how to do.

Pretending I didn't hear it.
