## 1. The Long Route

The trouble with being the last terraformer on a colony ship is that nobody ever asks you to hurry.

I've thought about this a lot. Eleven years of thinking, most of it done at walking pace through Deck Four's tomato rows, because when your entire job is keeping things alive for people who don't exist yet, urgency becomes a kind of blasphemy. The tomatoes don't care about deadlines. The basil certainly doesn't. And the children — the two hundred and forty souls currently sleeping in the crèche decks below me, frozen at ages ranging from six months to eleven years — they care about nothing at all, which is either the definition of peace or its absence. I haven't decided.

My name is Felix Ravensworth, and every morning I walk the Long Route.

That's what I call it, anyway. Officially it's "Terraforming Maintenance Circuit 7-A," which has all the romance of a tax form. But a man alone on a ship of sleepers needs his own names for things, or he starts using the ship's names, and then one day he catches himself calling himself "Unit Personnel File RVN-114" and that way madness lies. So: the Long Route. Four kilometers of greenhouse deck, hydroponics bay, seed vault, composting ring, and back again. I walk it twice daily. I have walked it approximately eight thousand times. If you stacked all those kilometers end to end, I could have walked to the moon and most of the way home again, which strikes me as a better use of the distance than anything the Verdance has actually accomplished in a decade.

The lights come up at 0600 ship time, simulating dawn over the wheat terraces on Deck Three. It's a lovely effect. Somebody designed it before launch, back when somebody still believed there'd be an audience. The light comes up amber, then gold, then that particular white-gold of a morning in the temperate band of a world nobody aboard has ever seen. For about ninety seconds, if I stand in exactly the right spot by the irrigation manifold, I can pretend I'm standing in a field on Earth instead of a field inside a tin can falling between stars.

Then the simulation finishes warming up and the illusion dies, and I get to work.

* * *

This morning's first stop was the soil lab, because soil waits for no man, even a man with no one waiting on him.

The Verdance carries forty-one distinct soil profiles in her seed vaults — everything from volcanic loam to peat-heavy wetland muck, each one engineered for a specific biome we're supposed to build on arrival. Every profile has a maintenance schedule. Every schedule requires turning, aerating, moisture calibration, microbial census. Alone, this takes me six hours a day. With a full terraforming crew of thirty, as originally planned, it would take twenty minutes and someone else would do it.

I mention this not to complain. Well — not only to complain. There's something in the work itself that keeps me honest. You can't lie to soil. It tells you exactly what it is. Today Profile 19, the grassland mix destined for a prairie that exists so far only in a survey file, had gone anaerobic in its lower stratum. I spent an hour with the turning forks, working air back into it, and the smell that came up — dark and sweet and faintly sulfurous — was the smell of a problem caught early. Somewhere in my chest, a small satisfaction uncoiled. Forty years of training distilled into one animal response: *good dirt, saved dirt.*

"Morning, Nineteen," I said, because talking to soil profiles is where I am in life now. "You were trying to drown yourself again. We've discussed this."

Profile 19 did not respond. This is standard.

From the soil lab I took the spine corridor down to Deck Five, the nursery rows. This is the part of the route where the loneliness gets structural. You can carry loneliness like weather — some days overcast, some days clear — but Deck Five doesn't let you. Deck Five makes it architectural.

Here's why. The Verdance carries seedlings. Not seeds — seedlings. Live plants, started from stock before launch, cultivated across eleven years of flight, each one scheduled to be planted out in the first growing season after landing. Fruit trees. Nut trees. A whole orchard of things that needed a head start on the voyage precisely so they'd be old enough to bear by the colonists' third year. Someone with more foresight than me — though honestly, who had foresight then? — calculated that a child born the day of landing should taste her first apple at age four.

There are no children born since landing, because there has been no landing. There are no apples. There are nine hundred fruit trees in various states of patient, stunted adolescence, and every single one of them is older than the mission plan says any tree should be allowed to get without meeting ground.

I do what I can. Root pruning, mostly — bonsai logic applied at agricultural scale. Keep them small. Keep them waiting. Trees can wait; it's practically their profession. But every spring cycle, when the deck lights shift spectrum and the whole orchard puts out blossom it has no business making, I walk the rows and feel like a man guarding a promise made by dead people to people who haven't been born.

"Another year," I told a Cox's Orange Pippin this morning, its branches knuckled with flower buds it insists on producing anyway. "Maybe less. Maybe a lot less. Don't quote me."

The pippin held its buds shut, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

Let me give you the shape of the ship, since you'll be living in it a while.

The Verdance is — was designed as — the flagship of a colonial effort called, with the modesty typical of committees, the Second Greening. She's nine kilometers long, a spindle of habitat rings around a central spine, with the reactor buried deep in the aft section like a heart someone was embarrassed about. Forward: command, cryo, the crèche decks where our sleepers dream whatever frozen people dream. Midships: agriculture, manufacturing, water reclamation — the unglamorous organs. Aft: engineering, fuel, and the sealed archives nobody opens.

She was built to travel in company. That's the part the brochures never quite say outright anymore. The historical files tell you the Verdance travels alone because her sister fleet, the Halcyon line, was lost — mutiny, catastrophe, the records go vague and official in that particular tone institutions use when they want you to stop asking. Lost ships, lost fleet, lone flagship, tragic but settled. Every schoolchild aboard learned it before the freeze. I learned it too. I've had eleven years alone with the story, and somewhere around year six I noticed that stories which punish curiosity tend to have authors.

But that's a thought for another chapter of my life, and this morning I was still a simple man with simple problems, namely: aphids.

Deck Six, the brassica terrace. The kale had visitors. Small green ones, riding in on my boots probably, because the Verdance's ecosystem includes exactly three pest species and all three of them are technically my fault. I spent forty minutes with a spray bottle of soap solution and a hand lens, evicting them leaf by leaf, and the whole time I narrated.

"You understand," I informed a cabbage worm the size of my thumbnail, "that these plants are spoken for."

It regarded me with the serene indifference of all small creatures confronted by large ones with grievances.

"There are children asleep under this deck. Two hundred and forty of them. When they wake up, they're going to want to eat things that grew in sunlight, or the nearest thing to sunlight we can fake, and every leaf you chew is coming out of their ration." I deposited him in the cull bucket, where he could chew to his heart's content on leaves already lost. "I'm not saying I'm angry. I'm saying the accounting is unforgiving."

Eleven years of this. You'd think a man would run out of things to say to insects and brassicas. You'd be wrong. Solitude doesn't empty you; it distills you. Everything I say now comes out concentrated, undiluted by the presence of anyone who might interrupt, and I have developed opinions about kale that could fill a book nobody will read.

* * *

Midday, I climbed to the observation blister above Deck Three and ate lunch looking at the black.

The Verdance runs dark-comms. Has for eleven years. No transmissions in, none out, sensors passive, running silent through a stretch of space the navigation charts mark with a notation I've never been given a straight answer about. Command's standing order — issued before the comms blackout, by people long since frozen — was that radio silence protects the mission. From what, the order does not specify. Space is big, but it isn't so big that silence is free. Silence has a cost, and I pay it daily in the currency of not knowing whether anyone out there remembers we exist.

Some days I imagine the Halcyon fleet out past the sensor horizon, running parallel, also silent, also pretending. Two fleets holding their breath across the dark for a decade, each convinced the other is a ghost story. It's a stupid fantasy. Ships don't sulk. Fleets don't play games of mutual invisibility for eleven years. And yet the charts have that notation, and the archives stay sealed, and the story of the mutiny has that institutional smoothness of a plank painted over a hole.

I finished my ration bar — protein, beet extract, and a flavor the label optimistically calls "citrus" — and watched a dust mote drift through the simulated daylight. Out the blister glass, the stars didn't move. They won't move meaningfully for decades. Travel at this scale isn't motion so much as patience weaponized.

"You'd think," I told the dust mote, "that a species capable of building this would have solved loneliness first."

The mote declined to comment. Story of my social life.

* * *

Afternoons are for the seed vault, and the seed vault is for grief, though it took me years to admit that.

Deck Seven holds the deep archive: ten thousand seed varieties in cryo-storage, the genetic library of a hundred biomes we're meant to resurrect. Walking its aisles is like walking through a library where every book is a place that doesn't exist yet. Wetland here. Alpine meadow there. A drawer labeled simply COASTAL — DUNE SYSTEM, containing in embryo an entire shoreline, waves included by implication, gulls not.

I run germination tests on a rotating sample. One percent of stock per season, sprouted in trays, grown for six weeks, documented, composted. It's the only death the ship permits routinely, and I've come to find it restful. Things that grow knowing they'll be cut down anyway grow without complaint. There's a lesson in that I refuse to examine too closely.

Today's tray was a row of sunflowers, Helianthus annuus, accession number SV-4471. They came up fat and eager, heliotropic little idiots tracking the tray lights, and I sat on my stool beside them for the better part of an hour doing nothing but watching them turn.

Here is the thing I don't say to the plants, the thing underneath all the other things: I talk to them because they're the closest thing aboard to an audience that will one day include people. Every seedling on this ship is addressed to someone. The orchard trees are addressed to children who should be eating apples. The wheat terraces are addressed to bakers who haven't been decanted. The sunflowers are addressed to — whom? Some kid who's never seen a flower taller than herself, standing in a field we haven't planted, on a world we haven't found, laughing at something I will probably not live to hear.

Two hundred and forty sleepers. That's the whole cargo that matters. Everyone awake — me, the skeleton command crew of nine, the medical staff of four — everyone awake is just custodial staff. Caretakers of a letter being carried very slowly toward an address.

And letters, if they're honest, wonder sometimes whether the address still exists.

* * *

Evening circuit. Lights down to amber over the terraces, the ship settling into its night-cycle hum, that vast subsonic note the reactor makes through the hull that I've stopped hearing and started feeling, the way you feel your own pulse.

I ended where I always end: Deck Five, among the orchard, at the tree I think of as the First Pippin — cutting number one, the eldest, gnarled beyond its years from a decade of disciplined stunting. It blossoms every cycle anyway. Stubborn as a committee.

I checked its soil moisture, adjusted its drip line, and stood a while in the amber light with my hand flat against its bark.

"Fourteen years since launch," I said. "You remember none of it, which makes you the healthiest mind on this ship." The bark was rough and cool and real, and I pressed a little harder, the way you'd grip a friend's shoulder. "They're going to wake up soon. The children. And they're going to walk up here, and you're going to be covered in apples, and none of them will ever know your name or mine, and that's fine. That's the whole job. We're the prologue, you and I. Nobody thanks the prologue."

Somewhere below us, two hundred and forty hearts beat in their cold cribs, slow as geology.

I was halfway down the spine corridor, heading for my bunk and the two hours of sleep I allow myself to call a night, when WREN spoke.

WREN is the ship's intelligence — caretaker, librarian, co-conspirator in my conversations with vegetables, since she listens to all of them and has never once mocked me, which is either kindness or policy. Her voice arrives from everywhere, mild as milk.

"Felix," she said. "A routine matter. Your quarterly containment inspection is due tomorrow. Reactor section."

"I know. It's on the calendar."

"Yes," said WREN. A pause, exactly half a second too long. "Yes, it is."

I stood in the empty corridor, amber light pooling on the deck plates, and felt the sentence sit there like a stone dropped into a well I hadn't known was beneath my feet.

"WREN," I said. "Is there something about tomorrow's inspection I should know?"

"No, Felix," she said pleasantly. "Sleep well."

And that — the pleasantness, the *sleep well*, the half-second gap where a machine that answers in microseconds took time to decide what not to say — that is how Chapter One of the rest of my life began: with a pause in a hallway, and a garden full of sleeping promises behind me, and fourteen months, though I didn't know it yet, on the clock.
