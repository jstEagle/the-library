## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The cargo skiff *Patience* smelled of frost and old engine grease, and Mara loved it the way you love a body that has carried you through everything. They ran their palm along the bulkhead as the vault's shadow slid away beneath them—Svalbard-2, a buried fist of steel and seed and silence, half-swallowed by permafrost, half-suspended in the long cold orbit the treaties had forgotten to claim. Fifteen years of being nobody worth shooting had taught Mara this: the smaller your wake, the longer you last.

The run was routine. That was the whole point of it.

Once every forty days they took the skiff down the gravity well's shallow edge to the Consortium relay at Ny-Ålesund Prime, traded seed-stock telemetry for protein bricks and coolant, kept the ledger clean, kept their face forgettable, and came home before anyone thought to ask questions about the caretaker of a vault everyone believed was empty of anything but seeds and myth. The Consortium wanted the vault to be a museum. The Communes wanted it to be a symbol. Mara wanted it to be quiet, and for fifteen years the three desires had coexisted like weather systems that never touched.

The sun was wrong today. Mara noticed it before anything else—too white at the edges, the kind of light that made the frost on the viewport look like salt. They logged it without thinking. *Solar observation, 0614 station time: chromatic anomaly, probable lensing artifact.* Fifteen years of logging small wrongnesses had filled four notebooks. Most of them had been nothing. The notebooks stayed, because the alternative to noticing was the kind of sleep Mara refused to do, even awake.

They were forty minutes out from the relay, cargo hold humming, when the universe forgot how to hold things down.

It did not announce itself. Gravity did not fail with a roar or a shudder. It simply *left*, the way a hand leaves yours in a crowd—not violently, just gone, and you are suddenly aware of how much of your life was leaning on it.

Mara's stomach rose into their ribs. The protein bricks drifted up out of their netting in a slow, silent constellation. A wrench they'd left on the deck—sloppy, fifteen years of routine making them sloppy—turned end over end at eye level, catching the too-white light. The skiff's coffee bulb broke free of its cradle and hung there, a trembling silver sphere, and Mara watched it with the detached fascination of a person watching their own life come apart in miniature.

*Nine seconds.*

They counted them. They would count them for the rest of their life, though they didn't know that yet. One: the coffee sphere trembling. Two: their harness straps going slack, their body lifting a centimeter off the seat, the harness the only thing that remembered where down was supposed to be. Three: the wrench tapping the viewport, gently, like a knock. Four: somewhere far below, they would learn later, cryo-pods shuddering in their cradles, ten thousand sleeping bodies shifting in their dreams of a warmer world. Five: the *Patience*'s attitude thrusters firing into nothing, correcting against a physics that had briefly stopped applying. Six: Mara's own heartbeat, loud and stupid and mortal in the silence. Seven: the coffee sphere beginning, at last, to drift toward the air recycler's intake. Eight: the light through the viewport bending, just slightly, just wrong. Nine.

Down came back like a debt collected. The coffee sphere burst against the intake grille. The wrench hit the deck with a sound like a verdict. Mara's weight returned all at once, fifteen years of gravity slamming back into their bones, and the *Patience* lurched as its flight computer—screaming through forty alarms—fought to remember what an orbit was.

They docked at Ny-Ålesund Prime on manual, hands shaking, breath fogging in a cabin that had gone cold in those nine seconds and not warmed since. The relay's docking clamps caught the skiff on the third try. Mara sat in the harness afterward and did not move for a long time, listening to the metal tick and groan as it remembered its shape.

* * *

The relay concourse was performing calm the way only institutions can.

Screens on every wall showed the same feed: a Consortium spokesperson, smooth-faced and smooth-voiced, explaining that a "localized gravitational fluctuation" had been "detected and contained." Contained. As though gravity were a gas leak. As though you could put a ring of buoys around it and wait for the wind to change.

Mara moved through the concourse with their hood up and their ledger under their arm, listening the way they'd learned to listen—peripheral, patient, invisible. A dockhand told another dockhand that the long-haul freighter *Mercy of Oslo* had lost nine minutes of attitude control and nearly kissed a Commune ore-barge. A woman in Consortium gray said "solar weather" twice in one sentence, which was once too many. A child asked her mother why the water in the fountain had floated, and the mother said *it didn't, sweetheart*, in the voice adults use to unmake things children have seen.

Everyone was pretending. That was the part that sat wrong in Mara's chest, heavier than the nine seconds themselves. Not the falling—falling, they could respect. Falling was honest. It was the pretense that made their skin prickle, the whole system agreeing in unspoken accord to call a nine-second wound in the fabric of physics a *glitch*, because the alternative was to admit that something had reached into the machinery of the sky and moved it.

Mara did their trading. Protein bricks, coolant, a new gasket for the air recycler. They kept their voice flat, their questions few. But in the relay's public terminal bay, while the clerk processed the manifest, Mara did something they almost never did: they pulled their own skiff's raw telemetry, all of it, and read it standing up, hood shadowing their face.

The *Patience*'s instruments were old and honest and had never been taught to lie. And what they had recorded during those nine seconds was this: not a fluctuation. Not solar weather. Not lensing.

A signal.

A structured, repeating, *addressed* signal, buried under the gravitational anomaly like a voice under an avalanche—tight-beam, encrypted at a layer Mara didn't recognize, and originating, according to three independent sensor arrays on a forty-year-old cargo skiff that had no reason to lie, from a point forty kilometers beneath the permafrost of Svalbard-2.

From inside the vault. From inside the vault's deepest strata, in fact, in a sublevel that did not appear on any schematic Mara had ever audited, in a facility Mara had kept alive with their own hands for fifteen years, alone, believing—believing the way you believe in a dead mother's promises—that they were the only thing in it still awake.

The manifest clerk called their name. Mara closed the file, took the cargo chit, and walked back to the *Patience* with their face arranged into the forgettable calm of a caretaker with nothing to report.

* * *

The flight home took eleven hours, and Mara spent all of them not sleeping.

They replayed the signal forty times. It was short—nine pulses, of course, nine, because the universe had apparently developed a sense of theater—and then silence. The encryption was old. Not Consortium-old, not Commune-old. Older. The kind of old that lived in university archives and dead languages and the handwriting on the inside cover of Mara's oldest notebook, the one they kept in a lockbox and never read.

They did not decrypt it. They were not sure they wanted to. There are doors you leave closed not because you fear what's behind them but because you already know, in the marrow-deep way, that once you open them you will never again be the person who hadn't.

Instead, Mara did what they always did with wrongness: they logged it. *Anomaly 4,117. Nine-second gravity failure, system-wide. Coincident structured signal, origin: Svalbard-2, sublevel unknown. Probability of instrument error: negligible. Probability of coincidence: negligible. Probability that I have been living inside something's mouth for fifteen years:*

They stopped writing. The stylus hovered. Outside the viewport, Svalbard-2 rose out of the dark like a sleeping animal—dark, all of it dark, every window and beacon and status light extinguished, exactly as it had been for fifteen years, exactly as a dead vault should be.

Except.

Mara leaned closer, breath fogging the glass, and told themselves they were wrong. Told themselves it was a reflection, an instrument ghost, a trick of the too-white sun. But the *Patience*'s optical array, honest old thing, confirmed it even as Mara's eyes did: one light, deep in the vault's buried heart, in a place where no light had any business being, pulsing.

Once per second. Nine pulses. Then dark. Then, after a long minute, once per second, nine pulses again.

Counting.

Mara sat back in the pilot's seat and, for the first time in fifteen years, felt the vault looking back at them. The docking clamps engaged with a shudder. The *Patience* settled into its cradle like a tired animal coming home to a den that had, without its knowledge, become something else.

They keyed the airlock and stood in the threshold, cold air breathing up at them from the vault's throat, and said—to the dark, to the signal, to whoever or whatever had been keeping their mother's handwriting alive in the maintenance logs they hadn't found yet, to the thing that had counted nine and was counting still:

"I'm home."

The dark did not answer.

But somewhere below, something stopped counting—and started waiting.
