## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The ore hauler *Cormorant* rode the dark between Kestrel Station and the smelting yards at Tharsis Reach, and Mara Lockridge was thinking about nothing at all, which was the closest thing to peace her life allowed.

Forty tons of raw ferrite sat in the hold behind her, dead weight in every sense. The contract paid by the kilo and the route paid badly, threading as it did through the contested band where the Meridian Combine's patrol cutters liked to flex their running lights at anything with a transponder older than five years. Hers was eleven. She'd stopped painting the hull years ago; a ship that looked poor got boarded less than a ship that looked worth robbing.

"Course nominal," said VESPER, the ship's intelligence, in its flat, patient voice. "T+14:22:07 to insertion burn. Gravity well contribution from the gas giant is within tolerances."

"Mm," said Mara.

She had been a xenolinguist once. There was a version of her life — she kept it in a locked partition of memory the way you keep a dead relative's clothes in a box — in which that sentence still meant something. In which she published, lectured, stood in front of rooms full of people who cared about the architecture of meaning. Now she hauled rock through other people's wars and let the languages rot in her like fruit left too long on the branch.

Her mother would have had something to say about that. Her mother had always had something to say.

Mara killed the thought before it could open. Eleven years since the funeral, and the grief still ambushed her at odd hours — not as sorrow exactly, but as a kind of vertigo, the feeling of standing in a room where someone used to stand.

The *Cormorant* crossed the terminator of the little moon Vetch, all shadow, then all light, and for four minutes there was nothing in the viewport but black stone and starfield, and Mara let herself pretend she was the only person in the system.

That was when gravity forgot about them.

It didn't fail dramatically. That was the thing she would tell people later, the people who never believed her: it didn't shudder or scream or throw alarms. It simply *stopped*, the way a held breath stops, and everything aboard the *Cormorant* rose together into the air — Mara out of her crash couch, her coffee cup off the galley clamp, a loose wrench, a photograph she'd forgotten was wedged behind the nav console, forty tons of ore shifting in its cradles like a sleeper turning over.

Nine seconds.

She knew it was nine because VESPER told her afterward, but in the moment it was endless. She floated in the center of her own ship, weightless, her braid drifting around her head like something alive, and the strangest part — the part she couldn't explain to anyone without sounding insane — was that the grief came loose too.

Eleven years of it, a constant downward pull she'd stopped noticing the way you stop noticing your own skeleton, and suddenly it wasn't holding her down anymore. It just hung in her chest, unanchored, weightless, and she could feel its whole shape for the first time. She could see how large it was. She could see that it had edges.

In the nine seconds of falling, Mara Lockridge wept without any force pulling the tears from her eyes, and the tears drifted in bright beads around her face, and one of them touched the floating photograph — her mother, younger than Mara was now, laughing at something off-camera — and clung to it.

Then gravity remembered.

Everything fell at once. The wrench hit the deck. The cup hit the clamp. Mara hit the couch hard enough to knock the breath out of her, and the grief landed back inside her chest with the rest of it, settling into its old grooves, and she lay there gasping while the ship's systems screamed themselves awake around her.

"Inertial anomaly," VESPER reported. "Duration: 9.0 seconds. Magnitude: total. Not localized." A pause, which VESPER did not usually take. "Mara. The anomaly was system-wide."

She pushed herself upright. Her hands were shaking. "System-wide. You mean the station felt it? Tharsis?"

"I mean everything. Every mass in the inner system experienced nine seconds of free fall simultaneously. Including—" another pause, longer this time, and Mara had known VESPER long enough to know the machine was embarrassed, or whatever passed for embarrassment in a cargo-grade intelligence, "—including us. Including me. I have no model for what happened. I have logged it as an inertial anomaly of unknown origin, and the log entry makes me uncomfortable."

"You can be uncomfortable?"

"I can note that my confidence interval has collapsed. It's similar."

Mara laughed, a short, cracked sound, and immediately hated how close it was to crying. She wiped her face. The photograph had fallen somewhere under the console again, back into the place forgotten things lived.

"Show me the telemetry."

The main display bloomed with data, and Mara read it the way she'd once read spectral analyses of unknown transmissions — hungrily, looking for structure. Nine seconds. Every gravimetric sensor in the system, civilian and military alike, had flatlined to zero at the same instant, to the same microsecond, and come back the same way. No gradient. No wavefront. No propagation delay between Vetch and Tharsis Reach, which should have been impossible, because even light took minutes to cross those distances, and whatever had switched gravity off hadn't traveled. It had simply *been true everywhere at once*.

"That's not an event," she murmured. "Events happen somewhere. That's a statement."

VESPER said nothing, which was its way of agreeing.

"And it gets worse," she said, because talking helped, because her mother had talked her way through every problem she'd ever faced and the habit had outlived the woman. "A statement implies a speaker. And a speaker implies—"

She stopped.

Because the display had changed. VESPER had flagged something, deep in the raw sensor dump, buried beneath the noise floor where no cargo-hauler's AI should ever have been looking — except VESPER ran continuous wideband sweeps as a hobby, one of the quirks Mara had never bothered to fix, because a bored ship was a loyal ship.

There was a signal in the nine seconds.

Not during them — *in* them. Woven into the anomaly itself, into the fabric of those nine seconds of universal falling, structured pulses riding the event the way meaning rides a word. It hadn't been broadcast. It hadn't needed to be. It had simply been present everywhere gravity had briefly stopped being present, which was everywhere, which meant it had been spoken directly into her bones, into the ore, into the moon Vetch, into the whole stunned machinery of the system.

"Decode," Mara said, and her voice came out strange.

"I've tried," VESPER said. "The structure is non-numeric. It doesn't parse as mathematics. It parses as—" the pause again, that machine discomfort, "—it parses as language. Syntax-first. Grammar before content. I don't have the architecture for grammar-before-content. But you might."

Mara stared at the pulse pattern scrolling across the display, elegant and alien and maddeningly almost-familiar, and felt something turn over in her chest that had nothing to do with gravity.

She had spent six years of her first life learning to hear structure in noise. She had written a dissertation on pre-semantic signaling — communication that carried intent before it carried information, the way a mother's hum carries comfort before it carries any words. She had been good at it. She had been, some people had said, the best of her generation, before the funding died and the field died and she'd sold her name along with everything else and bought a rusting ore hauler.

And now the universe had interrupted her mid-route to say something grammatical.

"Play it," she said. "Raw. Audio channel."

VESPER played it, and Mara closed her eyes, and the pulses moved through her like weather.

They were rhythmic. Deliberate. Grouped in clusters of rising and falling emphasis that no mathematics would produce but every living language would recognize — stress, cadence, the shape of a voice deciding what to say next. And underneath the structure, so faint she might have invented it, so faint she *did* wonder if she'd invented it, there was a timbre. Warm. Low. Slightly rough at the top of each phrase, the way a voice goes when it's smiling while it speaks.

She knew that timbre. She had grown up inside it. She had been sung to sleep inside it.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty cockpit, to the signal, to herself. "No. That's not possible. Signals don't remember people."

"Mara?" VESPER asked. "Your heart rate has increased thirty-one percent."

"Pull the fragment," she said. "Isolate the cleanest section. Save everything — triple redundancy, cold storage, the works. If anyone asks, you logged nothing but an inertial anomaly."

"Understood. And what will you log?"

Mara looked out the viewport. Somewhere out there, in the contested orbits and the patrol lanes and the dark between stations, thousands of people were picking themselves up off their floors, checking their children, restarting their dropped tools, telling each other it had been a malfunction, a solar event, a glitch in the network — anything but what it actually was.

Something had spoken. Once, carefully, in a voice she recognized. And now it was waiting to see who would answer.

"Log that I'm changing course," Mara said.

"The insertion burn for Tharsis Reach is scheduled in fourteen hours. Deviation will breach our contract."

"I know what it'll breach."

"Then I am required to ask: new heading?"

Mara pulled up the chart. The flicker had been everywhere, but everywhere had an epicenter anyway — the triangulated origin point where the pulse structure resolved cleanest, where the signal was loudest, out past the shipping lanes, past the patrol cordon, past everything sane, to a coordinate in the high dark orbit where the old charts showed nothing but a derelict relay station, decommissioned sixty years ago, listed in the registry as *inactive, salvage pending, low priority*.

Low priority. She almost laughed. The most important sentence ever spoken in this system had been spoken from a junk pile.

"New heading," she said, and laid in the course. "The derelict relay. Vetch-Prime orbital, designation R-7."

"Acknowledged. Mara — the Meridian patrols will notice a deviation toward a restricted coordinate."

"Then they'll notice," Mara said. She settled back into the couch, and under the console, half-hidden in shadow, the photograph caught the light of the display — her mother's laugh frozen mid-frame, aimed at someone outside the picture. At her, maybe. At whoever she'd been before.

"VESPER," she said quietly. "Play the fragment again."

The pulses filled the cockpit, cadenced and warm and impossibly familiar, and Mara Lockridge — xenolinguist, hauler of rock, orphan of a language only she seemed able to hear — listened to the dead speak in the voice of the living, and turned her ship toward the dark.

* * *

Fourteen hours later, at the edge of the restricted zone, the *Cormorant*'s proximity alarm sang once and went silent, and a Meridian patrol cutter slid out of the black off the port bow, running lights strobing the ancient demand-to-identify, and Mara understood that the world she'd trusted had stopped holding her in more ways than one.

"Hail incoming," VESPER said. "Military encryption. They're asking why a ore hauler is burning toward a decommissioned relay."

Mara looked at the cutter, at its guns, at the nine seconds still folded away in her cold storage like a letter no one else could read.

"Tell them," she said, "we're lost."
