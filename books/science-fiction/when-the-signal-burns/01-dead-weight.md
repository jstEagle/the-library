## 1. Dead Weight

There are two kinds of quiet in the Belt. The first is the honest kind—the vacuum kind—where nothing moves because nothing can. The second is the kind you find in a customs queue at Ceres Station at 0400 station time, when the clerks are running on stim-chew and spite and every freighter jockey in the line is doing arithmetic on how much of their cargo they can afford to declare.

I've spent six years learning to love the second kind. It's the closest thing to safety contested space offers: a room full of tired people too busy protecting their own secrets to go looking for anyone else's.

The Vela hung off Berth Fourteen with her belly full of water ice and machine parts, all of it legal, all of it boring, which is the whole point of hauling dead weight through orbits where half the traffic runs guns and the other half runs lies. I was neither, officially. Officially I was Domenica Kastellanos, sole owner-operator of a mid-life tramp freighter with a clean inspection history and a docking record so unremarkable it could put you to sleep. Unremarkable was my life's work. I'd built it plank by plank, like a coffin.

"Vela, Kastellanos," said the berth comm, in the flat voice Ceres customs used for everyone from admirals to smugglers, "you're flagged for full manifest verification. Proceed to Inspection Bay Six."

My stomach did something small and professional. Not panic—I don't do panic anymore, it's bad for the complexion—but a tightening, like a hand closing around a wrench.

Full verification wasn't routine. Full verification was what they did when a clerk got curious, or when someone up the chain wanted a ship delayed, or when the universe had decided it had been too long since it last kicked me personally.

"Copy," I said, and took us in.

* * *

Inspection Bay Six smelled like ozone and old coffee, the universal perfume of bureaucracy everywhere humans have dragged their paperwork. A junior inspector ran a scanner wand over my cargo seals while I stood at the counter and tried to look like a woman whose only worry was whether her coolant filters would pass.

They passed. Of course they passed. I'd replaced them myself on Titan, and I replace things properly. That's the irony nobody ever appreciates about people who disappear: we're meticulous. You have to be. Sloppy forgers get caught by accident; careful ones get caught by choice, or by God, or—in my case, as it turned out—by a customs clerk with a taste for anomalies.

Her name tag read OKONKWO, T., and she had the particular stillness of someone who has found something interesting and is deciding what to do about it. She was maybe thirty, hair cropped close, eyes that moved over my face the way her scanner had moved over my seals—not hostile, just thorough.

"Domenica Kastellanos," she said.

"That's me."

"You've been hauling out of Meridian lanes for four years."

"Five, next month." I smiled the smile I keep for officials, warm but slightly bored. "Time flies when your cargo doesn't."

She didn't smile back. She turned her console toward me, and there it was: my ship, my registration, my clean little history—and beside it, pulled up from some archive I didn't know customs clerks could reach, a document rendered in institutional amber, the color they use for records that are supposed to be sealed.

"This came up in a cross-reference sweep," she said. "Automated. Name-match against historical manifests. Do you recognize this vessel?"

I looked at the screen.

I looked at the screen for three seconds longer than a woman with nothing to hide would have needed, and I hope to God it read as boredom, because inside those three seconds my entire nervous system went off like a decompression alarm.

The Meridian's Grace.

Registry number MRG-7741. Meridian Fleet auxiliary transport, class retired before she was ever supposed to fly. Manifest dated eleven years back—no. Three years back. Three years, two months, and change, because I still counted, the way amputees count weather.

And there, in the crew list, in a typeface that had been forged by a very good forger working under very bad circumstances, was a name.

KASTELLANOS, D. — PURSER / RECORDS.

My name. My forgery. My handwriting, digitized, archived, and now sitting on a customs terminal at Ceres Station being looked at by a junior clerk who kept glancing between the screen and my face like she was comparing two photographs.

"That's not me," I said.

"I didn't say it was." Okonkwo tilted her head. "It's a name match. Kastellanos, D. Same initial, same surname construction. The system flags it; a human reviews it. That's me. I'm reviewing it." She tapped the screen. "Except this ship never flew. Registry says the Grace was stricken before commissioning. But there's a full crew manifest, cargo logs, departure authorization—all of it stamped, all of it signed. And then—" she scrolled, and I watched her scroll it, because refusing to watch would have been worse—"—an incident report. Loss of vessel with all hands. Three years ago. The one that ended the Border War."

"The armistice ship," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it.

"People still argue about what happened to her," Okonkwo said. "Officially it was a reactor cascade. Unofficially, depending on which bar you drink in, it was Compact saboteurs, or Fleet negligence, or a weapons test gone wrong." She looked at me again, that thorough, unhurried look. "Nobody ever says it was a purser named Kastellanos."

"No," I agreed. "They wouldn't. It's a common name."

"It's not, actually. I checked." She closed the file with a soft click that sounded, in the quiet of Bay Six, like a hatch sealing. "Here's the thing, Ms. Kastellanos. The automated sweep flagged forty-one thousand name matches across the archive last quarter. Forty thousand nine hundred and ninety-four were dismissed by the system itself. This one wasn't. Because the manifest signature on the Grace matches—algorithmically, within tolerance—the signature on your current commercial registrations."

The bay was very quiet. Somewhere behind the bulkhead, water pumps thudded in their slow station rhythm, and I thought, absurdly, about Sefu complaining that the Vela's own pumps had developed a rattle he couldn't trace, and how I'd told him we'd deal with it after Ceres, after the inspection, after the next job, always after, because that's how you live when you're waiting for something you refuse to name.

"What does that mean procedurally?" I asked.

"It means I have to escalate it." She said it without relish, without apology. Just fact. "A signature match between an active license holder and a sealed casualty manifest goes to Records Integrity. They'll verify. If it's a false positive—glyph noise, font drift, whatever—they'll dismiss it and you'll never hear about it again." She slid a chit across the counter. "You're cleared to load and depart. Provisionally."

"And if it's not a false positive?"

Okonkwo shrugged, and for a moment she looked less like a customs clerk and more like a person, tired, at the end of a shift, holding a question she hadn't asked yet. "Then somebody erased somebody badly, and Records Integrity will want to know who did the erasing." She paused. "For what it's worth, Ms. Kastellanos—you don't strike me as a ghost."

"You'd be surprised what people turn out to be," I said, and took my chit, and walked out of Bay Six at a pace I calibrated carefully to be exactly unhurried.

* * *

The walk from Inspection Bay Six to Berth Fourteen is four hundred meters of corridor, and I used every meter of it to think, which is to say I used every meter of it to keep my face pleasant while my mind ran the problem like a fugitive runs stairs.

Three possibilities. I'm a great believer in limiting yourself to three possibilities; more than that and you start chasing shadows, fewer and you start lying to yourself.

One: coincidence. Some other Kastellanos, D., some glyph-drift artifact, some algorithm hiccup. Possible. Probability, given what I knew about how that manifest was built: low. Quist hadn't built false positives into his work. Quist didn't believe in them.

Two: Records Integrity dismisses it, and this is a scare with no teeth. Also possible. Also dependent on the diligence of strangers, which is not a thing I had ever once been able to rely on in my life.

Three: someone wanted that file found. Someone had let it surface—or made it surface—through an automated sweep that no one would think to blame, on a station where I happened to be docked, attached to a signature that happened to be mine.

Six years ago I would have laughed at three. Six years ago I believed the universe was indifferent. Then I spent three years helping build a lie big enough to end a war, and I learned that the universe isn't indifferent; it's patient. It files things away. And every so often, someone goes looking in the filing cabinet.

I reached the Vela's airlock and stood outside her for a moment, hand flat against the hull plating, feeling the faint vibration of Sefu's pumps through my palm. Home. Sixty meters of secondhand steel and stubborn engineering, and the only place in two systems where I was entirely, verifiably nobody.

"Vela, this is Kastellanos," I said to the lock. "Open for one."

The lock cycled, and the smell of the ship hit me—coolant and tea and Marisol's incense, which she swore was regulation-permitted and which absolutely was not—and something in my chest loosened by maybe five percent. Five percent is a lot, these days.

Sefu was in the corridor with his head inside an access panel, which is where Sefu spent most of his life, his voice floating out backward: "If that's the customs result, I want you to know I already fixed the pump rattle, and if it's not the customs result, I want you to know I already fixed the pump rattle anyway."

"It's the customs result," I said. "We're clear. Provisionally."

His head emerged, dark face creased with suspicion. Sefu had been a Fleet engineer before he'd been anything else, and he retained the Fleet conviction that everything official is a trap until proven otherwise. "Provisionally. What's provisional mean?"

"It means the paperwork gods are considering our case."

"They don't consider cases. They sit on them." He wiped his hands on a rag that had once been white in the way that ancient myths were once true. "You okay? You've got the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You have a face. It's the one where you're smiling but your jaw's set. Marisol calls it your tribunal face."

"I don't have a tribunal face."

"You absolutely—" He stopped, because Marisol appeared at the end of the corridor with Juno behind her carrying a crate of protein stock, and some conversations aren't for audiences. Sefu gave me a look that promised the conversation would resume, because Sefu's promises were like his welds: structural.

* * *

That evening I sat in the Vela's cramped galley with a cup of tea going cold in front of me and a data slate balanced on my knee, and I did the thing I had sworn I would never do again.

I opened the archive.

Not the customs copy—that was logged, watched, already a thread leading back to me. I went the long way around, through three relay hops and a public academic mirror that some Belt university maintained and nobody monitored, because nobody had cared about eleven-year-old Fleet procurement records in a decade. I pulled the Meridian's Grace registry file, and I read it the way you read your own autopsy.

Auxiliary transport MRG-7741. Keel-laid at Vesta yards. Recommissioned under emergency wartime authority. Crew complement twenty-six. Departure authorized from Pallas High, standard date three years and two months past. Cargo: mixed industrial and medical stores, mass consistent with declared tonnage. Loss event: reactor cascade, uncontrolled, total. All hands. No wreckage recovered. No bodies recovered. The war ended eleven days later.

All true. Every word of it true, in the way that matters most to liars, which is technically. I had checked each line myself, six years of investigator's habit compressed into three weeks of terrified craftsmanship, because Quist had said: *if anyone ever audits this, it has to survive the audit. We're not building a story, Dominic. We're building a wall.*

And there she was, third column, ninth row. KASTELLANOS, D. Purser/Records. My signature beneath it, or rather the signature I had built for a woman who never existed, constructed stroke by stroke from samples of a real clerk's hand—a woman who had died in a yard accident years before, whose records I'd mined because dead women make the best donors. Quist had taught me that. Quist had taught me most of what I know about making people out of paper.

The tea went cold. I drank it anyway. On the Vela you learn not to waste things, including gestures of self-punishment.

Twenty-six names on that manifest. Twenty-six people who had never existed aboard a ship that had never flown, dying in an accident that had never happened, except that it had—all of it had, the deaths were real, the ship was real, the fireball over the Pallas approach lanes had been visible from the surface of Ceres like a second dawn—and none of it had happened the way the record said. That was the architecture of it. The truth underneath, wrapped in a lie on top, load-bearing lie, the kind you build a peace on.

Eleven days after the Grace burned, the Border War ended. Not because anyone won. Because both fleets looked at the burning wreck of a ship full of their own people and flinched, simultaneously, like two men touching the same live wire. The armistice was signed in the shockwave. I knew, because I'd helped forge the reason for the shockwave, and if I told you what actually killed those twenty-six people, I'd have to keep talking for another thirty-nine chapters, and right now I couldn't even say it to myself.

Someone had put my name where the world could find it.

I closed the slate. Opened it. Closed it again. In the galley doorway, Marisol leaned against the frame with her arms crossed, pilot-casual, watching me with the specific patience of a woman who reads turbulence long before the instruments do.

"You've read that same page four times," she said. "And you're doing the jaw thing."

"I'm fine."

"Mm." She came in, took the cold tea out of my hands, dumped it, and poured a fresh cup, because that's Marisol: she fixes things by refusing to acknowledge they're broken. "Ceres clerks give you trouble?"

"A formality. Signature cross-check. Automated." I took the cup. "It'll resolve."

"Automated checks don't put that look on you." She sat down across from me, close enough that her knee touched mine, which was either comfort or interrogation technique; with Marisol the two had never been distinguishable. "Dom. Whatever it is—you'll tell me when you're ready. But I'm telling you now, so you hear it plain: ready sooner. Things that eat a captain from the inside end up eating the rest of us too. Ship's rule."

"Since when is that a ship's rule?"

"Since right now. I just wrote it." She tapped the table. "Invisible ink."

I almost laughed. Almost. It came out as a breath that might have been one, and she accepted it, and left me alone with my tea and my ghosts, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

I waited until the ship was dark—until Sefu's pump-rattle had gone silent, until Juno's light under her cabin door had gone out, until even Marisol's incense had burned down to ash—and then I sat in the cockpit with the running lights off and did the last thing a careful woman should do.

I sent a message to a man who was supposed to be unreachable.

Quist's contact protocol was a fossil of a fossil: a dead-drop address on a civilian mail relay, keyed to a phrase. I typed the phrase—*the wall needs inspection*—and then, because the old protocols deserved honesty even if nothing else did, I added a line I had never once used in six years of silence:

*They found a stone. Ask me how.*

Then I encrypted it three ways, bounced it through four relays, and sat in the dark watching the stars wheel slow past the Vela's ports, waiting for the answer I already knew was coming, the way you wait for thunder when the lightning's already lit the room.

Somewhere out there, in the contested orbits and the quiet offices, someone had opened a filing cabinet that held the worst year of my life. And the first stone had rolled out at a customs desk on Ceres, under the hands of a tired clerk named Okonkwo, who had looked at my face and said, *you don't strike me as a ghost.*

No, I'd wanted to tell her. I'm worse than a ghost. Ghosts haunt houses.

I haunted a peace treaty.

The reply came at 0417, station time, while the queue outside was filling up again with tired ships and tired liars. One line, Quist's dry cipher, decoded against the key I'd kept in my head for six years:

*Come alone. Burn this. — Q*

I burned it. Then I sat a while longer in the dark, listening to my family breathe behind me in their bunks, and started planning how to slip away from the only home I had, without telling them why.
