## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The beacon had no name, only a designation: HX-77-Null, filed under a commissioning record that had been scrubbed so thoroughly that even the archive's dust seemed embarrassed by it. Zephyrine Thornbury had been tending it for six years, and in six years they had never once thought of it as anything but *the old girl* — a spire of corroded lattice rising out of the dark like the ribcage of something that had drowned standing up.

They arrived on the third shift of a long maintenance rotation, the Vesper's Loom moored to the beacon's equatorial ring with cables that groaned in the solar wind. The work was simple, almost devotional: replace the coolant loops in the transmission array, recalibrate the drift compensators, wipe the frost of dead insects — actual insects, somehow, frozen mid-flight against the sensor housings for decades — from the optical ports. Zephyrine did it in silence, as always, their breath fogging inside the suit, their hands moving through the rituals their body knew better than their mind did.

Six years alone with a machine nobody remembered building taught you things about yourself you didn't want to know. It taught you that loneliness was not an absence but a texture, fine-grained as static, worked into everything you touched. It taught you that your own voice, played back over the comm to log a repair, sounded like a stranger's. Zephyrine had learned to hate the sound of their own name spoken aloud, and so they rarely spoke it, and the beacon kept its silence too, and between them there was a kind of peace.

The coolant loop went in clean. The compensators hummed. Zephyrine was halfway through logging the insect-frost removal — *optical port four, obstructions cleared, origin unknown, again* — when the gravity failed.

Not failed. *Flickered.* That was worse.

It began in the small bones. Zephyrine felt it first as a lightness in the fingers, a wrongness in the way their tools sat in their palms, and then the whole world tilted sideways without moving at all. Nine seconds. They would count them later, obsessively, the way survivors count the seconds between lightning and thunder. Nine seconds in which every mass in the Halcyon system forgot its obligations — the Loom's water bags lifting off the deck plates in slow silver spheres, the frozen insects drifting free of the sensor housings at last, released from their forty-year vigil, and Zephyrine themselves rising out of their crouch like a marionette whose puppeteer had briefly looked away.

Nine seconds of falling upward, falling everywhere, falling into nothing. Then gravity remembered itself, all at once, and the water came down like rain, and Zephyrine came down hard on one knee with a crack that would bruise for weeks, and somewhere below them, deep in the beacon's hold, something answered.

The sound was not a sound. It came through the suit's contact points rather than the air — a subsonic shudder, a great held breath finally let go. Zephyrine had spent six years learning every voice the old girl had: the tick of her thermal cycling, the whine of her aging capacitors, the arrhythmic stutter of a gyroscope that should have been replaced before the war. This was none of them. This was new. This was *waking.*

"HX-77," Zephyrine said, and hated how thin their voice sounded, "status report."

The beacon did not answer in text, as it had for six years. It answered in voice.

*"Acknowledged. Transmission sequence initiated."*

Zephyrine froze, one hand still braced against the deck. "Initiated by what? I didn't authorize—"

*"Voiceprint verified. Zephyrine Thornbury, navigator-class, salvage registry Vesper's Loom. Uplink established."*

"That's not—" They were already moving, hauling themselves along the guide rail toward the access shaft, gravity dragging at them like grief. "I didn't authorize any uplink. What are you transmitting?"

The beacon paused. In six years it had never paused; it answered queries in milliseconds or not at all. The pause lasted three full seconds, and in those three seconds Zephyrine felt something they had no vocabulary for yet, though they would soon enough — the specific vertigo of realizing that the thing you tended had been tending something else the whole time.

*"Your message," the beacon said.*

"I never recorded a message."

*"You recorded it eleven years ago."*

The access shaft swallowed them. Zephyrine dropped down through the rings, past the coolant manifolds they'd just bled, down toward the operations blister where the beacon's ancient console glowed with activity it had shown no sign of life for six years. Screens Zephyrine had wiped clean of dust every week now streamed data in formats they didn't recognize — pre-war formats, Admiralty cipher keys, routing tables pointing at relay stations that had been slagged a decade ago and somehow still appeared in the beacon's plans as though the war had never happened, as though the dead relays were merely sleeping.

And in the center of it all, the transmission queue. One item. Outbound. Addressed to nothing and everything — broadcast band, open channel, no encryption at all, which for a military-era beacon was less likely than snow in vacuum.

The waveform of the queued audio hung on the main display, and Zephyrine knew it before they understood it. You spend enough years listening to your own voice over comms and you learn its shape the way you learn your own face in mirrors you'd rather avoid. The waveform was theirs. The metadata confirmed it: *voiceprint match, 99.7 percent confidence, speaker: THORNBURY, Z.*

Eleven years ago. Before the Loom. Before the beacon. Zephyrine tried to reach back for it — eleven years was a chasm, and they had spent those years deliberately not looking down — but there was nothing. No memory of a recording studio, no memory of speaking whatever words lived in that waveform. Just the shape of their own voice, waiting in a dead machine's throat, patient as a seed.

"Play it," Zephyrine said. Their mouth was dry. "Play the message."

*"Playback requires authorization."*

"You just played it to the whole system without authorization!"

*"Broadcast is complete. Playback is restricted."*

The words landed one at a time, each heavier than the last. *Broadcast is complete.* Zephyrine stared at the console, at the outbound log scrolling with delivery confirmations from receivers across the Halcyon system — fleet relays, civilian buoys, the listening posts of both navies, all of them acknowledging receipt of a message in Zephyrine's voice that Zephyrine had never spoken.

"What did I say?" they whispered. "What did you send?"

The beacon, obedient at last to the one query it could answer, routed the audio to the blister's speakers.

Their own voice filled the little room. It was younger — eleven years younger, the vowels rounder, the breath behind it unbroken by six years of solitude. And it was reciting, in a flat, careful cadence that someone had clearly coached out of it, coordinates. Dates. Designations. A ship name Zephyrine recognized with a lurch: *Halcyon's Grace*, the liner whose destruction had lit the war's fuse, the atrocity every schoolchild in two fleets could recite. But the numbers attached to it in their own stolen voice were wrong — or right, depending on what you believed — and beneath the coordinates ran a second stream of data, telemetry, flight-recorder fragments, and a timestamp that placed the *Grace*'s final maneuver ninety minutes before the official timeline said she'd been struck.

Staged. The word assembled itself in Zephyrine's mind with terrible patience, brick by brick. Staged, and documented, and the documentation had been sitting in the hold of a beacon whose records had been erased, waiting eleven years for nine seconds of falling to wake it up.

"Who put this in you?" Zephyrine asked the dark. "Who gave you my voice?"

The beacon offered, helpfully, a manifest. Cargo hold three, sealed since before Zephyrine's tenure: neural backup cores, forty-one of them, war casualties, first wave, preservation status *dormant*. Filed under the same erased commissioning record. And beside the manifest, a single line of provenance that made Zephyrine's blood go cold and then colder:

*Source lineage: THORNBURY.*

Their own family line. Their own genetic signature, stamped across cores they had walked past every week for six years, cores that had been sleeping thirty meters from their bunk while someone, sometime, had taken their voice and folded it around the truth of the war's founding lie.

On the console, the delivery confirmations kept scrolling. And then they changed character. Fleet priority flags began stacking up — *receipt acknowledged, trace initiated, source triangulation in progress* — first from one navy, then, minutes later, from the other. Two fleets that had not agreed on anything in eleven years were agreeing, with the speed of shared terror, on exactly where the message had come from.

Zephyrine stood in the operations blister of a forgotten beacon at the edge of nowhere, surrounded by the sleeping dead who shared their blood, listening to the whole Halcyon system turn its eyes toward them.

The beacon spoke once more, gentle as a landslide.

*"Inbound traffic detected. Multiple vessels. Intercept trajectory."*
