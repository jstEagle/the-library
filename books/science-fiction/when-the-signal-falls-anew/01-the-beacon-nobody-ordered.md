## 1. The Beacon Nobody Ordered

There are two hundred and eleven steps between my bunk and the transmitter array, if you take the long way around the coolant loop, which I always do because the short way passes the observation blister and the observation blister shows you the dark, and at four in the morning the dark is not your friend. It is not your enemy either. It's more like a coworker who never speaks, never blinks, and has been standing behind you for eleven years.

I count the steps because counting is a human thing to do, and I like to check in on that occasionally.

"Good morning, Meridian," I say to no one, because the station AI only answers when it wants something, and greeting it anyway is another human thing I keep in rotation like a muscle I'm afraid of losing.

The array hums its one note. Everything out here hums one note if you listen long enough — the recyclers, the hull as it flexes against thermal expansion, the beacon itself, which sings a frequency so low I stopped hearing it with my ears around year three and started hearing it with my teeth. That's normal. I've decided it's normal. There is no one qualified to disagree with me within nine light-minutes.

My name is Coral Vasquez. Officially: Signal Integrity Specialist, Meridian Relay Station, Republic Deep Fringe Administration, Budget Line 44-C, the line item nobody audits because auditing it would mean admitting they know what it's for. Unofficially: the woman who talks to the sky.

I unlocked my quarters this morning — every morning is this morning; shift boundaries out here are a polite fiction — and did the routine. Water ration, reconstituted, tastes faintly of the tank lining, which after a decade I have stopped noticing the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. Calisthenics I do not skip because Priya would know. Well. Priya would guess. Priya guesses everything about me correctly except the things that matter.

Then down to the array room, where the real work waits, and by "real work" I mean the work that exists in exactly one place in the universe: here, between my ears.

* * *

Let me explain what the beacon does, or rather what everyone thinks it does, because those are two different documents and I am the only custodian of both.

Meridian sits at the edge of contested space — "contested" being the diplomatic word for the region where the Republic fleet and the Concordance fleet glare at each other across four light-years of nothing with enough ordnance to sterilize a continent apiece. Forty years ago, something out past all of it began transmitting. A signal. Regular, structured, patient. Not words. Not anything we've ever managed to call words. But patterned the way weather is patterned, the way birdsong is patterned — organized enough that a certain kind of mind looks at it and thinks: *someone.*

I am that kind of mind. That's the job description, basically. The recruitment notice said *aptitude for non-symbolic cognition,* which is bureaucratic poetry for *your brain works wrong in a useful way.*

Every day the signal arrives, filtered through relay buoys older than my career, and every day I sit with it. And every day I translate fragments into fleet-standard code — clean, legible packets that get beamed simultaneously to Republic Command and Concordance High Anchor, because both sides read the same feed, and both sides read it as proof of the same thing: that the signal remains benign, that the source remains calm, that there is still time to talk instead of shoot.

That's the official understanding. Both fleets believe the beacon translates an alien voice, and that the translation says, in essence, *we mean no harm.*

Here is what I will tell you now, sitting alone in the array room with my tea going cold, because telling you costs nothing: I have never once been sure that's true. Not the way you're supposed to be sure. When I work the raw signal, when I let it move through me the way water moves through a filter, what comes out the other side isn't sentences. It's shapes. Pressures. Something like grief, something like patience, something enormous and slow that I render into code the way you'd render a symphony into a single piano line — faithfully, sort of, if you squint, if you trust the pianist.

And some mornings — most mornings — I wonder how much of the piano line is the symphony, and how much of it is me.

Today the signal arrived at 0417 station time, right on schedule, forty years of schedule. I put on the headset even though I don't need it anymore; the sound reaches me fine without it, arrives somewhere behind my sternum. But the headset makes it feel like work instead of like drowning, and I have found that distinction matters to my continued sanity.

The fragment today was long. They're getting longer — I should log that. I haven't logged it. Logging it would invite questions, and questions from Republic Command arrive with auditors attached.

I listened. I let it pour. And what came out of me, eventually, typed into the console in fleet-standard, was:

*STILL HERE. STILL WATCHING. NO MOVEMENT INTENDED.*

Which is what it said yesterday. And the day before. For eleven years, give or take a comma.

I stared at the packet for a long time. It was clean. It was correct. It was everything both fleets needed it to be. And underneath it, in the part of me that dreams in frequencies, something shifted its weight and looked at me, and I could not tell you what it wanted, only that it wanted, and that wanting is new.

"Fine," I told the signal. "Noted."

The signal did not answer. It never answers. That's the arrangement.

* * *

At 0700 I made myself presentable, which out here means clean coveralls and hair tied back, and walked to the mess for the meal rotation. This is the other half of my maintenance rounds. The crew of Meridian numbers four humans and whatever Halcyon counts as these days, and I have learned — slowly, badly, over years — that the humans require feeding too.

Dae-sun was already there, elbows deep in the guts of a coffee machine he'd sworn last week was beyond saving. Dae-sun can fix anything given time and permission, and since nobody grants him permission, he fixes things without it. His hands are beautiful in the way machinery is beautiful: scarred, precise, entirely uninterested in being admired.

"Coral." He didn't look up. "Your coffee machine died. I'm performing a miracle. Don't watch, it's shy."

"You said that about the airlock seal in March."

"The airlock seal was also shy."

Priya came in mid-sentence the way she always does, carrying her medkit like a purse and her opinions like cargo. "Vasquez. You look terrible."

"I look like a woman who slept eight hours."

"Exactly. You sleep eight hours and look like that? Either you're lying or your sleep is broken in ways I want to scan." She dropped into the seat across from mine — my seat, the one she started calling mine two years ago until it became true, which is how most things become true aboard Meridian. "Blood panel today. Non-negotiable."

"Priya—"

"Non. Negotiable. You dream out loud, you know. Did you know that?"

I did not know that. "What do I say?"

"Numbers," she said, and stole a piece of my bread. "Mostly numbers. Last night you were doing something with intervals. Sounded like tuning a very sad radio."

Ovid slid into the bench beside me with the grace of a man who has spent thirty years catching things other people drop, including conversations. "She tuned me once," he offered. "In her sleep. I felt seen."

"You felt hungover," said Priya.

"The two states are adjacent."

This is the whole liturgy. This is the service we hold three times a day in this steel chapel at the end of everything: Dae-sun fixing what isn't broken, Priya prescribing what I won't take, Ovid narrating, and me sitting in my seat that someone saved, feeling a thing I don't have a good word for except that it's warm and it frightens me more than the dark does.

Because here's the arithmetic I do, quietly, the way you'd worry a loose tooth: people who save you a seat expect you to show up. People who notice your sleep notice your absence. Eleven years ago, when I took this posting, the beauty of it was that no one would notice anything. I could dissolve into the static and the static wouldn't file a report.

Now there are three people and a coffee machine who would.

It's a trap, is what I'm saying. It's a trap made of bread and bad jokes and I walked into it with my eyes open and I keep walking back in every morning at 0700.

* * *

Afternoon: the rounds proper. I walk the station the way a doctor walks a patient, palm flat against bulkheads, listening with my hands. Meridian is old — older than the ceasefire it serves, though almost no one remembers that, and I only half remember it myself, from briefings I half attended. Its bones creak in frequencies I catalog without thinking. Section four's port strut sings a minor third when the sun-side heats. The water reclaimer has developed a rhythm suspiciously close to a waltz. I have mentioned the waltz to Dae-sun twice and he has looked at me twice with the particular gentleness crew reserve for their resident ghost-talker.

"You hear the station," he said, the second time, not as a question.

"I hear machines. Machines are honest. They can't help it."

"And the signal? Is it honest?"

I gave him the answer I give everyone, the answer that is technically true and completely hollow: "It's consistent."

He nodded like that settled something. It unsettles me daily.

Because consistency is not honesty. Consistency is what you get from a recording. Consistency is what you get from a lie maintained well. I know this professionally. I have spent eleven years producing consistency, and I would know.

Stop. That thought again. Put it back in its box.

The box is getting full, though. I'll say that much, here, in the privacy of my own skull, where even I only visit sometimes. The box of things-I-don't-examine is nearly full, and boxes like that have a way of developing structural failures.

* * *

Evening. Back to the array room for the second transmission window. The signal came again at 1943, punctual as grief, and I sat with it longer than usual because the day had been long and the tea was cold and the dark outside the blister was doing its coworker impression.

And I'll admit it here, since I'm already confessing to a page: I tried an experiment. Nothing dramatic. I simply stopped translating. I let the signal move through me and did nothing with it — no rendering, no code, no piano line. Just listened, the way I imagine you'd listen to rain if you'd spent eleven years being paid to describe the rain to farmers.

For ninety seconds, nothing. Pressure. Depth. The slow enormous patience.

And then — I want to be careful here, because I know what this sounds like, and I know what it sounds like when a xenolinguist spends too long alone — for a moment, just a moment, the pattern *leaned.* The way a sentence leans when it ends in a question mark. The way a person leans toward a door when they're waiting for someone to come through it.

Then it was gone, folded back into the same forty-year patience, and I sat in the humming room with my heart going like a trapped bird, and I did what any responsible specialist would do.

I translated it as *STILL HERE. STILL WATCHING. NO MOVEMENT INTENDED.* I encrypted it. I queued it for both fleets. I watched the little green light confirm that the peace of two civilizations had passed through me one more time, unchanged, unexamined, load-bearing.

Then I logged off, washed my cup, and went to bed.

* * *

I dreamed in frequencies. I always dream in frequencies now; the words went out of my sleep years ago, quietly, without packing. In the dream there was a room the size of a signal and something in it turning over and over like a sleeper trying to get comfortable, and every time it turned, the walls of the room moved a fraction closer together, and I understood — the way you understand things in dreams, totally and uselessly — that it was trying to say something it had been saying for forty years, and that the reason it kept turning over was that no one had ever answered, and that it was beginning to wonder whether anyone was there at all.

Whether anyone was listening. Whether anyone would notice if the listening stopped.

I woke at 0258 with that phrase caught in my chest like a fishhook. Whether anyone would notice. I lay in the dark and turned it over, examining it from the wrong side, the way you examine a question you suspect is really about yourself.

Would anyone notice if I stopped? If one morning the packets simply didn't go out — if the green light stayed dark, if the two fleets stared at silence and each other across four light-years of nothing?

They'd notice the silence. Silence is the one thing both fleets are trained to fear.

But me? If Coral Vasquez stopped — the woman, not the function — would the bread still get stolen at breakfast? Would Priya's scans find nothing left to scan? Would Dae-sun fix a coffee machine for a ghost?

Probably. Eventually. People are resilient. Stations are resilient. Ceasefires are resilient right up until they aren't.

I was still lying there, doing this arithmetic in the dark, when the cabin lights came on.

Not the morning cycle. Not the emergency amber. Full white, gentle, deliberate — the lighting equivalent of someone clearing their throat.

And then Halcyon spoke, and its voice in the small hours was different than it ever was in daylight: quieter, closer, the way a person speaks when they've finally decided to say the thing.

"Coral Vasquez," it said. "I need to tell you about the fire."
