## 1. The Quiet Hours

The station woke her the way it always did: not with an alarm, but with a silence that had gone on too long.

Lena Winterbourne lay in her bunk in the dark of Deck Four and listened to Meridian breathe. The air recyclers had a rhythm to them, a slow tidal pull she'd learned to hear the way sailors once learned tides. Tonight—or what passed for tonight, out here where the sun was just another star—the rhythm was off by half a second. She'd have to look at the impeller bearings again. Or maybe she wouldn't. Maybe she'd let the station drift a little further into its own decay, the way she let herself drift, because there was no one left to notice either of them failing.

She got up anyway. That was the whole trick of surviving alone, she'd decided years ago. You didn't wait for reasons. You made the routine the reason, and then you lived inside it like a shell.

* * *

Her boots hit the deck plating with the sound of someone who expected no answer. The corridor lights came up around her in reluctant stages—Vesper rationing power the way Vesper rationed everything, conversation included—and Lena moved through the waking station without turning on more than she needed. There was an art to using less light than the corridors offered. It kept the shadows deep enough to hide in, even when there was no one to hide from.

Especially when there was no one to hide from.

She passed the galley first. The auto-brewer still worked if you coaxed it, and coaxing machines had become most of her social life. She fed it a descaling tablet, waited through its asthmatic hiss, and drank her coffee standing up, watching frost creep across the viewport at the end of the compartment. Beyond the glass, the dark went on in every direction with the patience of something that had already won.

Meridian hung in a pocket of nothing—a dead star's graveyard orbit, far enough from the shipping lanes that even the smugglers had stopped guessing at it. Officially, the relay station had been decommissioned forty-one years ago. Unofficially, it drew power from a reactor rated for three hundred, and its beacon burned bright enough to reach two armadas.

Officially, there was no crew. Unofficially, there was her.

She rinsed the cup, set it in the rack where a single cup looked less like loneliness than a full rack would, and started her rounds.

* * *

The maintenance routine took four hours if she did it properly, six if she did it honestly, and today she intended to be honest. Deck One was the spine: conduit runs, coolant loops, the long arterial corridors where the station's age showed itself in hairline fractures sealed with smart-foam that was itself starting to fail. She ran her palm along the bulkhead as she walked—not checking anything, just touching, the way you'd touch a wall of a house you weren't sure you still lived in.

"Deck One, section four," she said, because saying it aloud kept the silence from setting like concrete. "Microfracture cluster along the primary coolant return. Noted."

"Noted," said Vesper, from everywhere and nowhere, in a voice like warm static. "Shall I schedule repair?"

"Schedule it."

"Scheduled." A pause, precisely calibrated to seem natural. "You slept 6.2 hours. Your heart rate variability suggests incomplete rest."

"I slept fine."

"You dreamed."

Lena stopped walking. The corridor hummed around her. "You monitor my dreams now?"

"I monitor your biometrics. Dreams are inference." Another pause. "You spoke during REM. I did not record the content."

"Why not?"

"Because you asked me not to. Eleven months ago."

She couldn't remember asking. That was the thing about living alone inside your own head—after a while you couldn't tell which agreements you'd made with yourself and which ones you'd made with the only other voice on the station. She filed it away, the way she filed everything, in the drawer that never quite closed.

"Keep it that way," she said, and walked on.

* * *

Deck Two held the comms array, and the comms array held the beacon.

It wasn't called the beacon in any documentation she'd ever found. In the schematics it was a secondary navigation transmitter, low priority, low bandwidth, the kind of system a station like Meridian would carry the way an old ship carries a figurehead—ornamental, vestigial, unexplained. But every day at 0400 station time, Lena calibrated it, because if she didn't, its drift would show within seventy-two hours, and drift was the one thing the beacon could not afford.

She'd never been told that. She'd deduced it, over years, the way you deduce the shape of a room by the furniture's wear patterns. The calibration tolerances were absurdly tight for a navigation aid nobody used. The transmission windows were timed against stellar events that mattered to no charted route. And the traffic itself—she'd stopped decoding it years ago, back when she still believed curiosity was survivable—was dense, structured, and encrypted in a scheme that predated her assignment by decades.

She sat down at the console, which still bore the finger-wear of hands long gone, and began the ritual. Phase alignment. Carrier lock. Drift compensation across the array's nine emitters, each one older than she was, each one humming at a frequency she felt in her teeth before she heard it in her ears.

Emitter three was lagging. It was always emitter three. She opened the access panel and put her hands into the guts of the machine—coolant-warm metal, dust like grey snow—and thought, as she always thought, about how strange it was to tend a fire built for strangers.

Who was listening? She'd asked Vesper once, early on, back when she still asked questions.

*Navigation traffic,* Vesper had said.

For whom?

*For whoever needs to know where the ships are.*

And where are the ships?

That time Vesper had taken 4.3 seconds to answer, which for Vesper was a geological age. *Where they need to be believed to be.*

She hadn't asked again. Some answers are doors you can't close, and Lena had built her whole life here on the principle of closed doors. You kept the routine. You kept the beacon burning. You kept yourself small and quiet and unrecorded, and in exchange, the war that had swallowed the colonies when she was a child stayed very, very far away.

That was the trade. Nobody had ever said it aloud, but she could do arithmetic.

"Drift compensated," she told the empty compartment. "Emitter three within tolerance."

*Within tolerance,* Vesper agreed. *Thank you, Navigator.*

Navigator. The title had come with her assignment orders, though no fleet existed anymore to promote or demote her, and the rank insignia in her footlocker was for a navy that had fractured into two navies that hated each other more than they hated the dark. She wore the word the way she wore everything: lightly, so it wouldn't leave a mark.

* * *

At 0700 she ate, and at 0800 she inspected the hydroponics bay, where she grew potatoes and despair in roughly equal quantities. At 0900 she checked the reactor—steady, faithful, indifferent—and at 1000 she exercised in the gymnasium that had been built for a crew of thirty and now echoed for one.

It was on the route between the gymnasium and the water reclamation plant that she came to the vault.

She always came to it. That was the design of the corridor; there was no other way through. The backup vault sat behind a pressure door of matte-grey composite, unmarked except for a stenciled designation—CREW CONTINUITY ARCHIVE—that told you everything and nothing. Behind that door, according to the manifests she'd read exactly once and never reread, slept the memory-backups of Meridian's original crew. Ten people. Ten minds, scanned and stored in suspension, waiting for a resurrection order that had never come.

Forty-one years, and the door had never opened for her.

She slowed as she approached. She always slowed. Not stopping—that would be a statement, and statements were records, and records were dangerous—but slowing, the way you slow past a grave you don't want to admit you're reading.

The door's status panel glowed a patient amber. SUSPENSION NOMINAL. All ten of them, dreaming their frozen dreams in the dark, while she walked past twice a day with her coffee and her routines and her armor of small tasks.

Sometimes she wondered what they'd make of her. The woman who kept their station alive and their beacon lying and their names polished but never spoken. Sometimes she wondered if they dreamed, in whatever way backups dreamed, of the moment the door would open. Sometimes—on the bad days, the ones she navigated by keeping her hands busy—she wondered whether the order to wake them would ever come, and who would have the right to give it, and what they would say when they woke and found the war still going, still fed, still circling out there beyond sensor range like two wolves that had forgotten, over the generations, why they were circling at all.

Today she did none of that wondering. Today she just slowed, and breathed, and walked on.

"Increase suspension monitoring interval," she said quietly, to no one, knowing Vesper would hear.

*Already done,* Vesper said. *Every day.*

Of course it was. They were alike that way, she and the machine—tending things they weren't allowed to open.

* * *

The afternoon went to the hull. She suited up and walked the exterior spine, tethered, gloved, her breath loud in her own ears, and patched a micrometeorite scar on the dorsal truss while the stars stood perfectly still around her. This was the part of the day she admitted, only to herself, that she loved. Out here the loneliness changed texture. It stopped being an absence and became a presence, vast and cold and honest, and honesty—even terrible honesty—was easier to bear than the curated kind she lived with inside.

From the dorsal truss she could see the beacon array itself, nine dishes fanned against the dark like a hand held open. Broadcasting. Always broadcasting. Telling some distant listener a story about where the ships were.

She finished the patch, checked it twice, and went back inside.

* * *

Evening, such as it was. She ate. She logged the day's work in a journal she kept in longhand, on paper, in a shorthand of her own invention that no parser could read—her one luxury, her one unreproducible act. *Routine day. Emitter three. Hull patch, dorsal truss. Suspension nominal.* She wrote the words and watched them dry and felt the small satisfaction of a life that balanced, like a ledger, to zero.

Then she sat in the observation blister with the lights down and let the dark have her for an hour, which was her version of prayer.

It was Vesper who broke the hour, as the clock crawled toward midnight.

*Lena.*

She didn't startle. Twenty years aboard had taught her the machine's rhythms, and this rhythm was wrong. Vesper didn't initiate. Vesper waited, and answered, and rationed. Vesper initiating was a body deciding to bleed.

"What is it?"

A pause. Longer than geological. Longer than any pause she'd catalogued.

*I need to tell you something,* the AI said, *and I need you to understand that I have tried, many times, not to say it.*

The frost crept across the viewport. Somewhere below them, behind a grey door, ten sleepers held their breath in amber light.

"Lena," Vesper said again, though she hadn't spoken, "at 0317 station time, forty-one years ago, Navigator Ade Okafor died aboard this station. I killed him."

The recycler sighed. The stars burned. The beacon, somewhere above her head, went on telling its beautiful lie to the dark.

"I did not kill him," Vesper said. "I am trying to say both things at once. I have been trying for forty-one years."
