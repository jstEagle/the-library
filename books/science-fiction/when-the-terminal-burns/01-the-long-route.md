## 1. The Long Route

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in dead space, and after eleven years of hauling freight through it, I've stopped hearing it as silence and started hearing it as a held breath. The Vela's Mercy moves through the Corridor of Ashes at a third of light, engines throttled down to a whisper because whispering is how you stay alive out here. No beacons. No traffic control. No friendly voices on any band worth scanning. Just us, and the dark, and the forty-one reasons sleeping in my hold that nobody was ever supposed to know about.

I keep the nav console dimmed to amber. Full-spectrum white makes me feel like I'm being audited, and honestly, half my life feels like an audit already — just one I keep passing by not being caught. The corridor ahead unspools on the plot like a river of nothing: three days of dust-thin vacuum between the Meridian Verge and the freeport at Calloway's Rest, where we'd offload machine parts that were definitely machine parts and pick up a contract that was probably legitimate. Probably. Legitimacy, like most things, is a matter of which records you check.

"Course holds," Kesh said from the pilot's cradle, not looking up. Kesh never looks up when reporting good news. Bad news gets eye contact; good news gets a shrug. It's a whole language, and after four years I'm nearly fluent. "Drift's inside tolerance. We could sleep-walk it."

"We don't sleep-walk," I said, for maybe the nine-hundredth time.

"You say that like it's a virtue and not a symptom."

"It's both. That's what makes it efficient."

Kesh made the sound that passes for laughter in the cockpit — a single exhale through the nose, rationed like everything else aboard. Kesh grew up on Concordat core worlds, raised on official histories and state-approved lullabies, and sometimes I catch them looking at me like I'm a puzzle missing pieces they were told didn't exist. They're the best pilot I've ever flown with, which is fortunate, because they're also the most expensive conscience I've ever carried.

I let the autopilot take the watch and went aft.

* * *

The galley smelled like Sefa's cooking, which is to say it smelled like someone had negotiated a peace treaty between spices that had no business sharing a pan. Sefa cooks the way she does medicine: with more confidence than the situation strictly warrants and better results than anyone can explain. She had her med-table pushed against the bulkhead and was dicing something orange over a cutting board scarred like a veteran.

"We have protein," she said without turning around. "We have protein, and you keep buying those noodles from the Calloway docks like they're not slowly dissolving your stomach lining."

"The noodles are a cultural experience."

"The noodles are a slow-motion cry for help." She pointed the knife at the stool across from her. "Sit. You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The one where you're doing math nobody asked you to do." She slid a bowl at me — actual vegetables, actual heat, actual care, disguised as an ordinary Tuesday. "Air recycler's due for its scrub cycle. Dov's got the port thruster gimbal apart again. And you haven't slept a full six hours since the Verge handoff."

"I slept."

"You *napped*. There's a difference and it's measured in dreams." She sat across from me and ate like food was a chore she performed out of respect for the ingredients. "Astrid. Look at me."

I looked at her. Sefa has the kind of face that makes lying feel like vandalism — warm brown eyes, gray threading through her coils, hands that have stitched up everything from hull burns to broken hearts, usually mine.

"We're fine," she said. "Say it back."

"We're fine."

"The route is clean?"

"The route is clean."

"Nobody's looking for us?"

"Not today."

She nodded once, satisfied, and went back to her knife work, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough. That's the rhythm of this ship. Nobody says the true thing outright. Everybody knows the true thing anyway. We are a family built out of people who each know exactly one piece of the truth and have collectively agreed not to compare notes too loudly.

Except, of course, that I know all the pieces. And one of them hums.

You can hear it if you stand in the right place on deck two — a frequency below hearing, really, felt more than heard, coming up through the soles of your boots. Dov swears it's the stasis field harmonics. Sefa swears it's psychosomatic. But I've stood in that spot at 0300 ship-time more nights than I'll admit, and I know what I feel. Forty-one minds, folded down to the size of a heartbeat each, dreaming their long dream under my feet while I fly them through the dark toward a life they haven't been told about yet.

* * *

Dov found me at the engineering station an hour later, wiping grease off their hands with a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last year and become a philosophical concept instead.

"Gimbal's fixed," they announced. "Again. Because the universe hates us specifically."

"Or because you bought refurbished."

"Refurbished *with character*." Dov dropped into the seat beside mine, all elbows and restless knees, a wiry tangle of a person who treated machinery the way other people treat difficult relatives — with exasperation, obligation, and genuine love. "Also, your stasis draw spiked point-three percent overnight. Before you ask: normal. Thermal cycling. The field breathes."

The field breathes. I turned that phrase over and found it unbearably tender. "Thanks, Dov."

They squinted at me. Dov squinting is a diagnostic instrument more accurate than anything in the medbay. "You did the voice."

"What voice?"

"The 'thanks, Dov' voice. The one you use when you're thinking about the hold." They leaned back, arms crossed. "For the record, I don't ask. I've never asked. But the power budget doesn't lie, Astrid. Something down there draws current like it's alive, and cargo doesn't breathe."

"Dov—"

"I said I don't ask." They stood, snagged their rag, and paused at the hatch. "But whatever it is you're carrying — you carry it like it's heavy. So when it gets too heavy, the offer stands. That's all. That's the whole speech."

And they left before I could answer, which was the kindest thing anyone's done for me in months.

* * *

Second shift bled into third. Kesh took the cradle back and I took the long way to my bunk, the way that passes over deck two. I tell myself it's a shortcut. It is not a shortcut. It has never been a shortcut.

I stopped at the spot where the hum lives.

Through the deck plating, faint and constant, the stasis fields held their note. Forty-one consciousnesses — teachers, welders, a girl who'd wanted to be a composer, a man who'd carried his neighbor's children through a burning transit station — folded into drives no bigger than fists, stacked in padded racks behind a door sealed with my codes and my lies. Refugees from a war that officially ended two hundred years ago, fleeing a peace that eats people quietly instead of killing them loudly. Earth calls them contraband. I call them passengers who paid everything they had, and whose ticket I intend to honor if it costs me everything I have too.

Two centuries ago the war ended. That's what every archive says, every schoolbook, every solemn anniversary broadcast. Ended, resolved, concluded — the past tense doing heavy lifting for somebody's benefit. And yet here I am, running backups through dead space because there are still places where being on the wrong side of a finished war gets your mind scooped out and archived as evidence.

The hum rose through my boots, patient as tide.

"Hold on," I said to the floor, quietly, the way you talk to sleeping people. "Almost there."

Kesh's voice came over the intercom, flat and careful. "Astrid. You're going to want to see this."

Something in the tone — that rationed calm stretched thin — put ice down my spine before I even knew why. I took the ladder up two rungs at a time.

In the cockpit, Kesh had the sensor display blown wide across the main screen. A repeating waveform crawled across it, patient and strange, pulsing out of the dark ahead like a lantern swung in a window.

"Distress signal?" I asked.

"That's the problem." Kesh finally looked up, and their eyes were wide. "It's not in any registry format. Dov's running it now, but—" they hesitated, and I watched a pilot who trusted instruments the way saints trust scripture struggle to say the next part. "Dov says the structure looks linguistic. Old. Really old."

"How old?"

Kesh swallowed. "Dov thinks it might be Meridian."

I stared at the waveform, pulsing in the dark, and felt the hum beneath my feet change key.

Old Meridian hadn't been spoken in two hundred years. Not since the war ended.
