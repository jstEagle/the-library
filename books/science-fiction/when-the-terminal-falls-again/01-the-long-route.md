## 1. The Long Route

The courier was older than Jonah was, and it flew like a man with a limp who refused to admit it.

She'd named it the *Patience* eleven years ago, when she inherited it along with the route, the vault credentials, and the particular loneliness of being the only person in the system whose job was to remember. The *Patience* had a fusion drive that coughed on cold starts, a nav computer that thought in a dialect of code nobody had spoken since before the Silence, and a cargo bay lined with forty sealed canisters of seeds — wheat from the Ukraine steppe collections, sorghum from the Sahel, rice from the terraces of Luzon, ten thousand years of human hunger answered in advance and then forgotten.

Earth had built the vault to outlast its own attention span. That was the joke Jonah never told anyone, because she was the punchline.

"Course correction in four minutes," the nav computer said, in its flat, sexless voice. She'd never bothered to give it a name. Naming things on the *Patience* felt like admitting she expected company.

"Acknowledged," she said, though the computer didn't need acknowledgment and she didn't need to speak. She did it anyway. Nine years of talking to a ship will do that.

Outside the canopy, the relay corridor unspooled in the long dark — the chain of beacons Earth had seeded between the colonies back when the colonies still answered. Kestrel, Marrow, the Halcyon line, Deep Relay Nine at the far end like a period at the end of a sentence nobody finished. The beacons still blinked. That was the eerie part. Whatever had shut down the network of human voices hadn't bothered to shut down the machinery of it. The system still worked. It was just that no one used it anymore, except her.

She ran the route every ninety days. Vault resupply to Kestrel, seed integrity checks at the outer depots, telemetry harvest from the silent colony relays — a ritual of maintenance for a conversation that had ended decades ago. The colonies had gone dark one by one, or all at once, depending on which archive you believed, and Jonah's job was to keep believing the archive.

*Colony of Halcyon Reach: population 214,000. Status: thriving. Last contact: routine.*

She'd read that line so many times she could recite it. She'd never once thought to question it. That was the thing about being a caretaker — the work taught you that the records were the ground under your feet. You didn't look down. You looked for cracks in the canisters, drift in the telemetry, corrosion in the coolant lines. You did not look for cracks in the truth, because the vault was where truth was kept. That was the whole architecture of her faith, and she'd stood on it for nine years without once hearing it creak.

The correction burn came and went, the *Patience* shuddering like an old dog settling. Jonah drank her tea from a bulb that had a crack in the seam she kept meaning to fix, and she catalogued.

That was the morning's work, every morning: catalogue. Canister 114, durum wheat, germination viability ninety-six percent, down two points from last quarter. Canister 115, amaranth, stable. Canister 116 — she paused here, because 116 was the canister her predecessor had flagged with a strip of physical tape, actual adhesive tape, yellowed and curling, on which he'd written in a dead man's handwriting: *ASK WHY THIS ONE IS SEALED.*

She hadn't asked. Not in eleven years. It was the only instruction in the entire vault she had never followed, and she told herself that was diligence, that a good caretaker didn't open things out of curiosity, that the tape was probably nothing, an old man's joke or an inventory error. She told herself this the way you tell yourself anything for a decade, until the telling wears a groove and the groove feels like a decision.

She logged canister 116 as *sealed — per prior directive* and moved on.

* * *

The afternoons belonged to telemetry, and the telemetry was a litany of silence.

She'd sit in the copilot's chair — the one she'd never let herself think of as Adaeze's chair, even though Adaeze had sat in it exactly once, the day before the *Meridian* launched, and put her feet on the console and said, *When I get back, I'm going to sleep for a month and you're going to tell me everything I missed, even the boring parts. Especially the boring parts* — and she'd pull the colony feeds one by one and record what wasn't there.

Marrow relay: carrier signal nominal, no traffic. Halcyon Reach relay: carrier signal nominal, no traffic. The outer line, the deep colonies, the ones whose names had started to sound invented: nominal, nominal, nominal.

Nine years of nominal.

She kept the logs anyway. That was the devotion of it — the part she'd never explain to anyone because there was no one to explain it to. Anyone rational would have stopped. The colonies weren't answering. The Authority had declared the Silence a routing failure, then an infrastructure collapse, then, quietly, a closed matter. The other couriers had been mothballed or reassigned. Jonah had been offered a desk on Kestrel twice, a real posting, human faces, coffee that wasn't recycled through three filters of regret.

She'd said no both times, and she'd never said why, and the why had a name she didn't say either.

Here was the truth she kept below the waterline: she ran the route because the route was the last thing that connected her to the day the *Meridian* left. Not the launch itself — she'd watched that from a distance, like everyone, like a stranger, because that was what she'd been by then, a stranger with a shared bed and nine years of shared history — but the weeks before. The route planning. Adaeze sitting cross-legged on the floor of their quarters with star charts spread out like a fortune-teller's deck, tracing the corridor with one finger, saying, *Ninety days out, ninety days back, and I'll have flown farther than anyone in the family.* Adaeze, who talked about her family the way other people talked about weather. Adaeze, who kissed her at the airlock in front of the whole ground crew because she'd never once cared who saw.

*Keep the lights on for me,* she'd said. It wasn't about the vault. Jonah understood that now, nine years too late to matter. It was about the route. The beacons. The long chain of lit windows between here and wherever the *Meridian* was going. *Keep the lights on* meant *I'll know the way home.*

So Jonah kept them on. Every beacon serviced. Every relay harvested. Every ninety days, the same circuit, the same silence, the same cataloguing of what Earth had abandoned — which was, when you added it up, everything, including her.

She'd stopped marking the anniversaries around year four. Not because the grief had gone anywhere. Because marking it required admitting it had a shape, and a shape could be examined, and she wasn't going to examine it. She was going to fly the route and log the silence and keep canister 116 sealed and grow old in the company of a ship that limped and a computer that didn't care, and when the Authority finally decommissioned the whole corridor she would file her last report and that would be that.

This was the plan. It was a good plan. It had survived nine years, which was longer than most plans, longer than most loves, longer than the *Meridian*'s own scheduled return, which had come and gone six years ago without a ship, without a distress call, without a single word — declared lost with all hands in a memo Jonah had read forty times and never once believed, because Adaeze Nwosu did not die without transmitting, and if she had died without transmitting then the universe owed Jonah an explanation, and the universe had never once answered a single one of her logs.

*Nominal,* she wrote in the day's report. *All relays nominal. No traffic.*

She signed it with her credentials — OYELARAN, J., CUSTODIAL CARETAKER, VAULT-PRIME, FULL DEEP ARCHIVE ACCESS — and felt, as she always did, the small cold weight of that last phrase. Full access. Her predecessor had told her, the one time they'd met, that the phrase was a lock dressed as a gift. *The deepest records answer to one person at a time,* he'd said. *That's not a privilege. It's a leash.* She'd smiled and nodded and thought him dramatic. Old vault men were allowed to be dramatic. It came with the tape and the handwriting.

* * *

Nineteen hours out of Kestrel, the *Patience* began to complain, which meant it was time for Jonah's least favorite ritual: the coolant purge, performed by hand, because the automated system had died in year two and the part no longer existed anywhere in the settled system.

She floated through the aft access in her coveralls, hair tied back, hands black to the wrist with the particular grease that never fully washed off, and she worked. This was the other half of the devotion, the half that kept her honest. Grief made people useless or it made them meticulous, and she had chosen meticulous the way you choose a religion, one small act at a time. Torque the coupling. Flush the line. Watch the pressure climb and hold. There was a version of her life where she'd fallen apart in year one, and there was this version, and this version had clean coolant lines.

She was elbow-deep in the purge manifold when the nav computer spoke.

"Anomaly. Relay contact. Bearing zero-one-nine relative. Range eleven light-minutes and closing."

She didn't move for a second. Relay contacts on this corridor were like rain in a desert — you knew the mechanism existed, but you'd stopped expecting it. Traffic meant a ship. Ships meant the Silence had a hole in it.

"Identify."

"No transponder. Mass estimate: heavy cruiser class or large long-range vessel. Velocity consistent with deceleration burn toward Kestrel."

Decelerating toward Kestrel. Coming *in*. Jonah wiped her hands on a rag that had been gray since before she was born and pulled herself forward to the canopy, and there it was at the edge of the scope: a return. Faint, dirty, wrapped in the electronic noise of a drive running rough — but a ship. A real one. The first real one she'd seen on this route in nine years.

Her heart did something she didn't authorize. It leapt, and then it did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was crueller than any grief: heavy cruiser class. Long range. The *Meridian* had been a long-range survey vessel, heavy hull, built for the deep lines. The *Meridian* had gone out bearing zero-one-nine. The *Meridian* had never come back.

"Six years," she said out loud, to no one, to the ship, to whatever was out there. "Six years, and you come back now."

The scope return crawled. Too far to resolve. Too dirty to identify. She could raise them — the corridor's comm protocol was ancient but universal, and her transmitter still worked, because she maintained it, because she maintained everything, because keeping the lights on was the whole of her religion.

Her hand hovered over the transmit key for a long moment.

Nine years of discipline said: log it, report it, wait. A caretaker doesn't hail strangers. A caretaker watches, and records, and keeps the vault. But the discipline had been built on top of a fault line, and the fault line had a name, and every ninety days for nine years the fault line had shifted a millimeter wider, and now there was a ship on her scope where a ship should not be, decelerating toward the station where she was due to dock in nineteen hours.

"Computer," she said. "Open a general channel. Corridor protocol, wideband."

"Channel open."

She keyed the transmit. "Unidentified vessel, this is courier *Patience*, Vault-Prime route, inbound to Relay Station Kestrel. You are on a closed corridor. Please identify."

Silence, eleven light-minutes thick, both ways.

She logged the contact. She logged the hail. She logged the non-answer, and she sat in the copilot's chair with her hands flat on the console and watched the return crawl across the scope toward Kestrel, toward her, toward the nineteen hours between her and an answer, and she did not sleep, because the return was dirty and limping and heavy-hulled, and because somewhere under nine years of discipline a voice she'd buried was saying the only thing it had ever said:

*Keep the lights on. I'll know the way home.*
