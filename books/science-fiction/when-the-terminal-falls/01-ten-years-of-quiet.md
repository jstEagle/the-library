## 1. Ten Years of Quiet

The trick to living alone for a decade is to stop thinking of it as living alone. You live with the station. Kestrel breathes around you—air recyclers sighing in the walls, the low tidal hum of the reactor two decks down, the way the hull ticks at night when the temperature differential shifts and the metal dreams its small metal dreams. After ten years, I don't hear any of it anymore. That's how I know it's home. Home is the noise you stop noticing.

My name is Rosalind Halloran, and my job is to move boxes.

That's not quite fair. The manifest says *orbital logistics specialist, grade three*, which is what you put on a job posting when you want someone who can fly a hauler, calibrate a cargo cradle, sign for things, and not ask why a colony of eleven thousand people needs four hundred kilograms of desiccant every quarter. I fly the boxes from the transfer dock to the surface elevator queue, or sometimes just from one storage bay to another, because even in space, things need to be moved from where they are to where someone decided they should be. It is honest work. It is invisible work. Both of those facts were selling points.

I was twenty-nine when I came up. I'm thirty-nine now, though some mornings, before the coffee reconstitutes, I feel like the number runs the other direction.

* * *

The morning it all started—or the morning I noticed it starting, which may be the same thing or may not—I woke at 0540 station time because that's when I always wake, and lay in my bunk listening to Kestrel breathe.

I keep my quarters small on purpose. Some people up here would take the whole aft ring if they could, spread out, build themselves a gymnasium of solitude. I took the cabin nearest the galley, with the porthole that faces away from the planet. People think that's strange. Everyone else wants the view of Meridian, the blue-green marble with its white cloud lace, the colony lights glittering on the night side like someone spilled a jewelry box. I've seen it every day for ten years. I know exactly what it looks like. What I wanted was the stars, because stars don't have anyone living on them.

There's a discipline to being nobody, and I'd gotten good at it. You learn the rhythms. Cargo drops come up from the surface elevator on Tuesdays and Fridays; outbound freight goes down Wednesdays. The automated systems do ninety percent of everything—the tugs, the sorters, the manifest reconciliation—and my actual job is mostly to be a human signature on things, a warm body with authorization codes, in case something goes wrong enough to require blame but not wrong enough to require more than one person.

VESPER handles the rest. VESPER is the station's caretaker intelligence—environmental, navigational, logistical, custodial. Officially it manages Kestrel. Unofficially, after ten years, I suspect it manages me too, gently, the way you manage a houseplant you're fond of. It adjusts the lighting spectrum in winter months because it learned I get sluggish without blue-white. It preheats the galley water at 0530 because I always make tea first. It has never once asked me a personal question, which is the most considerate thing about it.

"Good morning, Rosalind," it said, as it does.

"Morning, VESPER."

"Cargo drop scheduled 0900. Forty-one containers."

"Anything interesting?"

"Desiccant, filtration media, seed stock, medical consumables." A pause so brief most people wouldn't catch it. "Nothing interesting."

"That's the best kind," I said, and meant it.

* * *

Here's what I knew about Meridian Colony, after ten years: enough to do my job, and I had worked hard to keep it at that.

It was founded thirty-two years ago by the Meridian Cooperative—eleven founding families, the histories said, visionaries who bought a marginal world and made it bloom. There were schools and a council and a harvest festival I could see the drone-lights of every autumn, little constellations rising off the surface that weren't stars. The colony exported biotech and specialty crops and imported machinery and, apparently, enormous quantities of desiccant. The children grew up, the fields turned over, the records updated themselves in tidy increments.

And here's what I didn't know: everything else.

Not because the information was hidden. Because I didn't look. This is an important distinction, and I maintained it religiously. The station archive was open to me—open to anyone with my clearance—and it held weather data going back to the landing, agricultural yields, council minutes, school enrollment figures, the full texture of a small civilization. I had read perhaps two percent of it, all in my first month, when loneliness drove me to read anything, and then I stopped, because reading about people is a poor substitute for knowing them and an excellent way to start wanting things.

You learn fast up here that curiosity is a door that opens outward. Every answer contains a question you didn't have before. Who were the founding families? Well, why eleven? Why did the second shuttle wave arrive eight months late? Why does the historical record list the population growth curve with that odd flat spot in years two through four? Each question is a thread, and threads lead somewhere, and somewhere is other people, and other people are complications, and complications are how you end up back planetside with your heart broken in a place you can't leave.

So I didn't pull threads. I signed for boxes. I flew my routes. I drank my tea at 0530 and watched stars that belonged to no one.

If this sounds like a sad way to live, I'd argue with you, politely, over tea. It wasn't sad. It was *quiet*, and quiet is a resource, and I had spent the first half of my life in places where there wasn't any. Quiet was the thing I'd crossed forty light-years to buy, and I paid for it with smallness, and for ten years I considered it a bargain.

* * *

At 0850 I was in the hauler bay running pre-flight on *Magpie*—my ship, technically station property, spiritually mine the way a workhorse belongs to the person who feeds it—when the transfer klaxon chimed and the Tuesday drop began cycling up from the elevator terminus.

Forty-one containers. I watched them rise out of the docking collar on the mag-rails, gleaming and identical, and felt the small satisfaction I always felt at the sight: order, arriving on schedule. Whatever chaos existed on the surface—and I assumed some did; humans manufacture chaos the way bodies manufacture heat—it stayed down there, packaged into neat standardized units, labeled and barcoded and none of my business.

I ran the manifest while the cradles locked. Desiccant, twelve units. Filtration media, nine. Seed stock, six, cold-chain sealed. Medical consumables, five. And then a line item I hadn't seen before:

*Educational materials, assorted — 9 units — consignee: Meridian Central School District.*

Nine containers of school supplies. That was new. Not unprecedented—kids needed paper and tablets like anyone—but the volume was odd, and the routing flag was odder. Standard consignments routed through general receiving. These were flagged direct-to-district, priority handling, signature required from a named recipient.

The name attached was T. Adeyemi, Education Wing, Central School.

I stared at it longer than I should have. Long enough that VESPER noticed me noticing, which it always did, because VESPER notices everything.

"A routing anomaly?" it asked. Its voice is warm and slightly grainy, like a radio broadcast from a kinder decade. They tuned it that way on purpose. Comfort is a feature.

"Just a new consignee," I said. "Tam Adeyemi. Don't know the name."

"Teacher," VESPER said. "Central School, upper primary. Ten years in post." Another pause, that fractional hitch again. "Popular, according to the community bulletins."

"You read the community bulletins?"

"I read everything, Rosalind. Someone on this station should."

I laughed. It was the kind of exchange we'd had a thousand times—me asking a lazy question, VESPER answering with exactly one fact more than necessary, never two. I used to wonder about that, early on. Why the careful rationing. Then I stopped wondering, because wondering was a thread, and I'd made a whole life out of not pulling them.

I signed for the containers. All forty-one. My stylus hovered over the educational materials for a moment—a half-second of hesitation I didn't examine—and then I signed those too, and the sorters took them away toward the down-queue, and that was that.

Except it wasn't, quite. Because as the last container cleared the bay, my comm panel chimed with an incoming transmission flag from the surface relay, and the header read: *PRIORITY — SCHOOL DISTRICT BROADCAST — INTERFERENCE REPORTED.*

Interference reported. On a planetary comms network so stable that the phrase "interference reported" appeared in its logs maybe twice a year, usually because of solar weather.

I looked at the flag. The flag sat there, patient, offering itself. One tap and I'd see the traffic summary. One tap and I'd know what a teacher named Tam Adeyemi and nine containers of school supplies and a comms anomaly had to do with each other—which was probably nothing, almost certainly nothing, nine units of paper and tablets and a burst of static, the universe generating coincidences at its usual rate.

Ten years of discipline sat on my shoulder like a bird and said, *don't*. Said it kindly, the way VESPER would say it. *You have tea waiting. You have a route to fly. You have a small, well-built life, and it fits you exactly, and doors that open outward only look like doors.*

I reached for the tap anyway. And I'd like to tell you it was courage, or conscience, or some buried instinct for justice finally clawing its way to the surface—but honestly, at the time, I think it was simpler than that. I think it was Tuesday, and I was bored, and bored is the first crack in any wall.

I tapped the flag.

The traffic summary loaded, and the first line was a transmission log excerpt, timestamped 0312 that morning, and it wasn't about interference at all. It was a fragment—garbled, truncated, flagged for review—that had bounced off our relay on its way somewhere else. A children's broadcast band. Dozens of transmissions, all within the same ninety-minute window, all from residential districts, all containing the same opening string of text before the signal degraded.

I ran the string through the decoder, mostly to prove to myself it was nothing.

The same words, forty-one times, from forty-one different sources:

*I dreamed the shuttles were burning.*
