## 1. Cargo of Quiet Things

The manifest said agricultural seed stock, four hundred kilos, low priority. The manifest was a lie the way most of Sebastian Dravenmoor's life had become a lie: carefully constructed, boring on its face, and load-bearing in ways no inspector would ever think to check.

He ran the haul anyway, because running hauls was what he did now, and because a man who asked questions about his own cargo stopped being invisible very quickly.

*The Kestrel's Lament* — a name he'd chosen precisely because it sounded like a thousand other ships in the colonial registry, half-mournful, half-generic — slid through the debris belt at a respectful fraction of burn, her hull pocked and repainted so many times she looked like what she pretended to be: old, tired, owned by a man with no ambitions worth auditing. Sebastian kept the transponder chirping its dull litany. Origin: Halcyon Ring, Spur Fourteen. Destination: Meridian Reach, Docking Cradle Nine. Cargo: seeds. Crew: one.

One was important. One meant no one to lie to but himself, and he'd gotten good at that.

Outside the viewport, the contested orbits turned their slow violence. This stretch between the Ring and the Reach belonged to nobody and everybody — patrol drones from three different authorities swept it in overlapping arcs like gulls fighting over a harbor, and every cargo pilot who worked the lane knew the choreography by heart. Stay small. Stay slow. Stay forgettable. The drones didn't want trouble; they wanted paperwork, and paperwork wanted names, and names wanted histories, and histories were the one thing Sebastian Dravenmoor could not afford to have.

He adjusted his drift by two-tenths of a degree and thought about nothing, which was a skill, too.

His cabin smelled of recycled air and instant tea and the faint chemical sweetness of hull sealant he'd never gotten around to replacing. A paperback novel sat spine-cracked on the console — actual paper, salvaged, its title worn away — because reading gave his hands something to do during the long burns and gave any casual observer the impression of a man with small, harmless appetites. He'd read the same forty pages eleven times. The words went into his eyes and pooled there without meaning anything, the way rain pools on tarpaulin.

That was the thing about his dreams. That was the thing he circled every night and never landed on.

He used to dream in language. Everyone does, or believes they do — the murmuring committee of the sleeping mind, narrating, arguing, rehearsing arguments it would never dare have awake. For years after the decoding, after the Meridian signal had come apart under his analysis like a lock surrendering to the right key, his dreams had been *bilingual*. He'd walk through dream-cities where the signage flickered between English and that other grammar, the one made of pressure and interval, the one that didn't describe the world so much as *tune* it. He'd wake with syllables dissolving on his tongue like snowflakes, grieving them before they were fully gone.

Then, somewhere in the five years since — he couldn't name the day; there was no anniversary for a loss like this — the human words had gone too.

Now he dreamed wordless. Not silent — silence would have been a mercy. His dreams were full of *structure*, of shapes that insisted on being understood without offering themselves to understanding. He dreamed in diagrams he couldn't read, in rooms whose dimensions were sentences, in the sensation of being a phrase in a document someone else was editing. He woke with his jaw aching from clenching around speech that never came, lying in his bunk in the dark, thinking: *I am a translator who has lost both tongues.*

He'd told himself once that this was healing. That the signal had finally finished leaving him, the way shrapnel works its way out or doesn't. But some nights, drifting over the debris fields with the autopilot humming its idiot lullaby, he suspected the truth was worse: the language hadn't left. It had simply stopped bothering to translate itself *for him*, and what remained was the raw architecture underneath — the part of Meridian that had never been meant for human mouths at all.

He was still turning this over, the way you turn a stone you've turned ten thousand times, when the proximity alarm spoke.

Not an alarm, exactly. The *Kestrel's Lament* didn't believe in drama. A soft tone, amber light along the console's lower edge, and a schematic blooming: a contact, closing fast, transponder pinging the standard challenge. Patrol drone, Meridian Reach authority, registry prefix VR-7. It came out of the sun's glare like a wasp out of smoke, all sensor arrays and assumption.

Sebastian's hands moved before his fear did. Throttle down. Attitude adjust, presenting the broad, dull flank of a working freighter. He keyed the response channel and let the ship answer for him — the automated handshake, credentials offered in the flat cadence of routine. *Agricultural transport. Routine haul. Nothing here.*

The drone held station eight hundred meters off his starboard bow. Close enough that he could see its sensor clusters tracking him, close enough that its optical suite was almost certainly resolving his face through the viewport, matching it against whatever databases the Reach authority fed it these days. Five years ago, his face had been on every feed in three systems — the xenolinguist, the decoder, the man who'd heard the stars speak and told humanity what they said. Then the hearings, the committees, the quiet decision that a man who understood the Meridians' grammar too well might understand *other* things too well, things governments preferred to keep monolingual. He'd traded his name for a registry number and his reputation for a forklift certification, and it had worked, it had *worked*, for five years —

The drone's challenge changed.

It wasn't much. A second handshake request, identical protocol, which happened sometimes when packets dropped. But beneath it, on a channel the drone shouldn't have been using for traffic control, a brief data burst — encrypted, tight-beamed, gone in under a second. Sebastian watched it scroll past in the log and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear of death but from fear of *recognition*.

He knew that burst pattern. Not its contents — encryption saw to that — but its *shape*. Short, structured, rhythmic. Someone, somewhere behind that drone, had flagged the *Kestrel's Lament* and was asking a question about her captain.

Or he was paranoid. Paranoia was occupational. You didn't spend seven years inside an alien signal and come out the other side trusting coincidence.

"Routine," he said aloud, to the empty cabin, to the paperback, to the seeds that weren't seeds. "This is routine."

His voice sounded wrong in the recycled air. Too loud. Too *worded*.

The drone hung there for ninety seconds — he counted, because counting was something his mind could do instead of spiraling — and then, with the bureaucratic indifference of all machinery, it broke off and burned back toward its patrol arc, its transponder fading into the general chatter of the lane. Cleared. Or deferred. With drones, those were often the same thing.

Sebastian exhaled and discovered his hands were shaking, and hated them for it, and made tea anyway because ritual was ballast.

* * *

Docking Cradle Nine at Meridian Reach smelled of rust and hydroponic runoff, and the dockmaster — a heavyset woman named Ferro who'd stopped asking questions about anyone years ago — waved him through with two fingers off her tablet.

"Lament," she said. "You're early."

"Wanted to beat the traffic." Traffic. In an orbital lane with eleven scheduled movements a week. Ferro snorted, and that was the whole conversation, and it was perfect.

He unloaded the crates himself. Inside them, under the seed-stock labeling, sat components — filtration membranes, mostly, and a crate of medical imaging cores bound for a clinic in the Reach's lower rings that billed everything as agricultural to dodge the import tariffs. Smuggling, technically. The gentlest kind: smuggling *toward* people. He'd built his whole cover story on the principle that the best lie is a small kindness with paperwork attached.

Halfway through the third pallet, his comm chimed. Unknown channel, local band, text only. No sender ID.

**DRAVENMOOR.**

Just the name. He stood in the cradle's yellow work-light with a crate of contraband dialysis parts in his arms and read the single word until the letters stopped looking like letters.

Then a second message:

**THEY'RE ASKING ABOUT YOU AT THE SCHOOL.**

He set the crate down very carefully, as if it were sleeping.

The school. Mara Okonkwo's school, down in the residential ring — he knew of it the way he knew of everything in the Reach, through manifests and gossip and the careful peripheral vision of a man pretending not to look. He'd never been inside. He'd never let himself be inside anywhere children were, because children asked questions with none of an adult's tact, and because — though he'd never written this thought down, never spoken it, barely permitted it to exist — something in his wordless dreams flinched toward schools the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

Someone knew who he was. Someone had known long enough to watch him, and now someone was telling him — warning him? herding him? — that whatever was happening at the school involved him whether he consented or not.

He typed back: **Who is this?**

The channel stayed open for a moment. He could almost feel the hesitation on the other end, the weight of someone deciding how much truth a stranger could carry.

Then:

**COME HEAR WHAT THE CHILDREN ARE SAYING IN THEIR SLEEP.**

**YOU'RE THE ONLY ONE WHO WILL KNOW WHAT IT IS.**

The comm went dark. Out in the lane beyond the cradle's forcefield, a hauler burned for the outer docks, and the patrol drones wheeled their patient circles, and somewhere below in the residential ring, six children were presumably doing whatever children did — eating, squabbling, learning their letters — carrying inside their skulls a language that had taken Sebastian seven years and everything he had to decode, and that had, in the end, decoded *him* instead.

He looked at his hands. Steady now. Competent hands. Hauler's hands.

They had identified the burst pattern from the drone in under a second, he realized. They had known the shape of it the way he'd once known the shape of the Meridian signal — instantly, totally, before thought could intervene. Whatever was happening at that school, whatever the children were saying in their sleep, some part of him had already begun translating it.

And he hadn't heard a single word yet.

Sebastian closed the cargo bay, logged a fictional delivery delay with Ferro, and went to find out what he already knew.
