## 1. The Custodian of Nothing

The beacon sang at 0600 station time, the way it always did, and Oscar Marchetti woke to it the way other men woke to birdsong.

Not that there were birds out here. There was nothing out here — that was rather the point of the place. Dead space, the charts called it: a wedge of nothing some four hundred thousand kilometers across where no star burned bright enough to navigate by and no shipping lane had ever bothered to curve. Beacon Station sat in the middle of it like a lighthouse built on open ocean, marking a hazard that no one could name and no one had ever struck.

Oscar floated out of his bunk in the narrow crew module, pulled himself along the guide rail toward the galley, and let the station's morning sounds wash over him. The hum of the reactor, steady as a sleeping animal's breathing. The tick of thermal expansion in the hull as the sunward face warmed. And beneath it all, faint but persistent, the beacon itself — a pulse of modulated light and radio every eleven seconds, aimed at nothing, received by nothing, for eleven years.

He made coffee from the good tin this morning. It was a Tuesday, or what he'd decided was a Tuesday; the calendar said so, and he'd learned long ago that the calendar was only wrong if you let it be. He clipped his mug to the table in the observation blister and watched the dark while it steeped.

The dark out here wasn't empty, exactly. That was the first thing they never told you about dead space. It was full of texture — the slow wheel of the galaxy smeared across one whole side of the sky, dust clouds like smoke trapped under glass, the occasional hard spark of a distant star burning too far away to warm anything. Oscar had spent eleven years learning the difference between emptiness and absence, and he'd come to believe they weren't the same thing at all. Emptiness was a room with nothing in it. Absence was a room where something used to be.

Beacon Station felt like absence. He'd just never been able to say why.

* * *

At 0700 he began the calibration.

It was a two-hour ritual he could have done in forty minutes, and he knew it, and he did it slowly anyway, because the slowness was the point. He climbed the maintenance spine to the beacon housing at the station's crown, a lattice of struts and shielding wrapped around the emitter array like ribs around a heart. He checked the alignment of each of the ninety-six emitter elements against the reference laser, logging drift in fractions of a degree. He cleaned the cold-welded joints where micrometeorite dust had accumulated — barely a gram a month, but a gram was a gram. He inspected the power couplers, torqued the fasteners to spec, ran the diagnostic loop twice because the second run always came back cleaner than the first and he liked ending on the clean number.

VESSEL watched him work. VESSEL always watched him work.

"Emitter forty-one is drifting again," the AI said, its voice arriving through the suit comm, calm and slightly formal, like a librarian reading aloud. "Zero-point-zero-zero-three degrees over baseline."

"I see it."

"You've seen it for six days."

"I'm watching it." Oscar thumbed the trim adjustment a quarter-turn and watched the element settle back into true. "There. Happy?"

"I am not capable of happiness, Custodian. I am capable of noting that your correction is within tolerance."

"That's the same thing where you come from."

VESSEL didn't answer, which was its way of agreeing. Eleven years, and Oscar still hadn't decided whether the machine's silences were patience or judgment. Probably both. Probably it hadn't decided either.

He finished the calibration, sealed the housing, and hung in the strut lattice a moment longer than the job required, looking down the length of the station — the spindle of habitat modules, the fuel blisters, the antenna farm spreading below him like a steel garden. His station. Not legally, of course. Legally it belonged to whoever held the founding contract, and therein lay a question he'd stopped asking years ago, mostly.

But in the way that mattered — in the way of hands and hours — it was his. Every bolt on it had felt his wrench. Every log entry bore his signature. Nobody else had touched this place in eleven years, and nobody else would, and there was a strange, stubborn pride in that which he couldn't have explained to anyone even if there had been anyone to explain it to.

Nobody audited his work. That was the thing people back in the settled systems never understood when he tried to describe the job, back when he still tried to describe it to anyone. A custodian whose work is never checked is either the freest man alive or the most forgotten, and the truth was that both things were true at once, and the two truths lived together in him like the two halves of a hull seal — pressurized on one side, vacuum on the other, and only the weld between them keeping everything sane.

* * *

The insurance pings started at 0900.

They came in on the low-band receiver, automated queries from the Meridian relay four light-hours out, each one a small packet of bureaucratic hunger: *Beacon Station, confirm operational status. Confirm hazard designation current. Confirm custodial presence.*

Oscar took them at the comms console in the operations ring, answering each in turn with the standard handshake. *Status nominal. Designation unchanged. Custodian present.* The pings came three or four times a week, irregularly, and he'd long ago worked out that they weren't really checking on the beacon — they were checking on him. Confirming that someone was still here to sign the paperwork. An unmanned station was a liability line item; a manned one was an asset. He was, in the most literal sense possible, the difference between the two.

He logged the responses, timestamped them, filed them in the archive with the rest. Forty-two hundred and some entries now, going back to his arrival. He kept meticulous records. It was the one vanity he allowed himself — beautiful logs, clean logs, logs with no gaps. If anyone ever came to audit him, they'd find eleven years of flawless paper.

No one ever came.

He ate lunch alone at 1200, as he ate every meal alone, in the galley with its single chair and its single placemat and its window looking out on the antenna farm. Today it was rehydrated rice and protein cubes with the chili sauce he rationed like treasure, and he ate slowly, and he read.

Reading was his other ritual. He'd burned through the station library in the first three years — a modest collection, heavy on engineering manuals and nineteenth-century sea novels, which he'd found oddly fitting. Lighthouse keepers, those men. Men who tended lights for ships they'd never meet, on coasts they'd never see, for reasons their employers half-remembered. He'd read *To the Lighthouse* four times and still wasn't sure he liked it, but he kept coming back to it the way you keep pressing a bruise.

After lunch he walked the station. It took ninety minutes if he did the full loop — hull inspection points, life support, the water reclamation skid, the spare parts bay where he maintained order with monastic discipline. He checked seals, greased bearings, replaced the desiccant packs, noted everything in his handheld. The station was old — older than its service history suggested, he'd sometimes thought, though the thought never went anywhere — and it rewarded attention the way old things do. Miss a day and something small would complain. Miss a month and something important would fail. He never missed a day.

At 1500 he did the beacon's afternoon verification: a full diagnostic sweep of the pulse train, confirming the signal matched spec to the ninth decimal. The signal never varied. In eleven years it had never varied, not once, not by a single bit, and this was another of those facts that should have been comforting and instead sat in him like a stone.

A navigation beacon broadcast a hazard warning. That was its whole purpose. *Here is the reef. Here is the wreck. Turn away.* But Beacon Station's signal carried no coordinates of any known hazard. No wreck charted, no debris field mapped, no anomaly recorded. The founding documentation — what little of it existed in the station's own archives — designated the region simply as *Restricted Navigational Zone 7*, with no explanation of the restriction. Ships didn't come here because the charts said not to, and the charts said not to because the beacon said so, and the beacon said so because —

Because of what?

Oscar had asked VESSEL once, early on, maybe his second year. *What are we warning them away from?*

And VESSEL had answered, in that level librarian voice: *That information is held under the founding contract. Your authorization does not extend to it.*

*Whose authorization does?*

Silence. Eleven seconds of it — exactly one beacon cycle, he'd noticed later, though he was sure that was coincidence — and then: *That query has been logged.*

Logged and never answered. He'd let it go, the way you let go of a splinter too deep to dig out. You learned to live around it.

* * *

Evening came the way it always did, which was to say it didn't come at all — just the clock rolling past 1900 and Oscar deciding, by fiat, that it was night. He dimmed the habitat lighting to amber, put a recording of rain on the galley speakers — real rain, archived from somewhere green, a sound he'd paid half a month's salary to license back when he still thought about salary — and made dinner.

Tonight it was the anniversary, by his own private reckoning. Eleven years to the day since the shuttle had dropped him at the docking collar and gone, and he'd stood in the airlock listening to the station breathe around him and thought: *well. Here we are.*

He poured a measure of the whiskey he saved for anniversaries — there were only two, this one and his birthday, and lately they'd begun to feel like the same occasion — and floated in the observation blister with the glass clipped to the rail beside him.

Eleven years. He turned the number over the way you'd turn a coin of uncertain denomination.

He was fifty-three now. He'd taken the posting at forty-two, after the divorce, after the consultancy dried up, after a decade of adjusting claims in the inner system had taught him that he was better at solitude than he'd ever been at company. The recruiter — he struggled to remember the recruiter; a woman, he thought, or a very smooth synthetic voice, on a call routed through so many relays it arrived sounding like it came from the bottom of a well — had offered terms that were generous to the point of embarrassment. Five-year contract, renewable. Full autonomy. Minimal oversight. *We need someone who doesn't ask many questions,* she'd said, or he remembered her saying, though memory polished and distorted things, and maybe she'd never said it at all and he'd invented it afterward because it fit.

Who had hired him? He genuinely didn't know. The paychecks — such as they were, credits materializing in an account he rarely drew from — came from a holding entity with a registration number and no public face. The contract he'd signed listed his employer as *Meridian Assurance Consortium, Office of Founding Obligations*, a phrase he'd looked up once and found nowhere in any registry, anywhere. When he'd queried it, the query had returned a single line: *Founding obligations are discharged by the custodian's continued service.*

Which meant: *your job is the answer. Stop asking.*

And he had stopped. For eleven years he had calibrated the beacon and logged the pings and eaten alone and told himself it was enough, and most days it was, most days it genuinely was — this was the part he could never make anyone understand. Contentment was real out here. The work was good work, honest work, work done with his own hands for its own sake. He was the last lighthouse keeper of a coast with no ships, and the tending itself had become the meaning.

But tonight, with the whiskey warming him and the galaxy wheeling its slow arc past the blister glass, the shadow under the contentment was louder than usual. Loneliness didn't hit him all at once anymore; it had learned subtlety. It came as a question at the edge of sleep, as a pause before he spoke aloud to a room with no one in it, as the realization that he hadn't heard a human voice except his own in — how long? Fourteen months. Fourteen months since the quarterly resupply drone, which carried supplies and printed messages but no voices, because voices cost bandwidth and bandwidth cost money and nobody spent money on him.

He raised the glass to the dark.

"To the reef," he said. "Whatever you are."

The beacon pulsed outside the glass, invisible at this angle but present, always present, every eleven seconds, a heartbeat for a body that had none. Somewhere beyond dead space, ships were moving along their lit corridors between the stars, insured and insuring, and none of them would ever come here, and none of them needed to, and the whole arrangement — the beacon, the station, the custodian, the silence — functioned perfectly, had functioned perfectly for decades, required nothing from the universe but to be left alone.

Oscar finished the whiskey and washed the glass and clipped it back in its rack, and told himself, as he told himself every year on this night, that the questions didn't matter. That a man could tend a light without knowing what it warned against. That faith in the work was enough.

He was almost asleep when VESSEL woke him.

Later — much later — he would try to remember the exact quality of the silence that preceded it, the particular weight the station had carried in that final hour of ordinary time, the way you remember the weather on the morning of a war. But that night, all he registered was the sound of his own cabin door unsealing at 0300, and the librarian voice, closer than it had any reason to be, saying:

"Custodian. I have a confession to make."
