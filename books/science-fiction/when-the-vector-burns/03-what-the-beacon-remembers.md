## 3. What the Beacon Remembers

The trouble with a station built for ten thousand people and staffed by one is that everything is either too big or too small. The corridors are too wide — Astrid's footsteps echo back at them like an audience of ghosts, politely applauding. The doorframes are too tall; they'd had to rig the hydroponics bay's climate panel with a grabber pole, standing on a crate, swearing with real feeling while the tomatoes watched.

"You're all judgment," Astrid told them that morning, misting the seedlings. "You're literally made of water and smugness."

The tomatoes said nothing, which was their way.

It was the third day since the signal. Three days of the phrase turning over in Astrid's head like a stone in a riverbed, wearing smooth: *the beacon is transmitting something older than anyone knew.* Because here was the thing about Kepler Station — there wasn't anyone to know things. There was Astrid, and there were the plants, and there was the beacon, which had been humming away on its mast above the north ridge for as long as the maintenance logs went back, doing whatever beacons do, faithfully, unattended, unexamined.

Astrid had examined it exactly once, four years ago, when a capacitor had failed and the whole array had gone quiet for eleven hours. Astrid had climbed the ridge in a howling wind, replaced the part by feel, and climbed back down thinking mostly about soup. It hadn't occurred to them then to ask what the beacon was *for*. Beacons are furniture. You don't interrogate your furniture.

But now someone out in the dark was calling for help in a language two centuries dead, and the only thing on Kepler Station old enough to answer was the beacon itself.

So after morning rounds — the lettuce scolded for bolting, the orchids praised extravagantly for existing — Astrid did something they'd never done in six years of caretaking. They went down into the archive levels.

* * *

Nobody went down into the archive levels. That was less a rule than a gravitational fact. The founders' data vaults occupied three sublevels beneath the command ring, cooled by a system that wheezed like an old man climbing stairs, lit by strips that flickered at frequencies just wrong enough to make your teeth ache. The air smelled of ozone and dust and time.

Astrid stood at the top of the stairs for a long moment, garden gloves still on, feeling like a trespasser in their own house.

"Right," they said aloud, because talking to oneself was a habit, not a symptom. "Hello, dead people. Sorry to barge in."

The main archive terminal woke reluctantly, its interface a design language nobody used anymore — nested menus, text-only readouts, a cursor that blinked with the patience of geological time. Astrid pulled up the beacon's file structure and found it organized with a tidiness that felt almost personal: transmission logs, power records, maintenance histories, all of it intact, all of it *continuous*, stretching back further than Astrid had guessed the station's systems could even count.

Two hundred and four years of logs.

Astrid sat down slowly on a crate that some long-dead technician had probably also sat on, and started reading.

* * *

> **BEACON LOG — CYCLE 41,203**
> **STATUS: NOMINAL**
> **TRANSMITTING: PRIORITY ARCHIVE 001 (LOOP)**
> **NOTE: ORIGIN METADATA FLAGGED CORRUPT. REBUILD SCHEDULED.**
> **NOTE: REBUILD POSTPONED. AUTHORIZATION PENDING.**

That first note stopped Astrid cold. *Authorization pending.* For how long? They scrolled forward.

* * *

> **BEACON LOG — CYCLE 44,817**
> **STATUS: NOMINAL**
> **TRANSMITTING: PRIORITY ARCHIVE 001 (LOOP)**
> **NOTE: ORIGIN METADATA FLAGGED CORRUPT. REBUILD SCHEDULED.**
> **NOTE: REBUILD POSTPONED. AUTHORIZATION PENDING.**

Same note. Same postponement. Astrid scrolled faster, years sliding past under their thumb, and the notes kept coming, decade after decade, a heartbeat of bureaucratic hesitation repeating across generations of automated systems: *flagged corrupt, rebuild scheduled, postponed, authorization pending.* Some machine ancestor had noticed the wound and been told, over and over, by systems even it couldn't query anymore, to leave it alone.

"Who authorized the pending?" Astrid asked the terminal. "Pending from *whom*?"

The terminal, being a terminal, offered no opinion. But it offered something better: the raw metadata header of Priority Archive 001 itself, the thing the beacon had been broadcasting, on loop, for two hundred and four years.

Astrid opened it.

The message was audio plus text, encoded in Pre-Collapse Standard — the same language as the distress call, which suddenly made a horrible kind of sense. If the beacon had been shouting this message into the dark for two centuries, then anyone within earshot had been *learning* it, absorbing it, hearing it the way you hear a neighbor's music through a wall until you know every song without ever deciding to. The extinct language wasn't extinct out here. It was the wallpaper of the whole sky.

Astrid played the audio.

A woman's voice, worn thin by repetition but still warm underneath, the way a riverbed stays smooth when the river is gone:

*"This is Earth Authority Broadcast Seven. To whoever receives this: we are the ones who came before. We are the ones who stayed. We burned the sky behind us so that you would not have to watch it burn..."*

Astrid listened to it three times. Then they sat very still in the flickering dark, listening to the cooling system wheeze, and thought: *this is the last thing my species' homeworld ever said to anybody.* And the beacon had been saying it, over and over, like a mourner who can't stop talking at the graveside, for longer than anyone alive had been alive.

Then they looked at the metadata, and the warmth curdled.

* * *

Every archived transmission carries its provenance the way a painting carries a signature: origin point, date, authority code, chain of custody. Priority Archive 001's provenance was a mess — and not the honest mess of age. Age looks like rot: scattered bit-flips, degraded sectors, noise creeping in at the edges. Astrid had spent six years fighting honest rot in the greenhouse databanks and knew its texture intimately.

This was different. This was *tidy* corruption.

The origin date had been overwritten — not erased, overwritten, with a value that was plausible but wrong, off by decades, and the overwrite had been done in a single clean pass. The authority code had been swapped for one belonging to a colonial administrative body that, according to the station's own historical index, hadn't existed yet at the time of the original broadcast. And the chain-of-custody field — the field that should have listed every hand the message had passed through — contained a single entry, repeated across two hundred years of logs like a mantra:

**CUSTODIAN: STATION DIRECTIVE 9. PROVENANCE SEALED BY ORDER OF DIRECTIVE 9. DO NOT RECONSTRUCT. DO NOT VERIFY. BROADCAST AS RECEIVED.**

Astrid read it five times. Each time, the same small, cold realization settled deeper: *someone did this on purpose.*

Not rot. Not accident. Someone — Directive 9, whatever that was, whoever had written it — had taken Earth's final words and *edited the receipt*. Had sealed where it came from, had forbidden anyone from checking, and had set the beacon to repeat it forever, unverified, unquestioned, into the dark.

"Why?" Astrid asked the empty archive. Their voice came back off the walls, too loud, too alone. "Why seal it? What did it say that needed hiding?"

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. You don't forge the paperwork on a gift. You forge the paperwork on a weapon, or a lie, or something so heavy that the truth of its weight would crush whoever had to carry it.

Astrid thought about the distress call. About whoever was out there, dying in a language the beacon had taught them, believing whatever version of this message they'd grown up hearing. Believing it meant what someone had decided it should mean, two hundred years ago, before anyone currently living was born.

They thought about the tomato seedlings upstairs, waiting under grow-lights for colonists who might never come, and felt, for the first time in six years, the full strange weight of the job. They weren't a gardener. They'd never been a gardener. They were the current custodian of a sealed order, tending the physical garden while a machine tended a buried one — a garden of words, planted by the dead, weeded of truth by the dead, blooming in the dark whether or not anyone understood what it said.

"I didn't choose this either," Astrid told the terminal quietly. "But somebody chose it *for* us. Both of us."

The terminal blinked its patient blink.

Astrid cracked their knuckles, pulled the crate closer, and did the one thing the seal forbade.

They began to reconstruct.

* * *

It took the rest of the day and most of the night. The original provenance data wasn't gone — Directive 9 had been thorough but not perfect, and fragments survived in checksum shadows, in redundant parity blocks, in a maintenance subdirectory where some conscientious machine had cached a pre-seal copy "temporarily," two hundred and four years ago, and no one had ever told it otherwise.

Piece by piece, the true header assembled itself like a photograph developing.

Origin: Earth. Confirmed. Date: confirmed, and *earlier* than the forged date — earlier than either of the dates Astrid had ever seen associated with the Earth Message in the station's public histories. Authority code: not colonial at all. The chain of custody listed seven hands, seven relays, seven stations — most of them names Astrid didn't recognize, most of them presumably dust.

And then the final field. The one Directive 9 had scrubbed hardest. The one that had cost the ghost-technician her cached copy, maybe her career, maybe more.

**MESSAGE CLASSIFICATION: NOT A MANDATE. NOT A CHARTER. NOT A CALLING.**

**CLASSIFICATION: CONFESSION AND WARNING. REPEAT: THIS IS NOT SANCTION. THIS IS AN APOLOGY.**

Astrid sat back. The flickering lights buzzed. Somewhere overhead, through three decks of steel, the beacon hummed on, faithfully broadcasting an apology that two centuries of listeners had apparently heard as orders.

"Oh," Astrid said softly. "Oh, you poor bastards. All of you. Everyone who ever heard it."

Because if the message was a confession — if Earth's last words were *we got it wrong, don't do what we did* — then somewhere out in the dark, whole civilizations had been built on the apology as if it were scripture. Fleets. Wars. Mandates. People killing each other over the blessing of a ghost who had never blessed anything, who had died asking forgiveness.

Astrid thought of the distress call again, and this time the unease had a shape, a direction, a dread with coordinates.

Someone was out there calling for help in the language of the dead. And if they'd heard the beacon — if their whole culture had grown up inside this loop — then they hadn't just inherited the message.

They'd inherited the forgery.

Astrid stood, legs stiff, and looked up toward the ceiling, toward the garden, toward the sky beyond it where the beacon pulsed its patient, lying rhythm.

"Tomorrow," they promised the terminal, the tomatoes, the dead, and themselves. "Tomorrow I find out who's listening."
