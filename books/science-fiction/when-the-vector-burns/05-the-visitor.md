## 5. The Visitor

Sefa Idris had learned, over three years of war, to distrust silence. Silence on a fleet channel meant someone was thinking. Silence in a briefing meant someone was lying. And silence from Scout Wing Nine — silence that had stretched now for eleven hours — meant someone was dead, or worse, alive and telling stories.

She stood at the tactical table in the *Ashfall's* war room, watching the debris telemetry crawl across the display. The scout, *Kestrel's Lament*, had gone dark near the edge of the Kepler system, right where the charts said nothing was. No station, no beacon, no reason to be. Just a smudge of old survey data and a gravitational nothing that pilots called the Quiet.

"Last transmission?" she asked.

Lieutenant Mbeke, her signals officer, shook her head. "Fragmented, Commander. Something about engine failure, then a correction burn, then—" She played it. A young voice, clipped and calm the way trained voices get when they're terrified: *"—going down, I'm going down, there's a station here, there's a station, repeat, there is a station—"* Then static.

"There is no station," said Colonel Voss, who had a gift for stating the obvious as though it were strategy. "Kepler was abandoned two centuries ago. The surveys are clear."

"The surveys were made by people who died before my grandmother was born," Sefa said. "I'd like to think the universe has done *something* since then." She traced the crash trajectory with one finger. The *Kestrel's Lament* had come down hard — too hard for a survival pod to matter — but the angle suggested the pilot had fought the whole way, bleeding velocity in increments, buying meters with meters. That was not a person planning to die. That was a person planning to be annoyed about surviving.

"Send a recovery team," Sefa said. "Quietly. If there *is* a station, I want to know what's on it before the Compact does."

"And if there's a survivor?"

"Then we bring her home," Sefa said, "and I buy her a drink, and then I ask her what she saw."

* * *

What Lieutenant Adaeze Okonkwo saw, in the first hour after the crash, was a ceiling.

It was a good ceiling, as ceilings went — curved, paneled in pale composite gone soft with age, and, crucially, not on fire. She catalogued this with the detached professionalism of a pilot who had just spent forty minutes converting her scout into a very expensive sled. Her left arm was broken. Her ribs had opinions. The cockpit had sheared away entirely, which meant she had been dragged out, which meant someone had found her.

Someone was humming.

The humming drifted in from beyond a partition door — low, unhurried, tuneless in the way of a person who hums for plants rather than audiences. Then footsteps, and the door hissed open, and Okonkwo beheld a figure in a patched environment suit with the helmet off, holding a tray.

The figure was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the forearms of someone who hauled water for a living and the expression of someone who had been interrupted mid-sentence by a falling spaceship.

"You're awake," the figure said. "Good. I've been talking to you for six hours and you were terrible company."

"Where—" Okonkwo's voice cracked. She tried again. "Where am I?"

"Kepler Station. Agricultural deck. You're lying in my potting bay, and I want you to know that you crushed a tray of *Solanum lycopersicum*, seedlings, six weeks old, and I have chosen to forgive you." The figure set the tray down on a workbench and began, with great care, to splint nothing in particular — arranging bandages, checking a scanner. "I'm Astrid. Astrid Halloran. I live here. Alone, until recently, and I'll be honest, I had gotten used to it."

"Lieutenant Adaeze Okonkwo, Ashfall Fleet." She tried to salute and her ribs filed a formal complaint. "You pulled me out."

"You pulled yourself most of the way. I mostly stopped you from pulling yourself the rest of the way into the reactor wash." Astrid — and Okonkwo still couldn't tell if the name was a rank, a title, or a first name, and the person offered no help — lifted the scanner. "Broken arm, three cracked ribs, mild concussion. You're lucky. The ship's not, and neither are the tomatoes, but you — you're lucky."

Okonkwo stared at the ceiling again, and let the truth of it land: she was alive, in a station that did not exist, being scolded about vegetables by a stranger in a two-hundred-year-old suit. A laugh escaped her, which hurt, which made her laugh more, which hurt more.

"Something funny?" Astrid asked, but the corner of their mouth had moved.

"I reported a station," Okonkwo said. "My commander is going to think I hallucinated. I half think I hallucinated. Do you know what you're sitting on top of?"

"I know what I'm sitting *inside*. What I'm sitting on top of is a question I've been asking for about two weeks." Astrid's voice changed — lighter on the surface, heavier underneath. "But first, water, painkillers, and food. In that order. The garden and I have a rule: nobody makes big decisions hungry."

* * *

Sefa received the recovery team's report forty hours later and read it three times.

*Station confirmed. One inhabitant, self-identified as "caretaker." One pilot, Lt. Okonkwo, alive, injured, stable. Station appears pre-Collapse. Beacon active. Recommend immediate decision on disposition.*

Disposition. As if the station were cargo. As if Okonkwo were cargo. Sefa set the tablet down and looked out the viewport at the long dark, where three thousand ships of the Ashfall Fleet hung in their convoy formation like embers refusing to go out.

Her people had a founding story, and like all founding stories it began with a voice from Earth. *The Earth Message*, they called it — the final transmission, caught by the first generation ships as they burned away from a dying homeworld. *Carry the fire forward. Tend what you plant. The garden is the covenant.* Every Ashfall child memorized it before they could write. Every Ashfall officer swore their commission on it. Sefa had sworn on it herself, and had gone to war believing it, because the Compact's claim to the shipping lanes violated everything the Message stood for — or so her chaplains said, and the Compact's chaplains said the same thing in the other direction, and for three years the fire had been carried forward mostly in the form of torpedoes.

And now there was a station at the edge of nowhere with a live beacon and a single caretaker, and Okonkwo's fragmentary last transmission had contained one detail that Sefa had not been able to stop hearing since:

*"There's a message here. Commander, there's a message, it's old, it's in the old language—"*

The old language. Pre-Collapse Standard. The tongue of the Earth Message itself — the tongue in which no one alive was fluent, the tongue that scholars parsed the way archaeologists parse bones.

"Voss," she said. "How fast can we get a diplomatic team to Kepler?"

"That depends," Voss said carefully, "on whether we're calling it a diplomatic team or a salvage team."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. "Diplomatic," she said. "We are not going to shoot our way into the one place in the galaxy that might be telling the truth."

"And if the Compact gets there first?"

"Then we'll have finally found something we have in common," Sefa said. "We'll both be lying about why we came."

* * *

Okonkwo's arm itched inside the cast, which she was told was a sign of healing, and which she suspected was a sign of the universe's sense of humor. She had been on Kepler Station four days. She had eaten more fresh food in those four days than in the last four years — actual tomatoes, actual greens, grown in actual dirt, and she had cried a little at the first bite and pretended it was the painkillers, and Astrid had pretended to believe her, which was its own kindness.

It was on the fifth day, in the beacon chamber, that everything tilted.

Astrid had been playing her recordings — the distress signal, the loop fragments, the corrupted archives — and explaining, in the careful voice of a person who has rehearsed disbelief and found no way to soften it. Okonkwo listened the way pilots listen: completely, quietly, hands still.

And then the loop played a phrase — a rising, falling sequence of syllables, worn thin by two centuries of repetition — and Okonkwo sat up so fast her ribs screamed.

"Play that again."

Astrid played it again.

"Again."

"Adeze—"

"*Again.*"

Astrid played it. And Okonkwo, half-laughing, half-crying, said the words back. Not perfectly. The vowels were wrong, the cadence was her grandmother's cadence, softened by kitchen smoke and lullaby — but the words came, unbidden, from somewhere below training and below memory.

Astrid had gone very still. "You speak it."

"I *don't* speak it. Nobody speaks it." Okonkwo's hands were shaking. "But my grandmother sang songs. Old songs, in the old tongue. She called them *hearth-songs*. She said they were from before the ships, from Earth, from her grandmother's grandmother. She taught them to me when I was small. She said—" Okonkwo stopped. Swallowed. "She said the fleet had forgotten how to pray in its own voice, and someone had to remember."

The chamber hummed around them, the beacon cycling its ancient burden out into the dark. Astrid looked at her — really looked, the way you look at a door you'd stopped believing was a door.

"Those songs," Astrid said. "Could they be a key? The loop is degraded. I can reconstruct maybe sixty percent from context and archives. But the other forty — the grammar, the inflection, the *meaning* — I've been guessing. If your grandmother's songs preserve the living sound of it—"

"Then two women who weren't allowed to matter," Okonkwo said slowly, "kept a language alive in a kitchen."

They worked through the night. Astrid played fragments; Okonkwo sang back, halting, correcting herself, sometimes dissolving into laughter at her own accent, sometimes into something quieter. Piece by piece, the message began to stand up out of its own wreckage — and with every piece, Okonkwo felt the floor of her life shift. Because the words were not the words of the Earth Message. Not *carry the fire forward*. Not *the garden is the covenant*.

The words were older and stranger and heavier, and near dawn, Astrid translated the first complete sentence, and then stopped, and looked at her, and did not say it aloud.

"Say it," Okonkwo whispered.

Astrid said it. And Okonkwo understood, with the cold clarity of a pilot watching her fuel gauge, that her fleet had been at war for three years over a message that did not say what anyone believed it said — and that somewhere out there, three thousand ships were already turning toward this station, and not all of them wanted the truth found.

She also understood that she was, as of this moment, the only person in the Ashfall Fleet who knew.

"We have to decide," Astrid said quietly, "what we do before they get here. Because they *are* getting here. Both of them."

Okonkwo looked at the beacon, at the garden beyond the glass, at the first pale light of the station's artificial dawn.

"Then we'd better work fast," she said. "And Astrid? My commander is going to want to know why her pilot hasn't reported in."

Astrid's smile was thin and brave and a little terrified.

"Tell her the truth," they said. "It'll be good practice. For both of us."
