## 8. Burn the Vector

The tomatoes were the first to go.

Astrid had always assumed that if Kepler Station ever died, it would be slowly — a coolant failure here, a power drain there, the long polite fade of a place no one loved enough to save. They had not imagined it would end with a missile strike on greenhouse four, and they especially had not imagined that their first coherent thought upon seeing the fireball bloom against the dark would be *the Brandywines*, said in the same tone one might use for a murdered friend.

"They were nearly ripe," Astrid said aloud, because there was no one else to say it to. Okonkwo was two decks down, bleeding less than yesterday and swearing considerably more than usual, trying to coax the station's ancient targeting array into telling her which fleet the missile had come from.

The answer, when she got it, was: both. The missiles were coming from both.

* * *

It had started, as most catastrophes do, with a small administrative detail.

Astrid had spent three days reconstructing the final segment of the loop — the confession, the warning, the words of a dying Earth that neither fleet's founding myth resembled even slightly — when the station's proximity alarms woke them at what passed for dawn aboard a tin can orbiting a half-terraformed world. Not one contact signature. Not two. Forty-one.

"The Ashfall vanguard," Okonkwo reported from the ops chair, her voice carefully flat in the way of someone reading a casualty list. "And behind them — Compact cruisers. They've been shadowing each other for six hours. Everyone's pretending they just happened to be passing through."

"Passing through the middle of nowhere."

"People pass through the middle of nowhere all the time when the middle of nowhere has something worth killing for." She pulled up the tactical overlay, and the room filled with slow red and blue arcs converging on a single green dot. "That's us. We're the something."

Astrid looked at the display for a long moment. Then they went downstairs and checked on the seedlings, because that was what they knew how to do with fear: carry it gently, water it, give it light.

By midday, the first hails began.

* * *

Commander Sefa Idris's voice came through the comm crisp and righteous, every syllable pressed like a uniform. *"Unregistered station, this is the Ashfall Fleet vessel Perpetual Dawn. We have reason to believe you are harboring an artifact of Earth heritage and a fugitive of our fleet. Surrender both and you will be treated as custodians, not thieves."*

An hour later, Captain Yorick Vane's transmission arrived, silkier, more reasonable-sounding, somehow worse. *"Kepler Station, this is the Meridian Compact ship Covenant's Grace. We know what you've found. We also know what you intend to do with it. I'd like to believe you're a sensible person. Sensible people survive."*

"They're both so polite," Astrid murmured.

"That's how you know it's bad," Okonkwo said. She was standing at the viewport with her arms crossed, watching the distant engine-flares like a woman counting storm clouds. "My grandmother used to say the politest men she ever met were the ones burning her village. They apologized the whole time."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them nothing. Which they will interpret as everything." She turned. "Astrid. We have maybe two days before someone decides asking is slower than taking. You have to choose."

Astrid had been choosing, in the small hours, between three futures laid out on the workbench of their mind like seed trays.

Tray one: transmit the truth to whichever fleet arrived first. Simple. Immediate. And monstrous — because the truth was a weapon, and handing a loaded confession to one army meant the war didn't end, it just changed owners. The Ashfall would learn their mandate was a corrupted fragment; the Compact would learn theirs was a forgery stitched onto a dead man's apology. Whoever learned it second would be learning it from the wrong end of a gun.

Tray two: destroy the beacon. Burn the vector, as the old engineers would have said. Bury Earth's last words forever rather than let them be ammunition. That felt clean until Astrid imagined doing it — imagined pressing the override while Okonkwo watched, while the grandmother's songs still lived in only one head aboard this station. Two hundred years of faithful repetition, ended by the one caretaker who finally understood what was being repeated. It wasn't destruction. It was a second murder of the same message.

Tray three was the one Astrid kept circling back to and kept flinching away from, because it required the beacon to survive, the fleets to arrive, and a leap of faith across a canyon with no bottom they could see.

"You have that look," Okonkwo said.

"What look?"

"The look of someone about to do something insane and call it gardening."

Astrid almost smiled. "Give me until the shooting starts."

"Oh, the shooting's going to start," Okonkwo said grimly. "I know these people. Both of these people."

* * *

The shooting started fourteen hours early, over a misunderstanding involving a drone that both sides claimed the other had launched.

Astrid was in greenhouse two, hand-deep in loam, transplanting the last of the wheat starts into deeper trays — a pointless act of faith, since the trays might not outlive the week — when the first kinetic round punched through the station's outer ring. The sound was less an explosion than a vast indignant bell, and the loam jumped in its beds like startled birds.

Then the lights flickered, held, and died in the east wing.

Astrid ran. Of course they ran. They were the gardener; running toward the fire was the whole job description, even now, even when the fire was orbital weaponry instead of frost.

Greenhouse four was gone. Not damaged — *gone*, a ragged mouth of twisted alloy venting atmosphere and steam and the bright scattered corpses of forty years of cultivation. The citrus trees. The orchid wall that Astrid's predecessor, old Marta Voss, had tended through her final decade alone. The pollinator drones, tumbling out into vacuum like burnt snow.

Astrid stood at the sealed bulkhead and watched through the emergency glazing as the station's oldest living things froze and boiled simultaneously, and something in their chest came apart quietly, without drama, the way roots come apart in soil that's been salted.

"We can rebuild," Okonkwo said behind them. She'd come up at a run, suit half-sealed, blood on her collar from a cut Astrid hadn't given her time to notice. "The vault seeds are intact. The core collection—"

"I know."

"Astrid. Look at me. The vault is intact."

"I know." Astrid wiped their face with a soil-dark hand and left a brown streak, and found, absurdly, that they were almost laughing. "It's just — Marta grew those orchids. She talked to them every day for thirty years and they never once bloomed for anyone else, and now—" Their voice did something unsteady. "Now the conversation's over."

Okonkwo put a hand on their shoulder. She didn't say anything else, which was the correct amount of things to say.

* * *

By the second day, the station was a sieve.

Both fleets had moved to precision strikes — each side convinced the other was about to seize the beacon, each unwilling to simply destroy the station because destroying the message was losing the war by other means. So they picked at Kepler Station like two birds fighting over one shell, and Astrid moved through the corridors with a maintenance kit and a growing list of systems that were dead, dying, or lying about being alive.

The beacon itself still burned steady in its shielded core, patient as it had been for two centuries. Astrid sat beside it in the dark of the comms vault, running their palm along the housing the way they'd once run hands along tomato vines, and made the choice.

Not tray one. Not tray two.

Tray three — except tray three needed both fleets alive, close, and listening, and right now they were killing each other at a distance of eleven kilometers and closing.

"It won't work," Okonkwo said, when Astrid finally explained it. She'd listened all the way through, which Astrid appreciated, before demolishing it, which she probably thought was helpful. "You want two fleets at war to stop shooting and start *studying*? Mid-battle? You want their linguists — their military linguists — to sit down together and decode a language neither side can read alone?"

"Yes."

"Based on what?"

"Based on the fact that they've both spent three years claiming a message none of them can actually read." Astrid met her eyes. "They've been fighting over a book neither can open. I'm going to make them open it together or admit, in front of their own crews, that their god is illiterate."

Okonkwo stared. Then, slowly, terribly, she started to laugh — the cracked, exhausted laugh of a woman whose grandmother's forbidden songs were suddenly the most strategically valuable object in two star systems.

"My grandmother," she said, wiping her eyes, "would have adored you. And also lectured you for four hours about hubris."

"She sounds wise."

"She was. Hubris lecture included." She sobered. "It has to be soon. Another day of strikes and there won't be a station left to broadcast from. And Astrid — the moment you send it, they'll know someone down here understands the message. You'll be the most important person in the war."

"No," Astrid said quietly. "I'll be nobody. That's the point. The message goes out unsigned. No flag, no name, no origin. Let them fight over who sent it. By the time they figure out it was a gardener, it'll be too late to matter."

Outside, somewhere above the ruined greenhouses, the fleets wheeled around each other in their slow, murderous dance, and the station groaned like an old tree in wind.

Astrid opened the broadcast console and began, with steady hands, to compose the transmission that would burn the vector clean.

Below them, in the dark, the seed vault hummed at two degrees Celsius, holding ten thousand years of patience in suspension — waiting, like everything else aboard Kepler Station, to find out whether anything would ever grow again.

The first line of the true message glowed on the screen, in a tongue extinct for two centuries:

*We were wrong.*

Astrid pressed transmit.
