## 9. A Language Worth Learning

**Astrid**

The garden was burning, and Astrid Halloran was laughing.

Not because it was funny. Nothing about the Ashfall cruiser's strafing run through the outer ring was funny, and nothing about the Meridian frigate answering it in kind was funny either. But somewhere between the third explosion and the fourth, standing in the smoke-choked corridor of Kepler Station with a dead language in their hands and two fleets in their sky, Astrid had discovered that terror and laughter are neighbors — that they share a wall, and sometimes the wall comes down.

"Tell me you're ready," Okonkwo said. She was braced against the transmitter console, one arm still bandaged from the crash, her eyes red-rimmed from smoke or grief or both. "Tell me the vector is clean."

"The vector is clean." Astrid's hands hovered over the interface. Beneath their palms, the beacon hummed the way it had hummed for two hundred years, patient as soil. "It's not a message anymore, though. It's a door."

"Explain that quickly."

"I'm going to broadcast the true transmission — the confession, the warning, all of it — in Pre-Collapse Standard. Untranslated. No subtitles, no crib, no key."

Okonkwo stared at them. "Nobody speaks Pre-Collapse Standard. That's the whole *point*. That's why it survived — it was already dead when Earth sent it."

"Exactly." Astrid felt the terrible calm settle over them, the same calm they felt pruning back a blight, cutting away what was rotten so the rest could live. "Neither fleet can read it alone. But they don't have to read it alone. Ashfall kept the tonal registers — your grandmother's songs. The Compact kept the grammatical structures, the written forms. I checked Vane's archives when their boarding party tried to crack the vault. Each side has half the language. Each side has been calling the other half *heresy* for three years."

The station shuddered. Somewhere above them, the garden dome — the unborn garden, the one they had spoken to every morning for eleven years — took a hit that would have made Astrid scream if they'd had time. They didn't have time. The loop was decaying. In hours, maybe minutes, the original waveform would smear into noise forever, and two fleets would keep killing each other over a message no one alive had ever actually read.

"So they'll have to *talk*," Okonkwo said slowly. "To translate. They'll have to—"

"Sit at the same table. Share the same key. Admit, in front of everyone, that they need each other." Astrid's finger rested on the transmit key. "It's either this, or I pick a side, or I burn it all and let them fight in the dark. I'm done choosing between bad harvests."

Okonkwo put her good hand over Astrid's. "My grandmother used to say the old songs were planted, not written. That someday somebody would come along and water them." She managed a wet, crooked smile. "Do it."

Astrid pressed the key.

**Sefa Idris**

Commander Sefa Idris had been at war for three years, and in that time she had learned that battle sounds like a cathedral: the low organ-note of engines, the choir of alarms, the hushed reverence of people about to die. The bridge of the *Ashfall's Promise* was singing that song now, closing on Kepler Station, and Sefa stood at the center of it with the Earth Message burning in her chest like a coal she'd swallowed.

*We were sent. We were chosen. The words were given to us.*

Then every screen on her bridge filled with sound.

Not words — not at first. A voice, ancient and crackling, speaking in rolling cadences that meant nothing and everything, and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat, something else: a melody. A lullaby. Sefa's grandmother — no, older, older than grandmothers, older than the fleet — had sung melodies like that in the low decks where the old songs survived despite three generations of doctrinal correction.

"Commander." Lieutenant Okonkwo's voice, impossibly, on an open channel — the traitor, the survivor, the pilot they'd all mourned. "Commander, listen. *Listen.* Do you hear the tones? That's the fourth register. That's the register the Compact's copies flattened out. Without it, every translation Ashfall ever made was—"

"Was wrong," Sefa said. Her mouth was dry. "Our translations were wrong."

"Everyone's translations were wrong. That's the message, Commander. That's the *first* message: *you are hearing this wrong, and here is how to hear it right.*"

Sefa looked at her bridge crew. Forty faces, lit by the glow of a language none of them could read, all of them waiting for her to tell them what was true. Behind her, through the viewport, the Meridian frigate hung in space, weapons hot, three years of dead between them.

"Comms," she said finally. "Open a channel to the Meridian vessel. Audio only." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "And somebody find me every linguist, every archivist, every grandmother's-grandchild on this ship who ever hummed the forbidden songs. We're going to need all of them."

**Yorick Vane**

Captain Yorick Vane had known for eleven years that his copy of the Earth Message was altered. He had built a career, a fleet, a war on top of that knowledge the way you build a house on a fault line: carefully, prayerfully, refusing to think about the earthquake.

Now the earthquake had a melody.

It filled the *Meridian's Oath* from keel to bridge — the true transmission, raw and untranslated, and Vane stood in the middle of it feeling eleven years of careful construction slide sideways. His chief linguist, a gaunt woman named Perrin who had once, drunkenly, told him his founding myth was "grammatically impossible," was weeping at her station.

"Captain." Perrin's voice was wrecked. "The structure — the syntax — it's intact, it's *complete*, but the phonemes are encoded in a tonal system we stripped out of our copies during the Purification. We can read the bones but not the breath. We can't—"

"Can't or won't, Perrin?"

"Can't." She wiped her face with a sleeve. "There's a ship out there full of heretics who kept the breath."

Vane closed his eyes. He thought about ordering Perrin to force a translation anyway, about salvaging something, a fragment, a justification. He thought about the three thousand dead in the Lane War, about the children on the generation decks who had memorized the Message as their first prayer.

He thought about how tired he was.

"Comms," he said. "Open a channel to the Ashfall vessel." A pause. His whole crew was watching. "And Perrin — get me everything we have on Pre-Collapse tonal reconstruction. Every fragment. Every corrupted archive." He exhaled. "We're going to need help."

**Okonkwo**

The channel opened at 0400 station time, and Lieutenant Amara Okonkwo — dead woman, traitor, key — found herself speaking to both fleets at once, her voice relayed through a dying beacon, her grandmother's songs suddenly the most important documents in human space.

"All right," she said, and her hands were shaking, and she let them. "Here's how this works. Ashfall, you have the tones. Compact, you have the grammar. Neither of you can do this alone, and the waveform is degrading — you have maybe six hours before the original decays past recovery. So you're going to work *together*, in real time, on an open channel, where everyone can see."

Silence. Two fleets' worth of silence, three years of silence, pressing against the channel like deep water.

Then, from the Ashfall side, an old woman's voice — one of the archivists, someone's grandmother, someone's forbidden singer: "The fourth register rises on grief-words. Always. Whoever these Compact people are, they'd better be ready for that."

And from the Compact side, Perrin, sniffling but game: "We have the grief-words. We've been translating them wrong for two centuries, but we have them. Send us your tones, madam. We'll be gentle with them."

Okonkwo laughed — a real one, surprised out of her. Somewhere on the channel, someone from the Compact laughed too, and then someone from Ashfall, and the sound rippled down the open line like wind through a field.

"Commander Idris," she said. "Captain Vane. Cease fire while we work. Please. You can't translate over the sound of your own guns."

A long pause. Then Idris, quiet: "Ashfall batteries holding. Not standing down. *Holding.*"

Then Vane, quieter: "Compact batteries holding. Same terms."

It wasn't peace. It was a held breath. But Okonkwo had grown up on generation-deck songs about held breaths, and she knew what they could become.

**Astrid**

They worked for six hours, and Astrid Halloran — who had spent eleven years talking to plants because there was no one else to talk to — sat in the smoke of their burning garden and listened to humanity talk to itself.

The linguists argued first. Of course they argued. The Ashfall archivists insisted the rising tone marked questions; the Compact grammarians proved, with a flourish Perrin would later be famous for, that it marked *regret*. The first full sentence they decoded together took ninety minutes and two shouting matches, and when Perrin read it aloud, both channels went silent:

*"We did not mean for it to end this way."*

Not *we were chosen*. Not *the other fleet are heretics*. A confession. An apology, two hundred years old, from people who had run out of time to deliver it in person.

The second sentence took an hour. The third took forty minutes. They got faster as they went, as Ashfall tones found Compact grammar, as grandmothers' songs and purified archives braided together into something neither side could have made alone. By the fourth hour, linguists on both ships were finishing each other's sentences. By the fifth, someone on the Ashfall channel — a teenager, by the sound of her — asked the Compact archivists how they'd survived the Purification, and someone on the Compact side answered honestly, and the answer was terrible, and nobody fired a shot about it.

Astrid sat with Okonkwo and listened, and wept a little, and did not mind.

The final decoded passage came at 0958 station time, read aloud jointly — Idris's archivist for the tones, Perrin for the grammar, their voices overlapping on the open channel like two hands completing a knot:

*"To whoever finds this: we were not chosen. We were not sent. We were fleeing, and we were afraid, and we made our fear into a god so the children could sleep. The truth is smaller than the myth, but it is ours. Do not fight over our fear. Plant something instead."*

Silence, on both channels. The long kind. The kind that comes after a held breath finally, finally lets go.

Then Commander Sefa Idris, her voice raw: "Ashfall batteries standing down."

Then Captain Yorick Vane, quieter, as if the words cost him blood: "Compact batteries standing down."

* * *

**Astrid, after**

The ceasefire was not peace. Astrid knew that. Three years of dead do not translate in six hours, and there would be grief-words ahead — rising tones, whole sentences of them, years' worth.

But that evening, with the station's outer ring scorched and the garden dome cracked open to the stars, Astrid walked through what remained of the unborn garden and found it was not entirely unborn anymore. The seed vault had held. Half of it, anyway. And drifting in through the fractured dome came a request on the open channel — from the Compact's youngest archivist, of all people — asking, shyly, whether the station had any of the old Earth crops, and whether anyone might teach her to grow them.

Astrid looked at Okonkwo. Okonkwo looked at Astrid.

"Tell her," Astrid said, "that we have tomatoes."

And beneath the cracked dome, under the light of two fleets at rest, Astrid Halloran began, at last, to garden for an audience.
