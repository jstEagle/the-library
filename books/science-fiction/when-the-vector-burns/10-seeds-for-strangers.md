## 10. Seeds for Strangers

The first thing Lieutenant Amara Okonkwo learned about gardening was that Astrid Halloran lied.

"Water it when it looks thirsty," Astrid had said, handing her a can with a dented spout. "Plants are honest. They'll tell you."

The plants did not tell her anything. The plants, as far as Amara could tell, communicated exclusively through dying, and they did so with the silent, patient judgment of her grandmother's sisters watching her eat with her elbows on the table. She had killed four seedlings in her first month aboard the *Concord of Rivers*, and Astrid had simply appeared beside her each time, said nothing, and handed her another one, the way you'd hand a child another block after they'd thrown the last one at the wall.

"You're not going to yell at me?" Amara had finally asked.

"Gardens don't run on yelling." Astrid had crouched, pressed one soil-dark finger into the growing medium, and frowned at it like a doctor reading a pulse. "They run on showing up. Yelling's optional. Mostly counterproductive."

That had been eleven months ago, aboard a ship that had not existed a year and a half before — a ship built from two fleets' rivets and one fleet's grudge, crewed by people who had spent three years shooting at each other and now spent their mornings arguing in a dead language about whether *kwalu* meant "to plant" or "to bury," which, the linguists had eventually admitted, might be the same word on purpose.

* * *

The ceasefire held. That was the thing nobody quite trusted, the way you don't trust a bone that's healed — it works, you use it, but you're aware of the seam. The Ashfall Fleet and the Meridian Compact still flew their own banners, still kept their own councils, still flinched a little at each other's accents. But they shared the shipping lanes now. They shared the *Concord of Rivers*, which had begun life as an Ashfall hull and a Compact engine and had been welded together by engineers from both sides who discovered, somewhere around week three, that they liked each other's jokes.

And they shared the language.

That was the part Amara carried like a coal in her chest. When Astrid had broadcast Earth's true final message — the confession, the warning, the *please don't do what we did* — in Pre-Collapse Standard, in a tongue no living fleet could read alone, the war hadn't stopped because of the message's content. It had stopped because of the message's *form*. To understand it, Ashfall linguists needed Compact grammarians. Compact scholars needed the tonal songs of Ashfall grandmothers — songs like the ones Amara's grandmother had sung in the dark of a maintenance deck, songs the Compact had once called superstition and the Ashfall had nearly let die of embarrassment. The message had been built like a lock with two keys, and Earth, two centuries dead, had turned out to be the only neutral party either fleet still trusted.

Amara had stood in the *Concord*'s comm room the night the translation completed, surrounded by people who a year earlier would have been shooting at each other, and listened to a woman dead for two hundred years say, in essence: *We were wrong, and we are sorry, and we are sending you our seeds.*

Not a mandate. Not a divine sanction. An apology with an attachment.

It was, Amara thought, the most human thing she had ever heard. She'd cried. So had Commander Idris, though she'd deny it under oath, and Amara had watched her do it, so the oath would be a problem.

* * *

Kepler Station was a memorial now. That had been the fleets' idea — both of them, arriving at it independently, which was its own small miracle. The garden was gone, burned in the crossfire during those terrible six days, and no one had tried to hide that. The scorch marks were still on the walls. The memorial board, mounted at the airlock where visitors arrived, told the whole story: the beacon, the loop, the lie each fleet had built on the same broken signal, the woman who had chosen truth over both their myths and paid for it with the only home she'd ever known.

Astrid's name was not on the board.

That had been Astrid's condition, delivered in the flat, unyielding voice Amara had learned meant the discussion was over: no name, no honors, no statues. "The message wasn't mine," Astrid had said. "I just delivered it. You don't put the courier's name on the letter."

"You lived it," Amara had argued. "You *burned* for it."

"I had a garden. I have seeds. The arithmetic works out." And then Astrid had smiled — the small, crooked smile that Amara had come to understand was the entire emotional vocabulary of a person who had spent decades talking to plants — and changed the subject to soil pH.

The fleets, being fleets, had honored the letter of the request and violated its spirit with bureaucratic elegance. The board read: *Maintained by the Station's Last Gardener.* Visitors asked who that was. The guides, when pressed, said only, "They preferred to remain with the garden," which was true, in the sense that the garden's ashes were still there, and so, in every way that mattered, was Astrid.

* * *

The seed vault had survived. That was the detail Amara loved most, the detail she told at every school visit the *Concord* hosted: the vault had been buried deep in the station's spine, behind the water reclamation systems, and the fire had taken everything above it and left it untouched. Four thousand varieties. Earth's last garden, packed in foil and nitrogen, waiting.

The fleets had split it — that was the treaty's centerpiece, the clause both sides had signed fastest. Half the vault to the Ashfall generation ships, half to the Compact's. Tomatoes to one, wheat to the other, and a bitter little dispute about the potatoes that had been resolved, eventually, by splitting the potatoes.

But Astrid had kept a drawer back. A small one. Amara had been there when the vault was opened, had watched Astrid reach past the labeled trays to the unmarked drawer at the bottom, and had said nothing, because some things you don't put on a memorial board.

"What's in it?" Amara had asked later, aboard the *Concord*, watching Astrid sort the drawer's contents into hand-labeled packets.

"Everything I could carry twice," Astrid said. "The things I talked to the most. The ones that knew my voice." A pause. "And the beans."

"The beans?"

"The beans were rude. They grew sideways out of spite. I respected that." Astrid sealed a packet and wrote on it in careful block letters — not Pre-Collapse Standard, not Ashfall script, not Compact glyphs, but a hybrid alphabet the ship's children had invented during the long translation months, a script that belonged to no fleet because it belonged to all of them. "They're coming with us."

*Us.* Amara had turned the word over for a long time after that. The *Concord of Rivers* was a joint vessel, yes, but it was more than that now. It was where Astrid Halloran had gone, unrecognized and unrecognizable, having traded a name for a future. It was where Amara had learned to garden badly and then, slowly, less badly. It was where the ceasefire had a heartbeat.

* * *

Today was planting day.

The *Concord*'s garden bay was nothing like Kepler Station's cathedral of green. It was a converted cargo hold, forty meters of grow-lights and hydroponic racks, and it smelled like nutrient solution and ambition. But the whole crew had turned out — Ashfall and Compact, veterans and children born mid-war who had never seen either fleet as an enemy because as far as they could tell the adults had always argued about soil like this.

Astrid stood at the front with a tray of seedlings and the expression of someone about to conduct an orchestra of toddlers.

"Rule one," Astrid said. "The plants are in charge."

"They are *not* in charge," said Commander Idris, who had come aboard ostensibly for an inspection and had not left in four months. "This is a military vessel."

"They're in charge," Astrid repeated, with the serenity of a person who had out-waited two fleets and could certainly out-wait one commander. "Rule two: when they die, and some of them will die, you do not yell at them, and you do not yell at yourself. You hand the next one to whoever's standing next to you, and you keep going."

Amara caught the eye of a Compact gunner's mate across the bay — a woman she'd once tracked as a hostile contact signature, who now owed her a standing debt involving a card game and a very unfair bet — and saw her own feeling reflected there: that rule two was not about plants.

They planted for three hours. The children got the fast-sprouting radishes, because morale. Idris, over her own strenuous objections, was assigned the beans, and when Amara pointed out that the beans grew sideways out of spite, Idris had looked at the tray, looked at Astrid, and said, "I see the resemblance," and for one dangerous moment it seemed like the commander might actually smile in public.

Amara knelt at the end of the last rack with a seedling in her hands — a tomato, Earth stock, two hundred years and one war old — and pressed its roots into the medium the way Astrid had taught her: gently, like introducing two strangers who were going to have to live together.

Her grandmother had sung, in the old language, that a seed was a letter you wrote to someone you'd never meet. Amara had thought, as a child, that this was about the fleet — about generations ships and generations of patience. She understood now that it was about this: a dead woman's apology, delivered by a nameless gardener, planted by a pilot who'd once crashed out of the sky and been caught.

* * *

That evening, Amara found Astrid in the bay, alone under the grow-lights, doing nothing in particular — just standing there, looking at the racks, the way Amara had once seen her look at the burned garden's walls on Kepler Station.

"They'll come up in about a week," Amara said.

"I know."

"You could tell people, you know. Who you are. The fleets would build you a statue on every hull that has a hull to build it on."

Astrid considered this with the gravity the question deserved, which was none. "Statues can't water anything."

"No. But they remember."

"The seeds remember." Astrid touched a leaf, feather-light. "That's better. Statues just stand there. Seeds *go* somewhere. They end up in places you'd never think, in hands you'd never meet, growing sideways out of spite in soil you'll never see." A pause. "That's not a memorial. That's a plan."

Amara leaned against the rack beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and looked out through the bay's viewport at the dark — at the distant lights of the Ashfall flotilla on one side, the Compact formation on the other, and the small, stubborn constellation of the *Concord of Rivers* between them, growing things.

"Grandmother used to say the old language had no word for 'stranger,'" Amara said. "Just a word for 'someone whose seeds you haven't eaten yet.'"

Astrid snorted. "Your grandmother sounds like she would have gotten along with the beans."

They stood there a while longer, two gardeners in a hold full of next year, while the ship carried them all — the guilty and the grieving, the healed and the healing, the beans — toward whatever soil came next.

And in a week, the first green would come up, and it would belong to everyone.
