## 1. Contraband of the Mind

The Long Answer hung in the shadow of Meridian's customs ring like a stray dog waiting outside a butcher shop — patient, unloved, and certain of the outcome.

Mara Zhou watched the inspection drone crawl along his hull on the cockpit display and counted its pauses. Pause at the cargo bay seam. Pause at the coolant vent he'd never bothered to fix because fixing it would mean admitting the ship was worth fixing. Pause at the dorsal antenna array, where forty-one memory chips sat sealed inside a housing that was supposed to hold weather sensors.

Forty-one chips. Eleven hundred lives, give or take. Weddings, last words, a child's first steps recorded by a mother who'd died before the child could walk. The whole messy archive of people who had decided that forgetting was worse than dying, and who were willing to pay a smuggler to prove it.

"Answer, this is Customs Cutter Vireo," said the comm, in the flat cadence of someone reading from a script they'd read ten thousand times. "Hold position. Boarding party inbound."

"Holding," Mara said. He always held. Holding was free.

He leaned back in the pilot's chair and let his eyes drift to the viewport, where Meridian turned below him like something drowning in slow motion. From up here it still looked beautiful — blue-green continents wrapped in cloud, the southern ice cap catching the system's dim sun and throwing it back like a coin at the bottom of a well. Nine years of flying this route and he could still be fooled for almost three seconds before he remembered what the beauty was hiding.

The colony's orbital tether had failed six years ago. Its atmospheric processors were running on parts scavenged from ships that no longer existed. Its population had dropped from four hundred thousand to somewhere south of ninety thousand, not through any single catastrophe but through the slow arithmetic of neglect — the fleet wouldn't fund repairs because Meridian sat on the wrong side of the quarantine cordon, and the Outer Compact couldn't reach it because the cordon was enforced with live weapons. A colony caught between two governments that both agreed, in their different ways, that it wasn't worth the ammunition.

And yet the people down there kept making memories. Kept falling in love, burying parents, teaching kids to fish in the acid-tolerant streams of the southern valleys. Kept recording all of it on chips their failing archives couldn't hold, and paying men like Mara to carry those memories out past the cordon to relatives, to diaspora communities, to anyone in the wider human volume who might one day want to know that these particular people had existed.

It was, Mara thought, the only honest contraband in the system. Guns killed people. Stimulants hollowed them out. But a memory chip just said: we were here. We mattered. Somebody should know.

The boarding klaxon chimed, polite as a doorbell.

* * *

Customs Officer Dario Voss came up his ramp like a man walking into a dentist's office — shoulders braced, jaw set, already rehearsing his apologies. He was younger than Mara remembered, which meant Mara was getting older, which was the whole joke of deep-space work compressed into a single unwelcome observation.

"Zhou." Voss stopped at the top of the ramp, PADD clutched to his chest like a shield. "You know I have to do the full manifest check."

"I know you have to." Mara gestured toward the galley. "Coffee's fresh. Well. Fresh-adjacent."

"This isn't— I can't drink your coffee while I inspect your ship, that's a conflict of—"

"You drank my coffee in March."

Voss's ears went pink. "That was before the sector audit."

Ah. The sector audit. Mara filed that away and led the way aft at an amiable stroll, letting Voss see exactly what he expected to see: a freighter old enough to vote, hold frames patched with mismatched plating, a cargo bay full of legitimate salvage — decommissioned water recyclers bound for Meridian's black market, which was to say, for Meridian's actual market, since the legal one had stopped existing around the time the funding did.

Voss checked the recyclers. Voss checked the bilge. Voss checked the crew quarters, which contained one cot, one chair, and a photograph of a woman Mara had stopped being able to look at directly about four years ago. Voss did not check the dorsal antenna array, because checking the dorsal antenna array required a hull-walk permit, and hull-walk permits required a supervisor's signature, and Supervisor Ehli had been denying Voss's permit requests since the audit — because a junior officer who found nothing suspicious needed no supervision, but a junior officer who went poking around unescorted was a liability.

Mara knew all of this because knowing things was the job. The chips were almost incidental. What he actually smuggled, most days, was attention — small, careful observations of how systems worked and where they strained, gathered over years, paid for in patience. Everyone thought smuggling was about hiding things. It wasn't. It was about understanding what people needed to not-see, and why.

They ended up in the galley after all. Voss sat with his untouched coffee and stared at the bulkhead.

"How is she?" Mara asked.

Voss didn't pretend not to understand. That was the thing about grief — it made liars out of everyone except the grieving themselves. "She's sleeping more. The doctors say that's normal." His thumb moved against the PADD's edge, a small repetitive motion, like a man smoothing a wrinkle that wouldn't smooth. "The hospice wing on Ceres Station has a waitlist of eight months. Eight months. She doesn't have eight months."

"No," Mara agreed quietly. "She doesn't."

"My sister says there's a clinic on Titan doing experimental work. Memory-mapping therapy. They record the patient's — everything. Before the degradation takes it. So the family can—" His voice snagged and he hauled it back like a net line. "It costs more than I make in six years."

The coffee machine gurgled. Somewhere below them, ninety thousand people went on living without orbital support, and above them two fleets glared at each other across a cordon built from the wreckage of a war nobody had won, and here in the galley of the Long Answer a customs officer was telling a smuggler the exact shape of his desperation, because the smuggler was the only person in the system who'd asked.

"The Titan clinics don't take fleet scrip," Mara said. "But they take hard currency. And hard currency moves through channels that don't show up on manifests."

"I'm not asking you to—"

"You're not asking me anything. You're going to finish your inspection, log the standard delay for a salvage hauler with faulty transponder timing — thirty minutes, maybe forty, enough to put me outside the patrol window — and then you're going to clear me through. And in return, when I come back through this ring in five weeks, there'll be a chip in the return crate addressed to D. Voss, personal effects, no declaration required. Contents: whatever the Titan clinic needs from you to start the intake process. Payment routing. Medical records transfer. All of it clean."

Voss looked at him with an expression Mara had seen a thousand times across a thousand airlocks — the specific anguish of a decent person discovering that decency has a price tag and someone else is holding the register.

"And if I report you instead?"

"Then your sister watches her records degrade along with her mother, and you get a commendation, and in eighteen months the audit board rotates through and some other officer finds the discrepancy in your March inspection logs anyway, because there is one, Dario, there always is, and I keep better records than you do." Mara said it without heat, almost gently. That was important. Threats delivered gently landed harder than threats shouted. "I'm not your enemy. I'm the only person in this entire bureaucracy who wants your mother remembered."

Voss looked down at his coffee for a long moment. When he raised his head, his eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"Thirty minutes," he said. "Log it at thirty-five. Patrol window closes at 0400."

"0355," Mara said. "I like a margin."

* * *

He watched the Vireo recede on the display, a bright insect crawling back to its hive, and felt the familiar post-transaction emptiness settle over him like ballast. This was the part nobody warned you about — not the danger, not the chase, but the quiet afterward, when you'd just traded on a dying woman and a weeping boy and the ship was very silent and the coffee was cold and the next stop was five hours away and there was no one to tell about any of it.

Lonely competence, his first captain had called it, back when Mara was young enough to think competence was a destination rather than a trap. You get good at surviving alone, she'd said, and then alone is the only place you fit.

He ran the pre-departure sequence by hand. Old habit. The Long Answer's autopilot had opinions, and Mara had learned long ago that the only reliable intelligence aboard was his own.

"Dorsal array," he murmured, keying the diagnostic. "Weather sensor housing. Status nominal."

Nominal. Forty-one souls riding on the outside of his ship in a box labeled with a lie, and the box reported itself content. There was a sermon in that somewhere. Mara had never been religious, but he'd carried enough deathbed memories to know that most people found God in the last six months, and most of them described the same thing: not a voice, not a light, just a sudden unbearable clarity about what had mattered all along, arriving precisely when it could no longer be used.

He throttled up. The customs ring fell away. Below, Meridian turned, beautiful and doomed, its night side pricked with the lights of settlements that drew power from reactors older than Mara's career.

The comm crackled. Not the customs band — a tight-beam relay, encrypted, routed through a buoy that officially didn't exist. Mara's stomach performed its small practiced drop. Only one organization used that buoy.

*"Long Answer, this is Kestrel Actual. Confirm delivery schedule and hold position for tasking. Vess wants eyes on you before you dock. Acknowledge."*

Mara looked at the message for a long moment. Then at the planet. Then at the nav plot, where his approach vector curved down toward Port Anselm and the waiting families and Juno, who would be sitting on the dock cratelike as always, pretending he hadn't been counting the hours.

"Acknowledged, Kestrel Actual," he said. "En route. Out."

He cut the channel before they could add anything else, and put the Long Answer into her descent, and tried — the way he did every run, with diminishing success — to remember what it had felt like when this job was just a job.

Below him, through the thinning atmosphere, the lights of Meridian rose up to meet him like hands.
