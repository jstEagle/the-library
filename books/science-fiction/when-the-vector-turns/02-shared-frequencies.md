## 2. Shared Frequencies

The dream came back to Sef on the fourth night, and this time it stayed.

She stood in it the way you stand in shallow water, weightless but held, and far away a line of light turned. Not spun—turned, the way a key turns, the way a thought turns over in the dark before it becomes a word. The line bent around something she couldn't see, something patient and enormous, and as it bent it made a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard: a low, sweet frequency, like a mother's hum pitched down to the size of a planet.

When she woke, the sound was still in her molars. She lay in her bunk in the children's dormitory and listened to forty other children breathing, and wondered—not for the first time—whether any of them had been standing in the water too.

She didn't ask. Not yet. On Meridian, you learned early which questions made adults go quiet in a particular way, the way they went quiet about Earth, about the voyage, about the years before the colony had a name. Sef had a talent, the teachers said, for noticing what people didn't say. It wasn't a compliment. It got her moved to the back of the classroom and, once, to the counselor's office, where a kind woman with tired eyes asked her if she missed her birth-parents and Sef said, honestly, *I never had any, I was decanted here*, and the woman's face did the quiet thing.

But the dream kept turning. And on the sixth night, when she woke with the frequency humming in her jaw, she heard it answered—faintly, from three bunks over—by a child crying in his sleep. Not a nightmare cry. The other kind. The kind that means something beautiful has happened to you and you have no one to tell.

Sef got up. She crossed the dormitory floor on bare feet, past the heat exchangers that ticked like slow clocks, and crouched beside Tam's bunk. He was seven, all elbows, and his face in the dim emergency lighting was wet and shining and *smiling*.

"Tam," she whispered. "Tam. What did you see?"

His eyes opened without the fog of sleep in them, as if he'd been waiting. "The line," he said. "It turned toward me."

* * *

Astrid knew something was wrong with the power draw before he knew what it meant.

The colony's grid was a closed argument: what the reactors made, the colony spent, and the difference between those numbers was supposed to be a rounding error, a whisper. For thirty years he'd kept his beacon fed by skimming that whisper—siphoning milliwatts through routing tables so old that no living auditor had written them, hiding his theft inside the grid's own noise. It was the closest thing he had to the captain's voice: her handwriting, preserved in stolen current.

But for six nights, the draw had been wrong in a new way. Not larger. *Shaped.* Something was pulling power in pulses, in a rhythm he recognized because he'd built its mirror into the beacon's handshake protocol. Someone—or something—was speaking back.

He sat in the navigation alcove at 0300 ship-time, surrounded by the amber glow of displays that predated the colony's birth, and watched the waveform crawl across his oldest screen. The pulse pattern wasn't random. It wasn't noise. It was the beacon's own query, returned with modifications—answered, annotated, *continued*, the way a conversation continues when the other party has something to say.

"Captain," he said aloud, to the empty alcove, the way he did when the silence got structural. "What did you do?"

She didn't answer. She never did. But in the cryo-bay two decks down, forty-one backups of Captain Elin Vashti slept in their amber pods, and Astrid had spent thirty years walking past them without waking them, because waking her meant admitting the lie had outlived its author.

He pulled the return signal apart for the hundredth time, but this time he stopped filtering for structure and just listened—raw, unshaped, the way Sef's dream would have sounded if dreams could be recorded. And there, buried in the carrier wave like a body buried in a field, was a voice.

Her voice.

*"—if you're hearing this, Astrid, then the beacon answered, and I need you to know that I never told you the whole truth. The beacon wasn't built to warn. I built it to ask. And whatever is answering—"*

The recording degraded into hash. He played it again. And again. On the fourth pass he realized his hands were shaking, and that he had stopped breathing somewhere in the middle, and that the alcove's air recyclers were the only sound in a universe that had just quietly changed its shape.

She had lied to him. Not the lie he'd inherited—the *other* lie, the one underneath, the foundation under the foundation. Thirty years he had kept her secret, grieved her, guarded her sleeping copies like a priest guarding relics, and all that time the beacon had been doing something she'd never told him: not warning the dark away. *Calling* it in.

He should have felt betrayed. He catalogued the feeling, turned it over, looked for the wound. What he found instead was stranger: relief. Because if she had lied to him, then the lie was not a wall between them but a *handoff*—one keeper passing a secret to the next, each of them trusted with a piece too heavy to carry whole. She hadn't deceived him. She had *started* him, the way you start a story you can't finish yourself.

The waveform pulsed. The dream-frequency, he was almost sure now, though he had no proof and no language for it yet. Something on the other side of the jump window was humming, and the colony's children were humming back, and Astrid Ishikawa—invisible man, keeper of the lie—was the only adult on Meridian who knew the song had words.

* * *

By the ninth night, every child under fourteen on Meridian had the dream.

Sef knew because they stopped hiding it. It moved through the dormitories the way weather moves through grass—you could see the direction of it in how the little ones clustered at breakfast, heads together, drawing on napkins: a line, always a line, bending around a center none of them could draw. The teachers confiscated the napkins. The teachers talked in the low voices. The word *contagion* was used, and then the word *hysteria*, and then a council meeting was scheduled, and Sef, who noticed what people didn't say, noticed that nobody said *the beacon*.

She'd found the antenna array by following the adults who didn't want to be followed. There was a man who came up to the ridge at night—tall, gray, careful, the kind of person who moved like an apology. The other kids called him the Ghost of Deck Nine because he was never in the mess hall, never at the festivals, never anywhere a person was supposed to be. Sef had watched him for three weeks before she understood: he was watching the sky. And the sky, on the nights the dream was strongest, was watching back.

She climbed the ridge path after lights-down, breath fogging in the cold scrub of the highlands, and found him crouched at the base of the array with a diagnostic slate, looking—for one unguarded second—like a man at a graveside.

"You're the one drawing the power," Sef said.

He didn't startle. That was the first thing she liked about him. He turned slowly, and up close his face was older than old, weathered like the ridge itself, and his eyes had the particular exhaustion of someone who has been alone so long that company is a foreign language he's trying to remember.

"You shouldn't be here," he said.

"The children are dreaming about a turning line of light." She said it flatly, a fact offered to a fact. "You're the antenna man. So. Is it you?"

The wind moved through the array above them, a sound like the whole structure breathing. Astrid looked at this child—this decanted, unparented, impossible child who had walked out of the dark and named him in one sentence—and felt thirty years of silence crack along a seam he hadn't known was there.

"It isn't me," he said. "It's the beacon. And it isn't only broadcasting anymore."

"Broadcasting what?"

He looked up at the array, at the stars beyond it, at the place in the sky where the jump window hung like a held breath. When he spoke again, his voice was quiet enough that she had to lean in to catch it.

"A question," he said. "And something out there has started answering. In your dreams, I think. In all of theirs."

Sef felt the frequency stir in her molars, sweet and enormous, and for the first time in her life she understood what the adults' silences had been made of: not the absence of answers, but the weight of ones too large to say.

"Then you'd better tell me the whole truth," she said. "Because the little ones are starting to draw what's *inside* the turning. And the last one looked like a fleet."

Far below them, in the navigation alcove, an alarm Astrid had never heard before began, very softly, to ring.
