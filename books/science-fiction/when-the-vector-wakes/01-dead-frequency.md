## 1. Dead Frequency

The Meridian orbits were a graveyard that still charged tolls.

Noor Voss watched the traffic control feed scroll past on the freighter's aging console, half-listening to the automated customs drone demand their manifest for the third time. The *Long Answer* sat in queue behind forty other hulls, all of them hauling salvage, ore, and secrets through the contested lanes where three governments pretended at jurisdiction and none of them bothered with searchlights after dark.

"Tell the drone we're carrying agricultural substrate," Sefi said from the pilot's cradle, not looking up. "Again. With feeling."

"We are carrying agricultural substrate."

"We are carrying agricultural substrate and nine crates Dax won't let me look inside."

"That's called respecting the engineer," Noor said, and keyed the channel. "Meridian Control, *Long Answer*, confirming manifest: agricultural substrate, non-perishable, destination Kettle's Reach." A pause they'd learned to fill with boredom. "Requesting transit clearance."

The drone took ninety seconds to decide they were boring enough to exist. In those ninety seconds, Noor did what they always did in queues — let their attention drift into the static between channels. It was an old habit from the academy, before the academy had expelled them for methods the review board called "unsound" and Noor had called "listening." You could learn a lot from dead air. Smugglers forgot to mute secondary bands. Corporate couriers hummed. Grieving people left open mics pointed at nothing, because silence shared was still silence.

The static in the Meridian orbits was thick with all of it — chatter, leakage, the electromagnetic sediment of a hundred thousand ships passing through. Noor sifted it the way other people worried a loose tooth.

Then the static changed.

It wasn't a word at first. It was a shape under the words — a rhythm, a stress pattern, vowels that opened like doors instead of closing like locks. Noor's hands went still on the console. Their breath went shallow. Because they knew that shape. They knew it the way you know a face in a dream: completely, uselessly, without being able to say how.

*—shen varu kel issa—*

The syllables arrived whole. Not translated. Arrived, the way your own name arrives when someone says it across a room.

Noor hadn't spoken Vessari since they were six years old. Hadn't heard it since their grandmother died — a quiet woman who sang in a language no one else on the station spoke, who told Noor it was "just an old lullaby dialect," which was a lie so large it had its own gravity. The Vessari were extinct. Two centuries extinct. Purged, the histories said, in the polite way that made it sound like weather. There were recordings, fragments, academic reconstructions. There were no speakers. There had been no speakers for two hundred years.

And something out past the shipping lanes was calling for help in their grandmother's voice-tongue, fluent and desperate and *alive*.

"—kel issa toru, shen varu—" The voice broke apart, reassembled. It sounded old. Not elderly — ancient. Like stone learning to speak. "—no one answers. No one has answered. Please. Someone must remember how to answer."

Noor's hand hovered over the transmit key and shook.

They didn't respond. Not yet. Some part of them understood, with the cold clarity that had gotten them expelled, that answering would change everything — that there was a line between before and after this moment, and their hand was already crossing it. Instead they recorded. They tagged the frequency. They pulled the bearing off the signal's drift: deep past the last charted relay, out toward the edge of dead space, where the maps went gray and the insurance companies stopped pretending.

"Noor." Sefi's voice, careful. "You've been staring at that band for four minutes. Your face is doing something I don't like."

"Sefi." Noor's own voice came out wrong. Thin. Young. "I need you to listen to something."

They played it. The bridge went quiet around the sound — that door-opening vowel music, that stone-old voice asking to be answered. Sefi didn't understand a word of it. Noor watched her face anyway, watched her hear what Noor heard underneath: *please, someone must remember.*

"That's not any language I know," Sefi said slowly. "That's not any language anyone knows."

"It's Vessari."

Sefi turned in the cradle. She looked at Noor the way people look at a compass needle spinning. "Noor. The Vessari are dead. That's not a controversial position. That's history."

"I know what it is." Noor played it again. Their own mouth moved with it, silently, shaping syllables they hadn't shaped in twenty years and had never once forgotten. "I know what it is because I grew up hearing it. My grandmother spoke it. She said it was a lullaby dialect. It wasn't. It was the real thing, and she was hiding, and she taught me before she taught me to be afraid of it." They swallowed. "Someone is transmitting a distress call in a dead language from the edge of dead space, Sefi. And it's asking if anyone remembers how to answer."

"Could be a recording loop. Old beacon."

"The syntax shifts. Listen — here, here, here. It's improvising. Recomposing. Recordings don't grieve differently each time they play."

Sefi was quiet for a long moment. Then she did the thing Noor loved most about her and feared second-most: she believed them. "Dax is going to hate this."

* * *

Dax hated it with the thoroughness he brought to everything, which was to say he hated it structurally, historically, and with diagrams.

"A distress call," he said, spread-eagled over the galley table, "in an extinct language, from beyond the last relay, in contested space, during the exact week Vantara Consolidated has survey ships crawling through the Meridian orbits like flies on a wound." He tapped the table. Each tap landed like a rivet. "I can build you a trap that looks exactly like this. I have built traps that look less convincing than this. Whoever set it wants exactly one kind of person to come running: a xenolinguist with a savior complex and a ship full of unlicensed cargo."

"You flatter me," Noor said. "I don't have a complex. I have a calling."

"You have a warrant in two jurisdictions."

"One jurisdiction. The other one lost the paperwork."

Dax turned to Sefi. "Tell them."

Sefi leaned against the galley bulkhead, arms crossed, and Noor saw her doing the arithmetic she never showed anyone — the same arithmetic she'd been doing since the day she'd bought a quarter share of a failing freighter with money she didn't explain. "I ran the bearing," she said. "It's outside the patrol corridors. If we go quiet and burn dark, we can be there in thirty hours and back before our Kettle's Reach window closes. We'd lose the contract. We'd eat the penalty."

"We'd eat more than the penalty if it's a Vantara honeypot," Dax said. "You know what they do to salvagers who touch their claims. You know what they do to *anyone* they want gone. Ask my last crew. Oh wait." His jaw tightened. "You can't."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The *Long Answer*'s environmental system cycled, breathing its dry mechanical breath. Noor thought about Dax's last crew, about the story he'd told once, drunk, on a night neither of them mentioned afterward: a corporate purge dressed up as a quarantine, a station sealed with people inside, and Dax alive because he'd been out on a hull walk when the vents closed.

"Dax," Noor said quietly. "I'm not asking you to believe the call is real. I'm telling you I heard a language that has been dead for two hundred years, and it knew things only a living mind could know. Whatever's out there — a wreck, a ghost, a trap, a miracle — it's transmitting *grief*. Real grief. I've spent my whole life listening to languages, living and dead, and grief doesn't synthesize. Not that well. Not yet."

"And if it's real?" Dax's voice dropped. "If it's real, then somebody out there killed the people who spoke that language, and whatever's left of them has been waiting two hundred years for someone dumb enough to come close. You want to be the answer to a two-hundred-year-old distress call? Fine. But understand what you're volunteering for. Distress calls that old aren't requests. They're debts."

"Then we go settle one," Sefi said.

Dax looked at her. Looked at Noor. Looked at the ceiling as if petitioning a god he didn't believe in for a transfer. "Vote," he said finally. "Formal. And I want it noted — loudly, in the log, in writing — that the engineer objects."

"Noted," Noor said. "For the record: engineer Dax objects, and engineer Dax comes anyway, because engineer Dax has never once in six years let us walk into anything without him."

"That's not loyalty," Dax muttered, pushing off the table. "That's damage control."

It was, Noor thought, watching him go, both. It was always both with Dax.

* * *

They burned dark for thirty hours.

Noor spent most of them at the comm array with the recording, teaching themselves back their own inheritance. It came back faster than literacy should — syllables unlocking like doors in a house they'd been locked out of since childhood. By hour ten they could parse the structure of the plea. By hour twenty they could hear what they'd been refusing to hear: that the voice wasn't repeating its request anymore. It had changed. Somewhere in the last day, the transmission had shifted from *please answer* to something quieter, wearier, addressed to no one.

*Shen varu kel issa,* it said now, over and over, soft as ash settling. *We carried it alone. We carried it alone. We carried it alone.*

By hour twenty-nine, Noor couldn't stand it anymore.

They keyed the transmitter. They didn't ask Sefi. They didn't run it past Dax. They sat alone on the dim bridge with the dead-space edge crawling closer on the plot, and they opened a channel on the frequency where a dead language had been talking to itself for who knew how long, and they answered in Vessari — fluently, badly, their accent a child's accent, their grammar rusted, their voice cracking on the second word.

"*Kel issa toru,*" Noor said. *We remember. We answer.* "This is Noor Voss aboard the vessel *Long Answer*. I hear you. I'm coming."

The static took their words and gave back nothing.

For eleven seconds, nothing. Long enough for Noor to feel the specific vertigo of having spoken aloud into a grave — long enough to wonder if they'd hallucinated the whole signal, if two decades of dreams they couldn't parse had finally curdled into waking madness, if their grandmother's lullabies had been a fever all along—

Then the channel opened.

The voice came through changed. Closer. Warmer. As if something vast had leaned down toward something very small. And it said, in perfect, patient, two-hundred-years-hungry Vessari:

"*Issa vesh Noor Voss. We heard you the first time. We have been listening to you sleep for a very long while.*"

The console lights flickered. On the plot, at the edge of the gray, a derelict hulk woke up — transponders flaring in a design no registry recognized — and turned its attention, unmistakably, deliberately, toward them.

Behind Noor, unnoticed until now, the cabin intercom crackled. From somewhere below decks, faint through the ship's bones, came the sound of Dax swearing.
