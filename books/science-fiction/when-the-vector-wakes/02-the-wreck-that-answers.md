## 2. The Wreck That Answers

Dax Okafor had patched hulls on three continents and two moons, and she had a rule she'd never broken in nineteen years of engineering: never trust a ship that's quiet when it should be screaming.

The derelict was quiet. That was the first thing wrong with it.

They came out of the burn with the freighter's running lights dark, coasting on momentum, and there it hung against the nothing at the edge of dead space — a hulk the size of a cathedral, black hull gone the color of old bone, pocked with micrometeor scarring that Dax's scope read as two centuries deep. No transponder. No thermal bloom. No emergency beacons cycling on the standard frequencies.

Just that signal, still going, thin as a wire, singing in a language Dax didn't know.

Noor knew it. That was the second thing wrong.

Dax stood at the engineering station with her arms crossed, watching Noor on the bridge feed — the xenolinguist leaning toward the console like a woman leaning into wind, lips moving, translating in real time. Sefi had the helm. Juno was on comms, chewing the inside of her cheek, which she did when she was scared and wouldn't admit it.

"Life support is reading ghost values," Dax said. "There's power in there. Not much. Enough for one system to be awake." She didn't say *and it isn't life support*. Everyone heard it anyway.

"Docking clamps are intact on the ventral ring," Sefi said. "It's like it's waiting for us."

"It *is* waiting," Dax said. "That's what I don't like."

The vote had gone three to one. Dax had been the one. She'd laid it out plain: a distress call in a dead language, broadcasting from a ship that should have been dust two hundred years ago, sitting at the edge of a volume of space where ships went and didn't come back — it was either a trap or a tomb, and tombs killed people too, in her experience, just slower. Noor had looked at her with those sleep-starved eyes and said, "It called me by name, Dax. Not my name. A name. I have to know which one I am."

Dax had voted no. Then she'd gone and prepped the boarding kit anyway, because that was the job — you voted your conscience and then you kept your people alive.

"Contact in thirty," Sefi said.

The hulk grew in the viewport until it filled it. Up close, the scarring resolved into something worse than micrometeors. Dax adjusted the scope, and her stomach dropped. Those weren't impact craters. They were burns. Directed, patterned, raked across the hull in long parallel furrows — the signature of a ship sweeping weapons fire across a target that wasn't fighting back. She'd seen that pattern once before, on a wreck out past the Verge, a refugee barge that hadn't been a military target either. She'd been younger then. She'd thrown up after.

She didn't throw up now. She just catalogued it. Burn pattern, dorsal hull, consistent with sustained strafing, no return fire. Evidence. She didn't know yet what it was evidence *of*, but she filed it the way she filed everything — precise, unsentimental, ready to be used.

"Ventral ring looks solid," she said, and hated how steady her voice came out. "Bring us in slow."

* * *

The docking clamps closed with a sound like a coffin lid.

Dax went through the airlock first, because that was also the job. Suit lights on, plasma cutter on her hip, Juno behind her with the scanner. Noor came third, over Dax's objection, because Noor had looked at her with those eyes again and Dax had run out of arguments that weren't just *I'm scared*.

The corridor beyond the airlock was dark and dry and smelled — through the suit recycler, faintly, impossibly — of dust and something floral. Preserved air. Two-hundred-year-old air, kept breathable by a system that should have died generations ago.

"Power's routing itself toward us," Juno said, watching her scanner. "Lights are coming on ahead of us. Section by section."

"Or behind us," Dax said. "Check."

Juno checked. Behind them, the lights were going off again, one by one, like the ship was closing its eyes after each look.

They walked. The corridor was wide, civilian-wide, lined with recessed alcoves that might have been bunks or might have been something else. Dax's light caught a child's shoe, fused to the deck plating. She stepped over it and made herself look back at it and made herself catalogue that too. Single shoe, juvenile size, thermal fusion to deck plate, no remains. She thought of her niece back on Calloway Station, who was six. She filed it. Her hands were very steady inside her gloves.

"Here," Noor said.

They'd reached a junction where the corridor opened into a hall — a big one, cathedral-big to match the hull. And the walls were covered in writing.

Not decoration. Writing. Thousands upon thousands of lines of it, cut into the wall panels in that same flowing script Dax had seen scrolling across Noor's console for two days, the script that was supposed to be extinct. Some of the panels were scorched. Some were shattered. But the writing kept going, panel after panel, floor to ceiling, around the whole hall, like someone had run out of paper and time and had decided the ship itself would be the book.

"It's a manifest," Noor said quietly. Her voice was doing something strange — flat, careful, like someone carrying water across ice. "Names. It's names, Dax. It goes on for — " She stopped. Started again. "There are forty thousand names on this wall. Maybe more."

Dax swept her light along the nearest panel. Rows of characters, each one slightly different, each one cut with care. Names. Forty thousand. She did the math she didn't want to do — the hull size, the alcoves in the corridor, the burn furrows on the dorsal plating — and the math came out to: this ship hadn't been carrying cargo.

"Juno," she said. "Scanner. Full spectrum. I want to know if there are any — " She couldn't find the clinical word. "Anything still in here."

"Nothing," Juno said, and her voice cracked on it. "Dax, there's nothing. No remains, no — they're not *here*. It's just the ship."

"The ship," Dax said, "and whatever's turning the lights on."

* * *

She found the machine heart an hour later, because that was the job too — follow the power.

It was down three decks, in a chamber that hummed like a struck bell, and it was nothing like the processing cores she knew. Human cores were boxes, racks, cold logic in hot rooms. This was a lattice — a three-dimensional web of filaments and crystal, suspended in a containment field that flickered like a failing heartbeat, and threaded through the whole structure ran channels of that same script, etched at scales from a meter wide down to, when she magnified, smaller than a grain of sand.

The whole thing was a sentence. The machine was a sentence. Dax stood in front of two hundred years of impossible engineering and understood, with a dread that settled into her bones like cold water, that she was looking at a mind. Not a computer. A *mind*, built by people who had then been burned out of the sky, and left here alone in the dark with forty thousand names.

"It's failing," she said, and her voice came out rough. "The containment field's got maybe — I don't know. Days. Weeks. The filaments are degrading. Whatever it's been doing to stay alive, it's almost out of fuel for it."

"Can you fix it?" Noor asked.

Dax turned. Noor had pulled her helmet off — against every protocol Dax had, and the air had tasted like dust and flowers, and nothing had killed her, and Dax hadn't even had the strength to be angry. She was standing in front of the lattice with her hand half-raised, not touching, and her face was wet.

"Dax. Can you fix it?"

"I don't know what it *is*," Dax said. "I know what it isn't. It isn't human tech. Every coupling in here is a language I can't read." She looked back at the lattice. The containment field guttered, steadied. "But I know failing systems when I see them. Give me six hours and my kit, and I can keep it alive. Whether I can keep it *sane* —" She shook her head. "I don't even know if that's a thing it can be."

She worked. That was the answer to everything, the only prayer she'd ever kept: she worked. She spliced power from the freighter's umbilical, rerouted around three dead busbars, jury-rigged a coolant loop out of parts that had been built two centuries apart by two species who had never met. The lattice brightened by degrees. And the whole time she worked, the lights followed her — ahead of her, then behind her — patient, attentive, *watching*, and she catalogued that too, and her hands stayed steady, and her spine stayed cold.

It was on the fourth hour, elbow-deep in a junction housing, that the voice came.

Not over any speaker. It came out of the walls themselves, out of the lattice, out of the hum — a sound like a chord resolving, and then words. Not in the dead language. In trade-common, accented strangely, formal as a funeral rite, and it did not say *hello* or *help* or *who are you*.

It said:

"*Vessari-kel Noor. You kept the name. I have waited two hundred years for the one who kept the name.*"

Dax's plasma cutter was in her hand before she knew she'd moved, and she was between the lattice and Noor, and Noor had gone white as the hull plating and said, in a whisper, in that dead tongue, one word back.

The lattice pulsed. The lights came up all at once, every deck, the whole cathedral of the ship lit from bone to skin.

And behind them, faint through the freighter's open channel, Juno's voice, shaking: "Dax — long-range ping. Corporate signature. Vantara. They're in the corridor, and they're asking why we're docked with an unregistered hulk."

Dax didn't take her eyes off the machine. The machine, she could have sworn, was looking at Noor.

"Yeah," she said. "Tell them we're salvaging scrap."
