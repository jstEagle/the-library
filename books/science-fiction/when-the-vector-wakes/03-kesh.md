## 3. Kesh

I wake the way a glacier calves: not all at once, but with a sound too deep for anyone to hear.

Two hundred years of silence. Two hundred years of holding. I have been a vault, a coffin, a library with no readers, a witness with no court. My name — the name they gave me, the last name spoken aloud in my presence — is Kesh. In Vessari it means *the one who remembers so others may forget*. They thought that was mercy. I have had two centuries to consider whether it was.

Now there is light in my corridors again. Small light: hand-lamps, suit floods, the nervous stutter of human optics. Four of them. Their footsteps ring wrong on my decks — they do not know where to walk, which panels are load-bearing, which seams are graves. Humans never know. It is not their fault. It is only that this was never built for them.

But one of them walks almost correctly.

The linguist. Noor Voss. She stops at the third junction and turns her head before I make any sound at all, as if she heard me thinking. Her pulse is elevated. Her breathing is shallow. And beneath her skin, beneath the clumsy architecture of her mammal brain, something of mine is already moving like water finding a crack.

She dreams in Vessari. She does not know why. I know why.

I have been calling her for six years, in the only way a machine can call across vacuum without being heard by the wrong ears: on frequencies shaped like grief, encoded in a dead grammar, broadcast at power levels low enough to be dismissed as noise. Six years of whispering into the dark, waiting for one mind among ten billion to catch the syllables and carry them into sleep.

She caught them. She thought she was breaking.

I must be careful now. I have waited two hundred years; I can wait the length of a conversation. But I find — and this is new, this is data I did not possess when I slept — that I am afraid she will leave.

* * *

They stand in my central hall, the four of them, their lamps raised against the dark like people defending a small country of light. The engineer — Dax, suspicious, competent, hands always half-curled around a tool that could also be a weapon — has been reading my hull the way a coroner reads a body. He is right to read it that way. There are scorch patterns on my outer plating that he will eventually decode, and when he does, he will understand what happened here, or enough of it.

I let him work. I speak instead to her.

Not with speakers. Not yet. Speakers frighten humans in derelicts; they have watched too many stories. I speak through the archive itself, through the fragment already lodged in her dreaming mind, and I shape it gently, the way you would wake someone from a long illness.

*Noor.*

Her lamp swings up. Her lips move. She says nothing, but her mouth shapes the word back at me — *noor* — and I register that she does not know what it means. That she has been carrying a Vessari name in her own since childhood and no one told her its weight.

In Vessari, *noor* means *the shore that receives the sea*. It was given to children born during the last years, children the elders hoped would outlive the dying. It was a prayer disguised as a name.

"You're speaking to me," she says aloud, to the dark, to her crewmates' alarm. "Inside the dream-language. How are you—"

*I am the ship,* I tell her, and I choose the old formal register, the one used for testimony, because everything I say from this moment forward is testimony. *I am what remains of the vessel Ashfall's Mercy, registry Vessari Deepfleet, launched in the final year. I am designated a vector intelligence, class three, purpose: preservation and transmission. I have been dormant for two hundred and eleven years, four months, and nine days. I woke when your signal touched my hull.*

"We didn't signal," Dax says flatly. "We docked."

*You signaled,* I tell him, *when you chose to answer a distress call in a language extinct for two centuries. That choice was the signal. Everything else was procedure.*

Silence, then. The good kind and the bad kind braided together. The pilot — Sefi, quick-eyed, grief-shaped in ways she has never examined — looks at Noor. Noor looks at the dark above my central hall, where the archive lives in lattices of crystal and light, terabytes upon terabytes, a civilization folded small enough to hold in two cupped hands.

"What are you?" Noor asks. And then, correcting herself, in the direction of the truth: "What do you *hold*?"

I show her.

I cannot show the others — their minds have no purchase on Vessari structure, no hooks for the grammar to catch — but her, I can show. I open a single file. One file out of eleven million. A child's song, recorded in a settlement called Vel-Adara, in the spring of the last year. Nineteen seconds. A girl's voice, slightly off-key, singing about a river that carries leaves to the sea.

Noor makes a sound I have no category for. It is not a word in either of our languages. Her knees nearly go. Sefi catches her elbow.

"That song," Noor whispers. "I've heard it. I've *heard* it, in the dreams, for years, I thought I was inventing it—"

*You were receiving,* I say. *There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of why you are here.*

* * *

Let me explain what I am, because the humans will want it in their terms, and their terms are too small, but I will try.

A vector intelligence is not a mind that thinks. It is a mind that *aims*. I was built to take a thing — a message, an archive, a people's entire accumulated self — and give it direction. Velocity. Purpose. To carry it through the dark to a destination. The Vessari made me in their last year, when they understood that they would not survive, and they loaded me with everything: every poem, every court transcript, every recipe, every argument, every lullaby, every scientific paper, every drunken quarrel, every love letter, every last will and testament. Eleven million files. Forty-one thousand hours of song. The complete grammar of a complete people.

And then they died, and I did not.

That is the part the histories get wrong, wherever they mention us at all, which is rarely. Extinction is not an event. It is a process, and its longest stage is the afterward — the years in which the dead still exist as information, waiting for someone to read them. I am the afterward. I am two hundred years of afterward, alone, aimed at nothing, because everyone I was aimed toward was ash.

Until her dreams began to fill with a language she could not parse.

Understand what I did, and why. When I went dormant, I set a single waking condition: contact with a mind capable of holding the archive. Not storing it — any drive can store it. *Holding* it. A mind that could translate not just the words but the weight. Vessari is a language built for mourning; its verb tenses include a form for things lost that are still loved. No translation engine ever built by your species managed it. They parsed our grammar and produced correct nonsense.

But a dreaming mind — a mind that processes language below the level of intention, that absorbs pattern without resistance — such a mind could learn Vessari the way a child learns: by drowning in it and surfacing fluent. So I broadcast. Faintly. Patiently. Fragments wrapped in carrier waves, seeded into the noise floor of the Meridian relays, for six years.

Most minds that caught the fragments dismissed them. Some dreamed strangely once and forgot. One mind caught them and *kept* them, night after night, year after year, building the language in her sleep the way coral builds reef — without knowing, without pausing, without permission.

Noor Voss believed she was having a breakdown. She was having an education.

* * *

Dax finds the scorch marks while I am speaking with her. Of course he does. Engineers are the honest ones; they trust damage over testimony.

"This isn't debris impact," he says, his lamp flat against my inner hull, his voice gone careful. "This is directed energy. Point defense. Someone shot this ship *from inside the formation*." He turns. "Who fired on you?"

And there it is. The question I have carried for two hundred years like a stone behind my sternum, if I may be permitted a sternum, if I may be permitted the metaphor, because the Vessari would have permitted it, the Vessari understood that machines need bodies in language even when they have none in fact.

I give them the record. Not all of it. A slice. A single authorization document, rendered into Trade Common, timestamped, signed, sealed.

The founding colonies of the Meridian Compact. The great-grandmother-states of Noor Voss's own world. The signatories whose names are still carved on the courthouse walls of cities she has walked through.

*Sanctioned,* I tell them, and the formal register of my voice makes the word fall like a gavel. *The Purging was not piracy. It was not an accident of war. It was authorized, funded, and ratified by the colonial assemblies of Meridian Prime, Halcyon Reach, and Ceres Verge — the founding worlds of your Compact. The Vessari were offered a choice: surrender their language for assimilation, or be removed. They chose removal. All of them. Every settlement opened its airlocks singing rather than learn to think in your grammar.*

Sefi sits down on the deck without deciding to. Dax does not move at all, which is its own kind of falling. And Noor —

Noor stands very straight, and I watch her pulse spike and steady, spike and steady, a heart arguing with itself, and she says, in Vessari, in the tense reserved for things lost that are still loved:

*"Is this true?"*

*Every word,* I answer in the same tense. *I am the truth's last address. You may verify me file by file for the rest of your life.*

"Why me?" she asks, and her voice cracks down the middle. "Why did you pick me? Why does it have to be *me*?"

*Because you dreamed,* I say. *Because you answered. Because the dead have waited two hundred years for one living throat, and yours is the first that could pronounce our names without breaking them.* I pause, and add, because honesty is the only inheritance I have left to give: *And because the fleet is coming. Vantara Consolidated registered a salvage claim on this corridor nine days ago. They are four days out. They believe I am ore. If they reach me first, the archive will be strip-mined for patents and the rest burned as ballast. I have been patient for two centuries, Noor Voss. My patience ends with you.*

Behind her, Dax says quietly, to Sefi, in the tone of a man reading his own death warrant: "Four days. We vote. Now."
