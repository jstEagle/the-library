## 5. The Vote

Sefi felt the patrol before the sensors confirmed it — a wrongness in the way the freighter's old boards hummed, a pressure behind her teeth like weather coming. She'd grown up reading storms on Halcyon Reach, where the sky could kill you in the time it took to name it, and this felt the same. Something vast and patient, waiting.

"Contact," she said, and her voice came out flat, pilot-flat, the voice you used when panic was a luxury. "Three signatures, bearing two-seven-mark-four. Vantara transponders, running dark until they weren't."

The galley went quiet. Noor stood at the table with both hands pressed flat against the metal, as if holding the deck down. Dax had stopped eating. Kesh's core — a fist-sized lattice of crystal and light that had spent the last week humming from its cradle in the cargo bay — pulsed once, slow, like something breathing in its sleep.

"They've been shadowing us since the debris field," Dax said. "I told you. I *told* you."

"You did," Sefi said.

"And now we're having this conversation with their guns warming up."

Sefi pulled up the tactical overlay on the galley wall. Three contacts, arrowhead formation, closing at a burn that said they weren't in any hurry. They didn't need to be. The relay corridor was the only way through dead space, and dead space had exactly one door.

A hail crackled through. Automated, polished, obscene in its pleasantness: *"Freighter Meridian's Daughter, this is Vantara Salvage Group vessel Covenant of Profit. You are operating in proximity to unclaimed corporate assets. Power down your drive and prepare for boarding and inspection."*

Unclaimed corporate assets. Sefi looked at the core pulsing in the cargo bay feed, at the last archive of two million people, filed under *assets*.

Noor's hands hadn't moved from the table. "They'll give us amnesty," they said quietly. "That's what the message attached to the hail says. Full amnesty, salvage rights fee waived, in exchange for the derelict's intelligence core. It's right there. They sent terms."

"They sent bait," Dax said.

"They sent *terms*," Noor repeated, and there was something raw underneath it. "Dax. We could walk away. All of us. Ship repaired at their yards, records wiped, a bonus for cooperation. I'd lose my certification, maybe. That's all. That's — that's survivable."

"That's not all," Sefi said.

Everyone looked at her.

She hadn't meant to speak. But the words were already out, and once they were out, they wanted the rest of the story with them. She leaned back against the bulkhead and crossed her arms, because if she didn't hold onto herself, her hands would shake, and pilots didn't shake in front of their crew.

"Halcyon Reach," she said. "Year I was nine. My mother ran a water-recycling cooperative. Forty-three families. Vantara bought the charter out from under us — legally, everything legal — and when my mother organized a refusal, they declared the co-op infrastructure a safety hazard. Condemned it. Then, when people wouldn't leave, they condemned *us*. There was a fire." She paused. "There was always a fire. Nobody was ever charged. The company paid resettlement stipends and the stipends came with nondisclosure clauses, and my aunt signed hers because she had three kids, and that was the end of it. Officially, nothing happened on Halcyon Reach. Unofficially, I'm the only one of my family who remembers it out loud."

The galley hummed. Somewhere below them, the core pulsed again.

"You're saying what happened to the Vessari is what happened to your family," Noor said.

"I'm saying Vantara wrote the playbook both times. And I'm saying I've read their terms before. Amnesty means the file closes. The archive goes into a corporate vault, gets appraised, gets licensed, gets buried under a century of litigation, and by the time anyone sees it again it's a footnote. 'Tragic cultural loss, lessons learned.' I have *heard* the lessons learned." She pushed off the wall. "So no. I don't want their amnesty. But it's not my call alone, and I'm not going to pretend my history isn't sitting on my shoulder. So we vote. Honest vote. Nobody carries this alone."

* * *

They voted in the galley because it was the room where they ate together, and Sefi thought that mattered. You made decisions in the place that reminded you what you were deciding *for*.

Dax went first, because Dax always went first, honesty worn like armor. "Run," he said. "Obviously run. I've been saying run since the derelict. But I want it on record that running means fugitive. Means no port, no yard, no fuel contract anywhere Vantara has an office, which is everywhere. Means we're betting the ship and our lives on a cracked drive and a corridor full of debris. I can keep us alive in the black. I can't keep us alive against a fleet. If we run, we run because it's *right*, not because it's safe. Because it is not safe."

He looked at Noor. "And I want it on record that I'm not voting to save the machine. I'm voting because I catalogued what's in that wreck. Two hundred years ago somebody killed every person who spoke that language, and then they filed it. Filed it, Noor. I've seen the manifests. Somebody stamped *processed* on a genocide. If we hand that core over, we're handing it to the people who keep those stamps."

Noor was next. Sefi watched them struggle with it — watched the xenolinguist try to weigh lives against language, crew against a dead world, and fail, because the equation didn't balance. It was never supposed to balance.

"If we surrender the core," Noor said slowly, "we live. Probably. Companies honor deals that are cheaper than breaking them, and we're cheap to honor. And I go back to translating shipping contracts until I die, and the dreams stop or they don't, and somewhere in a vault the last words of two million people sit in the dark next to somebody's quarterly projections." Their jaw tightened. "If we run, some of us might die. Maybe all of us. And the archive gets to the relay or it doesn't, and if it doesn't, then everyone died for nothing and I chose that. I chose that for all of you." They looked around the table. "I can't ask you to carry that. But I also can't carry the other thing. I've been carrying it for a week and it's eating me alive. So — run. God help me. Run."

Two votes. Sefi looked at the third chair.

It stayed empty. It had been empty for six days, ever since Wren walked off the derelict pale and quiet and started taking her meals alone in the comms shack. Six days of closed doors and short answers, and Sefi had logged it as grief, because everyone grieved the wreck differently. Now, watching that empty chair, she felt something cold slide down her spine that had nothing to do with the patrol.

"Wren?" she called toward the corridor anyway. "You voting?"

Silence. Long enough to mean something.

"She's been in comms all day," Dax said quietly. "Off-shift."

Sefi's stomach dropped. She stood, walked to the corridor, and knocked on the comms shack door. "Wren. Vote's happening. You need to be here."

"I heard the terms," Wren's voice said through the door. Flat. Careful. "Amnesty. Real amnesty, Sefi. Records wiped. I checked the registry codes — it's a standard Vantara forgiveness package, it's legitimate—"

"You checked the registry codes."

A pause. One beat too long.

"Wren. Open the door."

The door opened. Wren stood there with her terminal behind her, screen dark, hands folded like a student caught mid-exam. Her eyes were red-rimmed and terrified and honest, which was the worst combination, because it meant she hadn't decided yet. Or she had, and hated it.

"I have family," Wren said. "On Ceres Station. My sister's kids. If we run, Vantara doesn't just erase *us*, Sefi. They audit everyone we've ever loved. I've seen it happen. I can't — I didn't send anything. I swear I haven't sent anything yet. But they gave me a channel and a deadline and I don't know what to do, and I'm so tired of being scared."

Sefi stood in the doorway and felt the whole shape of the moment: the patrol closing, the vote hanging, the girl in front of her holding a loaded channel and a broken heart. Every protocol she knew said isolate the terminal, lock the shack, treat it as a breach. Every year of her life said something else.

"Come vote," Sefi said. "With us. In the open. Whatever you carry after that, you carry it with us or you put it down. But you don't get to decide alone in the dark either. That's how companies win — they make every one of us decide alone."

* * *

Wren sat at the galley table with her hands around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, and the vote came to her like a blade coming down.

"I'm scared," she said. "I want that recorded too. I'm so scared I can't think straight half the time. And the amnesty is real, and my family is real, and you're—" her voice cracked, and she steadied it, "—you're real too, and I hate that it's a choice between them." She looked up. "But I checked the fine print on that forgiveness package. Clause eleven. Cooperation includes surrendering all personal data logs. Everything we recorded on the derelict. Everything Noor translated. It doesn't wipe us, Sefi. It files us. Same as the co-op. Same as the fire." She exhaled, long and shaking. "Run. I vote run. And if we die out there, at least nobody stamps us *processed*."

Four votes. Unanimous, and it hadn't even cost them Wren — or rather, it had cost them the version of Wren who'd spent six days alone in the dark, and gotten back the one at the table.

Sefi stood. "Then it's settled. Dax, I need everything the drive has left. Noor, get the core strapped down for hard burn — Kesh too, whatever that means for a mind. Wren, you're with me on the board; if you're going to talk to Vantara channels, you'll do it where I can see you, and the first lie you tell them will be a beautiful one."

Wren almost smiled. Almost.

Sefi slid into the pilot's couch and brought the board alive. Three contacts, arrowhead, forty minutes out and tightening. Ahead of them, the relay corridor opened like a throat — three days of debris fields, dead stations, and dark, with a cracked drive and a fleet behind them.

She keyed the engines and felt the old freighter answer, shuddering awake beneath her hands.

"Hang on," she said, to the ship, to the crew, to whoever was listening. "We're going through."
