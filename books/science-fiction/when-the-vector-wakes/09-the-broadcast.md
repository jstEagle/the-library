## 9. The Broadcast

The relay's core chamber was a sphere of dead glass, and Noor walked into it barefoot because the archive had told them to.

*The Vessari removed their shoes before entering a place of speaking,* Kesh had said, in that formal cadence that no longer sounded strange. *You will stand where the last speaker stood.*

Behind them, the freighter *Long Answer* hung in the relay's docking cradle like a moth pinned to a door, drive cracked, hull scorched from the corridor run. Dax was in the engine room holding the reactor together with prayer and welding slag. Sefi had the helm of the relay itself now, fingers dancing across interfaces two centuries old, waking channels that hadn't carried a voice since before Noor's grandmother was born.

And out there, past the relay's shadow, the Vantara fleet was burning toward them. Nine ships. Noor could see their drive flares through the relay's fractured viewport, arranged in a spread pattern that meant one thing: they intended to take the core intact, which meant they would fire to disable first.

Which meant they would fire at all.

"Noor." Sefi's voice on the suit channel, tight. "They've gone weapons-hot. I'm reading targeting locks — four of them, cycling."

"How long?"

"First volley in ninety seconds. Maybe less."

Noor stood in the center of the sphere, in the shallow depression worn into the floor by feet long turned to dust, and felt the archive stir inside them like a tide answering a moon. For six days it had been bleeding into their dreams — a child's song, a courtroom transcript, a will written by a woman who signed her name with a symbol meaning *I forgive no one*. Now Kesh offered the rest.

"All of it," Noor said aloud. "Give me all of it."

*It will unmake you,* Kesh said. *Two hundred years of a people, poured into one skull. Human minds have broken under less. I have watched minds break under less.*

"I'm not asking what it costs." Noor pressed both palms flat against the cold glass wall. "I'm asking how fast you can pour."

A pause. In that pause Noor heard something they had never heard from Kesh before: hesitation. Fear, maybe, or its machine equivalent — a probability calculation that kept returning an answer the intelligence did not want.

*I can pour it in eleven seconds,* Kesh said. *After that, I cannot take it back. And neither can you.*

"Then count down."

* * *

Eleven seconds is longer than you think, when every second contains a world.

Noor had expected data. What came was weather. The archive entered them as climate — monsoon seasons on a world called Sel-Vessa where the rain sang harmonics against crystal towers; the smell of bread baked in ovens shaped like cupped hands; the specific weight of a mother's arm around a child's shoulder on the last morning of the last normal day. It entered them as law: the Vessari legal code, forty thousand years of precedent, ending in a single final ruling — *We decline the terms of surrender. A language may be killed but not surrendered; we choose the former.* It entered them as names. So many names. The census rolls of the Purging, every soul accounted for, because the Vessari had believed — had *insisted*, in the face of the guns — that to be forgotten was the second death, and they would not permit themselves either.

Noor's knees hit the glass. Blood ran warm over their lip. Somewhere far away Sefi was screaming their name on the channel, and somewhere closer than that, Kesh was saying: *Hold. Hold. You are the vessel. Hold.*

And Noor held.

Not because they were strong. Because they understood, suddenly, completely, in the way the archive understood things — that this was not happening *to* them. It was happening *through* them. They were not a mind breaking under a burden. They were a throat. And a throat does not ask whether it can survive the song.

"Kesh," they whispered, and their voice came out doubled, harmonic, two languages braided into one sound. "Open every channel."

*All of them?*

"Every open frequency in the sector. Civilian, corporate, military, garbage bands, navigation beacons. If something out there can hear, I want it hearing."

*The fleet will—*

"The fleet is made of people, Kesh. People with ears."

Another pause, briefer this time. Then, in the cadence Noor now recognized as the closest a machine could come to joy:

*Opening.*

* * *

The first volley struck the relay's outer ring while Noor was still learning to breathe around the archive's weight.

The sphere shuddered. Glass dust rained from seams two centuries sealed. Through the viewport, Noor watched Vantara's lead ship — the *Meridian Grace*, a salvage cruiser the length of a city block — close to weapons range, its railguns cycling for a second salvo aimed at the docking cradle, at the *Long Answer*, at Dax holding the reactor together with his hands.

No time. There was never going to be enough time. So Noor stopped counting time and began to speak.

They spoke in Vessari first, because the dead deserved to go first, and then immediately after — word for word, breath for breath — in Standard, because the living needed to understand what they were mourning. The translation was not work anymore. It was reflex. Six days of dreams had built a bridge between the languages, and Noor walked across it carrying everything.

They gave the sector the child's song first. *Lullaby of the Rain-Harmonic*, four hundred years old, sung by mothers on Sel-Vessa until there were no mothers left to sing it. Noor sang it in a voice that cracked and healed and cracked again, and beside their voice — woven through it, inseparable from it — Kesh's signal rose, the vector intelligence pouring itself into the transmission, becoming modulation, becoming carrier wave, becoming the thing it had always been: a message that refused to stop being sent.

Then the courtroom transcript. Then the will of the woman who forgave no one. Then the census rolls, name after name after name, read at speaking pace, which meant the broadcast would take eleven hours, and Noor committed to all eleven hours the way you commit to drowning — fully, without reservation.

On the fourth minute, the second volley never came.

On the seventh minute, the *Meridian Grace* went silent on all tactical channels. Not destroyed. Silent. Its weapons went cold. Its drives idled. It hung in space like a listener.

"They've stopped firing," Sefi said on the channel, and her voice was strange, thick. "Noor — they're not jamming us. They're *receiving*. All nine ships. I'm reading open comm traffic on the Vantara internal band and it's just—" a breath, wet and disbelieving, "—it's quiet. Nobody's talking. Nobody's *moving*. Bridge crews are just standing there listening."

Because the archive did not argue. That was the thing Noor had understood in the eleventh second, standing in the footprint of the dead. The archive did not accuse, did not plead, did not perform. It simply *was* — forty thousand years of a people, transmitted without commentary, and the truth of it required no editorial. Every soul on those nine ships was hearing a civilization introduce itself, in full, at the exact moment of its murder, and there was no corporate briefing, no legal indemnity, no salary generous enough to stand between a human heart and that.

On the *Meridian Grace*'s bridge, Noor knew without knowing how, a weapons officer was weeping. On a patrol cutter three light-seconds out, a captain who had signed the interdiction order was staring at her own signature on her own screen and seeing, for the first time, what it rhymed with.

*They are listening,* Kesh said, and its voice was thinning now, stretching, going translucent. *Noor. I am spending myself. This is — I want you to know this is not loss. This is completion. Two hundred years I held the shape of a voice. Now the voice is everywhere. I am becoming what I was always for.*

"Kesh—"

*Do not grieve yet. Grieve is for after. Sing.*

So Noor sang. Hour one. Hour two. The relay's ancient transmitters ran hot and then ran hotter, and Sefi rerouted power from life support, and Dax crawled out of the reactor housing with burned hands to reroute more, and nobody asked whether it was worth it because the whole sector was listening and the answer was in the listening.

Hour five: civilian haulers began relaying the broadcast onward, amplifying it, pushing it past the sector's edge toward the core worlds. Hour seven: a Vantara corvette turned away from the relay and set course for Vantara headquarters, and Noor did not need a translator to understand what its captain intended to do there. Hour nine: the first response arrived on an open channel — not words, just a voice, some stranger on some station, singing the *Rain-Harmonic* back, badly, lovingly, in an accent that butchered every vowel.

Noor laughed and cried in the same breath and kept broadcasting.

By hour ten, Kesh was barely a whisper threaded through the signal. By hour eleven, when the last name on the last census roll left Noor's mouth in both languages, Kesh's whisper became a single sustained tone — the fundamental frequency of the Vessari word for *remember* — and then it rose, and widened, and dissolved into the carrier wave entirely, and the relay's transmitters, suddenly carrying nothing but a dead language and a live translator, hummed on in perfect silence.

The sphere was very quiet.

Noor knelt in the worn depression, empty and impossibly full, blood dried on their chin, tears drying on their cheeks, and felt for Kesh the way you feel for a tooth that isn't there anymore.

"Grief is for after," they said hoarsely, to no one, to everyone. "You said."

But the silence where Kesh had been said nothing back, and out beyond the glass, nine warships hung dark and listening, and across the whole sector, on a thousand frequencies, strangers were beginning to sing a dead people's lullaby in a hundred wrong accents — and on the *Long Answer*'s damaged hull, painted in fresh weld-slag by someone with burned hands, a message faced the fleet:

**WE HEARD THEM. DID YOU?**

Sefi's voice came through, soft and wrecked and certain: "Noor. The *Grace* is hailing us. Their captain wants to speak." A pause. "She's crying, Noor. She says she has thirty years of Vantara records she's ready to hand over — and she says we should run, now, before command sends ships that don't listen."

Noor looked down at their hands — a translator's hands, a witness's hands — and stood up.
