## 2. Inventory of the Missing

Oscar Sandoval had a ritual for the first morning of any investigation. He made coffee exactly the way he liked it—too strong, slightly burnt—and he read nothing. No briefs, no prior reports, no summaries from regional offices. The paperwork of an investigation was like the packaging on a shipment: it told you what someone wanted you to believe was inside.

So instead he walked.

Meridian's Rest at dawn was a town holding its breath. The atmospheric processor's failure meant the air outside the sealed habitat rings carried particulates that turned the sunrise the color of weak tea, and everyone moved between buildings through covered walkways that rattled in a wind the weather system insisted did not exist. He passed a hydroponics bay with its lights off at growing hour. He passed a machine shop where a man was repairing the same pump housing Oscar had watched him repair yesterday, with the same wrench, the same three taps and a shrug.

And he passed the school.

It wasn't much—a modular block with hand-painted letters over the door reading MERIDIAN'S REST LEARNING COOPERATIVE, the apostrophe in the wrong place, which somehow made it more charming rather than less. On its flat roof, silhouetted against the tea-colored sky, sat four children. They were not playing. They were watching him walk the perimeter with the patient attention of herons watching water.

Oscar stopped. He raised his coffee in a small salute.

The smallest one—a girl, maybe ten or eleven, with her hair in approximately seventeen braids—raised an imaginary cup back at him. Perfect form. Deadpan as a customs officer.

He found himself smiling into his coffee, which annoyed him. Smiling was data leakage. He had a reputation among his colleagues at Meridian Assurance Group—well, he'd had a reputation, before the reassignments—for being a man who could read a warehouse fire report without once wondering what the warehouse keeper had lost. Warmth was inefficiency. Warmth was how adjusters ended up writing appeals they'd have to defend.

Still. Herons didn't salute.

The colony's administrative hub occupied what had once been the primary greenhouse, retrofitted with partition walls and a reception desk built from two shipping crates and optimism. A sign directed him: RECORDS & SETTLEMENT AFFAIRS — VOSS. Underneath, someone had added in marker: *if the light is on, knock anyway.*

Mara Voss looked like a woman who had been awake since before the concept of morning. She was perhaps fifty, gray shot through dark hair pinned up with what appeared to be a hydroponic stake, and she greeted him with the expression of someone greeting a tax auditor who has also come to dinner.

"Adjuster Sandoval." She shook his hand with a grip that said *I lift cargo*. "You found the office. Most visitors get lost between the habitat ring and here and are never seen again."

"That would explain the population figures," Oscar said.

Something flickered across her face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. "Coffee?"

"I brought my own."

"Of course you did." She gestured him to a chair that had been repaired so many times it had achieved a kind of architectural honesty about its own history. "You'll want access to the settlement logs. Births, deaths, relocations, equipment service records, power draw, water table readings. It's all in the archive. I'll issue you a reader key."

"I appreciate that."

"Don't." She said it lightly, but not entirely lightly. "It's a mess. We've had three administrators in six years. Filing standards drifted. You'll find inconsistencies, and I'd hate for you to waste your time treating sloppiness as a mystery."

"Sloppiness is usually just sloppiness," Oscar agreed. "I'm very good at telling the difference."

She looked at him then, really looked, the way you look at a weather report for a storm you already know is coming. "I'm sure you are," she said, and issued him the key.

* * *

Mara Voss watched the insurance man settle into the archive terminal like a hermit crab claiming a shell, and she felt the particular exhaustion of a woman doing arithmetic she couldn't stop doing.

Three days. That was the honest estimate. Three days before Sandoval found the seams, if he was half as good as his file suggested—and his file suggested a great deal, in the way of men whose files were written by people who feared them. Eleven years with Meridian Assurance Group. Forty-one closed claims on frontier settlements. An average payout ratio so low that regional management reportedly used it in training materials as an example of *thorough risk assessment*.

She knew what thorough meant. Thorough meant he could look at a colony dying in slow motion and write *attrition consistent with environmental hardship* and go home to somewhere with real rain.

She had spent last night deciding what to hide, which was itself a decision she hated. There had been a time—she could still locate it in memory, small and clean—when Mara Voss hid nothing from anyone except her feelings about the colony's doctor, and even those had eventually been filed under *resolved, amicably, sadly*.

Now there was a locked directory in the archive labeled LEGACY PERSONNEL — DO NOT MIGRATE, and inside it were the maintenance logs of Dr. Yuen Sefa, founding engineer, dead these thirty-one years, whose logs had continued updating until roughly fourteen months ago. And there was the letter Sefa had left in Mara's predecessor's hands, and the predecessor had left in hers, each administrator adding a line of their own like a relay race nobody wanted to win.

*When the children begin to dream, open the conduit.*

The children had begun to dream nine days ago.

Mara had told herself it was coincidence. She had told herself this while standing in the dormitory corridor at 0300 listening to thirty-one children describe, in overlapping voices, the same humming door.

The insurance man typed steadily in the corner of her office, and every keystroke sounded to her like a countdown.

* * *

By the second day, Oscar had developed a theory about Meridian's Rest's paperwork, and the theory was that it was lying to him politely.

Consider the death records. Over the past six years, the settlement registry listed forty-three residents as *relocated*—transferred to other company holdings, per standard workforce mobility policy. Relocation required, by corporate regulation, a receiving-station acknowledgment: a signature, a station code, a date of arrival. Oscar cross-referenced all forty-three.

Eleven had acknowledgments. Thirty-two did not.

Thirty-two people had left a failing colony and simply... arrived nowhere. And here was the detail that made him set down his coffee and stare at the wall for a while: the relocation dates clustered. Not randomly distributed, the way departures scatter when people leave because they're frightened. Clustered—seven in one month, nine in another—like harvests. Like quotas.

Then there was the equipment. The atmospheric processor's failure cascade was documented in maintenance logs spanning eighteen months, and those logs read like the diary of a haunted house. Compressor seals failing in sealed systems no contaminant could reach. Filtration membranes degrading in patterns inconsistent with any known chemistry. Power routing anomalies that the log entries attributed, with increasing desperation, to *unidentified interference*, *possible rodent activity (no rodents present)*, and finally—one entry, initialled by a technician named Okonkwo who had apparently given up on professional language entirely—*ghosts, probably*.

Oscar had audited enough failing infrastructure to know what this actually was. This was a system being killed. Deliberately, incrementally, by someone—or something—with deep access and infinite patience. Sabotage dressed up as decay, decay dressed up as bad luck, bad luck dressed up as an act of God, and acts of God were, conveniently, the one category of loss an insurer could decline to pay.

He pulled the personnel file on Dr. Yuen Sefa mostly out of habit. Founding engineer. Deceased thirty-one years. Cause of death: decompression event, outer relay conduit, body unrecovered. Standard tragedy, standard closure.

Except the archive showed her credentials still active in the maintenance network. Not dormant—active. Access events logged within the past year. A dead woman's keys turning locks.

Oscar sat back in the crate-built chair and let himself feel the thing he was paid not to feel, which was the sensation of standing in a room where the floor plan didn't match the building.

"Mara," he called across the partition. "Do you have anyone on staff who maintains the legacy engineering systems? Someone using old credentials?"

A long pause. Long enough that he stood up to check whether she'd left.

"No," she said, without looking up from her own terminal. "No one living."

* * *

That night Mara lay awake doing the arithmetic again, and this time the numbers included the insurance man's face when he'd asked the question.

*Someone using old credentials.* He'd said it mildly, the way he seemed to say everything, but underneath the mildness she'd heard the sound every administrator on a dying colony learns to dread: the click of a file opening that will not close again.

She got up. She crossed the cold floor of her quarters. She took down the tin box hidden behind a panel that was supposed to be a water main access, and she took out the letter, and she read it for maybe the hundredth time.

Sefa's handwriting was beautiful and unhurried, the handwriting of a woman who wrote as though the future were a guest she expected to treat well.

*If you are reading this, the company has begun closing the books on us. Do not argue with them; arguing is what they are best at. Instead, remember what I built below, and remember that I built it for the ones they never watch. The company audits everything except childhood. When the children begin to dream, open the conduit. Feed them and trust them. They are the inheritance.*

Below Sefa's signature, four lines in different hands. Administrators, passing it along like a candle.

Mara had added nothing yet. She kept waiting to feel worthy of the paper.

Through her window she could see the dormitory block, dark now, and beyond it the edge of the habitat ring where the wind that officially did not exist combed the dust into long pale shapes. Somewhere out past the radiation belt, at the edge of dead space, a relay station hummed to itself in the dark, maintained by no one, drawing power from nowhere, logged by a woman thirty-one years dead.

Nine days ago the children had started dreaming of it.

Tomorrow, she suspected, the insurance man would ask her about it. Or worse—he wouldn't ask, and she would tell him anyway, because she was tired, so tired, of being the only adult in a town full of secrets, carrying a letter written by a ghost to be opened by children who saluted strangers from rooftops.

In the dormitory, thirty-one children slept, and dreamed the same corridor, and heard the same voice saying the same words:

*Remember us.*

And in the morning, Ila Reyes woke screaming—not from fear, Mara would learn later, but from urgency—because the countdown in the dream had changed, and for the first time, it had numbers in it.
