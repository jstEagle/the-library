## 12. Interrogation

They put him in the spaceport's old customs office, which had been a customs office for exactly eleven days in the colony's first year before everyone realized there was nothing worth taxing. The desk was still there, bolted to the floor, its surface scarred with the initials of colonists who had died or drifted away decades ago. Someone had set a chair on the visitor's side. Someone had set a second chair behind the desk. Neither of them had been dusted.

Oscar had been sitting in the visitor's chair for forty minutes when Kessler finally came in.

He knew it was a technique. Make them wait. Let the silence do the softening. He'd used it himself, back when he was junior enough to be sent to do the waiting-to rather than the waiting-for. So he spent the forty minutes productively: cataloguing the room. Two cameras, both older than Ila Reyes, positioned to catch his face and his hands but not, notably, the door. A heating vent that ticked every nine seconds—thermal regulation tied into the port's failing grid, which meant the building's power draw was logged somewhere, which meant someone could reconstruct how long the lights had been on in here. A water carafe and one glass. One glass. They wanted him comfortable and slightly off-balance, thirsty enough to notice the courtesy, uncertain whether the second glass was for him or for whoever else they expected to break.

He drank the water anyway. It tasted like the colony's processors always made water taste, faintly of iron and apology.

Kessler entered the way Oscar had seen a hundred auditors enter rooms: like the room owed him rent. She was tall in the way that suggested gravity worked harder around her, gray at the temples, wearing the charcoal field suit of Meridian Assurance's inspection division with the collar tab turned down—the informal setting, the *we're just talking* setting. Behind her came a security man who took up the doorway and stayed there, arms folded, radiating the patient menace of furniture.

"Mr. Sandoval." She sat without being offered anything, because she was the kind of person who was never offered anything; things were simply already where she needed them. "Auditor-Principal Kessler. You'll forgive the theater."

"What theater?" Oscar said. "This is a very nice room. Historic, even."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite amusement, more like an accountant noting a line item she'd have to come back to. "You've been on-world six days. Your preliminary findings were transmitted fourteen hours ago. Do you know how long it takes a priority packet to reach Meridian Assurance's home office?"

"I could guess."

"Eleven hours, if you route through the relay buoy, which you did." She folded her hands. "Which means your report arrived, was read, escalated, and generated a response ship already in-system within three hours. Does that strike you as normal response time for a claim on a decommissioned atmospheric processor?"

Oscar looked at her and did the arithmetic she wanted him to do. The *Charity's Reward* hadn't been dispatched by his report. It had been inbound already. His report hadn't summoned the erasure team; it had merely told them their timing was good.

"No," he said. "It doesn't."

"Good." She opened a slim case on the desk—a real paper folio, because Kessler understood props—and drew out a single sheet. "Your employment record. Nineteen years. Four hundred and six claims closed. Average settlement accuracy within two percent of projected loss. That is an extraordinary number, Mr. Sandoval. Do you know what it means?"

"It means I'm good at my job."

"It means you are *trusted*. There are perhaps thirty investigators in the corporation with your accuracy profile. Men and women whose numbers can be relied upon without verification. When you file a finding, it becomes fact. It enters the ledger as truth." She slid the sheet across the desk. "That is not a job description. That is a form of power. Most people never realize they have it. I'm given to understand you're having some difficulty with the concept."

Oscar didn't touch the paper. "Is this the part where you tell me my preliminary findings were inaccurate?"

"This is the part where I tell you your preliminary findings were *preliminary*." She leaned back. "You wrote—and I quote—that discrepancies in the colony's personnel records 'suggest systematic misclassification of mortality events.' Suggest. Systematic. Misclassification." Each word landed like a stamp on a form. "Do you know what happens to language like that when it reaches the actuarial division? Nothing. It dies in committee. Because it's hedged, Mr. Sandoval. It's the language of a man who saw something and flinched."

"And if I'd written it plainly?"

"Then we wouldn't be having this conversation in a nice historic room." She smiled, briefly, without warmth. "We'd be having it somewhere with better security."

The vent ticked. Nine seconds. Nine seconds. In his head, unhelpfully, Oscar heard children counting.

"Let me save us time," Kessler said. "Here's what we know. You landed. You audited. You found the discrepancies—we assumed you would; that's why we sent someone accurate rather than someone obedient, though I confess the distinction has become less useful than anticipated. And then, four days ago, twelve children in this colony stopped appearing at morning muster, and your field reports started containing errors."

"Errors," Oscar repeated.

"Small ones. A census figure off by twelve. A notation that the dormitory block was 'structurally unsound, access restricted' when our own survey shows it perfectly sound. A maintenance request filed for a crawler—vehicle seven—that our telemetry shows left the depot yesterday at 0300 local." She tilted her head. "You've been an investigator nineteen years. You know what it looks like when a man starts lying in his own reports?"

Oscar thought about vehicle seven, about Ila's face in the hatch light, about the falsified forms he'd filed with hands so steady he'd surprised himself. "I imagine it looks like a man whose numbers have stopped being reliable."

"It looks like a man who has decided which side of the ledger he's on." Kessler let that sit. Then, gently, in the tone of someone offering a hand across a gap: "I'm here to offer you the chance to get back on the right side of it."

* * *

She laid it out the way he would have laid it out, once. That was the worst part. She was good. She was, he suspected, better than he had ever been, because she had no flinch in her at all.

Reinstatement. Full clearance restored—his file flagged *provisional* since the Halcyon Deep matter, something he'd never been told officially and had deduced from the shrinking quality of his assignments. Back pay. Transfer off the forgotten-colony circuit, which she named with the casual cruelty of someone reading from his own private list: Perdita Shoals. Coldwater. The place whose name he still couldn't say. A posting in the home office, reviewing other people's findings instead of generating them. Desk work. Dry socks. Thirty years to retirement instead of ten.

"All of that," Kessler said, "in exchange for one thing. The children. Their location, their route, their timetable. You give me that, and whatever you think you saw on this rock gets reclassified as what it actually is—an administrative anomaly, handled administratively. No one disappears. No one is erased. The colony gets a managed wind-down with full relocation benefits." She paused. "You have my word on that."

"Your word," Oscar said.

"My word carries weight, Mr. Sandoval. Ask anyone."

"That's the problem," Oscar said. "Everyone does."

The security man by the door shifted his weight, a small creak of gear webbing. Kessler didn't look at him. She was watching Oscar the way he watched a claim file: looking for the discrepancy, the number that didn't reconcile.

"You're taking this personally," she said.

"Yes."

"That's a professional error. This isn't about you. It isn't even about them, much as I'm sure they've grown on you—children do that; it's a documented phenomenon. It's about a liability event forty years in the making that is currently, at this moment, threatening to metastasize. There is an archive, Mr. Sandoval. You've heard about it—I know you have, it's all over your late reports in the negative space between your lies. Dr. Yuen Sefa copied everything. Every debt cancellation, every engineered shortage, every colony that got the treatment Meridian's Rest is getting right now. Forty settlements. Perhaps more. If that archive reaches open channels—" She spread her hands, and for one moment the auditor's mask slipped and something almost human looked out: fatigue, maybe, or the particular exhaustion of people who clean up after other people's decisions. "It doesn't end the company. Companies don't end. But it ends *careers*. Thousands of them. People who signed forms without reading them. People like you."

"People like me," Oscar said quietly, "signed forms without reading what was underneath them. That's different."

"Is it?"

"Yes. Because underneath mine were numbers. Underneath theirs are people."

Kessler studied him for a long moment. The vent ticked twice. Somewhere outside, a cargo loader beeped its slow reversing song, and Oscar thought: *they're loading the transfer shuttles. They're already loading them.*

"Let me tell you what happens next," Kessler said, "if you keep saying no. We find the children. We will find them—it's a fixed-route conduit network, it has endpoints, endpoints are searchable. And when we find them, the incident gets classified per contingency protocol, which is a phrase I chose carefully, because you're an investigator and I want you to go look up what contingency classification means for minor witnesses in an active data-theft event." Her voice didn't rise at all. "You can't protect them by withholding. You can only choose whether you're standing next to them when it resolves, or standing over here with me, holding a form that says it was all handled properly."

"There it is," Oscar said.

"There what is?"

"The thing I've been trying to name for six days." He stood up. His knees cracked; he was fifty-one and the chair had been designed for someone younger. "Every bad claim I ever closed, there was a sentence like that in it somewhere. *You can't change the outcome, only whether you're on the record as reasonable.* And I always signed, because signing was the job, and the job was the man, and if I stopped being the man I didn't know what was left." He walked to the window—there was a window, small and grimy, looking out on the spaceport apron where the corporate shuttle squatted like something that had crawled out of the sea. "You're offering me my life back, Auditor Kessler. And the price is twelve children. And the truly remarkable thing—the thing I want noted in your report—is that until about ninety minutes ago, I would have taken it."

"Ninety minutes ago," Kessler repeated. "What happened ninety minutes ago?"

Oscar looked out at the apron. At the horizon beyond it, flat and rust-colored, where the dead-space ridge rose like a held breath. Somewhere under that ridge, in a conduit a dead woman had dug for them, a girl who dreamed in corridors was leading six kids toward a signal that had been calling their names for years. Somewhere above it, Mara Voss was presumably being charmingly obstructive at a checkpoint, buying minutes with paperwork, the bravest thing he'd ever seen anyone do with a clipboard.

"Ninety minutes ago," he said, "I finished filing my final report. All of it. The misclassified deaths. The engineered processor failure. The debt structure that required this colony to default. I routed it through the relay buoy on a delay loop—if I don't authenticate it again in seventy-two hours, it releases automatically to the colonial oversight board, the insurance syndicate, and three independent news services." He turned around. "So you see, the information you want from me? You already have it. I gave it to you fourteen hours ago. You just weren't listening."

For the first time, Auditor-Principal Kessler went very still.

"That's a bluff," she said.

"It's a claim," Oscar said. "File it however you like. But check the routing logs before you decide. You taught me that, actually—all those years of watching how your division works. Verify first. Trust is expensive."

The security man uncrossed his arms. Kessler raised one finger without turning her head, and he stopped.

"You understand," she said softly, "that if that release is real, you've just made yourself the most consequential witness in corporate history. And that witnesses in consequential events have historically short records."

"I've read the files," Oscar said. "Most of them, anyway. The ones that survived." He picked up the water glass, considered it, and set it back down half-full—one glass, one drink, hospitality rationed. "Here's my counteroffer, and then I'd like to go back to my quarters, which I assume is where you'll pretend to confine me while you verify. You leave the children alone. You let the shuttle of colonists you're calling *relocations* stay on the ground. And when the archive goes out—and it's going out, Kessler, that part isn't negotiable and isn't mine to stop—you stand very still and let history happen around you, and maybe in twenty years you tell the inquiry you had doubts. Everyone needs a version of events. Even you."

Kessler rose. She squared the folio on the desk, aligned its edges with the desk's edge, a small compulsive mercy of order in a room that had none.

"Mr. Sandoval," she said, at the door. "Nineteen years, four hundred and six claims. Do you know what I see when I look at your record?"

"A man who counts losses."

"I see a man who has never once been on the losing side of a negotiation." The smile again, thin as a blade's reflection. "Until today, perhaps. Or perhaps not. We'll see what the logs say." She paused in the doorway, silhouetted against the corridor light. "Seventy-two hours is a long authentication window, investigator. Things can happen to men in seventy-two hours. And things can happen to crawlers in the dead space, too. Fixed routes. Searchable endpoints. Do give my regards to whoever's driving."

The door closed. The lock engaged with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Oscar stood alone in the customs office of a colony that had existed for eleven taxable days, and he noticed that his hands were shaking now, now, when it no longer mattered, now that the words were said and could not be unsaid. He laughed once, quietly, at the sheer bureaucratic absurdity of it—he had spent his whole life making losses official, and today he had finally filed one against himself.

Then he went to the window and looked east, toward the ridge, toward the dark, and began, methodically, the way he did everything, to count the hours.

Seventy-two. Minus the time it would take Kessler to pull the routing logs. Minus the time it would take a crawler to cross the belt. Minus whatever head start a dead engineer had built into a dream.

Not enough. It was not going to be nearly enough.

Which meant, he thought, pulling the room's single chair to the window to wait, that somebody was going to have to buy the children more time—and he already knew, with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own final column of figures, exactly whose balance that loss was going to come out of.
