## 13. Dead Space Crossing

The crawler had a name stenciled on its flank in flaking yellow paint: *PATIENT HUMMINGBIRD*. Ila decided this was either very funny or a bad omen, and she couldn't tell which, so she laughed about it until Devi told her to stop laughing because laughing made the cabin feel smaller.

The cabin was already small. Seven children, one stolen crawler, and forty hours of nothing but rock dust and radiation between them and the edge of dead space.

Ila sat at the front with her forehead nearly touching the viewport, because that was where the dream was loudest. Not in her ears—the dream had never been in her ears—but behind them, in the place where you keep the memory of your mother's voice or the smell of rain on hot metal. The woman's voice lived there now. *Remember us.* And underneath it, new since they'd crossed the colony perimeter, something that ticked like a clock with a crack in it.

*Four days,* the dream said, without words. *Four days, four days, four.*

"Tell me again why we didn't bring more water," said Tomas from the back, for the eleventh time. He was nine and had appointed himself the expedition's official pessimist, a job he performed with real dedication.

"Because water is heavy," said Priya, who was eleven and had appointed herself the answer to everything. "And because Oscar said the relay would have reserves. Sefa built it to last."

"Sefa died thirty years ago."

"Her machine didn't."

"That's not comforting, Priya. That's the opposite of comforting."

Ila let them bicker. Bickering was good. Bickering meant everyone was still ordinary, still just kids in a metal box, still people whose biggest problem an hour ago had been whether the inspectors would notice a missing crawler from Bay Six. She pressed her fingers against the cold of the viewport and watched the terrain crawl by beneath them—a plain of gray regolith stitched with the fossil veins of old mining roads, all of it running toward the dark line on the horizon where the sky stopped pretending to be a sky.

The radiation belt lay past that line. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it out loud, which somehow made it louder.

* * *

They stopped at the third hour to eat, because Priya insisted rations be eaten on schedule even during a kidnapping-of-oneself. Devi passed around protein bars and reconstituted juice in pouches, and for a while there was only the sound of chewing and the tick-tick-tick behind Ila's eyes.

"It's getting faster," she said finally.

The others went quiet. Even little Samo, who was six and mostly along because nobody could figure out how to leave him behind without him telling someone, looked up from his juice pouch.

"The countdown," Ila said. "In the dormitory it used to be like... seasons away. Then months. Since we left the perimeter it's been days. It's counting down to when the relay shuts itself off. Sefa's voice said so, in the waystation. When it hits zero, everything she saved goes dark forever." She turned the juice pouch over in her hands. "We're racing a dead woman's battery."

"That's the most depressing sentence anyone's ever said," Tomas announced.

"It's kind of beautiful, though," Devi said softly. She was twelve, oldest of them, and she had a way of saying things quietly that made them land harder than shouting. "She knew she'd die. So she put herself somewhere the company would never look—in us. In kids' dreams. Because the company watches everything except what it thinks doesn't matter." She looked around the cabin. "We matter. That's the whole plan. We're the part that matters."

Samo raised his hand as if in class. "Can we matter quieter? My juice is sloshing."

Everyone laughed, and Ila felt the fear loosen its grip just enough to breathe around. That was the trick of them, she thought—seven kids who'd never chosen each other before this week, fitting together like tools in a kit. Priya the planner. Devi the heart. Tomas the alarm bell. Samo the pressure valve. And her. She still wasn't sure what she was. The one who dreamed first, maybe. The one the voice had found because she was the kind of kid who woke up screaming and then got curious about why.

*Four days,* the dream ticked. *Three and a half.*

* * *

The belt announced itself before they reached it.

It started as a shimmer on the viewport, faint as breath on glass—charged particles catching in the crawler's forward shielding, painting thin curtains of blue-green light across the black. Pretty, if you didn't know what it was. Ila knew what it was. Everyone on Meridian's Rest learned early: the belt was the reason the relay sat where it did, wrapped in radiation like a lock wrapped in thorns. Nothing company-built came out here. Nothing company-built *could*.

"We're really doing this," Priya whispered. "We're really driving into that."

"The road does," Ila said. She pointed. Below, half-buried, a conduit mouth gaped in the regolith—one of Sefa's shielded service tunnels, running straight through the worst of it, exactly where the dream had always shown it. "She dug us a hallway through the fire. Thirty years ago. Before any of us were born."

Tomas stared at the glowing curtain ahead. "Did she dig bathrooms too? Asking for myself."

Inside the conduit, the world became a hum. The crawler's lights swept over walls ribbed like the inside of a shell, and the radiation shimmered harmlessly beyond them, close enough to touch if the walls failed, far enough to forget if you tried. Ila tried. It didn't work. But the dream, strangely, had gone calm in here—no ticking, no urgency, just the woman's presence like a hand resting on the back of her neck. *This way. This way. You're doing well.*

"She's proud of us," Ila said, without meaning to say it aloud.

Devi looked at her. "You can feel that?"

"Can't you?"

A pause. Then, one by one, all six of them nodded, and Samo said, "Like when you do a somersault right and somebody claps," and Ila thought: yes. Exactly that. A dead engineer's applause, traveling through dirt and decades.

* * *

They slept in shifts inside the conduit, curled on the crawler's benches under emergency blankets. Ila took second watch, which meant lying awake listening to the vehicle breathe while Devi slept against her shoulder and the dream murmured its countdown in the back of her skull.

*Three days. Two and three-quarters.*

She did the math she'd been avoiding. If the relay died in three days, and the crossing took two, then they had one day—maybe—to find whatever Sefa had left and make it work. One day to become the heirs of a woman they'd never met, carrying a message none of them fully understood, to an audience of nobody they could name. Children didn't do things like this. Children hid under blankets during inspections and let the grown-ups handle the world.

But the grown-ups were gone. Mara and the others had stayed behind at the spaceport with their forged manifests and their quiet, stubborn sabotage, buying the crawler time with their own freedom. Oscar had gone into the tunnels toward Kessler's men with nothing but a falsified report and a face like a man settling accounts. Every adult Ila had ever trusted was currently spending themselves so that seven children could drive through a wall of radiation toward a voice in a dream.

So no. Children didn't do this. But they were doing it anyway, and the doing had changed them, the way a long walk changes your feet. She could feel her childhood sitting somewhere behind her, back at the dormitory block, small and warm and already fading—and she found, to her surprise, that she wasn't grieving it. She was carrying it. There was a difference. The dream had taught her that: you don't lose the things you remember. *Remember us.* That was the whole instruction. That was the entire inheritance.

Samo whimpered in his sleep. Ila reached back and found his hand, and he settled, and the crawler hummed on through the dark.

* * *

They broke out of the conduit at dawn—or what passed for dawn on a moon with no sun worth mentioning, just the distant star sharpening above the horizon line. And there it was.

The relay stood on a rise of bare rock at the edge of everything: a slender tower bristling with antennae, wrapped in scaffolding, crowned with a beacon that pulsed slow and red, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Around its base sprawled a low complex of domes and dishes, silvered and pitted by thirty years of weather. It looked less like a machine than like a ruin that refused to admit it, a cathedral someone had abandoned mid-prayer.

Except the beacon was still lit. Still counting. Still waiting.

"It's smaller than in the dream," Priya said.

"No," Ila said slowly. "It's exactly the size in the dream. We're bigger."

Nobody argued with that, which surprised her, because it was the sort of thing that deserved arguing with. Instead they all just looked, seven faces reflected dimly in the viewport, and the dream rose up in all of them at once—Ila could see it happen, could see the moment the voice stopped being hers alone and became theirs, a chord instead of a note:

*Two days. Come. Come. Remember us.*

And then something else happened, something that wasn't in the dream at all.

The beacon changed. The slow red pulse stuttered—once, twice—and then began flashing fast, urgent, strobing across the rock like a lighthouse in a storm. At the same instant every external light on the relay complex snapped on, flood after flood after flood, blazing up out of thirty years of darkness, lighting the rise like a stage.

"They can see us," Devi breathed. "Something in there knows we're coming."

From the largest dome, a hatch irised open, spilling a wedge of amber light onto the regolith. And standing in that light, silhouetted and unmoving, was a figure. Tall. Thin. Waiting.

It raised one arm—not a threat, Ila understood somehow, a greeting—and beckoned.

Behind her, Samo whispered the thing they were all thinking: "Is it her?"

Ila looked at the figure in the doorway, at the beacon hammering its bright alarm against the sky, at the last two days of the countdown burning behind her eyes, and she took the crawler off standby.

"I think," she said, "we're about to find out."
