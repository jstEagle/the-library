## 14. The Archive Below

Mara Voss had spent twenty-two years learning that the most powerful weapon on Meridian's Rest was a correctly filed form.

She'd learned it from Dr. Yuen Sefa, actually, though she hadn't understood the lesson at the time. Sefa had been dead for six years when Mara took the administrator's post, but her maintenance logs were still the backbone of everything—meticulous, patient, and full of small rebellions disguised as compliance. *Recalibrated pump assembly per manufacturer specification,* one entry read, dated three years after Sefa's death. What it meant, Mara eventually realized, was that Sefa had left instructions that would keep running long after she couldn't. Bureaucracy as a love letter to the future.

Tonight, Mara intended to write her own.

She stood in the spaceport office with its flickering overhead panel and its view of the transfer pad, where the corporate inspectors had established their operational hub. Two shuttles sat on the apron under floodlights. A third was being loaded with crates—and, if the manifests were honest, which they never were, with colonists bound for the orbital transfer station. *Relocation.* The word sat in Mara's chest like a swallowed stone.

"Manifest's ready," said Dev Okonkwo, sliding a tablet across the desk. He was sixty-one, former hydroponics chief, and had the particular calm of a man who had already survived two crop collapses and a marriage. "Every crate listed as agricultural equipment. Which is technically true."

"It is?"

"The crates used to hold agricultural equipment." He shrugged. "Now they hold me and Tomas and Priya and eleven cases of preserved protein and every backup drive from the admin archive that Kessler hasn't wiped yet."

Mara looked at him. "You're volunteering to be shipped to the transfer station."

"I'm volunteering to be *misfiled*," Dev corrected gently. "There's a difference. It's the whole theme of the evening."

* * *

The plan—if you could call something a plan that had been drafted on the back of a ration requisition—was simple in the way that all desperate things are simple.

The children were gone. That was the part Mara kept circling back to, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. Ila Reyes and six others had slipped into the old conduit two nights ago, guided by a dream none of the adults could share, and Oscar Sandoval—the insurance man, of all people—had falsified his own reports to cover their escape and then vanished into the mining tunnels himself. Mara had spent her whole career making sure people were where the paperwork said they were. Now seven children existed nowhere at all, and the only thing standing between them and the company's data-wipe team was time.

So: time. They would buy it.

The diversion had three parts, because Mara thought in triplicate. Part one: a fire alarm in the spaceport's cargo bay, triggered by a heating element Dev had liberated from a seed dryer. Not a real fire—a *reported* fire, which in corporate procedure was nearly as disruptive and considerably less lethal. Part two: a manifest dispute, formally lodged by the colony administrator herself, demanding a physical recount of all outbound cargo before any further loading. Bureaucratic sabotage. You could not wipe a colony's records if the colony's administrator had legally frozen the inventory, and even Kessler would need hours to untangle the jurisdictional knot.

Part three was the part Mara didn't say out loud, because saying it out loud made it real: parts one and two would fail. Eventually. And when they failed, the person who had lodged them would be standing right there, in plain sight, holding the paperwork.

"You could come with us," Dev said quietly, reading her face the way he read humidity gauges. "Into the crates. There's room for one more."

"And leave the manifest dispute unsigned? Kessler would smell it in a minute." She smiled, and was surprised to find the smile didn't hurt. "No. Someone has to be the distraction. That's the job I picked."

"You picked it about four hours ago."

"Then I've held it longer than most of my appointments with this company. Let's call that progress."

* * *

At 0300 station time, the cargo bay fire alarm went off with a shriek that Mara felt in her fillings.

She watched from the office window as the inspection team scrambled—floodlights swinging, security personnel jogging in their too-new gear, a junior technician sprinting toward the suppression controls with his helmet half-sealed. Dev's heating element had been tucked inside a pallet of insulation foam, producing exactly enough smoke to be alarming and exactly no flame at all. Somewhere in the confusion, four crates marked AGRI-SPARES rolled themselves onto the outbound shuttle via a loader arm Dev had programmed weeks ago, back when "weeks ago" had still seemed like a generous amount of future.

Mara took a breath, straightened her collar, and walked out onto the apron carrying her tablet like a shield.

"Auditor Kessler," she called, over the noise. "I need your signature."

The crowd around the cargo bay parted slightly. Kessler stood near the shuttle ramp, and even at forty meters, in the flat white floodlight, he had the quality Mara had noticed at their first meeting: a man who processed emergencies as scheduling conflicts. He turned now with the unhurried precision of someone who had never once been surprised in public.

"Administrator Voss. This is an active response zone."

"Then you'll want to resolve the administrative blocker quickly." She held up the tablet. "As of 0247, I have invoked Section 12 of the founding charter—emergency inventory freeze. No outbound cargo leaves this pad until a physical recount is completed against the original colonial manifest. Your signature acknowledges receipt of the dispute."

Kessler looked at the tablet. Then at her. Something moved behind his eyes—not anger; Mara suspected Kessler had outsourced his anger years ago—but the faint recalibration of a man discovering an obstacle he hadn't priced in.

"There is no founding charter in effect. The colony is under corporate administration."

"The charter is in effect until the claim on this colony is formally closed," Mara said. "And the claim isn't closed. I know, because I've read the file. Haven't you? It's remarkable reading. Page after page of deaths logged as relocations. Equipment failures logged as sabotage. A settlement written off on paper while eight hundred people were still breathing its air." She let that sit in the cold night air between them. "You can't close a claim while the claimant disputes the inventory, Auditor. That's not my rule. That's *your* rule. I learned it from your manuals."

For a moment—one long, floodlit moment—Kessler simply studied her, the way Oscar Sandoval must have studied ledgers, the way the company itself studied anything it intended to make disappear.

"You understand," he said finally, mildly, "that this changes nothing. The recount will take place. It will find nothing. And then the freeze lifts."

"Probably," Mara agreed. "But recounts take time. And time is the one asset you didn't bring enough of."

Something flickered across his face then—there and gone, so fast she might have imagined it. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who had once been an ordinary clerk somewhere, before the corporation found what he was good at.

"Escort the administrator to the operations trailer," Kessler said. "She'll wait there while the recount proceeds. Separately."

Two security officers flanked her. Mara went without resistance, walking slowly, deliberately, her boots loud on the apron. Behind her, she heard Kessler order the shuttle's loading paused—"pending recount"—and had to fight down the absurd, giddy urge to laugh. Four crates of smuggled adults and archived truth, frozen safely on a paused shuttle, while the company's own procedures strangled its own operation.

Sefa, she thought, you would have loved this. We're beating them with their own filing system.

* * *

The operations trailer smelled of recycled air and burnt coffee. Mara sat on a folding chair between two guards who wouldn't meet her eyes—they were young, colony-born both of them, hired as auxiliaries during the inspection, and she recognized one as the Okafor boy who'd once flooded the school hydroponics bay trying to grow strawberries on the ceiling.

"Hello, Tomas," she said.

He stared very hard at the wall. "Ma'am. I have to ask you to remain seated."

"I am remaining seated with tremendous discipline." She lowered her voice. "The strawberry incident. You were eleven. You cried for an hour and then ate the evidence."

A muscle jumped in his jaw. He didn't answer, but he also didn't stop her when she leaned back and closed her eyes, conserving herself, listening through the trailer wall to the muffled choreography outside: footsteps, radio chatter, the loader arm whining through its paces as the recount began.

It took them ninety minutes to find the first discrepancy, and Mara knew the exact moment they found it, because the radio chatter changed pitch. Crates AGRI-SPARES 4 through 7: contents did not match manifest. Contents: human.

The trailer door banged open. Kessler entered alone, closing it behind him, and stood looking down at her with an expression she might almost have mistaken for respect.

"Four of my auxiliary staff," he said, "are currently sitting in a crate on my shuttle, along with eleven cases of protein rations and a quantity of data drives that were supposed to be destroyed yesterday. Your doing."

"My doing," Mara agreed. "Though honestly, Dev Okonkwo deserves the credit. I just signed things. Signing things is my entire skill set."

"Where are the children?"

And there it was. The question underneath everything, the reason for the trailer, the guards, the mild voice. Mara had known it was coming since the moment she'd walked onto the apron, and she had prepared for it the only way that mattered: by deciding, in advance, what she would not say.

"I don't know," she said.

"Administrator." Kessler pulled out the chair opposite hers and sat, folding his hands. "I'm going to be transparent with you, because transparency is efficient. In approximately"—he checked his watch—"fourteen hours, a team arrives to complete the site remediation. After that, nothing that happened on Meridian's Rest will be recoverable. Not the records. Not the testimony. Not the people. You have a narrow window in which cooperation produces better outcomes than resistance. I am offering you that window."

"You're offering me a smaller cage."

"I'm offering you a future."

"So am I," Mara said. "To some children. We're both in sales, Auditor. The difference is my product exists."

Kessler regarded her for a long moment. Outside, the floodlights buzzed. Somewhere beyond the perimeter fence, past the dead fields and the shuttered dormitory blocks, a crawler was crossing dark country toward the edge of dead space, carrying seven children and thirty years of a dead woman's patience, and every hour Mara kept this man talking was an hour it traveled.

"Fourteen hours," Kessler repeated, rising. "Consider it."

He left. Tomas Okafor, still staring at the wall, said nothing at all—but when he brought her water an hour later, he set it down within easy reach, and he unlocked the door's inner latch on his way out, and pretended he hadn't.

Mara drank the water and watched the clock and did the only arithmetic that had ever mattered to her: seven children, minus one hour, plus one hour, plus one more.

* * *

They came for her at dawn.

Not roughly—that was the unsettling part. Two security officers, polite, apologetic, escorting her from the trailer toward the shuttle where Dev and Priya and the others had already been removed from their crates and lined up on the apron, wrists sealed with soft restraints, faces carefully blank. Dev caught her eye and gave her a small nod, the nod of a man confirming a delivery received in good condition.

Kessler waited at the ramp's foot.

"The recount is concluded," he said. "The freeze is lifted. Your procedural remedies are exhausted, Administrator. As of this moment, you and your associates are classified as material witnesses pending relocation." He paused, and for the first time, something almost human crossed his face—fatigue, perhaps, or the ghost of a question he'd stopped asking himself decades ago. "For what it's worth, the manifest fraud was well constructed. Sefa's methods, I assume. Her work always was difficult to unwind."

Mara stopped walking.

"You knew her."

"I audited her," Kessler said. "Thirty years ago. My first assignment. I recommended the colony be retained." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "My recommendation was overruled. I have since learned to write recommendations that don't get overruled." He gestured toward the ramp. "Please."

Mara climbed. At the top she turned, one last time, and looked out over Meridian's Rest—the spaceport lights, the dark dormitory blocks, the low hills beyond which a buried conduit ran toward a relay station that a dead woman had filled with the truth and given, of all things on any world, to children.

Whatever happened next, it had already been set in motion. All she had ever been able to offer anyone was time, and paperwork, and now she had spent both.

The shuttle door sealed behind her with a sound like a full stop.

Below, on the apron, a young auxiliary officer named Tomas Okafor looked down at his console, hesitated for exactly three seconds, and then—hands shaking, heart hammering, thinking of strawberries growing impossibly on a ceiling—opened a channel he was not authorized to open, and sent a single unencrypted message toward the relay frequency the whole colony's children had been dreaming about:

*They took the administrator. Crawler status unknown. Hurry.*

* * *

In the operations trailer, Kessler reviewed the transmission log, found the anomaly, and read the outgoing message twice.

Then he looked up at the eastern sky, where the stars were fading into morning, and for the first time in thirty years, Auditor Kessler found himself hoping—quietly, professionally, against every protocol he served—that whatever those children were carrying got where it was going before he did.
