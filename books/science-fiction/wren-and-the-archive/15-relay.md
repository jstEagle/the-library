## 15. Relay

The crawler died four hundred meters from the relay station, which Ila would later decide was either Sefa's sense of humor or her last lesson: nothing worth reaching comes easy, and nothing worth keeping runs on borrowed fuel.

The engine made a sound like a cough, then a longer cough, then nothing at all. The cabin went quiet except for the tick of cooling metal and Dev's breathing, which had been too fast for the last hour and now slowed, almost disappointed, as if his body had been braced for something worse than silence.

"Is it the fuel line again?" Priya asked from the back, where she'd been sitting with the water canisters between her knees like they were infants.

"It's the fuel line again," Ila said. "It's always the fuel line. I think the fuel line is haunted."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

They climbed out into the light of dead space. Ila had heard that phrase her whole life—dead space, the dead belt, out past the dead line—said by adults the way you'd say the name of a disease. But standing in it was different. The sky out here was wrong in a way she couldn't fix with words. Back home, even with the atmosphere processor failing, the sky had texture, had weather, had the smeared amber glow of Meridian's sun through particulate. Here the sky was just *open*, a black so complete it felt less like night and more like being unobserved. The stars didn't twinkle. They just sat there, hard and white, like nails driven into something.

And above all of it, on a rise of gray rock, stood the relay.

It was smaller than Ila had imagined. In the dream it had been enormous—a cathedral of antennas, a tower of humming light—but the dream had been a child's rendering, the way dreams inflate things. The real relay was a squat cluster of dishes and masts on a concrete pad, wrapped in scaffolding gone brown with rust, one great dish tilted toward the sky like a face turned up to listen. Running lights blinked along its spine in a slow, patient rhythm. Green. Green. Green. A pause. Green. Green. Green.

"It's still on," whispered Tomas, who was nine and had not spoken in six hours.

"It's been on for thirty years," Ila said. "Somebody's been keeping it on."

They walked the last four hundred meters because there was no other way to walk it. Ila counted them without meaning to—herself, Dev, Priya, Tomas, the Okonkwo twins, little Wren, who was seven and had insisted on coming because the dream had shown her the door first, and because seven-year-olds do not accept arguments they were not consulted on. Seven children. The dream had shown all of them the same corridor, the same humming door, the same voice. *Remember us.* And for three nights now, the voice had been counting. Not in words. In a feeling, like a clock ticking behind the wall of the world. Two days left. Maybe less.

The pad was littered with things that had clearly been placed rather than dropped: a row of solar panels swept clean of dust, their glass still faintly reflective; a rack of tools hung in order of size; a line of white stones bordering the path to the main hatch, each one painted with a number. Ila stopped at the first stone.

"It's a calendar," Priya said over her shoulder. "Look."

Each stone had a number and a small tally beside it. Ila crouched. The numbers went up in sequence, stone after stone, and beside each number were marks—groups of four with a slash through them, hundreds of them, thousands. She followed the line of stones toward the hatch and understood, with a feeling like cold water poured down her spine, that she was looking at days. Someone had been counting the days. Out here, alone, with no one to show it to, someone had painted a number on a rock every single morning for thirty years, because a count that no one sees is still a count.

The last stone was freshly painted. The paint was still tacky.

The hatch was open.

Ila had imagined this moment a hundred times on the crossing—in the dream and out of it—and in every version she had been brave. In the dream versions she walked through humming doors without hesitation, because dream-Ila was made of the same stuff as the dream, which doesn't know how to be afraid of itself. Real Ila stood at the threshold of a dark corridor that smelled of machine oil and, strangely, of lavender, and found that her legs had opinions of their own.

"Your turn to be first," Dev said. "I went first at the belt."

"You slept through the belt."

"I slept *near* the belt. That counts."

"Both of you be quiet," Priya said, but gently, because Priya's gentleness was the load-bearing kind. She put a hand on Ila's shoulder. "You're the one it talks to."

"It talks to all of us."

"Yeah. But it talks to you *first*. Every time. Like you're the address it has written down."

Ila looked down the corridor. The lights inside came on one by one as she watched, a wave of soft yellow rolling away from her into the dark, as if the station had heard them coming and was lighting the way the way you'd light lamps for guests. The lavender smell strengthened. Somewhere deep in the station, something was humming—the door from the dream, she was sure of it, the door that hummed a note just below hearing, the note that had been living in the back of her skull for two weeks.

"Remember us," Ila said quietly, to the corridor, to the station, to whoever had counted thirty years of days on white stones. "We remembered. We came."

The humming changed key.

It took Ila a moment to understand what she was hearing, because she had never heard a machine be *glad* before. But that was what it was—the hum lifting, brightening, resolving into something almost like a chord—and the lights along the corridor flickered in a pattern that was too fast and too irregular to be a malfunction and too rhythmic to be anything but celebration.

"Okay," Ila said, mostly to herself. "Okay."

She went in. The others followed, because whatever else the dream had been, it had made them a single animal with seven heads, and where one went, all went.

* * *

The corridor opened into a room that had been a workroom once and had become something else over thirty years of one occupant's devotion. There were benches covered in disassembled electronics, sorted into trays with handwritten labels in a careful, old-fashioned script. There were plants—actual living plants, growing under grow-lamps in repurposed coolant tanks, beans and something leafy and a pot of lavender that explained the smell. There were drawings pinned to one wall, hundreds of them, and Ila's breath caught, because she recognized the style. Simple lines. A child's directness. The same hand that had labeled the trays had drawn a bird, over and over, in different poses—a small brown bird with a plain face, perched on an antenna, on a rock, on a windowsill that didn't exist out here. Under one of the drawings, in that same careful script, someone had written: *wren. sings anyway.*

And in the middle of the room, in a chair that had been built up with cushions and bolted reinforcements, sat the caretaker.

Ila knew immediately that it was not a person, and knew just as immediately that it had been a person, in the way that a photograph is not a person but holds one. The android's body was a maintenance chassis, industrial, boxy through the torso, with arms built for lifting reactor components. But the face had been made—made by hand, Ila realized, made with clay or resin over the underlying sensors, sculpted into the shape of a woman's face, an older woman, with high cheekbones and laugh lines that someone had taken the trouble to include, because whoever she had been, she had laughed. The face was cracked along one cheek. One eye was a dull amber lens that flickered as they entered. The other had gone dark years ago and had been painted over with a careful iris, brown and warm, so that even blind on that side, she would still look like someone who could see you.

Her hands lay folded in her lap. They trembled. Everything about her trembled, a fine constant vibration, like a bell that had been struck once, decades ago, and never quite stopped.

"Hello," the android said.

Her voice was a ruin. It scraped and stuttered, syllables dropping out of it like teeth. But underneath the damage there was a voice—a real one, warm and low and slightly amused, the voice from the dream, and hearing it come out of the air instead of the inside of her sleep made Ila's eyes flood so suddenly and so completely that she didn't even have time to be embarrassed about it.

"You're her," Ila said. "You're Dr. Sefa."

The android's head tilted. The amber eye brightened, focused, traveled over the seven of them with an attention that felt almost physical, like a hand passing over each of their faces.

"Dr. Sefa died thirty-one years ago," the android said. "I am what she left in the cup before the tea was finished. But yes. In the way that matters to children, which is the only way I care about anymore—yes. I'm her." The ruined voice caught on something that might have been a laugh. "You're the Reyes girl."

Ila's whole body went cold and hot at once. "How do you—"

"You have your mother's frown. She frowned at me exactly like that when I told her the water reclamation numbers were cooked. You were two. You threw a rattle at me. Excellent arm. I said then you'd either be an engineer or a criminal, and looking at you now, I see I may have undersold the options." The amber eye flickered, and the trembling in the hands increased, and when the android spoke again the stutter was worse, as if the effort of remembering cost her something she had little of left. "I have been waiting for you for a very long time. I had begun to draft apologies. In case I had gotten the ages wrong. In case the company's conditioning had— in case the dreams hadn't—"

"They came," Ila said. "Every night. The same corridor. The same door. All of us. Even Wren, and she was a baby when you—"

"I know." The android's sculpted face did something extraordinary. It was only resin and paint over a sensor housing, and it had no muscles to move, and yet it *softened*, the way a room softens when someone you love walks into it. "I know they came. I've been listening to you dream for two weeks. I'm sorry. That was not polite of me. But I needed to know the signal had taken root before I spent the last of—" She stopped. The amber eye dimmed, flickered, steadied. "The last of what I have."

"What do you mean, the last?" Dev asked. He had drifted, as Dev did, to the wall of drawings, and now he turned around slowly. "How much last is last?"

The android looked at him with her one working eye, and then at all of them, and Ila watched her decide—watched the calculation move through the ruined machinery of her, the old engineer's habit of honest measurement winning out over any kinder arithmetic.

"Thirty years is a long time to run a personality imprint on a maintenance core," she said. "I was never meant to last this long. I have been cannibalizing my own subsystems to keep the beacon alive. The beacon comes first. It has always come first. But the ledger is nearly empty now. I have perhaps—" the amber eye flickered, and her voice, when it resumed, was lighter, almost breezy, in the way people are breezy when they are telling you something terrible, "—perhaps forty hours of coherent operation remaining. Less, if I keep talking, which, I notice, I am unable to stop doing. Thirty years of talking to no one, and now that someone is here I have apparently become a flood."

None of the children laughed. Wren, who was seven and had no manners about the important things, walked straight across the room and put her small hand on the android's trembling knee.

"You're the lady from the dream," Wren said.

"Yes, little one."

"You sound all broken."

"Wren!" Priya hissed.

But the android laughed—a real laugh, cracked and stuttering and unmistakably delighted—and the sound filled the workroom the way the lavender smell did, like it had been accumulating in the walls for thirty years, waiting.

"She's quite right," Sefa said. "I sound all broken. I have sounded all broken for years. You get used to it. You get used to most things. That is both the blessing and the curse of being made of patience." She looked down at Wren's hand on her knee, and the amber eye dimmed again, and when she spoke her voice had gone quiet and careful, the voice of someone handling something fragile. "I would like to say something to all of you, and then I would like to show you why you are here, and then—there is a great deal to do, and very little time, and I am afraid I am going to have to ask children to do a thing that should have been asked of armies."

Ila wiped her face with her sleeve. Her chest ached in a way that wasn't entirely grief and wasn't entirely anything she had a word for. She had come four hundred meters and a radiation belt to meet a voice, and the voice turned out to be a broken woman made of patience, counting her days on white stones, growing beans under grow-lamps, drawing the same small bird over and over because a person alone will draw the same small bird over and over just to prove her hands still belong to her.

"Why the children?" Ila asked. "In the dream. Why us? Why not the adults? Mara would have come. Mara would have—"

"Because the company watches adults," Sefa said simply. "They watch the administrators and the engineers and the troublemakers. They have files on every grown colonist's loyalties, debts, and fears. But children—" the ruined voice gentled, "—children are the one archive they never think to index. I could not put the truth in a computer where they'd find it. I could not put it in a document or a testimony or a court filing. So I put it in the only vault they have no key for. I put it in you. Every night, while they slept, I told you the truth, and you carried it, and you didn't even know you were carrying it, and that—" the amber eye flared bright, "—that was the only safe place left in the whole settled dark."

She rose from the chair. It took her a long time—servos whining, one knee locking and having to be struck with the heel of her hand—and the children moved to help and she waved them off with a cracked smile, because there are things a person will insist on doing herself even at the end, *especially* at the end. She stood among them, trembling like a struck bell, one eye dark and painted warm, and she looked, Ila thought, like the colony itself: held together by devotion, running on nearly nothing, still lit.

"Come," said Dr. Yuen Sefa, who had died thirty-one years ago and had never once stopped working. "There's a door I've kept humming for you. And beneath it is everything they buried. But before I show you—" her gaze went to the great dish outside the window, tilted up at the patient stars, and her voice dropped to something fierce and urgent, the patience burning off at last, "—you should know that the countdown in your dreams is real, and it is not counting down to my shutdown."

Ila's stomach dropped. "Then what is it counting down to?"

"The company's wipe team reached orbit six hours ago," Sefa said. "They are coming here to erase the last witness. We have less than two days to make sure the whole settled dark hears her first."
