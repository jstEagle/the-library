## 16. Kessler Descends

The mining tunnels under Meridian's Rest smelled the way Oscar imagined old money would smell if it rotted: dust and copper and something faintly sweet, like fruit left in a drawer. He had been walking for six hours by his own count, which was unreliable because his watch had died somewhere around hour two, and he was navigating by a paper map he'd stolen from a filing cabinet in Mara's office—the kind of map nobody had digitized because nobody had ever expected anyone to need it.

That, he reflected, was the whole colony in miniature. Meridian's Rest survived on everything the corporation had forgotten to look at. The tunnels were on no survey. The air recyclers down here ran on maintenance routines Sefa had written forty years ago and nobody had thought to cancel. Even Oscar himself, at this moment, was an asset the company believed it had secured—locked in a storage room at the transfer station with a guard who turned out to be a nineteen-year-old kid more scared of Kessler than of losing his job.

Oscar had talked his way out with the two things he'd learned in twenty years of claims work: most people want to be trusted, and everyone wants to sit down. He'd offered the boy both. "You're going to have to decide sometime whether you're the company's or you're ours," he'd said, and then, because it was true and because kindness disarms better than threats, "There's a chair right there. Sit down. I'll be gone before you finish deciding."

He'd been gone before the chair stopped rocking.

Now he walked through the dark with a hand lamp set to its lowest setting, following a line painted on the tunnel wall that some long-dead shift crew had used to mark the way to Shaft Nine, and he thought about the strange arithmetic of his life. For twenty years he had been a man who counted losses. He could tell you, to three decimal places, what a human life was worth on a corporate balance sheet: the actuarial tables lived in his head like hymns. What he had never once calculated was what it cost to be the one holding the pen.

Until Meridian's Rest. Until a twelve-year-old girl had looked at him across a dormitory table with flour still on her hands—Ila Reyes baked when she was nervous, he'd learned; the whole dormitory block smelled of bread—and said, not accusingly, just curious: "You write down true things for people who pay you to write down false ones. Which ones do you think are yours?"

He hadn't had an answer then. He was walking toward one now, one step at a time, in the dark, with his knees complaining about the grade of the tunnel floor.

* * *

The map said Shaft Nine surfaced four kilometers from the relay station's approach plain—a shelf of broken rock and dead solar arrays that the first colonists had abandoned when the radiation belt shifted. It also said, in faded stencil letters beside a junction marker, MIND THE GAP, which struck Oscar as the funniest thing he'd read in weeks, and he laughed out loud in the dark, alone, the sound bouncing off the tunnel walls like a small mad animal.

He was still smiling when he heard the crawler.

Not Ila's crawler—that was hours ahead of him, maybe already at the relay. This was heavier, tracked, running on the surface above with the particular grinding patience of military machinery. He killed his lamp and stood still in the blackness and listened, and what he heard told him everything: the rhythm of treads, yes, but underneath it the whine of active scanning sweeps, pinging downward into the rock every eleven seconds. They weren't just driving to the relay. They were hunting for tunnels.

Kessler knew about the conduits. Of course she did. She'd sat across from him in that gray interrogation room with her tablet and her patient voice and offered him his career back—"reinstatement, full back pay, a posting anywhere in inhabited space"—in exchange for the children, and when he'd refused she hadn't gotten angry. That was the worst part. She'd simply made a note, the way he himself made notes, and said, "Thank you, Mr. Sandoval. You've been very helpful after all." Because knowing where he *wouldn't* look was almost as good as knowing where he would.

They'd find the tunnels eventually. But finding them and surfacing through them took time, and time was the only currency Oscar had left, so he did what he had spent a lifetime doing: he assessed the loss exposure and moved to minimize it.

The scanner sweeps came every eleven seconds. He timed them against his pulse—sixty-eight beats per minute, steady, thank you, decades of bad coffee notwithstanding—and worked out that each sweep covered roughly eighty meters of tunnel before the echo faded. Between sweeps there was a silence of perhaps four seconds. If he kept moving during the ping itself, the return signature of a single walking man would drown in the general noise of settling rock.

It was, he admitted to himself, a terrible plan. It was also the only plan, and he had learned from watching Ila Reyes that terrible plans executed immediately beat good plans executed never.

He ran.

* * *

Shaft Nine's ladder was rusted through in three places, and Oscar climbed it the way he did everything now: badly, stubbornly, and with a growing respect for children who had done harder things than this while he was filling out forms. When he pushed the surface hatch, it resisted, groaned, and gave all at once, dumping him onto frozen ground under a sky the color of a bruise healing.

The relay station stood two kilometers out across the approach plain—a low dome and a single transmission tower, lit from within by a soft amber glow that looked, from here, almost domestic. Warm light in a dead place. Someone was home.

And between Oscar and that warm light, coming over the northern ridge, was the strike team.

He dropped flat behind the hatch housing and counted. Six figures in environment armor moving in a wedge formation, plus the crawler behind them with its scan array still sweeping, plus—his stomach tightened—two more figures on the ridge carrying something long and cylindrical. A portable jamming unit. They weren't going to assault the relay. They were going to strangle it, cut the tower off from any receiver, and then walk in and take the archive apart at their leisure. Kessler didn't need to storm anything. She only needed the station to go quiet.

Sixty percent, Ila had said in the last message Mara's diversion had bought them time to send. The upload had stalled at sixty percent.

Oscar lay on the frozen ground of a planet he had been sent to write off, and he understood, with the clarity that comes only at the very end of a long professional decline, exactly what he was worth. Not much. A middle-aged man with a bad knee, a dead watch, and a paper map. Against six armored operators and a jamming array, his market value was approximately zero.

But he had spent twenty years learning how systems failed. And every system fails somewhere.

* * *

The jamming unit was the weak point. He saw it within a minute of watching: the two operators carrying it had lagged behind the wedge, because the thing weighed a hundred kilos and nobody volunteers to carry a hundred kilos, and they were picking their way down the ridge through a field of half-buried solar panels—old ones, brittle, their glass faces crazed into mosaics by thirty years of thermal cycling.

Broken glass. Frozen ground. A slope.

Oscar had once processed a claim from a lithium mine on Ceres where a single misplaced pallet of scrap had triggered a cascade that cost the company eleven million credits, and he remembered, with the fondness of an old man remembering his first love, the exact physics involved. Loose material on an incline needs only an invitation.

The solar panels weren't anchored anymore. Their mounts had corroded through. They were lying against the slope the way cards lean against each other after a house falls.

He crawled backward down into the shaft, found the maintenance alcove he'd passed—an emergency locker, still sealed, still stocked, because Sefa had stocked everything—and helped himself to a pry bar, a flare pistol with three flares, and a coil of signal wire. Then he climbed back up and began, quietly and methodically, to commit sabotage.

He worked the pry bar under the lowest panel's edge and leaned. The glass face cracked with a sound like ice on a pond, and the panel slid ten centimeters downslope, taking its neighbor with it, and that neighbor took the next one, and for eight glorious seconds Oscar Sandoval stood in the dark and watched forty years of abandoned infrastructure remember how to fall. A river of shattered glass and dead silicon poured down the ridge toward the two lagging operators, who did exactly what trained people do when an avalanche starts beside them: they dropped, shielded, and shouted for support—which meant the wedge formation broke, which meant every helmet lamp on the plain swung toward the noise, which meant that for ninety seconds, nobody was looking at the southern approach to the relay station.

Ninety seconds. Oscar fired his first flare straight up over the strike team—not at them, just high and bright and red, because confusion is cheaper than heroism—and ran.

* * *

His knee gave out at the four-hundred-meter mark. He went down hard on the frozen gravel, tasted blood, and got up anyway, because the second flare was already in his hand and the wedge was re-forming behind him with the calm horrible efficiency of professionals who had decided the noise was a distraction and were resuming the plan. The jamming team was scrambling back up the ridge. Ninety seconds had become sixty seconds had become not enough.

So he stopped running toward the relay and started running *loudly past* it—angling south, firing his second flare low so it skipped and burned along the ground like a dying star, making himself the biggest, brightest, most obvious target on the plain. Come on, he thought at them. Come on. I'm the loose end. I'm the one who wouldn't sell. I'm worth chasing.

They took the bait. Two operators peeled off after him, and he heard their boots crunching closer with the metronome certainty of a deadline, and he put his head down and ran through the pain in his knee toward the one place he'd noticed on the map and prayed he'd find in reality: a maintenance cut, a shallow trench where the old power feed to the relay ran underground, half-collapsed, exactly wide enough for a man and too narrow for armor.

He found it. He fell into it. Boots hammered past overhead in the dark, close enough that he held his breath and counted heartbeats—one, two, five, ten—and then the footsteps slowed, circled, and a voice crackled over an external speaker, flat and amplified:

"Sandoval. We know it's you. Kessler says come in and this stays administrative."

Administrative. He almost laughed. He had spent his life inside that word; he knew it was where inconvenient people went to be filed until they stopped existing.

"Mr. Sandoval," the voice tried again, almost gentle. "You're an insurance man. You know how this ends. Nothing you do changes the number."

And that was true, probably. Lying in a ditch with blood in his mouth and one flare left, Oscar Sandoval knew better than anyone alive how the numbers usually ended. He had written the endings himself, hundreds of times, in clean typefaces, with the losses rounded down and the names removed.

But lying in the ditch, he could see the relay tower against the sky, and at its top, the beacon light was pulsing—irregularly, desperately, but pulsing—and he realized the upload wasn't dead. Stalled, yes. Jammed at the transmitter, yes. But Sefa's beacon was still fighting, still stuttering its little signal into the dark like a bird throwing itself against a window, and somewhere inside that dome a dead woman's caretaker and seven children were holding the line at sixty percent, waiting for a gap in the jamming that might never come.

Unless someone made one.

The jamming unit. Back up the ridge. Carried by two operators who had chased the wrong insurance investigator into the dark, leaving the array sitting unattended on the slope above the relay.

Oscar got up. His knee announced its formal resignation; he declined to accept it. He loaded his last flare, checked the angle the way he'd check a figure in a ledger—once, twice, because errors compound—and aimed not at the operators, not at the crawler, but at the slope directly beneath the jamming array, where forty years of shattered solar glass lay piled against the incline like a wound waiting to reopen.

"You're right," he said aloud, to no one, to everyone, to the voice on the speaker and the company that owned it. "Nothing I do changes the number."

He fired.

The flare hit the slope, and the mountain answered. The second avalanche caught the jamming array mid-lift, and Oscar watched it tumble end over end down the ridgeline in a shower of sparks and glass, its operators diving clear, its sweep pattern dying with a sound like a struck bell going silent—and across the plain, on the tower of the relay station, the beacon light changed. Steadied. Burned suddenly white and constant, the way a held breath becomes a voice.

Somewhere in the core of that dome, a child was shouting that the channel was open.

Oscar Sandoval, adjuster, closed his last claim. Then he turned uphill, toward the strike team regrouping in the dark, and started walking—not away, not hiding, but straight at them, arms raised, voice carrying across the frozen plain with twenty years of unpaid honesty behind it:

"Gentlemen! Before you arrest me—you'll want to look at the sky. Everyone in inhabited space is about to."
