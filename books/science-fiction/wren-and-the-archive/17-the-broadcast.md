## 17. The Broadcast

The relay core smelled like dust and old lightning.

Ila Reyes stood in the doorway of the chamber at the heart of Wren Station—though it wasn't called Wren yet, not officially, not until she named it—and tried to understand what she was looking at. The room was round, ringed with consoles that had gone amber with age, and in the center rose a column of light so old-fashioned it looked almost apologetic. A beacon. The thing Dr. Yuen Sefa had built her whole life around, the thing the dream had been walking them toward for three years of nights.

The android—the caretaker, the one who called itself *Caretaker* because Sefa had never gotten around to giving it a better name—stood beside the column with its hands folded, patient as furniture.

"It is ready," Caretaker said. Its voice was a woman's voice, worn smooth like a river stone. "It has been ready for thirty-one years. I would have preferred you arrive sooner, but I understand children have schedules."

Pem snorted from behind Ila. Pem was nine and laughed at everything, including things that were not funny, which Ila had decided was a kind of bravery.

"Thirty-one years," said Devi, who was fourteen and took everything literally, including jokes. "You waited thirty-one years?"

"I maintained," Caretaker corrected gently. "Waiting implies passivity. I replaced four hundred capacitors. I resealed the antenna housing twice. I composed eleven thousand apologies to Dr. Sefa for not being able to do more." It tilted its head, servos whispering. "She told me someone would come. She said it would be the ones the company never watched. She was very specific about that. *They watch the adults,* she said. *They watch the men with clipboards. They never once think to watch what the children dream.*"

Ila's chest went tight. Three years of waking up screaming, of being told it was nightmares, of being given warm milk and dismissed—and all along it had been an invitation. A dead woman's hand reaching through the dark, tapping twelve-year-olds on the shoulder because they were the only ones the company's eyes slid past.

"Okay," Ila said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her. "Show us how to send it."

* * *

The archive was not a file. That was the first thing Ila understood, standing before the main console while Caretaker walked them through it. She had imagined something small—a document, a list, maybe a long letter. But the archive was a *place*. It filled the lower levels of the relay, floor after floor of storage cores, each one holding a piece of what Meridian Corporation had spent eighty years burying.

"The debt cancellations are on level two," Caretaker said, leading them down a stairwell lit by strips of failing glowtape. "The colony ledgers are on levels three through six. The personnel records—" it paused at a door, and its voice did something Ila had never heard a machine do before; it hesitated, "—the personnel records are on seven."

"What's on eight?" asked Junie, who was seven and had appointed herself the expedition's question department.

Caretaker opened the door anyway.

Level eight was cold. Not temperature-cold—*other*-cold. The storage cores here hummed at a pitch Ila felt in her back teeth, and the labels on them were short, and the shortness was the worst part. Not incident reports. Not investigations. Just designations. *Colony 14 — Halcyon Reach.* *Colony 22 — Port Amity.* *Colony 31 — Solace Wells.*

"There are forty," Caretaker said quietly. "Forty settlements founded, bled for their resources, and then erased. Not destroyed—erased. Their charters dissolved, their debts transferred to shell entities, their populations relocated to stations where they were registered under new names and their histories closed. As far as the inhabited systems' records show, these places never existed." It gestured at the humming cores. "Meridian's Rest is Colony 41. You were always going to be last. Dr. Sefa found the pattern in year six and spent the rest of her life making sure there would be a witness."

Ila touched one of the cores. It was warm, faintly, the way a stone is warm after sun even when the sun has gone. Inside it were people. Whole towns. Birthdays and harvests and arguments about water rations, all folded into data because a corporation had needed them to be nothing.

"We're going to tell everyone," she said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't a plan yet either. It was just true, the way the dream had been true, and saying it out loud made the cold room feel less cold.

"Yes," said Caretaker. "That is rather the point of you."

* * *

They started with the debts.

It seemed right to start there, though later Ila couldn't have explained why—maybe because debt was the language the corporation spoke best, and if you were going to shout at someone it helped to shout in their own tongue first. Caretaker showed her the console, the upload sequence, the antenna routing. The relay could push the archive out on every open channel simultaneously: commercial bands, emergency bands, the civic frequencies every settlement in inhabited space kept tuned for weather and news. There was no encryption to break. Sefa hadn't needed any. The information had never been stolen—it had been *hidden*, and hiding was a much weaker magic than stealing.

"Upload will take approximately ninety minutes at full bandwidth," Caretaker said. "During which we should all sit very still and hope very hard."

"That's your plan?" Pem asked.

"I have been alone for thirty-one years," Caretaker said. "My plans have gotten simple."

Ila settled into the operator's chair, which was too big for her and smelled like machine oil and, impossibly, faintly like lavender—Sefa's scent, preserved in the padding across decades. On the screen, the first packet assembled itself: Meridian's Rest, fiscal year one. The founding loan. The interest structure designed to be unpayable. The restructuring clauses that converted missed payments into asset seizures, the asset seizures into labor levies, the labor levies into—

She stopped reading. Then she made herself read again, because that was the whole point, wasn't it? Someone had to look. That was what the dream had been asking all along: *remember us*, which really meant *look at us*, which really meant *count us*.

The engineered famines were worse. She'd known—they'd all known, in the bone-deep way kids know things adults pretend not to—that the food shortages hadn't been bad luck. But seeing it itemized, seeing the internal memos with their tidy subject lines—*Ration Adjustment: Q3, Projected Mortality Acceptable*—seeing a man named Okonkwo in Logistics write "the optics are manageable if we stagger the districts" about children who would stop having birthdays—Ila had to put her head down on the console for a while. Junie put a small hand on her back and left it there without saying anything, which was the most grown-up thing anyone had done all day.

When she lifted her head, Devi was standing at the window of stacked cores, arms crossed.

"They knew," Devi said. "All of them. Every name on these memos knew. And none of them ever had to say it out loud, because it was all just... paperwork. Numbers going down." He looked at Ila. "We're the numbers going up."

"Yeah," Ila said. "So let's go up loud."

* * *

At forty percent, the first responses started coming in.

The relay's monitoring board lit up like a festival—pings from relay buoys, from passing freighters, from settlement receivers three systems over. Most were automated acknowledgments at first, the polite machinery of the network confirming receipt. But then the human messages began, and Caretaker routed them to a side display because there were too many for the main one.

*Who is this? Say again, source?*

*Receiving full ledger dump, Halcyon Reach band. My grandmother was born on Halcyon Reach. Please confirm this is real. Please.*

*I'm a dispatcher on Thessaly Ring. I've got your broadcast on every channel. Whatever you're doing, don't stop.*

Junie read them aloud, one by one, in her serious reading voice, the one she used for homework. With each message the room changed. Ila could feel it happening—the fear draining out and something else pouring in behind it, something with weight and warmth, like ballast. They weren't screaming into the void. The void was answering. The void had *grandmothers*.

A message came in from a station medic two systems away: *My father was listed as relocated from Port Amity in '61. We never found him. Is he in your files?*

Ila looked it up. Level five, personnel records, cross-indexed. She found him in four minutes. Relocated, yes—to a labor levy on a mining platform, where he'd died in year two of a contract that had been sold three times without his consent. She copied the record, attached the original memos, and sent it back with a line she wrote herself, deleting and retyping it three times before settling on:

*He was real. We're sorry. Now everyone knows.*

The reply came back in under a minute. It was just one word: *Finally.* But the way it sat on the screen, Ila thought it might be the loudest word she'd ever read.

* * *

At sixty percent, Pem spotted it.

"Um," Pem said, from the sensor station where he'd been keeping watch mostly by spinning slowly in the chair. "Um. Ila. There's a ship."

Ila crossed the room fast enough to make Junie yelp. The sensor display showed a single contact rising off Meridian's Rest, burning hard for the relay—a corporate cutter, the same class of ship Oscar had described to Mara in hushed tones through the conduit's comm line, the class he'd seen used on other colonies that needed to disappear. The class that carried wipe teams.

"How long?" Ila asked.

Caretaker consulted displays older than any of their parents. "At current burn, they reach weapons-effective range of this station in approximately fifty minutes. Upload completion requires approximately fifty-four."

The number sat in the air. Fifty-four. Fifty. Four minutes short. Four minutes between testimony and silence, between forty colonies existing and forty colonies being buried twice.

"So we're four minutes short," Devi said flatly. "Of course we are. Of *course*."

"No," Ila said.

Everyone looked at her. She didn't have a plan. She wanted to be honest about that, at least inside her own head. But she had something else, something she'd been carrying since the first night she woke up screaming, something she was only now understanding the shape of: the dream wasn't just a message. It was a *connection*. Twelve children on the colony, more in the transfer station orbiting overhead, all of them wired into the same signal, all of them already holding hands inside each other's heads every night. Sefa had built the failsafe keyed to young minds. Keyed to *them*.

"Caretaker," Ila said. "The dream-link. The way Sefa talked to us. Can the relay use it?"

Caretaker went very still, in the way only machines can go still, every process visibly pausing to consider. "Dr. Sefa theorized it," it said at last. "The resonance carrier she embedded in the beacon's subspace layer—it propagates through neural patterns she never fully documented. She believed a synchronized group of receptive minds could act as a living antenna. She called it the choir protocol." Its worn voice dropped half a register. "She also believed it might be dangerous. She left instructions never to attempt it alone."

"We're not alone," Ila said. "We've never been alone. That's the entire point of us."

Outside, the corporate cutter burned closer, patient and certain, carrying men whose job was to make sure no one had ever lived on Colony 41. Inside, the upload crawled toward sixty-one percent, sixty-two, each percentage point a town refusing to be nothing.

Ila put her hand flat on the console, felt the beacon's hum climb up her arm and settle behind her sternum where the dream had always lived, and reached out the way she reached out in sleep.

*Can you hear me?* she thought, at all of them, everywhere. *It's time to sing.*

Far below, on a dying colony and in a crowded transfer station, dozens of sleeping and waking children opened their eyes at the same moment—and answered.
