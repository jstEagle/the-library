## 18. Signal Jammed

Oscar had spent twenty-two years learning what a stalled number meant.

A claim that sat at "pending review" for six weeks was not pending anything; it was dying politely. A payout frozen at ninety percent of assessed value was not an error; it was a message. Numbers, in his experience, never simply stopped. They were stopped—by someone, for a reason, and the reason was always the same shape: someone stood to lose more by letting the number finish than by strangling it in public.

So when the progress bar in the relay core froze at sixty percent, he did not need anyone to tell him what had happened.

He felt it first as a change in the room. The children had been holding hands around the transmitter pedestal in a loose ring, eyes half-closed, breathing in that eerie unison they'd slipped into somewhere over the last hour—the dream-link, Sefa's imprint called it, though she'd said it with the careful neutrality of a woman describing a fire suppression system she hoped you'd never use. The hum of the relay had been climbing, note by note, like a choir finding its pitch. And then, all at once, the choir hit a wall.

The hum flattened. The bar froze.

*60%.*

Ila's eyes snapped open. "It stopped," she said, in the flat voice of a child reporting something much worse than words could carry. "She's still singing but it's like—like someone put their hand over her mouth."

Oscar was already moving to the observation port on the relay's eastern face, because he'd learned long ago that when a number stops, you look for the hand.

The hand was descending out of the dawn sky in three separate pieces: two assault skiffs and a heavier transport, kicking up dust plumes along the ridge line where the crawler had come through twelve hours earlier. Kessler's team hadn't even bothered with subtlety this time. The skiffs fanned out toward the surface approach with the unhurried confidence of machinery that had done this forty times before and been thanked for it thirty-nine of those times.

"Everyone away from the ports," Oscar said. "Down to the core level. Take the little ones first."

"We're not leaving," Ila said.

"I'm not asking you to leave. I'm asking you to be somewhere harder to shoot."

"That's not the same thing and you know it."

God, he thought, not for the first time, *she's going to be a terror at fourteen.* He crouched to her eye level, which was getting easier every week, a fact he tried not to read too much into. "Ila. The archive is at sixty percent. Sixty percent is nothing. Sixty percent is a rumor. If that signal dies now, everything Sefa kept for thirty years dies with it, and so does everyone who helped us get here. Mara. Tomas. The people at the spaceport who are, right now, being very rude to armed men on our behalf." He held her gaze. "What I need from you is to keep those kids safe and keep that link alive. Can you do that?"

Something moved behind her eyes—the argument she wanted to have, folding itself away for later. She'd inherited more than a mission from Dr. Yuen Sefa. She'd inherited the ability to lose a battle in order to win one.

"Sixty percent," Ila repeated. "You'll get us the rest?"

"I'm going to go negotiate," Oscar said. "And I want you to understand that when I say negotiate, I am using the word in its broadest possible sense."

* * *

He met them at the surface approach, walking out alone with his hands visible and his insurance credentials displayed on his slate like a white flag, which in a sense they were. The skiffs set down in a triangle around him, and eight figures in corporate security gray deployed with the crisp economy of people billing by the hour.

Auditor Kessler came down the transport ramp last. He looked exactly as he had in the interrogation cell two days ago—immaculate, unhurried, the kind of man whose wrinkles were arranged rather than earned—but there was something new in his face now, something Oscar recognized from a hundred claim reviews: the expression of a professional watching a file slip out of his control.

"Sandoval." Kessler stopped six meters away. Behind him, two of his people were already running cable toward the transmitter array's external junctions. "You're supposed to be in holding."

"I got bored."

"You got *out.*"

"Those are compatible statements." Oscar nodded past him at the array. "You're jamming the broadcast."

"I'm restoring order. There's a difference, though I understand the distinction may be lost on a man currently committing about nine categories of felony." Kessler's tone was almost friendly. It always was. That was the worst thing about him. "Here's where we are. Your upload is contained. My technicians are severing the relay's external feed in"—he checked his watch, a real analog watch, because of course he owned one—"eleven minutes. After that, the archive stays exactly where it is, buried, forever, and everyone walks home. Well. Most everyone."

"And if I ask nicely?"

"You can ask however you like. You've been asking since Meridian's Rest, Sandoval. That's the whole problem with you." Kessler took a step closer, and lowered his voice—not for drama, Oscar realized, but because there were children in the relay behind him and Kessler, whatever else he was, did not enjoy being overheard doing his work. "You keep asking questions as though the answers were going to change something. They don't. Forty colonies, Sandoval. Do you think you're the first adjuster to find a discrepancy? Do you think you're the first to stand in some dying settlement and get *righteously upset* about it? There was a woman on Calloway's Fork who found worse than what you found. She filed everything correctly. Do you know what happened to her report?"

"It went into the archive," Oscar said quietly. "Sefa collected them. All of them. Every investigator who ever found the truth and got buried with it. She's been keeping your receipts for thirty years."

For the first time, something flickered across Kessler's face that wasn't arrangement. It lasted less than a second, but Oscar had built a career on less than a second.

"Ten minutes," Kessler said. "Walk away. I'll log you as coerced. It's even true—you were. Reinstatement, full back pay, a posting anywhere in inhabited space that isn't here. You're good at your job, Sandoval. That's not flattery; I've read your closure rate. Ninety-four percent, with documentation clean enough to teach from. Men like you don't grow on trees." He paused. "Men like you are *grown.* Come back inside the orchard."

Oscar laughed. He couldn't help it—a short, ugly, surprised sound that seemed to take them both off guard.

"Do you know what my job actually is?" he said. "Not the brochure version. The real one. My job is to make losses official. A colony starves, and I arrive, and I count what it cost, and I write the number down, and the number becomes true, and then everyone gets to stop thinking about it, because it's *official* now. It's in a ledger. Ledgers are where things go to stop being real." He shook his head slowly. "Twenty-two years. Do you know how many claims I closed without once opening the door marked 'why'? Not because I didn't see the door. Because my bonus structure discouraged it."

"So this is guilt," Kessler said, with the satisfaction of a man fitting a key into a lock. "That's fine. Guilt passes. I've seen it pass a hundred times. Give it a month and a salary and you'll be counting losses again, and you'll be *good* at it, because you were always good at it—"

"No," Oscar said. "You've misread the file. It's not guilt. Guilt would mean I thought I could have done better back then. This is arithmetic." He held up his slate. On it, open, was the preliminary claim he'd filed his first week on Meridian's Rest—the one that had gotten him ordered home, the one he'd rewritten eleven times since, each draft truer and less employable than the last. "I've spent my whole life assessing assets. So let me do it one final time, professionally, and you can tell me if my numbers hold.

"Asset: one Oscar Sandoval. Age fifty-three. No dependents. No property beyond a wardrobe of increasingly ironic jackets. Skills: documentation, negotiation, an encyclopedic knowledge of every regulation your company has ever used as a weapon—which, incidentally, means I know precisely which of your authorization codes expired when your own board went silent an hour ago, Auditor, because I've been reading your fleet traffic since before your skiffs cleared the ridge."

Kessler went very still.

"Liabilities," Oscar continued, "one: I will never stop talking about this. Not in a month, not in ten years. I am, and I say this with genuine humility, the single most expensive witness you could possibly leave alive. Depreciation: zero. Salvage value: zero. Which brings me to my offer."

"Don't."

"Eleven minutes, you said. Your technicians need most of that just to splice the feed. So here's the trade. You give the transmission its window—all of it, sixty to one hundred—and I walk down that ramp with you, voluntarily, no restraints, no incidents, and I sign whatever confession you like. Sabotage. Terrorism. Unauthorized use of company stationery, I don't care. I become your file. Closed case, tidy ledger, official loss. Everything you've wanted since you landed." He smiled, and was distantly amazed to find it didn't hurt. "One accountant of losses, spending himself as the final asset. Honestly, Kessler, it's the best claim I've ever structured. Even you should appreciate the elegance."

"I could just shoot you," Kessler said. But he said it the way men say things they've already priced and found too expensive.

"You could. But your board has gone dark, your escort is recording everything because escorts always record everything, and in approximately—" Oscar glanced at the sky, at the thin bright threads of response ships already falling toward Meridian's Rest from directions no company ship had ever come from, "—some number of hours, the people taking custody of you are going to want to know why the man who jammed a distress broadcast also executed an unarmed investigator on camera. You've read my closure rate. Consider mine for a moment instead of yours."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind the ridge, one of the technicians swore softly at a junction box that was, Oscar suspected, receiving very confusing instructions from a certain android caretaker who had thirty years of practice making company hardware misbehave.

Kessler looked at him for a long moment. And then, horribly, he smiled—the first honest smile Oscar had ever seen on him, and it was worse than all the arranged ones.

"You really think they're coming for *you,*" Kessler said. "The independents, the free settlements, the righteous chorus. Sandoval, I've watched this play out. Exposure doesn't save colonies. It just changes whose signature is on the next write-off." He turned and signaled his technicians. "Cut the feed."

"No—" Oscar started forward, and four rifles came up in a motion so smooth it was almost polite—

—and the world sang.

Every child on Meridian's Rest, every child in the orbital transfer station, every small sleeping mind within a thousand kilometers woke at once into the same corridor, the same humming door, the same voice—and this time they did not merely listen. They *held.* The relay's dead channels lit up like a struck bell. The jamming field, engineered to smother a transmitter, met instead a signal with no transmitter at all, carried on nothing but the oldest bandwidth in the universe, and the progress bar in the core below them shuddered, and climbed.

*61%. 62%. 63%.*

"What is that," Kessler said. It wasn't a question. It was the sound of a man watching his own ledger catch fire. "What *is* that?"

Oscar looked at the sky filling with light, and at the auditor whose authority was evaporating by the percentage point, and he found, to his enormous surprise, that he was grinning like Ila did when she'd won an argument he hadn't known they were having.

"That," he said, "is the rest of my claim filing itself."
