## 19. One Hundred Percent

The transmitter array screamed.

That was the only word Ila had for it. Not a sound exactly—more a feeling that lived in her teeth, in the backs of her eyes, like standing too close to something enormous that was trying very hard to be heard. Sixty percent of the archive had gone out into the dark before Kessler's people seized the uplink, and now the remaining forty percent sat in the relay's core like water held behind a cracked dam, pressing, pressing, wanting out.

"They've cut the physical line," said the android. Sefa—the imprint of Dr. Yuen Sefa, wearing the body of a caretaker unit that had been patching itself with scavenged parts for thirty years. One of her hands was original; the other was a mining servo wrapped in insulation tape. Her voice was calm in the way of someone who had already imagined every disaster and filed them all under *anticipated*. "They can hold the junction for hours. We cannot reach it without going through them."

Ila stood at the core console with six children behind her and an archive of forty erased colonies humming against her palms. The dream was louder than it had ever been. It wasn't a corridor anymore, or a door, or a woman saying *remember us*. It was everything at once—the whole archive, all of it, pouring through the link that Sefa had built into young minds thirty years ago because she had known, with terrible precision, that the company would never think to monitor children.

"The signal doesn't have to go through the line," Ila said slowly. The thought was forming as she spoke it, assembling itself out of pieces she hadn't known she was holding. "The dream goes through walls. It went from your beacon to our dormitory across half the colony. Through vacuum. Through rock."

Sefa turned. Even in the ruined face, even with one optic flickering amber, there was something like hope—and something like grief, because she understood what Ila was about to say before Ila said it.

"You want to use the children as the antenna."

* * *

It had started, really, back in the dormitory block, on the night every child woke screaming the same corridor. Twelve nights ago. Thirteen? Time had gone strange since the company ship came down like a held breath over Meridian's Rest. But Ila remembered the mechanics of it now with a clarity that felt less like memory and more like instruction. The dream had never been a message. It had been a *tuning*. Sefa had keyed the failsafe to young minds—minds still soft enough to synchronize, the way birds flock, the way crickets sing in unison without anyone conducting them. One child dreaming was a whisper. Every child dreaming together was a broadcast tower made of sleep.

And they didn't need the relay's jammed uplink. They needed the relay's *core*—the archive itself, pressed close enough to their sleeping, synchronized minds to pour through the link like water finding a crack.

"We'd need everyone," Ila said. "Every kid on the colony. Every kid in the transfer station." She thought of the transfer station hanging in orbit, where Kessler's people were 'relocating' colonists—where her friend Devi Okafor had been put on a shuttle two days ago with her little brother, waving through a porthole with a smile that meant *help me*. "Devi's up there. All the relocated kids are up there."

"Can you reach them?" Sefa asked. "The link has always flowed one direction. From me, outward. I designed it that way so no one could use the children to find the relay." A pause, servos whirring softly in her throat. "You would be asking them to open themselves. To let the archive flow *through* them. I don't know what that does to a mind that isn't mine."

"It's what we've been doing since the first night," Ila said. "We just didn't know we were doing it."

Behind her, Tomas—nine years old, missing both front teeth, the bravest coward Ila knew—raised his hand like he was in class. "What happens if it works?"

"Everyone hears," Ila said. "Everyone everywhere. Every debt cancellation, every engineered famine, every colony they erased and called a rounding error. Forty worlds' worth of *we existed*."

"And if it doesn't work?"

Ila looked at him. She thought about lying, because she was twelve and lying to smaller children was practically a civic duty. But this felt like the wrong moment for it. Maybe the last wrong moment she'd get.

"Then we tried," she said. "And then we're just kids in a room again, and the adults figure something out, or they don't. But nobody gets to say we stayed quiet."

Tomas considered this with the gravity of a much older person, then nodded once, decisively, the way he did before jumping off things.

* * *

Getting the word out took four hours and every scrap of cleverness the children of Meridian's Rest possessed, which was considerable, because children who grow up on a dying colony learn early that the official channels are decorative.

They couldn't broadcast—the relay's external comms were the first thing Kessler had seized. They couldn't send a runner to the transfer station—Ila checked, and even the boldest plan involving stolen maintenance pods ended with everyone captured. So they did the only thing left. They slept.

All eight of them, curled in the relay's warmest chamber among cable bundles that hummed like purring animals, holding hands in a chain because it helped, though none of them could have explained why. And Ila reached—not with her hands, not with her voice, but with the thing underneath the dream, the tuning fork Sefa had struck inside her years ago without her knowing.

*Devi,* she thought, as hard as she had ever thought anything. *Devi Okafor, transfer station, bunk section C. Can you hear me?*

For a long time: nothing. Just the archive's pressure, the array's scream, Tomas snoring faintly against her shoulder.

Then, faint as a voice through water: *Ila? Why do you sound like my mom's lullaby?*

Ila laughed and cried at the same time, which she was getting practice at lately. *Listen. Everyone's going to sleep tonight. Everyone. Tell every kid you can find. When you feel the dream come, don't be scared, and don't let go of it. Hold it open. That's all. You're the antenna now.*

*A what?*

*Just hold it open, Devi.*

*Okay.* A pause. Then, smaller: *Is this why we all dreamed the lady?*

*Yeah,* Ila said. *She's been talking to us our whole lives. Tonight we answer.*

* * *

On the colony below, in the dormitory block that the inspectors had sealed but not emptied, children were already telling each other. That was the thing Kessler had never understood, Ila thought, watching it ripple outward through the link like light through water—you couldn't stop a secret that lived in sixty minds at once. The inspectors had confiscated terminals, locked doors, posted guards. They had not confiscated bedtime, and they had not posted guards inside dreams.

Mara Voss had helped. Of course Mara had helped—she'd staged the spaceport diversion that bought the crawler its crossing, and she'd paid for it with cuffs and a holding cell, and somehow she'd still gotten a message out through a laundry requisition form, because Mara Voss could smuggle truth through any bureaucracy ever devised. The message had reached the dormitory via a nine-year-old laundry worker who read everything, including things addressed to adults. It said, in full:

*Tell them tonight. All of them. Whatever happens after, tell them I am proud of them and I will deny writing this. — M.V.*

So that night, across Meridian's Rest and in the transfer station's bunk sections, seventy-one children went to bed early, complaining loudly and convincingly about being tired, while their guards and minders exchanged relieved looks and planned their own evenings.

Seventy-one. Ila felt them the way you feel a room go quiet—one by one, small attentions turning toward her across kilometers of rock and vacuum. Devi in section C, holding her little brother's hand. The twins in Dormitory 3 who fought constantly and were, tonight, holding hands. A boy named Pell whom Ila had never met, in a part of the colony she'd never seen, who whispered into the dark, *I always knew the dream was real, I told everyone, nobody believed me*—and Ila wanted to laugh again, because of course there was one kid who'd been insufferable about it for years.

*Ila.* Sefa's voice, beside her and also inside the link, everywhere at once. The old android knelt at the core console, her mismatched hands poised above the manual release. "When I open the gate, the archive will try to follow the path of least resistance. The jammed uplink is closed to it. The children are open. It will pour through them—all of it, forty colonies of testimony, grief, evidence. Through their sleeping minds and out through whatever receivers their proximity can excite. Every comm in the settlement. Every suit radio. The transfer station's own public address system, if the resonance holds." Her amber optic dimmed, brightened. "It will be loud, Ila. Inside them, I mean. I cannot promise it won't hurt."

Ila looked down the chain of hands. Tomas, awake now, gripping her fingers. Little Anouk, five years old, who had insisted on coming because, quote, *the lady in the dream likes me best*, and who was currently asleep with her mouth open and one sock missing.

"She trusted us," Ila said. "You trusted us. With the whole thing. Since before we were born, basically." She swallowed. "We can hold it."

"I know," Sefa said quietly. "I watched you decide to. That is how I know." She turned to the console. "On your word, then. Isn't that how your generation says it?"

"On my word," Ila said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "Go."

* * *

Sefa opened the gate.

There was no way to describe what came next except that Ila had spent her whole life hearing the ocean described by people who'd never seen it, and then one day she stood in front of the ocean.

The archive moved through her.

Not data—she understood that much instantly, the way you understand falling. Data was numbers, files, the dry bones of things. This was the *flesh* of forty worlds. She felt a fishing fleet on a moon called Halcyon Reach, three thousand people, erased from the ledgers when their debts were worth more dead. She felt the taste of their bread. She felt a terraforming crew abandoned mid-shift on a world whose name had been scrubbed so thoroughly that even the archive could only call it *Colony Nine*, and she felt them waiting for a pickup that the corporation had cancelled by deleting the pickup, and she felt the exact weight of the silence that followed. She felt famines scheduled like product launches. She felt signatures. Hundreds of thousands of signatures, calm and corporate and certain, authorizing all of it, and beneath them the small honest voices of auditors and clerks who had objected and been reassigned, and she felt Oscar Sandoval somewhere in that lineage of objections, a man who had signed his share of quiet endings and hated himself in a way she recognized now, because she'd felt it through the link the night he first walked the colony's empty streets.

It hurt. Sefa had warned her and it hurt anyway, like crying with your whole body, like being every erased person at once for one endless second—

—and then it wasn't one second. It was seventy-one children breathing together, and the second stretched and held, because that was the design, that was the whole beautiful terrible design: children could hold a note longer than any machine. Machines overheated. Machines got jammed, seized, shot. But seventy-one sleeping minds, synchronized, tuned, *willing*—they were a choir, and the choir did not have an off switch that Kessler's people could reach.

Through the relay's hull, through the rock, through vacuum itself, the archive went out.

* * *

In the inspection camp at the relay's surface approach, Auditor Kessler's team watched their own equipment betray them. Suit radios crackled with testimony. The camp's command terminal began displaying debt cancellations no one had entered. A junior technician pulled off his helmet because his mother's voice—or someone's mother's voice, some erased colony's mother—was reading shipping manifests aloud inside his skull, and he sat down hard in the regolith and didn't get up for a while.

At the transfer station, the public address system came alive at 0300 station time and did not stop. Colonists in the holding sections sat up in their bunks and listened to the names of the dead read out in a chorus of children's voices, and the guards assigned to watch them found they could not bring themselves to make anyone stop listening, because some of the guards were listening too.

And in the relay core, Ila Reyes held on.

She held on through the worst of it, through Colony Nine's long silence, through the famine ledgers, through the moment the archive reached its own final file—Sefa's personal log, thirty years of entries, ending with the one Ila knew by heart now: *If you are hearing this, children, then you are the inheritance. Spend it well. Spend it loudly.*

Sixty-five percent. Seventy. Eighty.

Kessler's people tried everything. Ila felt it distantly, the way you feel someone shaking your shoulder during a deep dream—they cut power to relay sections, and the children's link simply routed around the dark like water around a stone. They vented atmosphere from a module, and Sefa sealed bulkheads with the calm of a woman who had maintained this place alone for three decades and knew every valve by touch. At eighty-eight percent, someone detonated a charge on the primary trunk line, and the whole network shuddered, and eleven children on the colony below woke screaming—but they woke *holding the note*, sobbing and synchronizing at the same time, because Ila sent one thought down the chain, simple and fierce as a hand grip:

*Nobody lets go. Nobody lets go.*

Ninety-four percent. Ninety-seven.

And then Kessler's own channel went quiet.

Ila felt it happen rather than saw it—a sudden hole in the pressure, like a door opening in a crowded room. Through the link, through the transfer station's PA system, through every open frequency the archive had flooded, she heard the thing she would remember for the rest of her life: Kessler's superiors, the ones who had ordered the erasure, going silent. Not answering. Recalling his authority in real time, in terse automated messages, because the board had just watched forty colonies' worth of crimes stream onto every screen in inhabited space, and the first law of corporations, older than any charter, had asserted itself: *when exposed, disavow*. His clearance revoked mid-sentence. His escort receiving new orders from new masters. The man who had come to erase Meridian's Rest becoming, in the space of ninety seconds, a liability that his own organization needed erased.

One hundred percent.

The last file went out. The choir exhaled.

* * *

Afterward there was no cheering. Ila had imagined, in the planning-that-wasn't-planning, some moment of triumph—shouting, maybe, or crying, or Tomas doing his victory dance, which involved spinning until he fell down. Instead the warm chamber was full of children waking up slowly, blinking, holding their heads, asking for water. Anouk sat up, located her missing sock with the unerring instinct of the very young, and put it on.

"That was loud," she said.

"Yeah," Ila said.

"Did we win?"

Ila opened her mouth. Closed it. Out the viewport, the stars looked exactly the same as they had yesterday, which seemed rude. Somewhere up there, response ships were already moving—independent settlements, free stations, people who had heard and were coming, actual rescue arriving not because anyone had begged for it but because the truth had been made impossible to ignore. Somewhere down there, Kessler was learning what it felt like to be a rounding error. Somewhere in between, Oscar Sandoval was presumably doing something stubborn and decent, because that was simply what he did now.

But her head ached like she'd cried for a year. Devi was sobbing with relief three hundred kilometers away and Ila could still feel it, a distant warmth like a hand on her back. Forty colonies of ghosts had passed through her chest and left footprints. And tomorrow—tomorrow there would be lawyers, probably, and hearings, and the long boring machinery of a world deciding whether it deserved to exist, which of course it did, but convincing it would take work.

"No," Ila said finally, and ruffled Anouk's hair. "Not yet. But everybody knows now. That's the part we did."

Tomas raised his hand again. "Can we sleep normally now? Like, regular dreams? About normal stuff?"

"I hope so," Ila said.

But even as she said it, she felt it at the edge of the link—faint, patient, unmistakable. Sefa's voice, gathering itself one more time. Not a countdown. Not a warning. Something else. Something that sounded almost like a woman clearing her throat before saying goodbye.

Down the chain of hands, seventy-one children went quiet at once, listening.

*"Children,"* the voice said gently. *"There is one more thing I never told you."*
