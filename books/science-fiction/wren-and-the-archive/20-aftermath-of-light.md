## 20. Aftermath of Light

Oscar Sandoval had spent twenty-two years watching ships come down out of the sky, and he had a professional taxonomy for all of them. Company haulers came down like they were doing you a favor. Survey vessels came down like they were sorry about it. Security cutters came down like the ground owed them money.

The first ship after the broadcast did none of these things. It came down slowly, almost shyly, a patched-together freighter with mismatched hull plating and a name stenciled on its flank in three different fonts, as though no one could agree on what to call it: *Kindly Answer*. It settled on the old survey pad with the delicacy of someone entering a sickroom, and when its airlock opened, the first thing down the ramp was not soldiers or auditors or claim adjusters.

It was crates.

Oscar stood at the edge of the pad with his hands in his pockets and watched a woman in a faded independent settlement jumpsuit walk up to him, glance at the colony's ruined atmospheric processor, glance back at him, and say, "Which one of you is in charge? We brought filters. And seeds. And a dentist."

"A dentist," Oscar repeated.

"And a midwife, two welders, and somebody who claims she can fix anything with a cracked housing." The woman squinted at the processor, then at the sky, then at him. "We saw the broadcast. All of it. Took us four days to get here because we argued about what to bring the whole way."

"Four days," Oscar said. "The broadcast went out—"

"Eleven days ago. Yes. Some of us are closer than others." She stuck out her hand. "Priya Okonkwo, Halcyon Flats. Population four hundred, give or take a goat. You?"

"Oscar Sandoval." He shook her hand and found he didn't know how to finish the sentence. What was he, now? He'd been an insurance investigator for Meridian Consolidated Growth for two decades, and as of eleven days ago that job description had been rendered either obsolete or criminal, depending on which tribunal eventually got hold of it. "I'm... staying," he said finally, and was surprised by how true it sounded.

"Well, Staying-Oscar-Sandoval." Priya looked past him at the colony—the dormitory blocks with their patched windows, the children already swarming toward the freighter with the fearless logistics of the young, Ila Reyes marching at their head like a foreman twice her age. "You're going to need more than a dentist."

"We're going to need everything," Oscar admitted.

"Good thing we brought everything." She grinned. "Well. Everything except the goat. The goat lost the argument."

* * *

By the third day there were six ships on the pads and the plain around them, and Oscar had stopped being able to classify them, which he found both professionally humiliating and privately wonderful.

There was the *Kindly Answer*, of course. There was a converted ore-hauler from a settlement called New Thessaly whose captain, a barrel-chested man named Duval, had apparently heard the archive read aloud over open channels and simply pointed his ship at Meridian's Rest without consulting anyone. There was a sleek little courier whose pilot turned out to be nineteen years old and had crossed more dead space than anyone at the colony thought survivable, and who, when asked why, shrugged and said, "Somebody finally said it out loud. Figured the least I could do was show up."

That was the part Oscar kept turning over like a stone in his pocket, looking for the flaw in it. *Somebody finally said it out loud.* Forty erased colonies. Engineered famines. Debt cancellations dressed up as weather. Every crime Meridian Consolidated Growth had buried under actuarial language and quiet paperwork, read into every open channel by a relay station and a dozen children holding hands around a transmitter array.

He had spent his whole career making losses official instead of real. And it turned out that the antidote to all of it was just—saying it. Out loud. Where people could hear.

He found this so simple it made him suspicious, and then it made him want to sit down, and then it made him go find something useful to do with his hands, which was how he ended up spending an entire afternoon helping unload seed crates while a Halcyon Flats agronomist explained soil chemistry to a rapt audience of colonists who hadn't dared plan past next month in years.

"You're staring at the sky again," said Ila Reyes, appearing at his elbow with the unerring timing of a child who has learned that adults are most honest when caught off guard.

"I'm inventorying the ships," Oscar said. "It's a professional habit."

"How many?"

"Nine. Two more on approach, if the relay's telemetry is right."

"And none of them are theirs." She said *theirs* the way other kids said *monsters*, except that Ila Reyes had met actual monsters and wasn't impressed anymore.

"No," Oscar said. "None of them are theirs."

She considered this, chewing on the inside of her cheek the way she did when she was deciding whether to say the bigger thing. She always decided to say the bigger thing. He'd learned that about her in the conduit, in the dark, when saying the smaller thing would have been safer for everyone.

"Do you think they'll come anyway?" she asked. "The company. Even now."

Oscar looked at the sky. It was late afternoon, the long amber light that Meridian's Rest did better than any world he'd worked, and against it the approaching ships were small bright points descending with the unhurried confidence of vessels that expected to be welcomed.

"I think," he said carefully, "that the company's whole method was silence. Silence about the debts, silence about the colonies, silence about people like Dr. Sefa. And right now there is no silence anywhere in inhabited space. Every channel, every port, every settlement—everyone is talking about Meridian's Rest. They can't operate in noise, Ila. It's the one environment they never built equipment for."

Ila absorbed this with the grave attention she gave everything, then nodded once, satisfied, and ran off to tell the other children that the monsters couldn't swim in loud water, which was not exactly what Oscar had said but was, he reflected, a better version of it.

* * *

Kessler's escort arrived on the fifth day.

Oscar saw the cutter first—a Meridian security vessel, corporate gray, coming down on standard approach vectors with all the personality of a form letter—and felt his stomach drop through the crust of the planet before his brain caught up and noticed what was wrong with the picture.

The cutter was flying with its transponder fully open. Broadcasting its registry, its cargo manifest, and its authorization codes to every channel, including the ones the company used to pretend didn't exist. That was not how Meridian ships behaved. Meridian ships traveled dark and apologized later, if at all.

It set down on the far pad, and the ramp dropped, and Auditor Kessler came out flanked by his own security team—which was to say, flanked by four people walking slightly too close to him and slightly too alert, in the manner of escorts who were no longer entirely sure whether they were protecting a prisoner or transporting one.

Kessler looked terrible. Oscar took a certain careful, guilty pleasure in noting it. The man's immaculate composure had developed cracks; he moved like someone whose internal ledger had stopped balancing and who could hear, faintly and forever, the sound of numbers refusing to add up.

His eyes found Oscar across the pad. For a moment neither of them moved. Then Kessler walked over—not quickly, not with authority, but with the grim momentum of a man who has run out of alternatives and knows it.

"Sandoval."

"Auditor."

"You're not in custody," Kessler said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I escaped," Oscar said. "Through mining tunnels. It was undignified and I'd do it again."

Something flickered across Kessler's face that might have been respect, or might have been indigestion. "For the record," he said, "I offered you reinstatement."

"For the record, you offered me a bribe, and I declined it, and then your strike team shot at a relay station full of children."

"My orders were—"

"Your orders," Oscar said quietly, "were to erase forty-one colonies' worth of evidence, and you were going to execute them, because that's what you do. You're an instrument. I know, because I was one too, for twenty-two years, and I remember exactly how it feels from the inside. It feels like following procedure."

Kessler's jaw tightened. Behind him, his own security team had drifted into a loose semicircle, and Oscar realized, with the slow clarity of a man watching paperwork resolve itself, that the semicircle was facing inward.

"Meridian Consolidated Growth has been dissolved," Kessler said. His voice was flat, reciting. "Provisional receivership. Charter suspended pending investigation by the Independent Settlements Compact. My authorization codes were revoked nine days ago." He paused. "My ship's crew voted."

"Voted."

"On whether to follow my last operational order. Which was to return to company headquarters." His mouth twisted. "Headquarters that no longer exists in any functional sense. They voted to come here instead. To surrender me to—" he gestured vaguely at the colony, at the ships, at the impossible crowd of strangers unloading crates and goodwill across the plain, "—to whoever represents the injured parties. Apparently that's you people."

Oscar looked at the security team. One of them, a broad woman with gray at her temples, gave him a small nod that contained, compressed into a single gesture, an entire career of following orders and the precise moment she'd stopped.

"There's a compact liaison aboard the *Kindly Answer*," Oscar said. "Priya Okonkwo's been coordinating. If you're surrendering, surrender to her. She'll do it properly. There'll be records."

"There will be records," Kessler repeated, and something in his face suggested that this sentence, of all the sentences available to him, was the one that hurt most—and also, perhaps, the one that relieved him most. A man who had spent his life making things disappear, delivered at last to a place where everything stayed written down.

He straightened his jacket, which was a small, absurd, human gesture, and walked toward the *Kindly Answer* between his own former subordinates, and Oscar stood on the pad and watched an auditor of losses become a line item himself, entered honestly into a ledger he couldn't control.

He found he didn't feel triumphant. He felt tired, and clean, in the way of a wound that has finally been allowed to close.

* * *

That evening the colony ate together on the plain between the pads, because there were too many visitors for the mess hall and because nobody wanted to be indoors. Someone from New Thessaly had produced actual bread, which caused a minor religious experience among the colonists. The dentist had already pulled three teeth and been paid in gratitude and one (disputed) goat.

Oscar sat on a crate with a tin cup of something warm and watched the sky do its evening work. The stars came out the way they always did over Meridian's Rest—thick, patient, indifferent—but tonight some of them were moving, and every moving one was a ship full of people who owed this place nothing and had come anyway.

Unfamiliar generosity. He kept circling the phrase. Twenty-two years in the loss-adjustment business had taught him that help was always priced, that aid was a loan with different paperwork, that nobody showed up without a claim to file. And here were four hundred people from Halcyon Flats who had argued for four days about what to bring and left the goat behind, and a nineteen-year-old pilot who'd crossed dead space because somebody finally said it out loud, and a security team that had voted.

Voted. On kindness. As if it were a motion worth passing.

"You've got the face again," said Priya Okonkwo, dropping onto the crate beside him. "The one where you're auditing happiness and suspecting fraud."

"It's a professional hazard."

"Find anything?"

Oscar considered the question seriously, because she'd asked it seriously, and because he was learning—slowly, at fifty-three, like a man taking up an instrument late in life—to answer questions honestly.

"Discrepancies," he said. "Everywhere. Kindness at scale doesn't balance. Nobody's keeping proper accounts. That dentist has treated eleven people today and billed none of them. Your captain hauled sixty tons of filtration equipment across four days of space and his only stated compensation is 'it was the right thing,' which is not a recognized unit of exchange in any system I've ever worked in." He shook his head. "Either the whole thing is the most elaborate fraud in colonial history, or generosity is real and I've spent my career unable to see it."

"And?"

"And I'm leaning toward the second one." He turned the cup in his hands. "Slowly. Don't rush me."

Priya laughed, a big unguarded sound that several nearby children turned to enjoy. "Take your time, Staying-Oscar-Sandoval. We've got a lot of rebuilding ahead. You'll have years to get used to it."

Years. He let the word sit in his chest and found it fit better than he expected.

Across the firelight, Ila Reyes was showing a Halcyon Flats kid the constellations—or rather, showing her the places where the constellations *should* be, narrating the relay station's position with the proprietary pride of a girl who had walked into dead space and switched on a light. Mara Voss sat nearby, actually asleep sitting up, her first real rest in weeks, while the colony she'd nearly died administrating hummed with unfamiliar abundance around her. Somewhere in the dormitory block, twelve children were probably falling asleep into the same dream, and Oscar found he was no longer afraid of what they'd find there.

The sky kept filling. Another point of light detached itself from the stars and began its slow descent, running lights gentle, transponder open, answering a call that had taken thirty years and a dead woman's stubborn love to send.

Oscar watched it come down and thought: *this is what it looks like when the claim gets paid.* Not in currency. In presence. In dentists and seeds and strangers voting to be decent.

Then the comm unit on his belt crackled, and Ila's voice came through, breathless with the particular urgency she reserved for discoveries:

"Oscar? Oscar, wake up, everyone's dreaming it again—Sefa's voice is back, and this time she says she left us something else. Something she says we haven't found yet. And Ila-pause-for-effect—she says it's under the school."
