## 21. Terms of Inheritance

Mara Voss had spent twenty-two years running meetings in the Meridian's Rest community hall, and she could count on one hand the number that had mattered. The water rationing session of her first winter. The vote on whether to keep the school open after the company cut funding, which they'd won by a margin of two votes and one very persuasive casserole. And now this.

The hall had been built to hold three hundred people. It held four hundred and eleven today, with children sitting cross-legged in the aisles and old Tomas Ferro propped up near the stove because his lungs couldn't take the walk home if it ran long. Someone had hung a banner over the door—hand-painted, crooked, the letters still wet enough to smell: *WE'RE STILL HERE.*

Nobody would admit to painting it. Mara suspected the Reyes girl, who had paint under her fingernails at breakfast and an expression of elaborate innocence that wouldn't have fooled a customs drone.

"Order," Mara said, without much hope. "Order, please. We have three options on the table and approximately nine hundred opinions about them."

That got a laugh, which was what she'd wanted. Laughter loosened people. She'd learned that from Sefa's logs, actually—one of the thousand small inheritances nobody had expected. *A frightened crowd can't think,* the founding engineer had written in a maintenance memo that was really nothing of the kind. *Feed them first. Joke second. Decide third.*

So there were sandwiches. There had been sandwiches at every meeting since the broadcast, because the colony had collectively decided that any gathering might be historic and history required provisions.

"Option one," Mara said, holding up the tablet. "The company—the *new* company, the restructured one, the one with the apologetic letterhead—has offered full restoration of services, debt forgiveness, and a ten-year operations contract. Signed by a director we've never heard of, who assures us the erasure of forty-one colonies was, quote, 'a governance failure of a previous administration.'"

"They erased us," called Rosa Ibarra from the back, where the hydroponics crew sat in a solid block like a jury. "You don't get to call that a *governance failure.* You get to call it what my grandmother would've called it."

"Which is?"

"Theft." Rosa let the word sit. "Grand theft. Of forty-one worlds' worth of lives."

Murmuring, shifting, the sound of four hundred people deciding how angry to be. Mara let it run its course. Option two was the independents—the loose federation of settlements that had answered the broadcast, whose ships still sat in orbit like patient, unfamiliar birds. Their offer was membership: shared defense, shared markets, no debts at all, but also their rules, their councils, their schedules. A dozen voices in the hall already belonged to them; the independents had sent liaisons who sat near the front taking notes with the cheerful relentlessness of people who genuinely loved consensus.

And option three.

Mara looked down at the document on her tablet for perhaps the hundredth time since Kessler's escort had carried him off-world. Dr. Yuen Sefa's charter, recovered intact from the relay, dated thirty years before her death and updated—Mara still couldn't say *how*—in the years after. It named no corporation. It named no federation. It named, as heirs and trustees of Meridian's Rest, in a clause so plain it read like a hand on the shoulder:

*The children of this settlement, being the only parties the company has never thought to monitor, shall hold its future in trust until such time as they are old enough to be believed.*

"They're twelve," said Dev Okafor, standing, arms crossed. He wasn't hostile; Dev was never hostile. He was frightened, which wore the same clothes. "Ila Reyes is twelve. Amara Sol is eleven. We're proposing to hand a colony to sixth-graders."

"We're not proposing to hand them anything," Mara said. "We're proposing to read a dead woman's will out loud and decide what we owe her."

"She's been dead thirty years."

"And she's kept our air processors running for thirty of them," said old Tomas, from his chair by the stove, in the voice he used when he wanted everyone to stop talking, which worked about half the time. It worked now. "I knew Yuen Sefa. She fixed my boots once, in the first winter, because the company wouldn't ship replacements. Sat on my floor for two hours with an awl. And near the end, when she started talking strange—about records, about keeping everything—I thought grief had bent her. Her wife had just died in the processor accident the company called weather." He coughed, settled. "She wasn't bent. She was *building.* And she built it under our feet and inside our children's heads because she knew exactly what the rest of us would do."

"Which is?" someone asked.

"Nothing," Tomas said. "Exactly nothing. Because doing something costs, and we were all so tired."

The silence after that was the worst kind—not angry, not thoughtful, just tired, the way the hall itself was tired, the way the whole colony was tired, walls patched and pipes singing their old complaints. Mara felt it settle over the room like frost and understood, suddenly, that this was the real vote. Not company versus federation versus charter. Tiredness versus the terrible, expensive decision to matter.

* * *

They broke for lunch, because Sefa's memo had also said *never vote hungry*, and because Mara needed ten minutes in the admin office with her forehead against the cool wall.

Oscar found her there. He'd stayed—of course he'd stayed, the man had spent himself like currency to buy the transmission window and then, when the response ships came, had quietly declined every offer of transport off-world with the awkward firmness of someone returning a gift he didn't feel entitled to. He stood in her doorway now holding two cups of the colony's coffee, which was less a beverage than a threat.

"You look like a woman reading a bill," he said.

"I am. Sort of." She took the cup anyway. "Sefa's charter has a trustee schedule. Did you know that? Staggered ages. Ila takes partial trusteeship at sixteen. Full authority at nineteen. Until then, the adults administer—but the adults administer *for* the heirs, not instead of them. Every major decision needs their consent, formally recorded." She laughed, without much humor in it. "Do you know what that means? It means tomorrow morning I have to ask a twelve-year-old to co-sign the fate of four hundred people."

"Sounded like she had opinions at breakfast."

"She has opinions about everything. That's not the problem." Mara turned the cup in her hands. "The problem is me, Oscar. Twenty-two years I filed those reports. Deaths as relocations. Failures as sabotage. I told myself I was protecting people by keeping the paperwork boring—that if the company wasn't alarmed, the shipments would keep coming. And maybe that was even true. But Sefa saw it. Sefa, who was busy keeping everyone alive, still saw what I was doing, and she decided the only people who could be trusted with the truth were the ones too young to have learned to look away." She met his eyes. "How do you ask forgiveness from a dead engineer? Because I've drafted it eleven ways and none of them fit in a form field."

Oscar considered this with the unhurried attention he gave everything, the quality Mara had come to recognize as his actual profession—not insurance, but *looking*. "You don't draft it," he said finally. "You file it live. You stand up in that hall this afternoon and you tell them what you told yourself, and you let them decide if it was protection or cowardice. Probably both. It usually is." He shrugged. "I closed two hundred claims without looking underneath. If I can learn to sleep, anyone can be forgiven."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true. Different product line entirely."

Despite herself, she smiled. Outside the office window, the afternoon light lay long and amber across the spaceport, where the independent ships caught the sun and the company's single remaining shuttle sat grounded, impounded, its hull already tagged with graffiti in three languages. One of the tags, in careful child's handwriting, read *WREN WAS HERE.*

"They're going to vote for the charter," Oscar said. "You know that."

"I know they're scared of the company and embarrassed by the federation and proud in a way that doesn't have anywhere to go yet. That's not the same as knowing."

"No," he agreed. "But it's a start. Everything I've ever investigated started with somebody feeling a thing they couldn't quite name yet." He drained his coffee, grimaced at it with genuine respect, and headed for the door. "Hall fills at fourteen hundred. Wear your good jacket, Administrator. History requires provisions, but it also requires witnesses."

* * *

The afternoon session opened with the children.

It had been Ila's request, delivered through Mara with the terrifying formality of a written note: *the heirs should speak first, per the charter.* So they sat at the front table—seven of them, Ila at the center with her hands folded like a board chair, Amara Sol beside her trying not to fidget, the others arranged in descending order of age—and the hall went quiet in a way it hadn't gone quiet for any adult.

"We talked about it," Ila said. No notes. Of course no notes; she'd carried the dream in her head for a year, she didn't need paper. "All seven of us. And we're not going to tell you what to choose, because the charter says you administer *for* us, which means you decide, which means—" she paused, and Mara watched her find the words with the care of someone handling something fragile, "—which means it's your job to be brave first. We shouldn't have to be brave first. That's backwards. That was always backwards here."

Someone in the hydroponics block made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"But we'll tell you what we dreamed," Ila went on. "Not the relay dream. The new one. Since the upload finished, all seven of us dream the same thing, and it's just—a room. Sefa's room, we think, from the recordings. And there's a table in it, and on the table there are three doors, and every night the doors get a little more worn around the handles, like someone's been practicing opening them." She looked out at the hall, at four hundred faces. "We think the doors are your choices. And we think she's showing us that whichever one you pick, you have to pick it with your whole hand. Not halfway. Not peeking. Whole hand on the handle."

Silence. Then old Tomas began, deliberately, audibly, to clap, and the hall followed him like a tide coming in.

The debate that followed lasted four hours, and Mara chaired every minute of it, and it was—she would write this later in the log she now kept honestly, deaths as deaths, failures as failures—the finest thing she ever witnessed. Rosa Ibarra argued for the federation with charts. Dev Okafor argued for the company with economics, and was answered not with jeers but with questions, which was its own kind of miracle. People spoke who had never spoken in twenty years of meetings. The baker argued jurisdictional law. Two teenagers argued interstellar shipping logistics. A woman from the transfer station, evacuated during the lockdown and returned only last week, described what disappearance actually looked like from the inside, and the room went so still Mara could hear the processors breathing under the floor.

And through it all, the charter sat on the main screen behind her, Sefa's plain clause glowing softly, patiently, like a lamp left on for someone expected home.

When the vote finally came, it wasn't close. But it wasn't unanimous either, and Mara insisted the dissent be recorded in full, because Sefa's logs had taught her that too: *an archive that only keeps agreement isn't an archive, it's a mirror.*

Two hundred and ninety-seven for the charter. Meridian's Rest would hold itself, under its own seal, heir-bound, applying to the federation for friendship rather than membership and to the company for nothing at all except, as Rosa put it, "the return of our stolen dead, by name, on paper, or we will make their erasure the most expensive mistake in corporate history."

The hall erupted—not triumphant, exactly. Something quieter and larger. Four hundred people discovering simultaneously that they were allowed to exist.

Mara found Ila afterward, in the emptying hall, standing beneath the banner with its crooked letters.

"You know," Mara said, "the charter makes me answerable to you. Formally. I'm supposed to ask your consent on major decisions starting tomorrow."

Ila considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "First decision," she said. "The school gets fixed roof-first. And the coffee gets replaced. Both of those are non-negotiable."

"Noted and co-signed." Mara hesitated. "Ila. Your note said the heirs should speak first. Where did you learn parliamentary procedure?"

Ila grinned, and for a moment she was just twelve, gap-toothed and paint-fingered. "From your old meeting minutes. The boring ones. I read all of them." She turned toward the door, then stopped. "Also—there's a ship coming in. Unregistered, small, running dark until the last minute. Oscar spotted it an hour ago. It's hailing on an old frequency. Sefa's frequency." Her grin faded into something else, something that made Mara's chest tighten. "It says it's carrying passengers. Forty-one names' worth of passengers' descendants. From the other colonies. They heard the archive." She looked up. "They're asking permission to land. They want to see where the signal came from."

Mara looked out the window at the darkening sky, at the stars where a voice had traveled for thirty years to reach a room full of tired people and a table with three doors.

"Tell them," she said slowly, "that Meridian's Rest has room. And tell the kitchen—we're going to need more sandwiches."
