## 22. Wren

The relay had a name now, and the name was Ila's doing, which surprised no one.

She stood on the observation deck of the rebuilt station—rebuilt was generous; *repaired*, *reinforced*, *made habitable again*—and looked out at Meridian's Rest turning slowly below. The colony's atmospheric processor hummed at full capacity these days, its new parts paid for by settlements from three independent worlds who had read the archive and decided that a debt forty years old was still a debt. The sky above the colony was clean for the first time in a generation. You could see actual stars through it instead of the brown smear the colonists had learned to call weather.

Below her feet, behind three layers of shielding and one very stubborn door, the archive sat in its cradle, still broadcasting on a low loop—not as an accusation anymore, but as a record. A lighthouse, Sefa's imprint called it. Lighthouses don't scream. They just stay lit.

Ila pressed her palm against the cold viewport and thought about birds.

There were no birds on Meridian's Rest. There never had been. But Mara kept a battered reader loaded with old Earth files, and one gray afternoon she'd shown the children a picture of a wren: small, brown, unremarkable, famous mostly for singing louder than any bird its size had any right to sing. *It sings anyway,* Mara had said. *Nobody knows why. It just does.*

The relay was small. The relay was brown—well, gray, but everything out here was gray. And it had sung, alone, at the edge of dead space, for thirty years, to an audience of nobody, because Dr. Yuen Sefa had believed someone should hear.

"Wren," Ila said out loud, testing it.

Behind her, the caretaker made a sound like a kettle deciding whether to boil. It was the closest thing the android had to laughter, and after months of living beside it, Ila had learned to hear the difference between that and its other sounds—the grinding of failing joints, the soft tick of coolant, the long patient silence it fell into when it was remembering being a woman named Sefa.

"You disapprove," Ila said.

"I approve," said the caretaker, "excessively." Its voice was still a ruin, half static and half warmth, but the warmth had been winning lately. Oscar had spent two weeks rebuilding its vocal processor with parts cannibalized from a cargo drone, and when he'd finished, the first thing it said was *thank you* in Yuen Sefa's own voice, recorded decades ago, held in reserve all this time for exactly such an occasion. Ila had cried. Oscar had pretended there was something fascinating about a bulkhead. "Wren is a good name. Small things that persist are the only things that ever matter."

"Oscar says you'd say that."

"Oscar is learning to listen." The caretaker folded itself down onto the deck bench with the careful choreography of a body that no longer entirely trusted its own knees. "He'll be up on the afternoon shuttle. He's teaching today, isn't he? Thursdays are investigation days."

"How do you know his schedule?"

"I have been maintaining this station for thirty years, child. Knowing schedules is the entire job." A pause, and then, gentler: "Also he talks to himself while he grades. Loudly. The station hears everything."

* * *

Thursdays were investigation days, and Oscar Sandoval had discovered, to his considerable bewilderment, that he was good at teaching.

Not good in the way he'd been good at adjusting claims—good the way a man is good at breathing once he's been pulled out of deep water. He sat at the front of the colony schoolroom's largest room (the school had expanded since the broadcast; there were more children now, arrivals from the transfer station and two independent ships) with a slate of case studies and eleven students aged nine to sixteen, and he taught them the only thing he truly knew: how to look underneath.

"Rule one," he said, tapping the slate. "Paperwork is a confession. Every document is somebody telling you what they want you to believe. Your whole job is asking why they wanted that."

"Rule two?" asked Tomas Reyes, Ila's younger cousin, who had appointed himself class skeptic and took the role seriously enough to be annoying about it.

"Rule two: follow the money until it gets embarrassed."

"That's not a real rule."

"It's the realest rule there is." Oscar clicked to the next slide—a redacted shipping manifest from the old company days, which he'd spent three weeks un-redacting. "Now. This manifest says forty tons of protein concentrate arrived at Meridian's Rest in the famine year. The colony's intake logs say twelve tons arrived. Somebody's lying. Who?"

"The company," said half the room.

"Maybe. Prove it. Ila?"

Ila raised her hand from the back row, where she always sat, near the window, near the sky. "The discrepancy's thirty-eight tons, not twenty-eight. The manifest also lists a second shipment six weeks later that doesn't appear in the intake logs at all. So either it never came—and the company billed for it anyway—or it came and was diverted. Either way the lie is bigger than the missing food. The lie has a shape."

Oscar felt the thing he always felt when she did this—that complicated ache, pride braided with grief, because she was fourteen now and talked like an investigator twice her age and he knew exactly what it cost to learn to talk like that. "The lie has a shape," he repeated. "Write that down. That's the whole profession. Everything else is just reading."

After class, the children scattered toward the greenhouses, and Ila lingered, the way she always did.

"Mara wants to know if you're staying for the charter vote next month," she said. "The real one. Not the practice one you ran for us last week."

"I ran that so you'd understand parliamentary procedure before someone uses it against you."

"So you're staying."

Oscar looked at her. Outside the window, the atmosphere processor's cooling towers vented slow white breath into a sky that was actually blue now, actually blue, and somewhere past the horizon a second settlement ship was landing at the spaceport Mara had fought to keep operational through sheer administrative spite.

"I filed my resignation," he said. "Eight months ago. My employer sent back an automated acknowledgment and then, four months later, a human being, who offered me a division directorship if I'd come back and help them 'rebuild trust.' I told him trust was a claim I couldn't adjust." He shrugged. "They stopped writing."

"So you're staying."

"I'm staying, Ila."

She nodded once, satisfied, investigator accepting a verified statement, and ran off after the others. Oscar watched her go and thought about the man he'd been on the shuttle down, all those months ago, with his checklist and his detachment and his quiet professional certainty that some losses were simply meant to be made official. He'd come to close a file. Instead the file had closed over him, and he had surfaced somewhere else entirely, blinking, in a classroom, holding chalk.

* * *

That night, the dream came one last time.

Ila knew it was the last even before it began, the way you know the final page of a book by the thinness of the remaining paper. She stood in the corridor—the corridor, the one every child on two worlds had walked together for a year and a half—except now the corridor was different. It was bright. The humming door at the end stood open, and beyond it there was no relay core, no countdown, no red arithmetic of remaining time. There was a garden.

It wasn't a garden that had ever existed anywhere. It was compiled from fragments—Ila recognized greenhouse tomatoes, the smell of the hydroponics bay, the sound of rain on the dormitory roof—but assembled with a care that could only be love. And in the middle of it stood a woman Ila had never met in life and knew better than most people she'd met in life: dark-skinned, silver-haired, wearing a maintenance jumpsuit with the sleeves rolled up, hands stained with thirty years of honest grease.

"Yuen Sefa," Ila said.

"You don't have to call me that in here, little bird." Her voice was whole here, unbroken, warm as a heated circuit. "In here I'm just the voice. That's all I ever was. The rest of me—" she gestured at herself, at the garden, at everything— "the rest of me got to be finished. Do you know how rare that is? Most people die mid-sentence."

"The signal's changing," Ila said. "The caretaker told us. The beacon's shifting to standby."

"Because it's done. You're done. The work is done." Sefa crouched down to her level, the way adults in the dream always did, the way they never quite managed in waking life. "I built the failsafe for children because children were the only ones the company never watched. I chose you without knowing you, and I have regretted nothing, and I am going to tell you the thing I've been waiting to tell you since the beginning."

Ila braced herself. In eighteen months of shared dreams, the voice had given them coordinates, warnings, history, grief. It had never once given comfort. Comfort hadn't been on the mission profile.

"You can put it down now," Sefa said. "The watching. The counting. The keeping-awake-at-night. You carried a dead woman's alarm across dead space and you rang it, and it was heard, and now—" she smiled, and it was a smile Ila would keep for the rest of her life, folded away like the picture of the wren— "now it's just a lighthouse. Somebody else can tend the light. You get to be a child. Or whatever you are now. Whatever you've decided to become. That was always the inheritance, Ila. Not the mission. The *choosing*."

The garden began to soften at its edges, gently, like a drawing being erased by someone who loved the drawing.

"Will we dream you again?" Ila asked.

"No. And yes. Every time you fix something nobody asked you to fix. Every time you check the paperwork. Every time you refuse to let a loss be official instead of real." The light was everywhere now, and Sefa was mostly light, and her voice was coming from further and further away and getting no quieter at all, because that was the trick of her, that was the whole trick. "I'm going quiet, little bird. Sing anyway."

Ila woke with tears on her face and, strangely, no weight on her chest at all.

Across the colony, in dormitories and family quarters and the transfer station's repurposed berths, two hundred children woke in the same minute, and none of them screamed, and in the morning they compared notes over breakfast and found that everyone had heard the same last words, and Tomas Reyes—who doubted everything—said quietly, "Well. That's that, then," and ate his toast.

* * *

Oscar dreamed it too.

He hadn't expected to. He was not a child; the failsafe had never been keyed to him. But the dreams had started including him around month four, without ceremony, the way you get added to a family mailing list, and he had learned not to question gifts.

He woke in his small quarters above the schoolroom and lay in the dark listening to the building settle, and found that his eyes were wet, and did not investigate why. Some findings you just accept.

He got up, made coffee that was terrible and getting better as the colony's agricultural program improved, and climbed the path to the comm tower, because it was Thursday, and because there was one more thing he'd been meaning to do.

Mara was already there, wrapped in a coat against the morning chill, running the day's traffic on the main board. Independent ships, supply requests, a message from the Kessler tribunal on Ceres—Kessler himself, testifying, cooperating fully, apparently having discovered late in life that a man who counts losses can count his own. Mara glanced at Oscar, saw his face, and didn't ask. Administrators learn when not to audit.

"She went quiet," Oscar said.

"I know. Half the kids told me before breakfast. The other half drew me pictures." Mara's voice caught, just slightly, and she covered it with efficiency, which was her way. "The beacon's on standby. Low power. Just the name repeating."

"The name?"

"New designation. Someone uploaded it to the registry last night." She turned the display toward him.

**STATION WREN — RELAY AND ARCHIVE — PERMANENT. TENDED.**

Below it, in the registry's free-text field, someone had added a line:

*Sings anyway.*

Oscar read it twice. Then he laughed—a real laugh, rusty, the kind he hadn't produced in years—and Mara laughed too, and outside the tower window the sun came up over Meridian's Rest, over the greenhouses and the schoolroom and the spaceport and the cooling towers breathing white against the blue, over a colony that had been written off by everyone except the people who lived in it and the dead woman who had refused to stop believing in them.

"Charter vote is in three weeks," Mara said. "Sefa's charter. The children as heirs. Us as... whatever we are."

"Caretakers," Oscar said. "Tenders of the light."

"Guardians," Mara said, and looked at him, and did not say *yours*, and did not need to.

Oscar looked out at the sky, where a transport from the independent worlds was descending, bringing teachers, and seed stock, and a violin instructor someone had petitioned for, and he thought about the ledger he used to keep—the great internal accounting of every claim closed, every loss made official, every place he'd certified as unsalvageable without ever once looking underneath.

He was closing that ledger. The final entry wrote itself easily, in the old professional shorthand he'd never quite lost:

*Claim: Meridian's Rest.*
*Status: not a loss.*
*Status: home.*
*Filed under: Wren.*
*Adjuster's note: sings anyway.*

Down in the schoolroom, already, the first students of the day were arriving, and one of them—Ila, early as always, sitting by the window with the sky all around her—looked up at the tower as if she could feel him thinking about her, and raised one hand, not waving exactly, just confirming contact.

Signal received, Oscar thought. Signal understood.

He went down the stairs to begin the day.
