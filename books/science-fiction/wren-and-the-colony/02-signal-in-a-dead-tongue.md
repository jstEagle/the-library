## 2. Signal in a Dead Tongue

The signal came on the two hundred and eleventh day of the year, at the hour when Gideon Vasquez was arguing with a tomato vine.

"You're crowding the basil," she told it, easing a stake deeper into the loam. "I know you think you own this bay. You don't. The basil was here first, and unlike you, it doesn't sprawl."

The vine said nothing, which was its way. Eleven years aboard the Verdance had taught her that plants were excellent listeners and terrible conversationalists, and that she had long since stopped caring which was which. She talked because the alternative was silence, and silence aboard a station built for a crew of forty was not quiet—it was a pressure, a weight of empty rooms, of chairs no one sat in, of a mess hall with forty settings and one used cup.

She was still lecturing the tomato about spatial courtesy when the beacon console chimed.

Not the pulse. The pulse was a heartbeat she'd set herself, a slow four-second exhalation of coded light and radio aimed at the dark every ninety minutes, eleven years, four hundred and thirty thousand exhalations, and not once had anything exhaled back. She'd stopped flinching at it years ago. The pulse had its own sound in her mind, a low patient tone she could hear even when the console was muted, the way you can hear a clock in a house you've lived in long enough.

This was different. This was a *chime*—an incoming-priority alert she had configured on day one and never once heard, set to trigger only on an inbound signal matching certain parameters: narrowband, structured, non-repeating, originating inside the system.

Gideon stood very still, hands still buried in soil to the wrist.

The tomato vine, she would think later, had been the last thing she spoke to before the world changed.

She washed her hands. It mattered, somehow, to wash her hands—not because of contamination protocols, though the protocols were drilled into her, but because some part of her understood that whatever happened next, she did not want to touch it with dirt under her nails. She dried them on her thighs, crossed the growing bay, passed through the humidity curtain into the operations ring, and sat down at the console where she had spent eleven years being nobody to everybody.

The alert screen showed a waveform. Narrowband. Structured. Non-repeating.

Origin: somewhere in the system's outer dark, past the gas giant, past the debris belt, in the cold volume of space the survey charts labeled with a number and the word *EMPTY*.

Her heart was doing something unprofessional.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the waveform, to herself. "Okay. Let's find out what you are."

***

The first thing to rule out was an echo.

She'd been warned about echoes in her training. The system was old; the war that had ended it—the Collapse, capital letter, the event the Conclave's histories treated like weather rather than atrocity—had filled nearby space with debris and dead transmitters. A dying relay on a tumbling hulk could catch the Verdance's own beacon pulse and throw it back, warped, delayed, chopped into fragments that looked almost like meaning. Ghost transmissions, the Terraforming Office called them. *You will log them and disregard them,* her orientation materials said. *The beacon is a lighthouse. Lighthouses attract reflections.*

So she ran the echo analysis first, because procedure was a handrail and she needed something to hold.

The result came back in ninety seconds. Not an echo. An echo would carry the Verdance's own carrier signature, her own encoding scheme, her own timing intervals. This signal shared none of them. Its modulation was older—clunkier, wasteful by modern standards, spending bandwidth like a civilization that had never learned scarcity. Its timing intervals didn't match anything in the Conclave's current standards library at all.

Its power source, the analysis suggested, was failing. The signal had degraded across the forty minutes she'd been listening, dropping fragments, losing coherence, like a voice calling from a long way off with a throat going hoarse.

Not an echo. Not a ghost. Something with a transmitter and a clock and, presumably, a hand—or the equivalent—pressing the key.

The second thing to rule out was a Conclave test. The Terraforming Office had, in eleven years, run exactly three readiness drills, each one a synthetic inbound signal designed to test whether she'd follow logging procedure. She pulled the drill archives, matched the signal against them. No correlation. The drills had all been in Conclave Standard, crisp and bureaucratic. This was not Conclave Standard.

This was not anything she had a decoder for.

She ran it through the linguistic analysis suite anyway—the station had one, because the beacon's original designers had apparently anticipated contact with someone, though her orientation materials had never said who. The suite chewed on the waveform for six minutes and returned its verdict in the flat, apologetic tone of machines delivering bad news: *No match in database. Confidence of structured language: 94.7%. Translation: unavailable.*

Structured language. Not a machine handshake, not telemetry, not noise. *Words.* Someone was out there saying words.

And then the analysis suite, in the way of old software that knows more than it admits, appended a single line it had no business appending:

*Partial phonetic similarity to archived corpus: Meridian (extinct).*

Gideon stopped breathing.

***

Her grandmother's name had been Ilsa Vasquez, and she had sung in a language the Conclave had no word for, because the Conclave had no words for anything it had decided not to exist.

She'd sung while cooking, while mending, while sitting on the hab-module's single chair in the workers' quarter where Gideon grew up, songs with melodies that looped back on themselves like rivers, and words that the Conclave's education system had no letters for. *Old Meridian,* her grandmother called it, when Gideon asked. *The tongue from before. From the ships, from the first dark.* And when Gideon asked why nobody else spoke it, why her schoolmates' grandparents didn't sing in it, Ilsa had gone quiet in a particular way—her hands stilling in her lap, her eyes going to the door, as if the question itself were something that might be overheard.

*Some songs were left behind on purpose,* she said, once. *And some people were left behind with them.*

Gideon had been nine. She'd thought it was just the way old people talked, circular and strange, full of meanings she'd grow into. She had learned the songs the way children learn everything—by osmosis, by repetition, by the shameless mimicry of a voice that wanted to please. She could sing perhaps thirty of them still. She could not have translated a single line. The language had gone into her as melody, not meaning, a shape without content, a shell without the animal inside.

Until now.

She pulled the archived corpus. It was small—fragments, mostly, recovered from pre-Collapse data salvage, the kind of thing the Terraforming Office kept because the Terraforming Office kept everything and understood nothing. Phonetic tables. A partial lexicon, maybe four hundred words, assembled by some dead linguist's patient ghost. And recordings—actual recordings, voices two centuries dead, speaking a language that had been pronounced extinct in documents Gideon wasn't cleared to read.

She played one. An old voice, cracked and formal, reciting what the metadata called *Standard Navigation Prayer.*

The hair on her arms stood up.

Because the melody of it—the *shape* of it, the rise and fall, the way the syllables clustered and broke—was her grandmother's lullaby. The one about the boat on the black water. The one Ilsa had sung so many thousands of times that it lived in Gideon's spine, in the rhythm of her walking, in the way she hummed when she worked the soil.

Her grandmother hadn't been singing nonsense. She'd been singing *Meridian.* A language the Conclave had erased, surviving the way languages survive everything—carried in the mouths of women, passed hand to hand below the line of official attention, sung to children who didn't know they were memorizing a corpse.

And now a transmitter in the outer dark was speaking it.

***

She worked through the night. She told herself she was being thorough. She was not being thorough; she was being afraid in the most productive way available.

The signal was fragmentary—maybe thirty seconds of clean transmission per burst, arriving every few hours, degrading each time. She recorded four bursts before dawn-cycle, and laid them against the lexicon like a woman laying bones on a table, trying to make a body of them.

Four hundred words was almost nothing. But distress calls, she reasoned, would be built from the oldest, hardest, most necessary words—the words a language keeps even when everything else rots. She cross-referenced the phonetic matches, and the fragments assembled themselves into something that was not translation but was not nothing either:

*...this is... [vessel name unknown]... we are... [unclear]... still... [unclear]... we are still... [unclear]... answer... please... answer...*

She played it back a dozen times. *We are still.* We are still what? Alive? Here? Waiting? The lexicon had three candidates for the missing word and she hated all of them, because all of them were terrible in their own way.

*Answer. Please. Answer.*

Eleven years she had sent her pulse into the dark. Four hundred and thirty thousand unanswered exhalations. She had made her peace with it, or something adjacent to peace—had told herself the beacon was a duty, a lighthouse, a candle in a window for colonists who would arrive someday and find the garden ready. She had never once, not in eleven years, considered that the silence might not be empty. That the dark she was calling into might have someone in it. Someone who had been calling *her*, or calling anyone, or calling God, with a voice going hoarse, for a very long time.

She sat back in the operator's chair, which still held the shape of no one but her, and she felt the wonder arrive like weather.

Someone was out there. Someone was alive. Someone was speaking the language of her grandmother's lullabies, from the *EMPTY* volume past the debris belt, and they were asking, in the politest, most broken grammar, for someone to answer.

She was smiling. She registered the smile the way you register sunlight—warmth on the face before thought catches up. Someone out there. Someone to—

The smile curdled.

Because the next thought arrived on schedule, the way dread always does, dressed in the uniform of a simple question:

*Why doesn't the Conclave know?*

The Verdance's sensors covered the system. Not perfectly—the outer dark was vast and cold and easy to hide in—but the Conclave's survey sweeps were thorough, and the Conclave's records were, she had been assured in her orientation, complete. Every hulk, every derelict, every dead relay catalogued. *You will log them and disregard them.* The system was accounted for. The system was *closed*. That was the whole architecture of her posting: one garden, one beacon, one dead world, one fleet of frozen colonists in the ships above, and nothing else. Nothing else was the promise the Conclave had made her, the ground her eleven years stood on.

And a transmitter was speaking from a place where nothing was supposed to be.

Which meant one of three things. Either the Conclave's records were wrong—possible, but the Conclave did not tolerate wrongness in its records; wrongness got people reassigned. Or the signal was a hoax, some elaborate ghost from the war—possible, but the language was real, the language was *her grandmother's language*, and hoaxes didn't reach into workers' quarters and steal lullabies. Or—

Or the Conclave's records were not wrong but *edited*. Complete the way a censored letter is complete. Accounted for the way a grave is accounted for.

She thought of her grandmother going quiet, eyes on the door. *Some songs were left behind on purpose. And some people were left behind with them.*

The console chimed again. Fifth burst. Weaker. The voice in the dark was going hoarser.

Gideon looked at the logging prompt blinking on her screen—the field where, per procedure, per orientation, per eleven years of handrails, she was supposed to enter the classification of this event. *Ghost transmission. Logged and disregarded.* Two words and a click, and the signal would vanish into the archive, and the Verdance would go back to being a lighthouse for no one, and she would go back to being safe.

Her hands hovered over the keys.

The tomato vine needed staking. The soil chemistry needed balancing. There were four hundred hours of routine between her and the next decision, and every one of them would be easier if she clicked the button, if she logged the ghost, if she let the dead language stay dead and the dark stay empty.

*Answer. Please. Answer.*

She thought of her grandmother singing to a child who didn't know she was hearing a survivor's voice.

Gideon entered the classification field. She typed: *Inbound structured transmission, non-Conclave origin, ongoing. Under review.*

Then she opened a channel, keyed her own transmitter to the signal's frequency, and—heart hammering, hands steady, eleven years of silence breaking like ice under her—she sent back the only Old Meridian she had.

She sang the lullaby. The one about the boat on the black water.

And then, because the melody carried a shape she'd never had reason to parse until tonight, she added the four hundred words of a dead linguist's lexicon, assembled into the simplest sentence she could build, and sent that too:

*I hear you. I am here. Answer me.*

The transmission went out into the outer dark, and the Verdance's growing bays hummed on around her, and somewhere past the debris belt, in a place where nothing was supposed to be, a failing transmitter had just learned that the silence was over.

Gideon sat in the operator's chair and waited for the dark to answer back—and understood, with a certainty that arrived colder than any chime, that whatever answered would change what her garden had been *for*.
