## 3. Inventory of Ghosts

Gideon did not sleep.

She told herself she would—told the seedlings in Bay Four, told the recirculating water, told the small green world that depended on her rhythms to keep its own—but sleep was a country she could not reach. The signal sat in her workstation like a stone dropped into still water, and every time she closed her eyes she felt the ripples move through her chest.

*Help us. We are still here. We were never lost.*

Old Meridian. Her grandmother's voice rose unbidden, humming in the galley of a habitat module two decades and a lifetime away: *Lumen veth, lumen kai, the lantern knows the road though the road forgets the walker.* She had thought it nonsense then, a child's comfort, a dead woman's dead language. Now the words had coordinates.

She ran the verification a fourth time because the first three felt like luck, and Gideon Vasquez did not trust luck with anything more fragile than tomato pollination. The result held. The transmission originated from somewhere past the heliopause of the system's outer dark—not reflected off debris, not an echo of the Verdance's own beacon folding back on itself after eleven years of lonely repetition. It was pointed. It was addressed. And it was dying, its carrier wave fraying at the edges like cloth left too long in weather, which meant whatever was sending it had been sending it for a very long time.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the workstation, to the basil growing in its light-tray beside it. "Okay."

The basil did not answer. Nothing ever did. But talking steadied her hands, and her hands needed steadying, because what she intended to do next was a crime.

She knew this the way she knew the station's air pressure—absolutely, without needing to check. Article Nine of the Conclave Charter, the one they'd made her memorize along with her posting orders: *Station personnel shall not access archived Conclave cryptographic materials above clearance tier three. Violation constitutes breach of covenant and forfeiture of protection.* Forfeiture of protection. Eleven years ago those words had sounded bureaucratic, bloodless. Tonight they sounded exactly like what they were: a threat dressed in a clerk's grammar.

But here was the thing about being alone for eleven years: you got very clear about your own company. And the woman sitting in this chair, the one who talked to plants and logged a beacon pulse into silence twice a day, was not going to sit in this chair knowing someone out there was alive and drowning, just because a document told her to look away.

"I'm sorry," she said—to whom, she wasn't sure. To the Conclave. To the version of herself who had signed the covenant and meant it. To the plants, maybe, whose quiet order she was about to risk everything to disturb.

She opened the archive.

* * *

The Conclave keys lived in a partition of the station's core that she had never once touched, a sealed stratum of the system like permafrost under a garden bed. Her clearance—tier two, gardener-class, the lowest tier that still permitted independent judgment—let her see the door but not open it. Except.

Except that eleven years is a long time, and Gideon was thorough by nature and curious by affliction, and in her third year aboard, during a long dark-season maintenance cycle when the days blurred together like wet paper, she had found the flaw. Not hacked it—she wanted to be honest with herself about that, even now, even alone, especially alone. She had *noticed* it. A legacy authentication routine from the station's original build, predating the current security architecture, still running quietly in the background like an old root left in the soil after replanting. It accepted credentials in an older format. And the Verdance's own commissioning documents—the ones she was authorized to read—contained, buried in an appendix nobody had touched since the station went operational, a full credential set in exactly that older format.

Someone had been careless. Or someone, generations ago, had left a door unlocked on purpose, and waited two hundred years for a gardener curious enough to find it.

Her hands hovered over the console. The bay lights hummed. Somewhere below her feet, water moved through the nutrient lines with its patient sound, the sound of a world continuing whether or not she deserved to be part of it.

"You can stop," she said to herself. "You can log the signal, flag it as anomalous, send it up the chain, and let them handle it. That's the protocol. That's the *safe* thing."

And the safe thing, she understood now, with the signal's dying words still ringing in her skull, was probably also the thing that made the signal disappear. Because if the Conclave had answers about Old Meridian transmissions—and some cold, quiet part of her suspected they did—then flagging it would be less like reporting a fire and more like reporting it to the arsonist.

She entered the legacy credentials.

The partition opened without resistance, which was somehow worse than a fight would have been. No alarms, no lockout, no warning chime. Just a soft expansion of the directory tree, layer after layer unfolding like something that had been waiting to exhale. Files with dates reaching back before the Collapse. Files with dates reaching back before *her*. Tier-five encryption, tier-six, tiers she hadn't known existed, all of it suddenly legible under the old key like frost melting off a window.

"There you are," she whispered, and hated how much she sounded like her grandmother, who had said the same thing over bread dough and buried seeds and any small thing finally coaxed into the open.

She didn't know what she was looking for. That wasn't quite true—she knew exactly what she was looking for, she just didn't have a word for it yet. Something that connected the beacon to the signal. Something that explained why a garden station at the edge of a dead world carried a transmitter powerful enough to reach the heliopause, when the nearest living Conclave authority was nine months away by courier. You didn't build a lighthouse where no ships passed. Unless ships passed that weren't supposed to be seen.

She searched the beacon's own documentation first. Commissioning specs. Transmission schedules. And there—filed under *secondary functions*, in language so dry it crackled—a line item she had read a hundred times without ever reading:

*Beacon pulse interval calibrated to legacy Meridian standard. Purpose: continuity of archival signaling protocols. See Annex K.*

Annex K did not exist in any index she had access to. But it existed in the partition. She pulled it, decrypted it, and sat very still while two centuries of careful lying rearranged themselves around her.

The beacon wasn't calling anyone home. The beacon was a *listening post's* handshake—a pattern broadcast outward so that anything built to Meridian standards, anything still running on the old protocols, would recognize it and answer. The Verdance wasn't a garden with a radio attached. The radio came first. The garden was the cover story, the patient, beautiful, two-hundred-year cover story, and she was its gardener, and she had never once wondered why a terraforming support station needed to talk to the dark.

"They knew," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "They built me on top of a question and told me not to ask it."

*Ask no questions about its origin.* Her posting condition, delivered by a pleasant Terraforming Office functionary with a smile like a sealed envelope. She had taken it for vanity—bureaucracies loved their secrets the way old men loved their chairs. It wasn't vanity. It was a load-bearing wall.

Her heart was going too fast. She made herself breathe the way she breathed during atmospheric failures: four counts in, seven counts hold, eight counts out. The plants around her kept their green patience. She envied them.

Then she searched the signal itself.

* * *

It took six hours. She worked through what should have been night, through the station's dimmed cycle, lit only by the console's glow and the grow-lamps' steady violet wash. She ate nothing. Twice she caught herself talking—to the console, to Sefa-knows-who, to the ghost of her grandmother—and both times she stopped, and both times she started again within minutes, because the alternative was silence, and the silence had teeth now.

The distress transmission was fragmented, but fragmentation was a puzzle with edges, and Gideon had spent eleven years learning to reconstruct whole organisms from partial samples. She mapped the broken packets against standard Meridian burst formats—which she now had, thanks to Annex K, complete technical specifications for—and began reassembling. Header. Authentication block. Origin identifier.

The origin identifier stopped her cold.

It wasn't a ship's registry. It wasn't a station code. It was a hull designation, formatted in the old fleet style, and when she cross-referenced it against the Conclave's own historical archive—tier five, sealed, *never meant for her eyes*—the file that opened was thin in the way files get thin when someone has been deleting things.

**VESSEL: CSV PERPETUA**
**CLASS: Cryogenic dispersal ark (long-range)**
**COMPLEMENT: 10,412 souls (manifest sealed)**
**STATUS: LOST WITH ALL HANDS — [DATE REDACTED]**

Lost with all hands. Two hundred and eleven years ago, according to the file's last modification stamp. Lost, and yet transmitting. Lost, and yet using authentication codes that matched the Verdance's beacon handshake like a lock matches its key.

Gideon pushed back from the console and stood, arms wrapped around herself, in the middle of her garden in the middle of the night-cycle, and shook.

Because here was the arithmetic her mind refused to finish and finished anyway: the Perpetua had been lost *in this system*. Not beyond it, not in the deep black between stars where lost things stayed lost. Here. Close enough that its distress call, worn down by distance and decades, had finally reached the one station in the whole Converse built to hear it. Ten thousand four hundred and twelve souls, sealed manifest, lost with all hands—and the Conclave's records contained no search operation, no memorial, no wreckage survey. Just the status line, the redacted date, and a file so clean it might have been written yesterday.

Files that are cleaned are files that matter. Her grandmother had taught her that too, in her way—had taught her that the foods people hid were the foods worth stealing, that the songs sung quietly were the ones that mattered. Empires tell you everything about what they're proud of. It's the shame they bury deepest.

She sat back down. She pulled the navigation annexes, the deep-system charts, the ones marked for survey vessels with clearances she could spell but not hold. And there, in a chart revision log spanning ninety years, she found what she had no right to find: a single automated entry, flagged *instrument anomaly*, noting a mass reading in the outer system consistent with—"and here the archivist's language turned almost embarrassed"—*a stationary object of significant tonnage, classification unresolved, observation discontinued per directive.*

Observation discontinued per directive.

Somebody had seen the Perpetua. Somebody had reported it. And somebody above somebody above somebody had said, in effect: *no.*

Gideon pressed her palms flat against the console, against the cool surface, grounding herself the way she grounded seedlings after transplant—firm, gentle, giving the roots something true to hold. The coordinates from the distress signal's header sat decoded in front of her now, precise to within a few thousand kilometers, hanging in the dark of the outer system like a splinter under skin.

She could go there. The survey skiff had the range. The garden could run on automated care for weeks—she'd built the systems herself; they were better than the Conclave knew. She could go and see with her own eyes what ten thousand four hundred and twelve souls looked like after two hundred years of being lost.

She could also stay. File nothing, do nothing, let the signal die the rest of the way into static, and spend whatever remained of her life pruning tomatoes for colonists who would wake into a world curated by liars, tended by a woman who had heard ten thousand people knocking and pretended it was the wind.

"No," she said, and this time the word had no audience problem at all. It was for her. "No. I don't accept that."

But even as she said it—even as the thrill moved through her, bright and electric, the thrill of the threshold, of the door about to open—guilt came with it, quiet and cold, the way frost comes. She was trespassing. Every byte she'd pulled tonight was stolen goods in the eyes of the law that fed her, housed her, protected her. If they found out—if the logs betrayed her, if the partition kept receipts she hadn't thought to check—they would take the Verdance from her. They would take the *work*. Eleven years of living soil, of genetic lines coaxed back from dormancy, of a green world raised by hand in a place that had never known one. Forfeiture of protection. They would strip it all away and leave her nothing, and worse, they'd be right to, by their own lights. She had broken covenant tonight. There was no version of this where she hadn't.

She looked at the basil. At the rows of seedlings sleeping under their violet lamps. At the whole breathing organism of the station that was, she understood now, simultaneously a garden and a lie.

"If you're real," she said to the coordinates, to the dark beyond the hull, to whoever or whatever was left out there, "if even one of you is still breathing—I'm coming. Hold on. *Lumen veth, lumen kai.* Hold on."

The console glowed. The signal's decoded header pulsed softly in the corner of her display, patient as a seed.

Outside the viewport, the dead world turned beneath her, gray and silent, waiting—as she now understood it had always been waiting—for someone to decide what it was for.

Gideon Vasquez began, quietly and with trembling hands, to plan a theft.
