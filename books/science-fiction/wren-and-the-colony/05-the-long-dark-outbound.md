## 5. The Long Dark Outbound

The skiff had a name. Of course it did—everything on the Verdance had a name, because Gideon could not bear to tend anything anonymous—but it was a bureaucratic one, stenciled on the hull in Conclave gray: *Survey Unit 7, Terraforming Office, Property of the Meridian Authority*. She called it the Mule, because it was stubborn, ugly, and carried more than it was built to.

She had never once taken it past the station's service radius.

Eleven years, and in all that time the farthest she'd flown was the comms relay at Lagrange-two, a forty-minute round trip she made quarterly to swap the relay's cooling cartridges and to feel, briefly, what it was like to be somewhere that wasn't home. The Mule had no cabin to speak of. A cockpit with two seats—the second still wrapped in its factory film, because no one had ever sat in it—a cargo bay sized for soil amendments and replacement pump housings, and a drive rated for exactly the kind of boring, short-haul work the Conclave had purchased it for.

The coordinates from the decrypted signal were nine days out at the Mule's best burn. Nine days there. Nine days back. Eighteen days of automated care running the garden's circadian systems without her hands anywhere near them.

She stood in the airlock bay at 0400 station time, which was a time that existed only because she'd invented it, and told herself for the hundredth time that this was insane.

*You are the sole custodian of a Class-Terra beacon installation,* said the voice in her head, and it was not her voice. It was the orientation officer's, a woman named Deputy Administrator Okonkwo who had interviewed her eleven years ago through a delay-plagued uplink and whose face Gideon could no longer quite recall, only the tone: patient, faintly amused, the tone of someone reading terms aloud to a person who has already signed. *Your presence is required for continuous manual oversight. Automated systems are a redundancy, not a substitute.*

But the garden had run itself before. Not for eighteen days—never for eighteen days—but during her one illness, six years ago, a fever she'd ridden out half-conscious in the medbay while the fertigation loops cycled and the pollinator drones worked their rounds and the tomatoes ripened without anyone to tell them they were doing well. She'd come back to find everything alive. Everything patient. Plants, she reminded herself, were the original masters of waiting. They had taught her everything she knew about it.

It wasn't the garden she was afraid for.

She ran the preflight with hands that knew the sequence so well they seemed to be doing it independently of her, checking the same gauges twice, then a third time, because ritual was what fear looked like when it put on a uniform. Fuel cells at ninety-eight percent. Life support scrubbers fresh. Water recycler flushed. She loaded three days of rations for an eighteen-day trip and then, standing in the cargo bay surrounded by crates of desiccated nutrient stock, went back and loaded three more, because the arithmetic of *what if I'm wrong about how long this takes* refused to close.

The signal fragment sat in her tablet where it had sat for six days now, playing its loop when she asked it to. *...Perpetua... requesting... any station... this is...* Old Meridian, the language of her grandmother's lullabies, worn down by distance and decades into something half-eaten, but unmistakably a voice. Unmistakably a person. Or people. The signal strength suggested a transmitter running at a fraction of designed power, which meant either a dying ship or a dying crew or both, and every hour she spent preflighting the Mule was an hour that signal got weaker.

*Any station.*

There was one station. There had only ever been one station, as far as anyone in the system's official memory was concerned, and it was hers, and it had never once been asked for help by anyone except the tomato blight of year four.

She closed the cargo bay and stood a moment in the corridor outside, looking back down the long spine of the Verdance. Through the observation ports the grow-lights glowed their false dawn, tier after tier of green receding into the dark like a staircase going down into a lit country. Somewhere in Tier Six the citrus trees were flowering out of season because she'd adjusted their photoperiod as an experiment last month and hadn't had the heart to correct them. Somewhere in the seed vault, forty thousand varieties slept in their foil envelopes, each one labeled in her handwriting, each one a small argument that the future was worth preparing for.

"I'll be back," she said to the corridor, and heard how it sounded—like something you say to a sick relative—and hated herself a little for it, and went.

* * *

Breaking orbit was the easy part. The Verdance's traffic protocols consisted of Gideon filing a flight plan with herself, so she filed one, dutifully, listing her destination as *relay maintenance, unscheduled*, which was technically true if you considered the entire outer system a kind of relay maintenance. The lie sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone for the first hour. Then the station shrank behind her—a spindle of light, a star among stars, then just a star, then nothing her eyes could pick out from the field—and the stone dissolved into something else. Something colder and cleaner.

Space, unmediated.

She had lived inside space for eleven years the way a fish lives inside water: totally, invisibly, without once feeling it move against her skin. The Verdance's ports framed it, curated it, gave her the black in tasteful portions. But the Mule's canopy was a bubble, and the bubble did not curate. The dark came at her from every direction at once, vast and indifferent and so complete that her mind kept trying to file it as night, kept failing, because night ended, and this did not. She was nine days from anything human-made except the thing she was sitting in, and the thing she was sitting in was the size of a delivery van.

Her hands shook on the yoke. She noticed them shaking the way you notice weather—as a fact about the world rather than a fact about herself—and made herself look at the fuel gauge instead, and the heading indicator, and the little green line of the plotted course stretching out ahead of her across a chart that was mostly, politely, blank.

*Conclave charts end here,* she thought. Not officially—officially the charts continued, dotted with survey markers and resource annotations, the tidy cartography of ownership. But she'd spent enough nights in the archive by now to know that the outer system's maps were fiction maintained by inertia. Nobody surveyed the dark beyond the heliopause fence because nobody was supposed to have a reason to. The blankness wasn't ignorance. It was policy.

She was flying off the edge of a map that had been drawn to keep everyone home.

The first day, she talked to the Mule. This embarrassed her for approximately twenty minutes, until she remembered that she talked to tomatoes, and citrus trees, and once, memorably, to a malfunctioning humidity sensor she'd named Gerald, and the Mule at least had the decency of being a vehicle with opinions—it shuddered when she pushed the burn too hard, hummed when she didn't, complained in the language of warning lights. "I know," she told it, easing off the throttle. "We're both doing more than we were built for."

The second day, she stopped talking altogether, because the silence out here had a texture, and fighting it was exhausting, and eventually she found that if she let it settle around her instead it became almost companionable—the way deep water is companionable if you stop trying to stand up in it.

She thought about her grandmother.

Not deliberately. The old songs simply surfaced, the way they always did when Gideon was frightened, rising out of whatever flooded cellar of her mind held them. *Duerme, mi barco, duerme*—sleep, my ship, sleep—the song the ark-born women used to sing to their children in the berths of the first ships, back when humanity was a rumor traveling between stars. Her grandmother had sung it while pruning, hands moving through the bean rows, voice pitched low and rough. Gideon had asked her once, aged maybe seven, what the words meant, and her grandmother had said: *They mean we were never supposed to forget that we are passengers. Even when we arrive. Even when we build. Passengers.* And young Gideon had nodded and understood nothing, and now, alone in a bubble of light crossing a darkness wider than any story she owned, she understood it completely, and wished she didn't.

Because the song had a second verse. One her grandmother only sang when she thought Gideon was asleep. The verse about the second ship.

*Two boats left the burning shore,* it went. *One was counted, one was not.* As a child she'd assumed it was metaphor, the way old songs were mostly metaphor—grief wearing costume. But she had spent the last six days with the decrypted signal, and the archive keys, and the word *Perpetua* glowing on her screen like a coal, and she no longer believed in the metaphor. Her grandmother had sung a passenger manifest. Her grandmother had sung a census of the erased.

"Who were they?" she asked the dark, and the dark, characteristically, declined to answer.

* * *

On the fourth day, the loneliness arrived properly.

It caught her at shift-change, which was to say at the arbitrary hour she'd decided to sleep, strapped into the pilot's seat because the Mule's bunk was a joke involving a hammock and a sense of humor she didn't share. Sleep came hard out here anyway. The dark pressed against the canopy with a physical weight, and her body, eleven years trained to read the glow of grow-lights through her eyelids as safety, read this absolute black as something closer to burial.

She woke at hour nineteen of the sleep cycle with her heart slamming and her breath fogging the canopy, certain—not thinking, *certain*—that something was outside the hull watching her. There was nothing. There was never anything. That was the whole horror of it: the void's perfect emptiness was indistinguishable, to a primate nervous system, from the presence of a perfectly hidden predator. She lay strapped in the hammock-sway of zero thrust and laughed at herself, quietly, the laugh coming out wrong, and then she did the thing she had done every night of eleven years alone aboard the Verdance when the walls felt too close.

She talked to someone who couldn't answer.

"You would hate this," she told her grandmother, in the old tongue, because some complaints deserved their native language. "You would tell me to turn around. You'd say, *Gidi, mija, the dead don't need rescuing.*" A pause. The recyclers breathed. "Except they're not dead. That's the whole problem, abuela. They're not dead, and somebody decided they should be, and I know—I *know*—you sang about them, which means you knew, which means everyone knew, which means—" Her throat closed. She finished it in Meridian, barely above a whisper: "*Which means I grew up inside the lie, and the lie fed me.*"

The silence took the words the way it took everything: completely, without judgment, giving nothing back. It occurred to her, drifting, that this was what prayer must have been like for the people who invented it. Speaking into a dark that holds you precisely because it will never hold you back.

And underneath the fear—she could admit this now, four days out, with the station too far behind to shame her—underneath all of it ran a current she hadn't felt in over a decade, warm and strange and almost painful in its novelty:

Someone needed her.

Not the way the plants needed her—that was real love, but it was the love of tending, of stewardship, of a duty she had chosen and that chose her back only in the mute vegetable way of all growing things. This was different. Out there, at the end of a course line drawn from a dying transmitter, were people who had put their voices into the dark and said *any station* and meant *please*. Eleven years she had broadcast her beacon pulse into nothing and gotten nothing back, and she had made her peace with it, had even built a philosophy on it—*the garden is the message; the answering is not required*—and now, in the cruelest possible irony, the answer had come, and it was a distress call, and it was addressed to her.

*Any station.* She was the any station. She was, as far as she knew, the only *any* in existence.

She fell asleep smiling, which frightened her more than the dark did.

* * *

Day six. The turn.

Halfway points have a tyranny all their own: the moment when going back costs exactly as much as going forward, when retreat stops being an option and becomes merely a slower form of arrival. Gideon flipped the Mule end-over-end with a burn that pressed her into her harness, and when the rotation settled, the sun was behind her and the destination ahead, and she checked the nav solution against the decrypted coordinates for perhaps the fortieth time.

Still empty. The chart showed nothing at those coordinates—no marker, no hazard flag, no notation of any kind. Just the smooth printed absence of official disinterest. She'd cross-referenced every archive she could reach from the Mule's limited uplink, and the result was always the same: the region was listed as *unremarkable*. Two centuries of Conclave astronomy, and the patch of sky she was falling toward had been catalogued as nothing worth seeing.

Which was, she was beginning to understand, the most remarkable thing about it.

She spent the seventh day rehearsing. This was absurd and she knew it—there was no script for *hello, I'm the gardener, I received your distress call, also your existence is a state secret and I've committed approximately nine kinds of treason to reach you*—but she rehearsed anyway, mutting greetings in three languages, testing the acoustics of her own courage. In Meridian, the formal greeting of the ark generation: *We see your fire. We come toward it.* In Conclave Standard, the flat procedural version: *This is Station Verdance responding to your transmission. State your status.* She liked the first one better. She suspected, given what she was beginning to guess about who these people were and what had been done to them, that the second one might get her shot at an airlock.

Day eight, the long-range scope picked it up.

At first she thought it was an error—a smudge of return where the chart promised clean vacuum, a ghost in the optics. She recalibrated. Ran the sweep again. The smudge resolved by degrees as the hours passed, the way a ship resolves, the way anything real resolves: reluctantly, then all at once.

It was big.

That was her first coherent thought, and it was inadequate, and she knew it was inadequate even as she thought it, because *big* was a word for stations and she was not looking at a station. Stations had spines and solar arrays and the tidy geometry of things built by committees. This had scars. Even at extreme range, even as a gray suggestion against the black, she could see the hull was wrong in the specific way that only very old damage is wrong—craters micrometeorite-fused into glassy craters, a gash along the ventral spine that some engineer had sealed with a weld-bead visible from a thousand kilometers, drive nacelles that had clearly been shut down cold for longer than Gideon had been alive. It drifted with the slow, helpless tumble of something that had stopped choosing its orientation a very long time ago.

And along its flank, catching the distant sun in a band of faded paint or inlay, ran characters.

She zoomed until the image degraded into noise, backed off until it sharpened, and read them, and her grandmother's voice rose in her blood singing the second verse, and Gideon said the translation aloud to the empty cockpit because someone had to:

"*Perpetua.*"

The word hung in the cockpit. Perpetual. Everlasting. Someone had named this wounded, freezing, tumbling hulk *forever*, and the universe had spent two centuries testing whether they meant it.

The Mule's proximity alert chimed, soft and stupid, announcing what her eyes already knew: *object ahead, closing velocity nominal, recommend course adjustment.* She didn't adjust. She watched the ark swell in the canopy by millimeters, by meters, by kilometers, and felt her fear transmute—not vanish, nothing so clean, but transmute—into the focused, humming intensity she usually only felt with a scalpel in her hand and a graft failing under her fingers. The fear of the open dark was still there, would always be there, but it had been overtaken by something with better traction.

Somewhere inside that wreck were pods. Thousands of them, if the archive fragments were honest. People. Frozen people, in a ship whose power signature, even at this range, flickered like a candle in a doorway.

*Please still be burning,* she thought, and did not specify whether she meant the reactor or the people, because she suspected it was the same question.

She keyed the thrusters for final approach and opened a channel on the old Meridian emergency bands, the frequencies her grandmother's songs had been born on, and spoke into the microphone with her heart going like a bird in a closed room.

"This is Station Verdance." Her voice cracked; she steadied it. "We see your fire. We come toward it."

The dark answered with static.

Then—faint, broken, but unmistakably a voice, unmistakably *alive*—the static answered back.
