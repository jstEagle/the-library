## 6. Perpetua

The dark out here had a texture.

Gideon had never known that before. Inside the Verdance, darkness was a thing you made—drew the shutters over the grow-lamps at night cycle, let the tomatoes and the sleeping ferns rest in it. Darkness with a purpose. But this, four days outbound from the station with the skiff's running lights damped to almost nothing, was darkness as a substance, as a country you crossed. It pressed against the viewport like deep water. It made her aware of her own heartbeat, of the small wet sounds of her own breathing, of how much noise a single living body made in a universe that had apparently stopped listening.

She talked anyway. She couldn't help it.

"Four days," she told the skiff's cabin, to the sample vials rattling in their case, to the dried sprig of meridian lavender she'd tucked into the console's seam for luck because she was alone and no one would know. "Four days and I'm talking to cut flowers. Grandmother would say that's how it starts."

Her grandmother had said other things too, in Old Meridian, in the low singsong she used only for the old songs: *the ships that went out and the ship that never came home.* Gideon had thought it was a lullaby about the sea, some drowned-world memory from before the Collapse. She was beginning to suspect it wasn't.

The coordinates from the decrypted signal put the target in a slow, wide orbit beyond the system's outer gas giant—a region the charts labeled, with bureaucratic finality, *NON-VIABLE SURVEY ZONE — NO ASSETS*. No assets. She'd flown through Conclave space her whole posting life, and she'd never once seen those words on a chart. Space was always an asset. Space was always claimed, taxed, logged. A hole in the ledger that big wasn't an oversight. It was a door someone had welded shut.

The skiff's passive array had been whispering for six hours now. Not the beacon pulse—that clean, patient tone she'd maintained for eleven years, the one nobody answered. This was different. Ragged. A carrier wave so degraded it was less a signal than a scar of one, repeating on a cycle so long she'd nearly missed it between sleep shifts.

She'd run the analysis nine times. The modulation matched Old Meridian phonemes. Someone—or something—was still saying words out here, in the dead tongue, in the dark.

"Almost there," she said, and the lavender didn't answer, and neither did anything else.

* * *

She saw it first as an absence.

That was the strange part, and later she would try to explain it to people who hadn't been there, and they would nod and not understand. You'd expect a ship—a ship ten thousand souls large—to announce itself. To catch light. But the Perpetua had been out here so long that the dark had gotten into it. It didn't reflect; it absorbed. What Gideon saw, when she finally swung the skiff's floodlamps across it, was a hole in the starfield shaped like a promise broken.

Then the lamps found the hull, and she stopped breathing.

It was vast. Vaster than the Verdance by an order she couldn't compute at first glance—her eye kept trying to scale it down, kept insisting it was a rock, a reef, a ridge of mountains someone had rolled into a cylinder and set adrift. Its skin was gray not from paint but from time: micrometeorite scarring laid down in layers, like bark, like the rings of a felled tree. Whole panels had been burned away and never replaced. Along its flank ran a seam of exposed framework where something had torn free long ago—an engine section? a docking ring?—and the wound had frozen open, jagged metal curled back like petals.

And on the hull, stenciled in letters two hundred meters tall, worn but legible under her floodlamps:

**PERPETUA**

Beneath it, in smaller script, in characters she knew the way you know a face from childhood photographs—the script of her grandmother's songs, the script of the distress signal:

*We were promised the same dawn.*

Gideon sat very still in the pilot's seat. Her hands had gone cold on the yoke.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, because the cabin needed to hear a voice, any voice, even hers. "That's not—there was one ark. There was one ark and the garden and the colony contract. That's the whole story."

But the story was written on the hull in front of her, in a language the Conclave had declared extinct, and it was promising dawn to somebody.

* * *

Docking took three hours because everything took three hours when your hands shook.

The Perpetua's dorsal access port still answered to hardline contact—she felt the shudder through the skiff's frame when the magnetic collar caught, a sound like a held breath finally released. Her suit lamps swept the airlock interior: frost-grown walls, dead consoles, a drift of particulate that hung motionless in zero-g like a held snowfall. The air inside, when she cracked her visor for a taste, was old. Stale the way a sealed room is stale, but not rotten. Not dead. Scrubbers somewhere were still turning over, running on fumes, keeping a body's worth of atmosphere alive in a hull built for thousands.

Someone had been maintaining this ship. Recently enough that the scrubbers hadn't quit.

She pulled herself along the main spine corridor, gloves squeaking on handrails slick with rime, and the ship spoke to her in small ways. A light panel flickering amber instead of red—amber meant degraded, amber meant trying. A door that opened on manual override after a pause, reluctantly, like an old man rising from a chair. And everywhere, everywhere, the script. Meridian glyphs etched into bulkheads, painted on floor plates, stitched into a banner hanging limp in a junction bay: *Perpetua — Second Fleet Ark — Manifest 10,412.*

Ten thousand four hundred and twelve.

She stopped in the junction bay and hung there in the frozen snowfall-dust and read the banner three times, because the number refused to mean what it said.

There had been one ark. The Concordance. Twelve thousand colonists, the founding generation, the ones whose descendants now lived—or were supposed to live—on the world below. Every child learned it. One ark, one garden, one beacon, one covenant. The Conclave's histories were very clear. The Conclave's histories were very clear about everything.

So what was a second ark doing with a manifest bigger than the first?

* * *

The cryo-bay doors were tall as cathedral gates, and they were warm.

Not warm-warm. But above freezing, which after the frost of the corridors felt like stepping into spring. Her suit read the ambient at four degrees Celsius and climbing by fractions, and beneath it, faint as a pulse under skin, she heard the sound.

A hum. Layered. Rhythmic. Thousands of small machines breathing in imperfect unison.

Her lamps rose along rows of pods that receded into the dark overhead and away into the dark beyond, tier upon tier, a cliff face of glass and frosted steel. Each pod held a figure. She could see them through the ice-fogged lids: faces, hands, the soft shapes of shoulders and hips under thermal sheeting. Women. Men. Children. A child no older than eight, curled on her side, lashes furred with frost, mouth slightly open as if mid-question.

Gideon drifted down the central aisle with her hand trailing along the pods, touching each one, though she couldn't have said why. Habit, maybe. The way she touched soil. The way she touched seedlings, checking for life.

"Hello," she whispered, absurdly, to a pod holding an old man with a white beard and a tattooed wrist. "Hello. I'm here."

The diagnostic panel beside him glowed a weak green. Status: STABLE. Power reserve: 11%.

Eleven percent.

She checked the next pod. Stable. Nine percent. The next. Fourteen. The next—status: CRITICAL, power reserve: 3%, and the face behind the glass was a teenage boy with a healing scar on his eyebrow, and the panel beside him blinked its slow red blink like a heart deciding whether to keep going.

She followed the aisle and the numbers fell as she went, tier by tier, row by row, a tide going out. Pods near the spine, near whatever residual generators still turned, held their charges at ten, twelve, fifteen percent. Pods at the far reaches of the bay showed zeros. Not stable. Not critical. Just zeros, and behind their glass, faces that had gone smooth and waxy in the way faces do when the machines stop asking questions of them.

She counted the zeros until she couldn't anymore. Then she stopped counting and hung in the aisle of the dead and the dying and pressed her forehead against the cold glass of a pod marked with a name she couldn't read through the frost, and she wept into her helmet, silently, the way you learn to weep when you've spent eleven years where no one can hear you.

* * *

It took her most of a day to find the engineering deck, and longer to understand what she was seeing there, and when she understood it she wished she hadn't.

The Perpetua's reactors were dead. Both of them—scavenged, she realized, tracing the empty cradles and severed conduits with a growing coldness that had nothing to do with the ship's temperature. Removed. Cut out with care, with proper tooling, with Conclave-standard salvage protocols she recognized because the same protocols governed every supply crate that ever reached her station. The fuel cells that remained were auxiliary units, emergency-rated, designed to hold a ship's systems for weeks. They had been holding ten thousand lives for two hundred years.

Two centuries of triage. Two centuries of cannibalizing one section to feed another, of shutting down decks to warm the pods, of engineers—generations of them, waking in shifts, dying at their posts, training successors—spending their lives buying hours for strangers asleep in glass. She found their logbook in the engineering office, a physical ledger, paper, because paper doesn't need power. Hundreds of entries in Meridian script, then in colonial standard as the generations lost the old tongue, then in both, then in a shorthand of numbers and arrows that needed no language at all.

The last entry was recent. Days old. It matched the distress signal's cycle exactly.

*Aux cells at 6%. Cannot hold all bays past next quarter-cycle. We have begun letting the outer tiers go. Forgive us. We have begun choosing.*

*We have begun choosing.*

Gideon closed the ledger with hands that would not stop trembling, and the horror arrived all at once, the way floodwater arrives when the dam goes—not gradually, not in pieces, but whole.

They hadn't lost this ship. You don't lose a second ark. You don't lose ten thousand people. The manifest was in the archives; the salvage crews had come with manifests and manifests are checked against ledgers; the reactor removal was logged, signed, stamped with the Terraforming Office seal—the same seal on her own commission papers, the same seal on the supply crates that fed her garden.

Her garden.

The Verdance grew cultivars she'd never been able to trace in any seed registry. Gene-lines the Conclave had simply *provided*, generation-stable, miraculous, with paperwork that explained nothing. She'd stopped asking years ago. Good soil was good soil. Green things growing was green things growing.

She stood in the dead engineering deck of a murdered ship, surrounded by the evidence of two hundred years of deliberate, documented abandonment, and understood that every tomato she had ever grown had been planted in stolen ground.

"They didn't lose you," she said to the empty deck, to the ledger, to the ten thousand sleeping faces she could not see from here but could feel, the way you feel weather coming. "They left you. They took your engines and wrote you out of the books and left you out here to go dark slowly, slowly, so slowly no one would ever have to hear it happen."

Her voice broke on the last word and echoed anyway, because the ship was built to carry voices, built to carry ten thousand heartbeats, and it had been waiting two hundred years for anyone at all to speak in it.

* * *

She stayed three days.

She hadn't planned to. The skiff had consumables for a week and protocol said return immediately, file report, await instruction—but protocol belonged to the Conclave, and the Conclave had signed the ledger entries she'd found in the administrative archive, the ones stamped with dates and authorization codes, the ones that used phrases like *asset redistribution* and *manifest reduction approved* and, in one memorandum she would remember for the rest of her life, in flawless bureaucratic Meridian: *the second population is not to be recorded as extant.*

Not dead. Not lost. *Not extant.* As if existence itself were a clerical matter, revocable by stamp.

So she stayed, because leaving felt like abandoning them twice. She mapped the bays. She counted the living and the zeros and the criticals and built, in her agronomy software, a triage model she hated herself for needing. She jury-rigged a power tap from the skiff's cells into the nearest failing tier and watched, with a fierce stupid joy that made her dizzy, as three panels ticked from red to amber. Three pods. Three people. It was nothing. It was everything.

On the third night-shift, drifting in the cryo-bay with the hum all around her like a congregation murmuring, she stopped beside the pod of the little girl with the question-shaped mouth, and she read the name plate through the frost, sounding out the Meridian syllables the way her grandmother had taught her, letter by letter, song by song.

*Sefa-vari Anselm. Age 8 at immersion.*

Eight years old when they froze her. Two hundred years ago. Which meant somewhere aboard, if the generations of waking engineers had kept records—and they had, she'd found them, crates of them—there might be people who remembered the world before. Who remembered the schism. Who remembered why.

Gideon rested her palm flat against the pod's glass, over the child's small frost-rimed hand.

"I'm going to come back," she said. "I don't know how yet. I don't know what it costs. But I'm going to come back with help, or I'm going to come back with a plan, or I'm going to come back and I'm going to wake every single one of you myself, one at a time, for the rest of my life. I promise. Gardener's word."

The pod hummed. The boy with the scarred eyebrow blinked his slow red blink. Somewhere in the tiers, another zero quietly became permanent, and Gideon felt it pass through her like a stone dropping into deep water.

She pushed off toward the airlock, toward the skiff, toward the long bright lie of the Verdance waiting four days home—and behind her, in the frozen snowfall-dust of the junction bay, the banner swayed once in the ventilation current and hung still.

*We were promised the same dawn.*

The skiff's flight computer chimed softly as she undocked, logging her departure, timestamping her approach vector, auto-composing its routine telemetry packet for Conclave relay.

She didn't notice.
