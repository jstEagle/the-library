## 7. The Voice in the Pods

The pod's status light was the color of an old bruise—amber going brown at the edges—and Gideon had been staring at it for eleven minutes without moving.

She had told herself she would only look. That was the bargain she'd struck with herself somewhere in the long burn out from the Verdance, hands white-knuckled on the skiff's yoke while the outer dark pressed against the canopy like a held breath: *you will find the ship, you will confirm the signal's origin, and then you will turn around and file nothing and tell no one.* A clean reconnaissance. A surveyor's errand. She was, after all, still technically a Conclave employee, badge number T-4471, Terraforming Office, Station Verdance, sole occupant.

But now she stood on the Perpetua's medical deck with her helmet lamp shaking slightly against the ceiling, and the pod in front of her was dying, and looking away had stopped being a thing her body knew how to do.

* * *

She had spent four hours aboard before she found this deck.

The ark was a wound of a ship. Her first sight of it through the skiff's scope had been almost beautiful—a kilometer and a half of hull turning slowly in the starless cold, frost-silvered, its flanks scored by micrometeorite streams that looked from a distance like calligraphy. Then she'd closed to two hundred meters and seen the scorch pattern across the stern: not damage. *Deliberate* damage. Someone had cut the main drive couplings with shaped charges, cleanly, the way a butcher severs a spine. The Perpetua hadn't broken down. It had been *stopped.*

Inside, the corridors were dark but not dead. Emergency power crawled through the walls in thin amber veins, enough to keep the cryo decks breathing, barely, the way a candle keeps a room from being a grave. Gideon had drifted through the dark with her suit lights sweeping over bulkheads marked in Old Meridian script—the same flowing characters from the distress signal, the same ones from her grandmother's lullabies, *sleep little seed, sleep till the rain*—and every meter of it had felt like trespass and homecoming at once.

Ten thousand pods. She'd counted the racks herself because she hadn't believed the manifest. Row after row after row, floor to ceiling, receding into the dark like orchard lines, each one holding a person folded into frosted glass. Some pods showed green. Most showed amber. A heartbreaking number showed nothing at all, their readouts flatlined decades ago, their occupants beyond even the mercy of knowing they'd been left.

Power failure curves projected on her wrist display: forty years of margin remaining, if nothing else went wrong. Forty years for ten thousand sleeping people who were supposed to have died two centuries ago—or worse, who were supposed never to have existed at all.

Gideon had sat down on the deck plating between two pod rows and cried into her helmet until her sinuses ached, and no one heard her, and that was somehow the worst part.

Then she'd wiped her face, checked her oxygen, and started working the problem, because grief was a luxury and she was, whatever else she was, a station keeper. Things that failed got diagnosed. That was the whole of her religion.

* * *

The pod in front of her now belonged to a woman whose vitals readout was degrading faster than the others—not catastrophically, not tonight, but within months, maybe weeks. Pod 7-114. The name field rendered in Meridian script; the translation overlay in Gideon's wrist display stuttered through it twice before settling:

**SEFA OKONKWO-VASHTI. AGE AT FREEZING: 71. STATUS: PRIORITY WAKE — MEDICAL.**

Priority wake. The system itself was begging for someone to take her out.

Gideon crouched beside the pod and rested her gauntleted palm against the glass. Through the frost, the woman's face was calm, deeply lined, her white hair floating loose in the cryo medium like a halo that had given up trying to be respectable. She looked like someone who had laughed a lot before the freezing. She looked like nobody Gideon had ever met, because everyone Gideon had ever met fit inside a single station and a single bureaucracy and a single sanctioned genome registry.

"You're dying," Gideon said aloud, and her voice sounded obscene in the vast quiet of the medical deck. "And I don't know if waking you kills you faster."

The diagnostic sub-menu answered her anyway, the way machines do when humans won't. BRIEF REVIVAL PROTOCOL AVAILABLE. WINDOW: 20–40 MINUTES. SURVIVAL PROBABILITY OF RETURN TO CRYOSTASIS: 61 PERCENT AND FALLING. RECOMMENDATION LINE BLINKED, PATIENT, IN AMBER: **WAKE SOON OR NOT AT ALL.**

Sixty-one percent. Gideon turned the number over. If she did nothing, the woman died in her sleep sometime this season, alone in the dark, two hundred years from anywhere, and no one would ever have heard her voice. If Gideon woke her, there was nearly a two-in-five chance Gideon would be the instrument of her death instead.

But there was a chance—one—that Sefa Okonkwo-Vashti would get twenty minutes of being alive again. Twenty minutes of warmth and light and another human face. And there was a certainty, absolute as gravity, that whatever she said in those twenty minutes was information ten thousand people might need.

Gideon thought about the beacon. Eleven years of pulses into the void, *anyone receiving, anyone receiving,* and the Conclave's cheerful assurance that the silence was normal, expected, a matter of orbital mechanics and patience. She thought about the drive couplings cut clean as surgery. She thought about the word buried three layers deep in the signal's header, the one her translation layer kept flagging as low-confidence because it appeared nowhere in her sanctioned lexicons, and which the archived keys had finally resolved at 3 a.m. two days ago, making her drop a mug of tea in the Verdance's galley:

***ERASED.***

"Okay," Gideon whispered. "Okay. Hold on."

* * *

The revival took nineteen minutes, most of which Gideon spent learning the pod's interface by trial and error, swearing softly in three languages, and once—in genuine desperation—praying to no one in particular, which was new for her.

The warming cycle ran slow. Frost retreated across the glass in shrinking continents. The woman's vitals climbed out of their glacial trough degree by degree, heart rate stuttering up from six beats a minute toward something human, and Gideon monitored each step with her own pulse hammering in her ears, ready to abort at the first sign of cascade failure, rehearsing the emergency re-freeze sequence under her breath like a psalm.

At minute seventeen, the pod hissed. The lid seals released with a sound like a long exhale, and warm fog rolled out across the deck, smelling of antiseptic and something older, faintly sweet—preservative gel and, beneath it, impossibly, the ghost of lavender.

Sefa Okonkwo-Vashti opened her eyes.

They were dark eyes, deep-set, clouded at first with two centuries of ice, and they found Gideon's face through her open visor with a slowness that was not confusion. It was *assessment.* Even half-dead, even thawed after two hundred years, the old woman looked at her the way a magistrate looks at a witness.

"Well," Sefa said. Her voice was gravel and paper, cracked from disuse, but the words came clear, in accented but fluent Standard. "Either I'm finally dreaming, or they sent someone young." A pause. A flicker of something sharp moving behind the cloudiness. "You're not wearing Justice colors. So. Dreaming, then."

"My name is Gideon Vasquez." Her own voice came out strange, too loud. "I keep a station in the inner system. The Verdance. I—I received your signal. The distress transmission. I traced it here."

Something moved through the old woman's face—hope arriving like weather, sudden and enormous and painful. Her hand, blue-veined and trembling, rose from the pod and caught Gideon's wrist with a grip far stronger than it had any right to be.

"The signal went out?" she rasped. "It reached *someone?*"

"It reached me."

"How many others?"

Gideon hesitated. "Just me. I'm sorry. I came alone, against protocol. There's—" She swallowed. "There isn't anyone else coming. Not yet."

The grip on her wrist didn't loosen. If anything it tightened, and Sefa pulled herself up out of the pod's cradle by degrees, gasping, refusing help until she'd gotten her back against the raised lid and could sit upright, wrapped in the thermal blanket Gideon fetched from the skiff's stores. Up close, in the light, she was smaller than she'd seemed behind the glass—bird-boned, ancient—but her eyes had cleared to a fierce wet black, and they did not let go of Gideon's face.

"Alone," Sefa repeated. "Of course alone. They'd have made sure of that." She coughed, wet and long, and Gideon knelt to steady her, and the old woman let her, this time. "Child. Listen to me. My time is short—I can feel the cold waiting to take me back, or take me altogether, and either way we haven't got long. So I'm going to say the things that matter, and you're going to listen, and when I'm done you're going to decide what kind of person you are. Do you understand?"

"I think so."

"No, you don't. But you will." Sefa drew a breath that shuddered all the way down. "Ask me. Ask me why we're here. You've been asking yourself since you saw this ship. I can see it all over you. Ask."

Gideon's mouth was dry. "Why are you here? The records say the Perpetua was lost with all hands during the Crossing. Two hundred years ago. A tragedy. There's a memorial day."

That produced the thing Gideon would remember for the rest of her life: a laugh. Dry, bitter, genuinely amused, rattling up out of the old woman's chest like a stone rolling downhill.

"A memorial day." Sefa shook her head slowly. "Oh, they were thorough. We didn't sink, child. We weren't lost. Look around you." Her hand swept, trembling, at the endless dark ranks of pods. "Look at us. Ten thousand souls, and every one of us put aboard this ship *on purpose*, by the people who built your pretty garden station and wrote your history books and taught you that memorial song."

"They put you here to—to preserve you. For the colony—"

"To *store* us." The word cracked like a whip. "Like seed stock. Like a *reserve.*" Sefa's breath hitched; she pressed on anyway, riding the anger the way a sailor rides a squall. "You've seen the genetic registries. Of course you have—you work for the Terraforming Office, they make you recite them in your sleep. Tell me what the Conclave calls a genome variance above threshold Class Three."

Gideon's blood went cold, because she did know. Every station keeper knew. "Genetically redundant."

"*Redundant.*" Sefa savored the word like poison. "Dissidents first. Anyone who marched, who published, who refused the Unity Accords when the arks were loading. Teachers. Lawyers. Half the artists on two worlds. And then the rest—the sick, the old, the divergent, anyone whose bloodline didn't fit the template they'd drawn up for their perfect little colony. Ten thousand of us. They couldn't kill us outright—not with the whole population watching the evacuation. Too much grief makes people ask questions. So they gave us berths on a second ark. Promised us we'd be woken on arrival. Promised us land, shares, a future." Her voice dropped to something terrible and quiet. "And then they towed us out past the heliopause, cut our drives, blacked us from every ledger, and told the survivors of the first ark that we'd died in transit. That they should grieve, and plant their gardens, and be grateful."

The medical deck was very silent. Somewhere far off in the ship's bones, a coolant pump ticked through its failing rhythm, patient as a clock.

Gideon became aware, distantly, that she was shaking.

"The Verdance," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "The terraforming program. The soil cultures, the gene-bank, the—the biomass feedstock. Where did the Conclave source the original cultivar base?"

Sefa looked at her for a long moment, and in the old woman's eyes there was no satisfaction at all, only a sorrow so worn and old it had gone smooth as river stone.

"Now you've understood," she said gently. "Yes. Everything. Our seed libraries. Our tissue archives. Four hundred years of agricultural science from two homeworlds, carried out of the fire by people who believed it belonged to *everyone*—confiscated at the loading docks along with us. Your garden grows on top of our graves, child. Every harvest you ever logged, every crop you ever coaxed out of that red dirt—they ate the fruit of what they stole from the people they erased." She reached out and, with surprising tenderness, touched Gideon's cheek. "You didn't know. I can see you didn't know. That's not your sin. But it is your inheritance, and what you do with it—that's yours to choose."

Gideon couldn't speak. Something in her chest had come apart quietly, some load-bearing wall she hadn't known was structural. Eleven years. Eleven years of tending plants grown from stolen seeds, logging growth cycles for colonists who slept above a lie, singing her grandmother's songs in a language the Conclave had told her was extinct—*extinct,* when its speakers were frozen in the dark a hundred astronomical units away, deliberately drowned in silence.

Her grandmother. Old Meridian. The songs. The Conclave hadn't just erased these people. They'd let the language survive in nurseries and kitchens, harmless, sentimental, a folk curiosity—while its last living community drifted unrecorded through the outer dark.

"Sefa," Gideon managed. "Your pod. The diagnostics say—if I put you back under, there's a risk. If I leave you awake, your body may not hold. I don't—I don't know how to choose, I'm not a doctor, I—"

"Put me back." No hesitation. None. "Wake the others first. The engineers, the medics, the young ones. I'm seventy-one years thawed-out of a hundred and nine; my heart has done its arithmetic already." She caught Gideon's hand again, and pressed something into it—small, cold, metallic. A data chit, old-pattern, its casing etched in Meridian script. "My full testimony. Recorded the week before the freezing, when we still thought the truth might save us. Everything: names, dates, the orders, the signatures. It's all here. I've waited two hundred years to give it to someone who wasn't ice."

Gideon closed her fingers around the chit. It burned cold against her palm like a coal from the wrong side of a fire.

"Why give it to me?" she whispered. "You don't know me. I could be anyone. I could be one of *them.*"

"I watched your face for five minutes," Sefa said. "Nobody lies that badly." The ghost of the laugh again, fading. "Besides. You came alone, against protocol, across the dark, for strangers. That's the oldest kind of courage there is. Don't waste it."

The pod's systems chimed softly—vitals drifting, thermal envelope decaying, the machine politely insisting on a decision. Sefa's breathing had gone shallow. She was fading in front of Gideon's eyes, and still her gaze stayed level, patient, certain.

"Re-freeze me," she said. "And then go home to your garden, Gideon Vasquez, and decide what it's *for.*"

* * *

The return to cryostasis took eleven minutes. Gideon performed every step with hands that only shook a little, and when the frost began reclaiming the glass, Sefa Okonkwo-Vashti looked at her through it until the very end—until the ice took her expression mid-smile, calm and unafraid, a woman keeping faith with the future.

Afterward Gideon stood alone on the medical deck for a long time, the data chit in her fist, listening to ten thousand hearts beat at six beats a minute in the dark.

Then she keyed her wrist display, opened a channel to the Verdance's long-range array, and began, very quietly, to plan how to steal a miracle.

Behind her, unnoticed, the skiff's flight computer finished compressing its navigation logs and queued them for automatic upload to the Conclave's Terraforming Office—standard procedure, mandatory reporting, due to transmit the moment the skiff re-entered beacon range.

Three days early, and none the wiser, Gideon undocked from the ghost ship and turned for home.
