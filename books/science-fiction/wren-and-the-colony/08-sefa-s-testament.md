## 8. Sefa's Testament

*Personal log, Sefa Okonkwo-Vale, Elder of the Meridian Compact. Recording initiated in the hour before scheduled cryostasis, Perpetua, deep-system drift. If you are hearing this, then either the Compact survived to hear it, or someone else did. Either way: listen.*

*I am one hundred and four years old by the old calendar, and I am about to lie down in a box of ice because the world we built could not hold both its conscience and its children. Let me begin where all confessions begin — with what we were given.*

* * *

*Before the Collapse, Earth was not a myth. It was a memory. I was born in the last decade of the Long Evacuation, in a launch cradle above what had been Lagos, and my mother held me up to a viewport so I could see the planet burning below us — the brown smear of the equatorial fires, the white scars of the flooded coasts. She said, "Remember that it was beautiful once." I have spent a hundred years trying to remember, and I have learned that remembering is a kind of work, and that most people would rather not do it.*

*Eleven arks left the cradle. Eleven ships carrying what remained of us — some nine hundred thousand souls, if the manifests were honest, and they were not, but that is a later part of this confession. We were not explorers. Do not let anyone tell you we were pilgrims or pioneers or any of the pretty words. We were refugees with engines. We fled a house that was on fire, and we argued the whole way out the door about who had started it.*

*The voyage took ninety-one years. Three generations lived and died inside hulls, and something happened to us in that dark that no one planned and no one admitted until it was too late to undo. We stopped being passengers and became two peoples.*

*It did not happen in a day. It happened in committee rooms, which is where most catastrophes are born. The arks had been funded by different blocs, governed by different charters, and for the first decades we traded between ships like neighbors over fences — seed stock, machine parts, music, marriages. My parents met on a transfer shuttle between the Perpetua and the Halcyon Dawn; my father used to say he fell in love across forty meters of vacuum and never regretted the distance. But as the generations turned, the ships drifted apart in more than trajectory. The Verdant Charter fleet — five arks, the ones that would become the Conclave's ancestors — adopted what they called Stewardship Doctrine: strict genetic curation, rationed births, a single approved cultural canon. They called it discipline. They called it survival. And I will grant them this much, because a fair confession must be fair: their way worked. Their ships ran clean. Their crops failed less often. Their children were healthy and obedient and terribly, terribly quiet.*

*The other six of us — the Compact fleet — chose differently. We kept the old chaos. Open birth registries, open archives, open arguments. Our ships were louder, messier, poorer in some materials and richer in others. We preserved languages the Charter fleets had voted to retire. We kept religious traditions they had ruled inefficient. We kept the sick and the old alive past the point where Stewardship accounting said they were worth the oxygen. My grandmother was such a case — ninety-six years, blind, beloved, consuming resources that a Charter auditor once calculated aloud at a joint council meeting as "a deficit of eleven point three person-years." My grandmother laughed at him. She had a laugh like a door swinging open. She said, "Young man, when you can tell me exactly which person-year bought you your soul, I'll start keeping accounts."*

*That is the schism in miniature, if you want it. Not doctrine. Arithmetic. One half of humanity decided that a life had to justify itself, and the other half decided that justification was beside the point. Everything that came after — the Collapse of trust, the Cold Centuries, the erasure — grew from that single root like everything grows from a single root. I was a gardener's daughter before I was anything else. I know how roots work.*

* * *

*The Cold Centuries lasted thirty years. No shots fired between the fleets — I want that understood, because the Conclave's histories, if they still exist, will tell you there was a war. There was no war. There was a slow freezing of every channel between us. Trade ended first. Then shared infrastructure — the relay network went Charter-only, and our ships went quiet on their frequencies, talking among ourselves in the dark. Then the councils stopped meeting. By the time I was grown, a child born on the Halcyon Dawn might live her whole life without speaking to a soul from the Verdant Charter except through an airlock intercom, formal and cold as a customs declaration.*

*And then came the Reconciliation Congress — oh, the irony of that name sits in my chest like a stone even now. Year sixty-four of the voyage. The Charter fleet proposed, at last, reunification. Full merger of the fleets, shared governance, one human future. We celebrated. I was twenty-two, and I remember dancing in the Perpetua's central ring while the announcement played on loop, and an old man named Tomas wept into his beer and said, "I didn't think I'd live to see it."*

*We should have read the terms. That is the second lesson of this confession, and I deliver it free of charge to whoever is listening: always read the terms. The Charter's proposal was not a marriage. It was an absorption. Under the unified charter they drafted, the Compact fleets would submit to Stewardship Doctrine within ten years — genetic curation, archive consolidation ("for preservation," they said, meaning removal), and the phased retirement of "non-viable cultural practices." Non-viable. As though a language were a failing crop. As though a song were a defective gene. The vote was scheduled for year sixty-seven. We had three years to decide whether to stop being ourselves.*

*We voted no. All six Compact fleets, overwhelming majorities. And I want to be precise here, because precision is the last dignity available to the erased: we did not vote for separation. We voted for continued coexistence — separate charters, restored trade, shared destination. We offered them their own proposal back, minus the swallowing. It was the Conclave's founding generation who could not tolerate a "no." A no meant we were not theirs. A no meant their arithmetic had failed somewhere, that there existed human beings who had counted the cost of Stewardship and declined to pay it. They could forgive us almost anything except that.*

*What followed took another twenty years, and it was conducted entirely in paperwork. I mean that literally. There were no marines, no boarding actions, no burning hulls. There were audits. There were safety inspections that found our older ships "structurally non-compliant for continued habitation" — inspections performed by Charter engineers who had never set foot aboard us. There were insurance rulings that made transfer shuttles between the fleets prohibitively expensive. There was a fuel allocation formula, revised quietly in year seventy-nine, that gave the Compact fleets eighty-two percent of what our trajectories required and dared us to complain, because complaining would mean admitting we were being starved, and admitting that would mean acknowledging we still existed.*

*By year eighty-five, six of the eleven arks were dying of bookkeeping. Our power margins thinned. Our recycling systems aged past replacement. We cannibalized, we improvised, we taught our children engineering as a survival prayer. And in year eighty-eight, the Charter councils made us the offer they had been building toward all along: evacuation. Relocation of all Compact populations to the five Charter arks — as assisted migrants. Registered. Curated. Assimilated under Stewardship Doctrine, our archives "consolidated," our birth registries merged into theirs, our names entered into their ledgers as a footnote category: Incorporated Populations, Pre-Charter Origin.*

*My mother stood up at the final joint assembly — she was eighty then, small and straight-backed, a woman who had crossed the whole of the dark — and she asked the question I have carried ever since. She said: "If we come aboard your ships and enter your ledgers, will our grandchildren know there was ever another way to be human?" And First Minister-designate Aldous Ren, ancestor of the man whose signature I found on a certain document, looked at her with perfect courtesy and said, "Madam, there is only one way to be human. We are offering you a place in it."*

*That was the moment I understood we were already dead in their eyes. Everything after was just the burial.*

* * *

*So we refused. And because we refused, they made us disappear.*

*Not kill us — I need you to hold the distinction, because the distinction is the crime. Killing leaves bodies. Killing leaves grief, and grief leaves records, and records leave questions. What the Conclave did was cleaner. In year ninety-one of the voyage, as the fleet approached this system and the colonization phase began, the Charter councils published the Official Fleet Manifest — the document that would become the founding archive of the new colony, the ledger against which all future history would be written. Six arks appeared in it as lost. Lost with all hands, the entry reads, in transit casualties, years eighty-three through ninety. Structural failures. Reactor accidents. Unfortunate, tragic, mourned — and closed. No investigation recommended. No survivors listed. No descendants accounted for.*

*Nine hundred thousand people became a rounding error. My parents, my grandmother with her door-swinging laugh, Tomas and his beer, the children born in the Perpetua's ring during the Cold Centuries who had never seen a sky — all of us filed under lost, and the file sealed, and the seal countersigned by every minister who would go on to build the Conclave's bright and orderly world.*

*They did not destroy us because we were dangerous. Understand that. We were dying anyway; they had seen to that with formulas and fees. They destroyed the record because a colony cannot be founded on a schism. Because the story they needed — the story your history books tell, the story of one humanity, united, fleeing together, building together — required that there never have been another way. We were not erased out of hatred. We were erased out of narrative convenience. That is somehow worse. Hatred at least admits you matter.*

*The Perpetua was the last to go dark. We had scrounged and saved enough power for one choice, and the Compact council chose it in the open, with every family voting: we would not scatter as beggars into the Charter fleet. We would seal our ships and sleep, all of us, ten thousand souls in this hull alone — the youngest, the hardiest, the ones chosen by lot and skill to carry the Compact forward — and we would set our drift toward the outer system, away from the Conclave's sensors, and wait. Power reserves calculated for two hundred years. Two hundred years for the Conclave to soften, to forget, to die of its own arithmetic. Two hundred years, and then we wake, and we knock politely on the door of the world, and we ask to be remembered.*

*Two hundred years. The reserves are failing early. I can read the numbers as well as anyone; the decay curves are steeper than the models promised, because the universe does not honor your projections. Whoever finds this log will find us either sleeping or gone, and either way, the confession stands.*

* * *

*A few things before I lie down, because a testament that is only grievance is only half a life.*

*We were not saints. I want that in the record too. The Compact fleets had our cruelties — hoarding during the lean decades, faction fights that broke families, a shameful period on the Halcyon Dawn when we nearly replicated the Charter's own curation logic out of pure fear. We were ordinary humans doing an extraordinary thing badly, the way humans do everything. If the Conclave's sin was arithmetic without mercy, ours was sometimes mercy without structure, and both fail, and both failed. I do not offer this as balance. I offer it as truth, because a people who return from erasure demanding to be written back into history as heroes have not understood the lesson of their own erasure. We want to be written back as people. Complicated, mistaken, stubborn, singing people.*

*Sing. Yes — I should leave the songs. Gideon, if it is you hearing this, if the gardener of the green ship has found her way out to the dark: your grandmother's songs are ours. Every one of them. The lullabies in Old Meridian, the planting songs, the keening for the dead — those came up out of the Compact fleets, out of kitchens and hydroponics bays where women like my mother sang to keep the children from hearing the pumps wheeze. The Conclave let the songs survive because songs seemed harmless, and then forgot whose they were. So you have been carrying us all along, gardener. You have been singing the dead awake without knowing it. I find that I am glad — glad past all reason — that it was you who came.*

*To whoever governs that world when this log surfaces: I make no demand. That will surprise you; the erased are supposed to rage. But rage is cheap and I am old, and I have watched my civilization shrink to the size of a recording. Here is what I ask instead. Ask your archives why the manifest says lost. Ask who signed the seal, and who countersigned it, and what their descendants govern today. Ask why the gene-banks of the new colony bloom with cultivars registered to ships that officially never existed. You will find, if you ask honestly, that the erasure was not an event. It is a maintenance program. Someone has been keeping us deleted, century after century, tending that deletion like a garden. Find the gardener. That is all. The rest follows the way water follows a slope.*

*One more thing. The pods. Ten thousand of them, and the power failing, and I know — I knew when we sealed the hatches — that not everyone will wake. The decay is not uniform; the forward sections will fail first. If you can save some of us, save the young ones first, and save the archive cores, and save the seed vaults in bay seven, because a people is not its elders. A people is its next planting. I say that as one of the elders, and I say it gladly. My whole long life was spent getting this ship to the edge of a future I will barely see. That was the deal we all made in the dark, and I keep it willingly.*

*The ice is cold already; I can feel it waiting for me like a bed made by someone who loves you. Strange, to be grateful to a coffin. Strange, to spend your last waking hour dictating to a darkness that may never open its ears. But my mother held me up to a viewport once and told me to remember that the world was beautiful, and I have done the remembering, and now I pass the work on.*

*Remember us. Not as victims — as the other half of the story. The half that counted lives differently. The half that sang in Old Meridian while the pumps wheezed. The half that said no.*

*End of log. Sefa Okonkwo-Vale, going down into the cold with her people. Wake us gently, if you wake us at all.*

*Recording ends.*

* * *

*Addendum, appended ninety seconds later, voice unsteady:*

*There is a light on the drift scope. Small, moving wrong for debris, moving deliberate. Someone is coming out here. After two hundred years, someone is actually coming.*

*I don't know whether to hope or be afraid. Perhaps, at my age, those have finally become the same thing.*
