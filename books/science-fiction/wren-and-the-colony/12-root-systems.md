## 12. Root Systems

The Verdance slept in the way that stations built by patient people sleep: not silent, but murmuring. Water through the irrigation mains, a low hum from the atmospheric scrubbers, somewhere below the deck plates the tick and sigh of thermal expansion as the station turned its long face away from the dead world and toward the outer dark. Callum Reyes lay in the guest berth he had been given without being asked for it—Gideon had simply said *you'll want to rest after transit, the gravity here runs light*—and listened to the garden breathe.

He could not remember the last time he had slept anywhere that breathed.

He got up at 0300 station time because his body still kept Conclave hours, dressed in the dark, and let himself out into the corridor with the practiced quiet of a man who had spent nineteen years entering rooms where he was not wanted. The resupply crates sat stacked in the cargo bay where the dockhand drones had left them, seals intact, manifests honest. He checked them anyway. Habit was the only religion he had never lapsed from.

Then he went to the office Gideon had lent him—a converted seed laboratory with grow-lights stowed overhead and the smell of loam soaked so deep into the walls that no amount of sterilization would ever get it out—and unlocked his case, and began to work.

The file on Gideon Vasquez ran to four hundred pages. He had read all of it twice during the six-week transit out, once as an investigator and once as something he refused to name. Now he read it a third time, slowly, because tomorrow he would sit across a dinner table from her and perform the role of a bored inspector doing a routine audit, and he wanted every detail of her life fresh enough to weaponize or to protect, whichever the night required.

Born aboard the ark *Meridian's Promise*, third generation. Parents: hydrology techs, both deceased. Aptitude scores off the ceiling in botany and systems integration, flagged at age eleven by the Terraforming Office's talent trawl—the same trawl that had found him at twelve, except his flag had been for pattern recognition and a documented indifference to authority. She had been posted to the Verdance at twenty-three. Eleven years alone. Performance reviews glowing. Psychological evaluations noting, in the careful language of Conclave assessors, *"subject demonstrates elevated solitary tolerance; recommends no rotation."*

He had underlined that phrase on his first pass. *Recommends no rotation.* Every other deep-posting file he'd ever audited recommended mandatory rotation precisely because solitary tolerance degraded into instability. Someone had read these evaluations and decided that the woman who could be trusted alone should stay alone.

Tonight, on the third pass, he finally asked himself the question he'd been circling for weeks like a moth around a cold lamp:

Who benefits from a gardener who never comes home?

* * *

The Justice Division's secure terminal was a slab of matte-black composite older than he was, its encryption stack layered so many times that using it felt like descending into a well. He jacked in, authenticated with his warrant-code and biometrics, and pulled the cross-reference routine he was legally entitled to run on any subject file: association mapping. Who else appeared in Gideon Vasquez's records? Who had signed her postings, funded her supplies, reviewed her beacon logs?

It was standard procedure. He had run it a hundred times. It took four minutes and returned a network diagram that bloomed across his display in soft amber nodes.

Most of it was what he expected. Terraforming Office administrators. Supply chain clerks. The beacon maintenance contractors who had never once visited. A long spine of names running back two centuries to the station's commissioning.

But at the bottom of the diagram, rendered in the same amber as everything else and therefore almost invisible unless you were looking, was a sub-routine tag he did not recognize. Not a name. A designation: **CONTINGENCY ATTRIBUTION — ACTIVE**.

He stared at it. In nineteen years he had never seen that designation attached to a civilian file. Contingency attribution was a Justice Division instrument—he knew it from his training days, from the doctrine seminars where instructors spoke in the flat voices people used for things they hoped you'd never need. It meant: *if this operation fails catastrophically, this is the person who will be recorded as responsible.*

It was supposed to attach to operations. To missions. To things with codenames and threat assessments.

Not to a gardener. And not—

His finger hovered over the node. The interface waited, patient as everything else in the Conclave.

He opened it.

The sub-routine unfolded like a root system under lifted soil: branching, interconnected, feeding on something buried. At its center was a single operational directive, timestamped fourteen months ago—fourteen months before Gideon Vasquez had ever received her mysterious signal, before Inspector Callum Reyes had ever been assigned to a "routine resupply audit" at a station so far off the shipping lanes that the Conclave had to invent a reason to send him.

The directive read:

> **In event of unauthorized information release originating from asset V-1 (Verdance), primary attribution shall default to field operative R-7 (REYES, C.) per standing order 44-C. Operative is positioned proximal to asset with documented investigative contact. Attribution package pre-seeded in regional judicial archive. No exculpatory review required.**

He read it three times. The words did not change. That was the thing about text—it lacked the mercy of memory, which softened what it held. The words stayed exactly as cruel as they had been when some administrator wrote them.

*R-7.* Himself. His own warrant number, which he had spoken aloud perhaps five hundred times in his career, recited like a prayer before every search, every seizure, every arrest. *Operative is positioned proximal to asset.* They had sent him here not to find out whether Gideon Vasquez had done something wrong. They had sent him here to be standing next to her when the record needed someone to blame.

*No exculpatory review required.*

Meaning: no one would even check whether he was guilty. Meaning: guilt was not the question. Guilt was the answer, pre-written, filed, waiting for events to catch up to it.

Callum Reyes sat very still in the seed-smelling dark of a borrowed office on a garden station at the edge of everything, and felt the floor of his life tilt beneath him with the slow, tectonic patience of a thing that had been moving for years.

* * *

He thought about his first day at the Justice Academy, when he was seventeen and still called Cal by people who loved him. An instructor named Ovid Maras—gray at the temples, a voice like dry paper—had stood before the cohort and delivered the lecture every recruit remembered for the rest of their lives. Reyes could still recite it.

*"The law is not the Conclave,"* Maras had said. *"Do not confuse them. The Conclave is a government, and governments are made of men, and men are made of appetite. But the law—the law is the shape we agreed to give each other's freedom. It is the promise that power must explain itself. You are not enforcers. You are the explanation. When the law and the government part ways, you will know it, because your stomach will tell you first. Trust the stomach. It is older than the oath."*

He had believed it the way you believe in gravity—not as a proposition but as a condition of existence. For nineteen years he had served as the explanation. He had walked into smugglers' dens and secessionist cells and one memorable compound of tax-protest mystics on Ceres Station, and everywhere he had carried the same conviction: that he was the mechanism by which power explained itself, and that this made him clean.

Now the screen glowed in front of him with the proof that he was not the explanation at all. He was the fuse. Positioned proximal. Pre-seeded. A man wired into a building so that when it burned, the record would show where the fire started—and the fire would have started with him.

And Gideon Vasquez?

He pulled her file up beside the directive and looked at them together, side by side, and the architecture of it assembled itself in his mind with the terrible clarity that only came after the fact, the way you only see the trap's geometry once your foot is in it. Asset V-1. *Asset.* She wasn't a person in their system; she was a component. Placed alone at a beacon station for eleven years, rotated never, monitored always—because she was sitting on top of something. Something worth a contingency attribution package. Something the Conclave had already decided, fourteen months ago, might come loose.

Fourteen months ago. What happened fourteen months ago? He queried the regional log, fingers moving faster now, the investigator in him waking fully for the first time since docking. Archive access requests against the old Collapse-era records: none logged. Beacon pulse anomalies at the Verdance: flagged, reviewed, dismissed—"routine drift"—by an office that no longer existed, its personnel reassigned in a restructuring so thorough it looked less like reorganization than erasure.

Someone had touched the Verdance's records fourteen months ago and then removed themselves from history while doing it. And then the Conclave had sent him.

He thought about the dinner she had offered. *Tomorrow, 1900, the greenhouse annex. I cook better than the ration printers do.* She had smiled when she said it, and he had catalogued the smile as data—friendly affect, possible rapport-building, possible genuine warmth, unresolved—and filed it in the place where he kept everything about her, a place that had grown embarrassingly large over six weeks of transit and three days of watching her move through her garden with dirt to the elbows and joy she didn't bother to hide.

She had cooked for him. She had given him the room with the view of the orchard bays. She had talked to her plants in a low voice like a woman singing to children, and when she'd caught him listening she hadn't been embarrassed—she'd just said, *they grow better if someone talks to them,* as if it were the most obvious fact in the universe.

And somewhere in a judicial archive, a package waited with both their names in it. Hers as the criminal. His as the fuse.

Unless.

He leaned back and looked at the ceiling, at the stowed grow-lights with their dust-furred housings, and let himself think the thought he had been refusing to think since the transit, the thought that had no procedural home, no doctrinal shelf to sit on.

*What are they protecting?*

A beacon station orbiting a dead world. A terraforming project that had consumed, according to the supply manifests he'd audited on the way out, resources on a scale wildly disproportionate to its stated purpose—a colony support garden for ten thousand frozen sleepers did not require gene-bank capacity for forty thousand cultivars. He had noticed the discrepancy and filed it as bureaucratic padding. Bureaucracies padded. Everyone knew that.

But bureaucracies also hid. And a contingency attribution package was not padding. It was a confession wearing a procedure's clothing. You did not pre-seed blame for a gardening error. You pre-seeded blame for the kind of truth that, if it got out, would need someone to be guilty fast, and completely, and without appeal.

His stomach spoke, the way Ovid Maras had promised it would. It told him the law and the government had parted ways, and that he was standing on the wrong side of the seam, and had been since the day he boarded the cutter with his warrant code and his clean conscience and his orders he had not read closely enough.

* * *

He worked until station dawn—or what passed for it, the lights along the corridor seams warming from white to gold in a slow simulation of morning that Gideon had told him the plants needed more than the people did. He closed the terminal. He wiped his access traces, though he suspected it was theater; an organization that pre-seeded attribution packages did not leave its operatives unwatched. Let them watch. Let them see him run a standard association map on his assigned subject, exactly as protocol required. There was a small, cold comfort in that: whatever they had made him, he could still be competent at it.

He showered, dressed in the civilian clothes regulations permitted for embedded audits, and looked at himself in the mirror of the tiny fresher. Forty-one years old. Nineteen years of service. A face that interrogators were trained to make trustworthy, which was its own kind of irony he no longer had the stomach to enjoy.

"You're the explanation," he said to the mirror. It sounded like a eulogy.

At 1850 he stood outside the greenhouse annex with a bottle of wine from the resupply manifest—one of the few items Gideon had requested by name, a red from the Meridian's Promise vineyards, grown from cuttings her grandmother had carried across the dark—and knocked on a door made of glass and growing things.

She opened it with flour on her hands and steam behind her and the particular unguarded brightness of a woman who had set a table for two for the first time in eleven years, and the sight of her hit him somewhere beneath the armor he had spent the day discovering was ornamental.

"Inspector," she said. "You're punctual."

"Old habit."

"Come in. Mind the tomato vines, they're opinionated about personal space."

He stepped inside, into the green smell and the lamplight and the small domestic miracle of a table set with actual cloth, and smiled the smile they had trained into him, and hated how easily it still fit.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not escape, the root system under the root system: he could not warn her. Warning her was disclosure of classified investigative material—treason, technically, or something adjacent enough that the distinction wouldn't survive contact with a tribunal. And he could not stop the machinery either, because he did not yet know what the machinery was protecting, and a man who burns down a building to save one person while ten thousand sleepers freeze in the ships above has only changed the order in which everyone dies.

All he could do tonight was sit across from her, eat her food, drink her wine, and look for the truth in her face—while knowing that somewhere in a regional archive, the Conclave had already written the ending, and in that ending, the fire started with him.

Gideon poured the wine, and her hand paused, the bottle hovering, and for half a second her eyes came up and met his with an expression he had seen a thousand times across interrogation tables and had never once seen across a dinner table: the look of a person deciding, in real time, how much of themselves it was safe to show.

"I hope you like it," she said. "It's the last bottle of anything my grandmother's vines ever made."

He raised his glass. "To old roots," he said, and heard, in his own voice, the sound of a man testing the ground beneath him.

Her glass touched his. "To roots," she agreed—and held his gaze one beat too long, and Callum Reyes understood, with the certainty of his oldest training, that he was not the only one at this table performing innocence.

Somewhere above them both, out past the garden's golden simulated dusk, in the outer dark where nothing was supposed to be alive, something waited that had already taught them both what the Conclave did with inconvenient truths.

They just didn't know yet that they'd learned it together.
