## 13. The Garden Remembers

The greenhouse smelled of rain that had never fallen.

Gideon stood in the third bay with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hands buried in a tray of seed-starting medium, and let the work do what it always did: quiet her. Two days since the skiff had docked. Two days since she'd stood in the airlock with Sefa's voice still living somewhere behind her sternum, saying *they called us redundant*, and Reyes's questions arriving like weather she couldn't forecast. She had filed nothing. She had told herself the story she'd practiced on the long flight home—*I'll decide slowly, I'll think it through*—and the story had held for exactly as long as it took her hands to find soil.

Now, on the second morning, the thinking was done with her whether she liked it or not. It arrived in the form of a discrepancy so small that anyone else would have missed it, and she might have too, if she hadn't spent eleven years learning this garden the way other people learned faces.

The discrepancy was in Bay Three, Row Nine, Tray 41: the winter wheat.

She'd noticed it first as a wrongness in the germination rate—ninety-one percent where the archive said ninety-four. Small. The kind of drift that happened when a seed line aged, when the vault's cold centuries nibbled at viability. But when she'd pulled the accession record to log the decline, the record itself had been wrong in a way records shouldn't be.

The wheat was listed as *Triticum aestivum var. Meridian Frost*, accession date 14 March, Year 2 of the Verdance's operation. Provenance: Conclave Terraforming Office, Central Repository.

Except Gideon had grown Meridian Frost for eleven years, and Meridian Frost did not behave like repository stock. Repository stock was uniform—bred down through generations of certification until every plant in a lot was a twin of its sisters, predictable as clockwork. That was the whole point of certified lines. You didn't feed colonists surprises.

Meridian Frost was not uniform. It never had been. Some trays came up tall and pale, some short and dark, one plant in forty always threw a strange bronze awn that Gideon had privately named *the old man's whiskers* and talked to more than she should admit. She'd assumed the variation was heritage—some old landrace the Conclave had thoughtfully preserved. She'd loved it for that. She'd told the sleeping colonists about it, in the early years, narrating her days aloud to the pods because silence was worse: *Your wheat has personality. One of them is a show-off.*

Now she pulled the full genetic profile from the vault's deep archive, and the variation resolved into something else entirely.

Meridian Frost wasn't a landrace. It was a composite—a deliberate blend of at least six distinct lineages, three of which matched nothing in the Conclave's public cultivar registry. They matched nothing anywhere she could query. They existed only here, only in her trays, only in the Verdance's sealed gene-bank.

Plants don't come from nowhere. Every gardener knows this the way they know their own heartbeat. A seed is a biography; you can read where it has been in what it wants to become. And these three lineages wanted things no certified stock wanted—they craved trace minerals in ratios that matched no colonial soil survey, they carried disease resistances against blights that had never appeared in any Conclave agricultural report, they were shaped by selection pressures from a world Gideon had never seen.

Or rather—from a world no one was supposed to have seen.

Her hands went still in the tray. The medium crumbled between her fingers, cool and damp, smelling of peat and something faintly metallic underneath, and she became aware that she was holding evidence and had been holding it, planting it, pruning it, singing to it, for eleven years.

"No," she said aloud, to the row, to the green. "No, that's not—you're mine. I grew you."

The plants, being plants, said nothing. But the data said plenty.

She dried her hands and went to the terminal in the potting room, pulling up the gene-bank's master index—the full catalogue, all four thousand accessions, the inventory she knew better than her own history. And now, with the question finally asked, she saw the pattern everywhere, the way you see a face hidden in wallpaper once someone points it out.

There were clusters. Dozens of them. Accessions whose provenance read *Central Repository* but whose genetic signatures clustered together, apart from everything else—old varieties, unregistered varieties, varieties carrying adaptations to cold and low light and radiation flux that no colonial breeding program had ever needed to select for. Varieties that had been selected for survival in conditions Gideon could name precisely, because she'd spent two days reading the Perpetua's environmental logs.

Deep-space conditions. Ark conditions. The exact spectrum, the exact flux tolerances, the exact mineral profiles of a ship's hydroponics run for two hundred years by people growing food in the dark because no one was coming.

They weren't Conclave varieties. They were ark varieties. Someone had taken the Perpetua's crops—the wheat those ten thousand sleepers' ancestors had bred across generations of darkness, the beans selected for pod set under artificial light, the greens engineered to thrive on recycled water and thin nitrogen—and brought them here, to the Verdance, and filed them under false provenance, and grown them in the garden built to feed the colony that was allowed to exist.

Gideon sat down slowly on the potting bench, because her legs had opinions about standing.

"They harvested them," she said to the empty room. Her voice came out flat, factual, the voice she used for logging dead plants. "They boarded a ship full of dying people and took their seeds."

And then, worse, the thought arriving with the slow inevitability of frost:

No. Not boarded. The Perpetua was still out there, still sealed, still failing. Whatever had carried these cultivars off the ark had happened before the sleep, or during it—or the Conclave had simply kept the manifest. Had known, all along, exactly what grew aboard the ship they'd erased, and had treated it as an inventory. A pantry. When the Verdance needed hardy stock for a marginal terraforming project, someone had opened the file marked *Perpetua, biological assets* and requisitioned.

Sefa's voice, from the recording Gideon had played eleven times since the skiff: *We were not lost. We were allocated.*

She had thought it was metaphor. It was bookkeeping.

* * *

She worked through the morning without deciding to. Her body defaulted to routine the way a heart defaults to beating—misting cycle, pH checks, the pollination wands in Bay Five—and her mind ran the audit underneath, cross-referencing accession dates against the Verdance's construction timeline, and the picture assembled itself with terrible tidiness.

Year 1: station commissioned. The initial gene-bank shipment arrives from Central Repository: 3,900 accessions, certified colonial stock, clean provenance.

Year 2: a supplementary transfer. Unremarkable in the logs—*additional heritage varieties for resilience diversity*. 214 accessions. Filed, shelved, forgotten. Gideon had been twenty-six years old and learning to keep orchids alive in vacuum-adjacent humidity; she had signed for the crates without reading past the cover sheets.

Those 214 accessions were the ark's ghost. Every one of them dated to the same transfer window. Every one of them carried the deep-space signature. Every one of them was a crop that ten thousand people had refined across two centuries of exile—bred in the dark, for the dark, by hands that believed they were the last gardeners humanity had.

And the Conclave had taken them the way you take cuttings from a neighbor's hedge. Without asking. Without acknowledging there was anyone to ask.

Gideon stood in front of the tomato rows in Bay Six and felt something crack along a seam she hadn't known was load-bearing.

These were the tomatoes she'd grown from her first year. The ones she'd talked to through the long quiet seasons, the ones whose first ripe fruit she'd photographed and logged with genuine ceremony—*first fruit, day 1,204, wish you were here to taste it*—sent to no one, transmitted into the beacon pulse nobody answered. She knew their vines like handwriting. She'd grafted them, saved their seed, mourned the ones that succumbed to a fungal scare in Year 6.

And now every vine was a document. Every leaf was testimony. The garden she'd built her whole self around was, she understood suddenly, not a garden at all—it was a crime scene that had been very carefully landscaped, and she was the groundskeeper who'd been hired not to notice.

*Ask no questions about its origin.* The old instruction from her recruitment, the one she'd filed away as bureaucratic eccentricity, rose up and re-read itself in her memory with new teeth. She'd thought it referred to the beacon. It referred to everything. It referred to the soil under her fingernails.

She pressed her palm flat against the nearest tomato vine, feeling the rough stem, the small stubborn life in it.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "I didn't know whose you were."

The vine didn't answer. Vines never did. But for eleven years she'd pretended they did, and the pretense had been the kindest thing in her life, and now even that was contaminated—not the plants themselves, never the plants, they were innocent, they were just seeds doing what seeds do—but the love. The love had been spent on stolen goods, and there was no way to unknow it.

* * *

In the afternoon she did the thing she'd been avoiding since the skiff docked: she opened the vault's restricted layer.

Not the deep archive—she'd been in there a hundred times, it was hers, it was the garden's memory. The restricted layer was different. It was the Conclave's shadow-index, the administrative metadata that rode beneath the botanical data like ballast: transfer authorizations, disposal classifications, chain-of-custody flags. She'd never had reason to look. Gardeners don't read the shipping manifests of their own hearts.

Her clearance—Level Four, Terraforming Office, standard—shouldn't have opened it. But the same archived keys that had decrypted the distress signal's coordinates were still resident in her system, still warm from the Perpetua, and they turned the lock the way a master key turns every door in a building.

The transfer authorization for the 214 accessions appeared on her screen in clean, bloodless Conclave formatting:

**TRANSFER ORDER TF-2291-C**
**Source: Asset Recovery Directorate**
**Contents: Biological materials, 214 lots, ex-vessel PERPETUA (stricken)**
**Disposition: Integration, Verdance Station gene-bank**
**Provenance notation: Suppressed per Directive 7**

*Ex-vessel PERPETUA.* In writing. In her own system. Signed, countersigned, timestamped, filed by someone who had gone home afterward and slept.

*Stricken.* Not lost. Stricken—the word you used for a ship you had written off, written out, struck from the rolls while it still breathed. And *Asset Recovery Directorate*, which meant there had been a recovery. Which meant someone had gone out to the Perpetua—had stood in its corridors, among its pods or its frozen gardens or whatever remained of its living crew at the time—and had made lists. Had graded the seed stock. Had packed it in cryo-crates and shipped it inward to the colony that deserved nice things, and left the breeders asleep in the dark with their power failing.

Gideon read the order four times. Then she did what she always did with unbearable information: she wrote it down. Her grandmother had taught her that—*put the grief on paper, little bird, paper holds it so you don't have to.* She opened a plain text file, no encryption, no header, and typed:

*The Verdance gene-bank contains 214 cultivars taken from the Perpetua. Transfer Order TF-2291-C. Directive 7 suppressed the provenance. My garden is built from their garden. I have been tending stolen things and calling them mine.*

She looked at the words for a long time. Then she saved the file, named it *row nine notes*, and buried it in a directory of soil chemistry logs where no one would ever look, because she was not yet brave enough to do anything else with the truth except hide it in a place that felt safe.

*Directive 7.* She queried it. The registry returned a single line: *Classified. Heritage Directive, Terraforming Office. Access restricted.* No text. No author. Just the shape of a law with nothing inside it, a hole dressed up as a rule. She thought of the beacon, pulsing its patient summons into the dark for eleven years—*ask no questions about its origin*—and wondered how many Directives it stood on top of, how many suppressed notations, how many holes dressed as rules, all of them stacked like strata above the same buried ship.

Everything she'd trusted was load-bearing masonry over a grave.

* * *

Evening came down over the Verdance in its usual way—the grow-lights dimming through their amber phase, the misters sighing, the station settling into its nighttime sounds of pumps and thermal creaks. Gideon walked the bays without working, hands clasped behind her back like a mourner at a viewing, and looked at each row of plants the way you look at a friend who has just been revealed to be someone else's child.

Bay Three, the wheat with the old man's whiskers. Bay Six, the tomatoes. Bay Eight, the beans—she checked the accession tag on the nearest rack and there it was, *heritage supplement, Year 2*, the deep-space signature she could now see as clearly as a brand. The greens in Bay Ten. The potatoes in the tub racks, selected by some Perpetua agronomist two hundred years dead-or-sleeping for yield under lights that never dimmed.

Ten thousand people, and their entire agricultural civilization was here, in her care, feeding the colony that had replaced them. The Conclave hadn't just abandoned the Perpetua. It had digested it. Taken the body's best parts and incorporated them into the survivor, and called the survivor a miracle of colonial engineering.

And she—Gideon Vasquez, Level Four, keeper of the beacon, talker-to-plants—was the digestive tract's final stage. The friendly face of the theft. The one who made it grow.

She stopped at the propagation bench where the cuttings rooted, and found herself laughing—a short, ugly sound with nothing in it.

Reyes had sat at her table two nights ago and asked about her records with that careful courteous voice, and she had performed innocence, and he had performed procedure, and underneath it all they'd both been circling the truth. But she'd thought the secret was out there—in the dark, in the coordinates, in the drifting ship. She hadn't known the secret was also in here. That if he pulled the gene-bank's shadow-index the way she'd just pulled it, he wouldn't need her confession at all. The garden would testify against her. Every tray, every tag, every lovingly maintained accession record—eleven years of meticulous documentation, and she'd documented herself into the evidence locker.

Unless he already had. Unless that was why he was really here—not the flight logs, not the skiff, but this. An inspector from Justice, sent to verify that Directive 7 was still holding. Sent to check on the suppression.

The thought moved through her like cold water through soil, and what it left behind was not fear, exactly. It was clarity. Fear scattered you; clarity gathered you up. She had spent two days dithering, telling herself she'd decide slowly, and the decision had been making itself the whole time, quietly, in the language she spoke best.

The plants had told her. They'd been telling her for eleven years—the bronze awns, the stubborn yields, the flavors no certified stock produced. She'd heard it as personality. It had been provenance. The garden remembered where it came from, because gardens always remember; that's what a seed is, a memory with a coat on. She had simply never been permitted to ask the memory its name.

Well. She was asking now.

Gideon went to the terminal and began, methodically, to build a list: all 214 accessions, cross-referenced against the Perpetua's own agricultural manifest from the data she'd copied aboard the ark. Match confirmed, match confirmed, match confirmed—the two catalogues lying side by side at last, separated by two centuries and one suppressed directive, agreeing with each other perfectly. When she finished, she had a document that proved, in the Conclave's own formatting, in the Conclave's own filing system, that the Verdance was grown from the bones of the erased.

It wasn't a broadcast. It wasn't justice. It was a seed.

And seeds, she knew better than anyone alive, only need one thing: to be planted somewhere they can't be taken back.

She was still looking at the list when the station chime sounded from the hab ring—two tones, polite, the door alert. Someone was at her airlock, and there was only one person it could be, and he was three days early for his scheduled inspection.

Gideon closed the file, wiped the terminal, and went to answer the door with dirt still under her fingernails and the whole garden burning quietly behind her like a witness waiting to be called.
