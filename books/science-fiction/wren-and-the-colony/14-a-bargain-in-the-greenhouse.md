## 14. A Bargain in the Greenhouse

The greenhouse smelled of wet loam and tomato vines, and Inspector Callum Reyes stood in the middle of it like a man who had wandered into the wrong century.

Gideon watched him from behind the propagation bench, her hands buried in a tray of seedling medium she had no intention of working. He'd asked to see the growing decks—"for the resupply manifest," he'd said, with that careful courtesy of his—and she'd said yes because saying no would have been worse. Now he moved down the rows slowly, gloved fingers hovering over leaf surfaces without touching, reading the garden the way she imagined he read case files: for what it confessed.

He stopped at the rice paddies. Tier three. The old cultivar.

"These," he said. "I haven't seen this strain outside the historical archives."

"They do well in low light." Gideon kept her voice level, kept her hands moving through the soil. "Good yield per calorie of lamp power."

"Mm." He turned. In the green-tinted light his face was difficult to read, which she suspected was a professional skill and not an accident. "Inspector Reyes has a question, Ms. Brightwater. Not Inspector Reyes on the manifest. The other one."

"And which one is asking?"

"The one who spent last night going through your flight logs."

The words landed in her chest like cold water. She didn't let it show. Eleven years alone had taught her how to be still—how to let a moment pass over her like weather. But inside, everything was suddenly loud: *the skiff, the coordinates, the Perpetua's scarred hull hanging in her memory like a wound.*

"Did you find anything interesting?" she asked.

"I found a survey run to the outer system. Unlogged destination. Nineteen hours of burn, nineteen hours back, and a sensor sweep pattern that doesn't match any mineral survey protocol I know." He took a step closer, past the lettuce racks, into her territory. "What I didn't find was you filing a deviation report. Which means either you're careless—and your station logs say you are not—or you didn't want anyone to know where you went."

"Or," Gideon said, "I went looking for a quiet place to think, and I'm entitled to my own sky."

"You are." He said it gently, and that gentleness frightened her more than menace would have. "You've earned eleven years of your own sky. That's exactly why I'm standing here instead of transmitting an incident report. Give me something, Ms. Brightwater. Because right now I have a choice to make, and I don't want to make the wrong one."

* * *

Reyes watched her weigh it.

He'd seen a thousand people at this moment—the pause before confession, before lie, before the calculation of odds. Most of them blinked too fast. Some went aggressive. Gideon Vasquez did none of these things. She simply looked at him, this tall woman with soil under her fingernails and a lifetime of silence in her posture, and he understood suddenly why the Conclave had chosen her for the posting: not because she was obedient, but because she was patient. Patience was its own kind of armor.

"If I tell you," she said, "you'll have to arrest me."

"Not necessarily."

"That's what they all say in the stories."

"I don't much care for stories." He pulled off one glove, finger by finger, and held out his bare hand—an odd gesture in a room full of plants, but he wanted her to see skin, to see there was no recorder on his wrist. "My authority here is discretionary. A survey flight with no findings is a paperwork error. I can lose the error. What I can't lose is being lied to after I've offered you the chance not to."

She studied his hand as if it might be a trap. Then, deliberately, she wiped her own hands on her apron and shook it. Her grip was strong and calloused and warm.

"There's a ship," she said. "Out past the heliopause shadow, in the Kuiper debris field. A cryo-ark. Ten thousand pods. It's called the Perpetua, and according to every record in the archive, it was lost two hundred years ago with everyone aboard."

The greenhouse seemed to hold its breath with him. Somewhere in the irrigation lines water moved with a soft arterial sound.

"It wasn't lost," she continued, and now her voice changed—lower, harder, the voice of someone who had been carrying something heavy and had finally set it down. "It was abandoned. Deliberately. The distress signal came in Old Meridian, Inspector. Do you know what that is? My grandmother sang it to me. It's the language of the people your Conclave erased. And their ship is dying out there while we grow tomatoes."

Reyes felt the floor tilt under assumptions he'd carried across four systems. "Ten thousand," he repeated. "You've verified pod count?"

"I boarded. I revived one person. Briefly." Her eyes were dry but her jaw trembled once, and she mastered it. "An old woman named Sefa. She told me what the Perpetua was carrying. Dissidents. Union organizers. People whose genetics the Conclave had flagged as redundant. They put them on a ship, told them they were a second colony wave, and then cut them loose and wrote them out of history."

"And you came back here and filed nothing."

"I came back here and tried to decide what kind of person I am." She laughed, short and bitter. "Eleven years of deciding nothing harder than phosphorus ratios. And then in one week I'm holding ten thousand lives and wondering if the law is worth the paper it's written on."

* * *

He should arrest her.

The thought arrived with bureaucratic clarity, dressed in the language of procedure: unauthorized access to sealed archives, unregistered deep-space flight, contact with a designated-lost vessel, failure to report. Any one of those charges would end her career, such as it was. All of them together would end her freedom. Justice Division training ran deep—it ran in him like groundwater—and it said: *the anomaly is contained, the anomaly is one woman, process the anomaly.*

But Sefa's name hung in the humid air between them. An old woman, woken for minutes only, spending them not on fear but on testimony.

"Show me the signal," Reyes said.

Gideon blinked. "What?"

"The original transmission. The Meridian fragment. I want to hear it."

She led him out of the greenhouse proper into the monitoring alcove, a closet of screens wedged between seed vaults, and pulled up the file without hesitation—which told him she'd already decided, sometime in the last day or hour, that she was past the point of protecting herself. The waveform bloomed on the display, and when she played it, the room filled with a language he didn't know, syllables rising and falling like something sung rather than spoken, old and formal and unmistakably human.

"*Vess-ah kohl-em, vess-ah kohl-em, dahn mirah sohl...*" A pause, static, then again. "We are here. We are here. Our light is failing."

"My grandmother's lullabies," Gideon said quietly. "That's the same word. *Kohl-em.* Here. Home."

Reyes listened to it loop twice. He thought about the oath he'd taken at twenty-two, hand on the codex, the phrase about the inheritance of all humankind. He thought about how many times he'd recited it without once asking who *all* included.

"Here's my problem," he said. "If I report this, it goes up the chain. The chain ends at people who signed whatever order abandoned that ship. Those people will not send rescue ships, Ms. Brightwater. They'll send erasure. You, me, the pods—all of it becomes a regrettable accident. I've read enough closed files to know how accidents get manufactured."

"So you *do* believe me."

"I believe the signal is real and I believe your logs are honest, which is the same thing." He turned to face her fully. "And I believe that in six days I'm scheduled to transmit a routine inspection summary, and whatever I put in that summary is the truth the Conclave will live by. So we have six days to decide what the truth is."

Gideon stared at him. "You're proposing falsifying a Justice Division report."

"I'm proposing we finish the conversation you started when you flew out there." He heard his own voice steady, and marveled distantly at it, the way a man marvels at ice bearing weight. "Here's what I can offer. The Perpetua's power failure—if it's as advanced as you say—is a systems emergency. Emergencies fall under Terraforming Office jurisdiction, not mine. If the ark's own automated distress protocols triggered a revival cascade—a cascade failure, waking pods in sequence—that's a tragedy of engineering. Two centuries of decay. Nobody's fault. Certainly nobody alive's fault."

"A cascade," Gideon repeated slowly. "You want us to wake them and call it an accident."

"I want them *alive*, and I want the story airtight. If we wake them quietly, in controlled sequence, and document it as autonomous system behavior, there's no crime to investigate. There's no conspiracy to expose. There's just a dead ship doing what dead ships sometimes do." He paused. "And by the time anyone asks hard questions, there will be ten thousand witnesses the Conclave can't disappear."

* * *

Gideon walked back into the greenhouse because she couldn't think in the alcove, couldn't think with the lullaby still ringing in her ears. Among the plants she could think. The tomatoes, the rice, the orchard rows—eleven years of her life arranged in evidence of her own patience.

"It won't work," she said. "Waking them takes power. The Verdance doesn't have surplus to spare—we run at ninety-four percent capacity just keeping the beacon and the gene-banks stable. To revive even a fraction of ten thousand pods I'd have to shut down whole decks. Your inspectors would notice a dying garden."

"Then we shut down the beacon first."

She turned. "The beacon is the mission. It's the entire reason I exist out here."

"The beacon has answered no one in eleven years," Reyes said gently. "You told me that yourself, the first night, over tea. You log the pulse and nothing ever answers. Ms. Brightwater—Gideon—" and hearing her first name in his mouth did something strange to the air, "—that beacon isn't calling anyone home. It's a tripwire. It always was. Think about your recruitment. Think about the condition they gave you: maintain it, ask no questions. Ask yourself what kind of installation needs a gardener who's forbidden to wonder why."

She wanted to argue. She reached for argument the way her hands reached for soil. But the memory rose anyway, unbidden: the Terraforming Office interview, nine years ago, the pleasant administrator with the folder full of her credentials and the single strange clause, delivered without emphasis, almost as an afterthought—*the beacon's origin specifications are above your clearance; your task is maintenance, not comprehension.* She'd been so grateful for the posting, so hungry for purpose after the Collapse orphaned her generation, that she'd signed without reading the clause twice.

Now it read like a warning. It had always read like a warning.

"If I divert power from the beacon," she said, "and someone notices—"

"Then we're both finished. Yes." Reyes moved along the row beside her, and she noticed his reflection ghosting in the glass of the hydroponic panels, two figures walking the same line. "I won't pretend the risk away. If this goes wrong, you lose the garden and I lose everything I've been since I was twenty-two. But I keep coming back to one fact, and I need you to check my math on it, because you've been out here long enough to see clearly." He stopped walking. "The Conclave left ten thousand people to die and built its whole legitimacy on never having existed. There is no version of reporting this honestly where the good guys win. So the question isn't whether we break the rules. The question is whether we break them fast enough to matter."

The greenhouse hummed around them—pumps, lamps, the slow respiration of leaves. Gideon looked down the long axis of the deck, at the green she had raised from spores and cuttings and stubbornness, the garden grown from seeds whose provenance she had never questioned until Sefa's voice cracked through a failing pod.

*Your garden was planted with our confiscated harvest,* Sefa had said. *Every seed you love was taken from us.*

Betrayal made botanical. And yet—

"And afterward?" she asked. "When they're awake. When ten thousand dissidents wake up in a dead ship and learn what was done to them. What then? You can't cascade-failure your way out of that. Someone will tell them the truth, and the truth has teeth."

"Then they'll wake up with one friend in the system," Reyes said. "One inspector who chose them over the code, and one gardener who grew a world ready for them to land on." He hesitated, and for the first time since arriving, something unguarded crossed his face—not the investigator, not the instrument of the Justice Division, just a tired man in a beautiful room making a terrible bet. "I've spent my career believing the law and justice were the same animal. This week I found out the law has a leash and someone else holds it. I'd rather be guilty of mercy than loyal to that."

Gideon exhaled. It sounded, in the humid air, almost like a laugh.

"You realize what you're describing," she said. "You're describing treason with a paperwork alibi."

"I'm describing hope with a deadline." He checked the time on his bare wrist. "Six days until my report. The Perpetua's power fails completely—I'd estimate from your data—in under three months. We need to start the revival sequence within weeks to save even half the pods. So: tonight, you show me the ark's systems remotely. Tomorrow we draft the cascade scenario. And Gideon—" he met her eyes, "—from this moment, we trust each other completely or not at all. There's no partial version of this."

She thought of Sefa's hand against the pod glass. She thought of the empty beacon log, year after year, her voice going out into the dark and nothing coming back—until finally something had, and it had been a plea for help, and help was standing in her greenhouse offering to commit treason.

"Complete trust," she said. "From a stranger who came here to bury me."

"From a stranger who's about to bury himself next to you." He extended his hand again, and this time the shake was brief and hard, a contract in skin. "Partners, then."

"Partners." The word felt enormous and fragile, like a seedling in unsuitable soil. "Then you'd better see this. Tonight. The ark's command architecture—it's locked, and I can't crack it alone, and there's a reason for that I haven't told you yet."

His head tilted. "Which is?"

Gideon led him toward the seed vaults, her heart hammering in a rhythm that had nothing to do with fear anymore—or not only fear.

"When I boarded the Perpetua, the locks weren't damaged. They weren't decayed. They were *changed*—re-keyed, decades after launch, by someone using Conclave cipher keys. Someone has been inside that ship since it was abandoned, Inspector. Someone has been tending it. And whoever they are, they re-keyed the doors two months ago."

Reyes went very still among the green things.

"Two months ago," he said quietly, "is when my orders were cut."

* * *

Outside the greenhouse glass, the dead world turned below them, serene and secret, and far out in the outer dark the Perpetua counted down its remaining hours of light, waiting—as it had waited for two hundred years—for someone to decide it deserved to be found.
