## 15. Conclave Static

The report took Callum Reyes four hours to write and eleven seconds to send.

That ratio would haunt him later. Four hours of choosing each word the way a surgeon chooses instruments—*irregularity detected in station telemetry, possible automated survey excursion, no confirmation of crew misconduct, recommend continued observation*—and then a single press of his thumb against the cipher pad, and the truth of his last nine days left his hands forever, wrapped in Justice Division encryption, hurled across forty light-minutes of vacuum toward a desk on Meridian Station where some clerk with a clean uniform and clean conscience would read it over morning tea.

He sent it at 0400 station time, when the Verdance was dark except for the grow-lamps in the propagation wing, when Gideon Vasquez was asleep in her narrow cabin with her door open a hand's width because she'd told him she liked to hear the hydroponics cycling, it sounded like rain.

He sent it because he had to. That was the whole of it, and it was not enough, and he sent it anyway.

* * *

The Justice Division field manual, section twelve, paragraph three: *An Inspector's first obligation is the accuracy of the record. An Inspector's second obligation is the integrity of the investigation. An Inspector's personal obligations rank third, and only third, and the Inspector will be held to account if this ordering is ever reversed.*

Reyes had the manual memorized. He'd had it memorized since he was nineteen years old and a Justice proctor named Halloran had pulled him out of a Meridian gutter-precinct and told him, *You've got a face that lies well, boy, which means you're exactly the wrong person to trust and exactly the right person to hire. Learn the manual and it'll hold you up when nothing else will.*

For twenty-two years the manual had held him up. Through the grain-famine tribunals on Cassini Reach, where he'd watched three farmers hang for hoarding what turned out to be rotted stores. Through the Purge audits, where he'd catalogued the assets of the condemned with the same detached precision he'd once used to catalogue evidence of murder, because that was what the record required. Through every one of the nine postings since, each one farther from the center, each one quieter, until the Verdance—two hundred souls' worth of garden orbiting a dead world, tended by a single woman who talked to her tomato vines—had felt less like an assignment and more like a retirement.

He'd told himself that was why he'd come out here. The quiet. The last easy years before the pension.

He'd told himself a lot of things in the last nine days.

* * *

The transmission went out at 0400. The reply came at 0417.

Reyes was still awake, still in the resupply module that was nominally his quarters for the duration of his "cargo inspection," sitting in the dark with a cup of tea he'd forgotten to drink, when the console chimed.

He knew before he opened it. There was a weight to a Justice Division reply that announced itself even before decryption—a priority flag, a routing header, the bureaucratic equivalent of a knock on the door at the wrong hour. His tea went cold in his hand while the cipher wheel turned.

**PRIORITY DIRECTIVE 7-KESTREL-9. EYES ONLY. INSPECTOR C. REYES, J.D. 4471.**

**RE: REPORT OF ANOMALOUS SURVEY ACTIVITY, VERDANCE BEACON STATION.**

He read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way he'd learned to read verdicts, because the second reading was where the lies lived and the third was where you found out which lies were yours.

*Your report of unauthorized survey excursion has been escalated per Standing Order 114. Justice cutter **Vigilant** (J.D. hull 88) is dispatched to your position, current ETA six weeks, two days. You will take no independent action. You will make no further transmissions regarding this matter except per the attached schedule. You will maintain normal station relations and normal resupply operations. Upon arrival, the Vigilant's senior magistrate will assume full investigative authority.*

*Be advised: Standing Order 114 authorizes, at the senior magistrate's sole discretion, full remediation of the site. Remediation protocols are attached. Acknowledge receipt.*

*Remediation protocols.*

Reyes had been in the Division for twenty-two years and he had never once seen those words in a directive. He'd seen *sanction*. He'd seen *asset recovery*. He'd seen *personnel adjustment*, which was the Division's way of saying someone would be quietly ruined. But *remediation*—that word belonged to a different vocabulary, an older one, the vocabulary of the Collapse itself, of the emergency years when the Conclave had done what it called what needed doing.

He opened the attachment.

It was a single page. A checklist, almost gentle in its formatting. Orbital bombardment, kinetic, three yield options. Atmospheric seeding, biological, two agent options. And at the bottom, a signature block with a field already filled in: *Pre-authorized by order of the First Minister's Office, ref. Verdance File 1-A.*

*Verdance File 1-A.*

There was no such file. He'd pulled everything on this station before he shipped out. There was a terraforming charter, a maintenance history, a personnel record one name long. There was no File 1-A. Which meant the file existed somewhere he hadn't been permitted to look, and had existed for a long time, and had a number low enough that it had been created in the first years of the Conclave's archive system—created before Gideon Vasquez was born, before her posting existed, before the garden she thought she'd been hired to build had been anything but a line in someone's contingency plan.

*Pre-authorized.*

Not *if the investigation warrants*. Not *at the magistrate's discretion following review*. The order was already signed. The cutter wasn't coming to investigate. The cutter was coming to execute a decision that had been made decades ago and was only now, because of his report, becoming inconvenient enough to carry out.

Reyes sat in the dark of the resupply module and listened to the Verdance breathe—the air scrubbers, the water pumps, the faint rhythmic hiss of the irrigation lines running their night cycle through eleven years of a woman's patient work—and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what he had done.

He had reported an anomaly. That was all. That was the manual, that was his obligation, that was the job. And the machine he had fed that anomaly into had looked at a garden and a gardener and returned a checklist for killing them, pre-signed, six weeks out.

* * *

He did not sleep. At 0600 the station's day-cycle lights warmed on schedule and he heard Gideon moving in the greenhouse module, the soft percussion of her morning routine—she'd told him once that she tapped each of the seed trays twice, a habit from her grandmother, *so they know someone's up*.

He dressed. He checked his face in the mirror, the way Halloran had taught him, the face that lies well. It held. It always held. That had never felt like a curse before.

In the greenhouse, Gideon was elbow-deep in the rice paddy, her sleeves rolled, her hair tied back with a strip of cloth that had probably once been a seed sack. She didn't look up when he came in.

"Inspector. You're up early."

"Couldn't sleep. Station's too quiet. I've spent too many years listening for trouble."

"Mm." She lifted a handful of seedlings, checked their roots against the light, settled them back into the nutrient mat with a tenderness he had watched her extend to every living thing on this station and, in nine days, to him. "Trouble's overrated. Most of it's just news that arrived too early." She glanced at him then, and her eyes—brown, unguarded, tired in the way of someone who had been alone a long time and had stopped pretending otherwise—lingered on his face a half-second longer than courtesy required. "You look like a man who got news."

"Routine acknowledgment. My division confirming the resupply schedule." The lie came out smooth and warm and he hated it with his whole body. "Six weeks for the next supply run. Then I'll be out of your hair."

"Six weeks." She smiled and turned back to the seedlings. "I might actually miss the interrogations. The plants are poor conversationalists."

He laughed. It sounded right. It sounded so right it made him sick.

*Six weeks.* He turned the number over as she talked him through her planting schedule, as he nodded in the correct places, as he performed the version of himself that the manual required. Six weeks until the Vigilant arrived with a magistrate and marines and a pre-signed checklist. Six weeks to decide what he was.

Because here was the thing he could not stop circling, the thing that had kept him awake since the night in the greenhouse when he'd found her gene-bank records and instead of arresting her had heard himself say *there might be another way*: the manual said his first obligation was the accuracy of the record. But the record—the true record, the whole record, File 1-A and everything behind it—had been kept from him deliberately. The Conclave had not merely hidden a crime. It had built a garden on top of one, hired a woman to tend it, and then, when the truth surfaced, had drafted the sentence before the trial. That was not law. That was not even order. That was a machine for making sure the dead stayed dead and the guilty stayed comfortable.

And he had just fed it the last piece of information it needed.

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing what the directive told him to do: nothing. He inspected cargo seals that didn't need inspecting. He recalibrated the module's air filters. He walked the ring corridor twice, past the greenhouse, past the seed vault, past the small cabin where Gideon kept her grandmother's music recordings and a single framed photograph of a world neither of them would ever see.

And at 1900 he sat down at his console and opened the directive again and read the acknowledgment line.

*Acknowledge receipt.*

One word from him—*acknowledged*—and the clock was official. The Vigilant's course was already burned in; his silence wouldn't stop it. But the acknowledgment was a thread, and he understood with a proctor's instinct for procedure that the moment he pulled it, everything downstream became *his*—his report, his confirmation, his name in the record as the Inspector on site who had verified conditions warranting remediation. He would be the fuse. Not the bomb. The fuse.

Unless.

He thought about the other way. The way he'd proposed to her in the greenhouse, three nights ago, with his hand shaking around a cup of her tea: wake them quietly, blame a systems failure, give the Perpetua's ten thousand a chance before the Conclave's arithmetic finished them. He'd said it like a plan. It had been more like a prayer.

He thought about Halloran, dead eleven years now, and what the old man would say if he could see his prize pupil sitting here weighing a signed extermination order against ten thousand lives in the dark. Halloran would say: *The manual holds you up, boy. But it doesn't tell you what you're standing on. That part you figure out yourself.*

Reyes looked at the acknowledgment line for a long time.

Then he typed: *Receipt acknowledged. Inspector will maintain normal operations pending Vigilant arrival. No further anomalies observed.*

He hesitated over the send key. Outside the module's small viewport, the dead world turned below him, its terraformed southern hemisphere a smear of green no larger than a thumbprint at this distance—Gideon's life's work, visible from orbit, pre-targeted. Somewhere out past the system's edge, in the cold where the Conclave's maps went blank, ten thousand people slept in a scarred ship, waiting for someone to decide they had ever existed.

He sent the acknowledgment. There was no other honest move left; the cutter was coming regardless, and a silent Inspector would only bring the magistrate down on the station suspicious and fast. This way—this way there were six weeks. Six weeks was not mercy. But it was time, and time was the only currency he had.

The console chimed confirmation. **ACKNOWLEDGMENT RECEIVED. VIGILANT ETA 42 DAYS. REMEDIATION AUTHORITY CONFIRMED. THIS TRANSMISSION WILL SELF-PURGE.**

The directive dissolved from his screen, leaving nothing—no record, no copy, no trace that it had ever existed. That was the Division's way. The order to kill a station and everyone on it, and the machine that issued it kept no paperwork.

Reyes sat very still. Then he did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding, the way you avoid pressing a bruise.

Six weeks. To wake enough of the Perpetua's crew to fly her. To move a ship built for the long dark behind a moon the Conclave's survey charts hadn't updated in a century. To falsify eleven years of station logs well enough to fool a senior magistrate with a pre-signed kill order and no reason to doubt it. To do all of it with a crew of two—a gardener who talked to seedlings and a lawman whose entire career had been built on never once choosing a side.

And one more thing, the thing that sat coldest in his chest, colder than the directive, colder than the checklist: his report hadn't just gone to a desk on Meridian Station. It had gone into a system, and the system had answered in seventeen minutes with a pre-authorized extermination and a file number that shouldn't exist. Seventeen minutes. Machines that fast didn't deliberate. They executed standing instructions, which meant the instructions had been written long ago, which meant somewhere in the Conclave there was a list of conditions under which the Verdance died—and his anomaly report had checked one of the boxes.

Which raised a question he could not answer and could not stop asking: if the Conclave had planned for the possibility of discovery, what else had it planned for? Who else was on the list? A gardener, certainly. An Inspector who filed the report—

He stopped.

He got up. He walked the corridor to the greenhouse, where the day-cycle lights had dimmed to evening amber and Gideon was crouched over the rice paddy doing something unnecessary and gentle to plants that would grow fine without her.

"Gideon," he said.

She looked up, and whatever she saw in his face made her rise slowly, wiping her hands on her thighs, giving him her full attention the way she gave it to everything alive.

"We need to talk," Callum Reyes said. "Not about the resupply. About the six weeks we have left."
