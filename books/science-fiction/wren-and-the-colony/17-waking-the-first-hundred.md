## 17. Waking the First Hundred

**Gideon's log — Verdance Station, day 1 of revival operations. Private entry.**

The first pod opened at 06:12 station time, and I was not there to see it.

I want to record that honestly, because everything else in this log will be about what I saw. The truth is that Reyes and I agreed the first hundred should wake without a stranger's face being the first thing they found — and I am the stranger here, not them. I have lived eleven years thinking of myself as alone, but I am the only person in this system who was never abandoned. That distinction matters more than I knew.

So the pods were triggered remotely, and I watched on a feed from the Perpetua's medical bay while ten thousand years of sleep — no, two hundred and forty-one years, Iyo will correct me, she corrects everyone — ended for one hundred people at once.

The first to move was a man with a shipwright's calluses and a name stenciled inside his pod lid: OKAFOR-DELACROIX, T. He coughed for four minutes straight. Then he looked directly into the recovery camera, eyes swimming but focused, and said, in accented but unmistakable Old Meridian: "If you're Conclave, know that we counted the days. We counted."

I did not understand him then. I do now. I've had eleven hours and three language lessons.

He wasn't talking to me. He was talking to history.

* * *

**Gideon's log — day 2.**

They are waking into a world that has no room for them, and they know it, and they are kind to me anyway. I don't know what to do with that.

Revival is ugly. No one tells you. The Conclave's training materials described cryo-recovery as "a gentle return," which is the sort of phrase written by someone who has never watched a human body remember it owns muscles. A hundred people are learning to walk again in a ship built for people who could already walk, gripping rails installed by their own great-great-grandparents, cursing in a dead language with impressive fluency.

I walk the corridors with Sefa's chair hovering beside me — she insisted on coming, propped up in a mobility frame the ship's fabricators printed overnight, wrapped in blankets because her circulation still runs cold as deep water. She is my translator, my credential, my bridge. When I enter a compartment full of newly woken strangers, she says in Meridian: "This is Gideon. She kept the garden. She came for us when no one else remembered we existed."

And every single time, the room goes quiet in a way I can only describe as religious.

I don't deserve it. I came for them by accident — a signal, a stolen key, a skiff ride I had no business taking. But Sefa has decided my accident was providence, and providence, apparently, does not accept corrections.

Today an engineer named Vashti Okonkwo-Bell grabbed my wrist — first contact, skin to skin, and her grip was iron even after two centuries of ice — and asked me, through Sefa, how many hectares. I didn't understand. She pointed at my hands, at the soil still under my nails from the morning's transplanting, and repeated herself until I got it: *how much land, how many growing things, what did you build?*

"Fourteen thousand square meters under glass," I said. "Sixty open-field trial plots on the surface. Seed bank of nine hundred cultivars."

Sefa translated. Vashti closed her eyes and said something long and low, and Sefa laughed — actually laughed, wet-eyed — before translating: "She says you built more than the whole Terraforming Office promised in the feasibility studies. She helped write those studies. Before."

Before. Everyone here speaks in befores and afters. Before the schism. Before the pods. Before the Conclave decided they were a rounding error.

* * *

**Gideon's log — day 4.**

Iyo woke yesterday, and everything changed again.

She came out of pod 44-C fighting — thrashing before the sedation cleared, shouting numbers, and it took three medics and Sefa's voice in her ear to bring her back to the present tense. When she finally focused, the first thing she asked was not where she was or what year it was. She asked: "Who holds the command deck?"

Nobody, we told her. The command deck has been sealed since the Perpetua went dark, locked behind an authorization protocol nobody aboard could break — not the crew who chose to sleep in shifts, not the automated systems, not me with all my stolen keys. The ark's higher functions, navigation, records, the sealed archives: locked. Two hundred years of lock.

Iyo smiled with cracked lips and said, "Then I'm exactly who you needed."

She is the Meridian linguist. I understood that word — linguist — but not what it meant until she explained, sitting up in her recovery cot like a woman getting ready to lecture, which apparently she was, before, at the University of New Halcyon, before it burned: Old Meridian isn't just a language. It's a key architecture. The Perpetua's builders wrote their deepest systems in it deliberately, so that the Conclave's standardized code-languages couldn't touch them. The locks aren't encrypted. They're *literary*. You have to read your way in.

"The Conclave banned the tongue," she said, "because a language they cannot parse is a door they cannot open. We made ourselves illegible." Her hands shook as she said it — revival tremor, the medics say, though I think some tremors come from older places — "and it nearly worked. It cost us everything. But illegibility saved ten thousand lives, and now it's going to save us again."

She stood today, on legs that haven't borne weight since before my grandmother's grandmother was born, and walked to the command deck door, and put her palm against the panel, and read aloud — I recorded it; it sounded like water over stones, like my grandmother singing — and the door opened.

The crew watching in the corridor made a sound I have never heard a crowd make. Not cheering. Something lower, older. Relief with a spine in it.

* * *

**Gideon's log — day 6.**

Fragile is the word. Everything is fragile. I keep writing that word and crossing it out and writing it again.

A hundred people awake, and every hour brings something new to grieve or celebrate, usually both. This morning a woman named Adaeze Nwosu-Okafor learned that the family names she carried — both lines — appear nowhere in the Conclave's colonial registry, because the registry was purged. Her ancestors were not lost. They were *edited*. She took the news standing very still, holding a cup of tea I grew the herbs for, and then she thanked me for the tea, and that courtesy nearly broke me worse than grief would have.

In the afternoon, three engineers got the hydroponics bay's auxiliary systems running — systems designed two centuries ago, humming back to life like old friends finding each other in a market — and a man named Tomas Reyes-Ferrer (no relation to Callum, they checked, awkwardly, and then laughed about it for ten minutes) wept openly at the sight of growing lettuce. Just lettuce. Ordinary lettuce under ordinary lights. He said, "We designed for this. We never got to see it work."

I gave him the tour of my own grow-rooms by relay feed, and he critiqued my nutrient scheduling the entire time, gently, precisely, and it was the best conversation I have had in eleven years. Someone correcting my work. Someone caring enough to correct it. I logged the whole exchange and I will listen to it again tonight, and I am not ashamed.

* * *

**Gideon's log — day 8.**

Trouble today, and I want it on the record honestly, because joy without friction is a lie and this log is the only honest document in the system.

Council-forming has begun. It happens naturally — the woken gravitate toward the voices they trusted before the sleep, and Sefa's authority is unquestioned, and Iyo's is rising fast now that she reads the ship like scripture. But there are others. A faction around a man called Dagny Holt-Vasquez (another Vasquez — the universe has a sense of humor) who were military before the Collapse, security detail for the Perpetua project, and who wake asking tactical questions instead of agricultural ones. How many ships does the Conclave have. What is our defensive posture. Where are the weapons bays.

Iyo answered the last one: sealed, same as everything else, and she'll open them when there's a reason.

"There's a reason," Holt-Vasquez said. "It's called ten thousand sleeping lives and a government that wants us dead."

"And opening weapons bays announces us," Iyo said, "to every listening post in the system. Patience is also a weapon. It kept us alive for two hundred years."

"It kept us *frozen* for two hundred years," Holt-Vasquez said, and the room split down the middle, quietly, the way rooms do — not with shouting but with the direction people choose to look.

I said nothing. I am the stranger's stranger; this is not my argument yet. But Sefa caught my eye across the compartment, and something passed between us, and I think I have been drafted into a war I don't fully understand yet — the war between the people who survived by hiding and the people who intend to survive by standing up.

Both sides are right. That's what frightens me.

* * *

**Gideon's log — day 10.**

Tonight I taught a class.

Not formally. I was in the mess hall — the Perpetua's mess hall, rebuilt around a hydroponic cutting I brought over in a case held against my chest the whole skiff ride home, a tomato vine, the first living thing to cross between the arks — and people gathered, the way people do, and someone asked me what Earth smelled like.

I've never been to Earth. Nobody alive has. But I told them what the Verdance smells like at shift change: warm soil and cut stems and the mineral tang of the nutrient lines, and rain — I built a rain cycle into the third greenhouse, year six, because I missed it and there was no one to tell me I couldn't — and the crowd went silent, and then an old engineer said, "That's it. That's the smell. That's what the feasibility studies promised," and half the room was crying and the other half was pretending not to be.

Afterward, a girl — young woman, maybe twenty biologically, born aboard during the last waking generation, so she slept as a child and woke as nearly an adult with a child's missing years — asked if she could visit the garden. The real one. The Verdance.

"Not yet," I said, and hated myself for it. "Soon. There are things we have to finish first."

She nodded like she understood, and maybe she did. Children of long emergencies always understand.

* * *

**Gideon's log — day 12.**

Iyo broke into the sealed archives today. Not the command deck — deeper. The historical vaults, the ones even the pre-sleep crew called the Quiet Rooms.

She came to find me afterward, walking unassisted now, moving through the Verdance's relay feed like she'd lived here all her life, and she didn't look triumphant. She looked ill.

"You should sit down," she said, in my own language, which she has been learning at a frightening rate. "No. Stand. You'll want to stand for this."

She had found the manifest. Not the passenger manifest — the cargo manifest. Everything the Perpetua carried when it left: seed stocks, genetic libraries, atmospheric processors, the terraforming core designs. The founding inheritance of a second humanity, loaded onto a ship the Conclave officially listed as lost with all hands.

"Gideon," she said. "Your garden. Your beautiful garden. Where did the Conclave's Terraforming Office get its starting stock?"

I told her: standard issue, office archives, the founding collections.

She shook her head slowly. "The founding collections were ours. Confiscated when they seized the ark. Every cultivar you've spent eleven years loving was grown from seeds they stole from these people's hands."

I stood there, in my greenhouse, surrounded by everything I have ever grown, and felt the ground tilt under a floor that doesn't move.

Every plant is evidence. Every harvest is testimony. The garden I built to keep faith with the frozen colonists above was planted, root and branch, from the inheritance of the frozen colonists below — the ones the registry erased, the ones whose names Adaeze cannot find, the ones who counted the days.

I don't know yet what we do with this. Iyo says the archive contains more — documents, orders, names. She says she needs another day to read her way through the inner seals.

She said one more thing before she left, and I will end this log with it, because I have turned it over all evening and it has not gotten smaller:

"They didn't just steal your garden's roots, Gideon. They stole its reason. The Verdance was never a beacon for the colonists in the ships above. Read the original specs. Ask yourself why a summons beacon needed fourteen thousand square meters of growing space."

I asked her what she meant.

She looked at the tomato vine — the cutting, thriving, already flowering — and said:

"I think the garden was always meant for us. I think they knew that. And I think somewhere in these archives is the order that proves it."

Tomorrow she opens the inner seals.

Tomorrow we find out what the garden was for.
