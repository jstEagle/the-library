## 18. Iyo's Key

The door to the Perpetua's command deck had been locked for two hundred and eleven years, and Iyo Nakamura-Reyes had been waiting her whole life to open something like it.

Not this door specifically. No one had known this door existed. But a door like it—the kind that stood between a people and their own history, sealed with someone else's authority—had been the shape of her entire career. She had spent thirty years aboard this ship studying a dead language, cataloguing the fragments of Old Meridian that survived in the crew's speech, in the hymns sung in the hydroponics bays, in the names parents still gave their children against all fashion. She had done it because words were the last thing the Conclave hadn't managed to confiscate. They had taken the ships' records. They had taken the archives. They had taken, in the end, the very fact of the Perpetua's existence, striking it from every ledger in the fleet until the ten thousand sleepers inside her hull became a rumor, then a ghost story, then nothing at all.

But they had not taken the language. And Iyo had spent three decades making herself its keeper.

Now she stood before the command deck's inner seal with a tablet in one hand and her own heartbeat loud in her ears, and the lock was speaking to her in the tongue she'd spent her life rescuing.

*MERIDIAN KEY REQUIRED. SPEAK, AND BE KNOWN.*

"It's a voice lock," she said aloud, to no one, because there was no one else in the corridor and because saying it made it real. "It wants a voice."

The corridor lights were at emergency amber, the ship's power so far gone that the environmental systems had begun triaging whole decks. Gideon Vasquez had explained it in her quick, practical way, standing in the revival bay among the first hundred: *The reactor's failing in stages. We have maybe three weeks of full power, less if we wake more pods. And the command deck is sealed—ship's charter says only the Captain or her designated successor can open it, and the succession protocol requires a key phrase in Old Meridian that nobody aboard remembers.*

*Nobody,* Gideon had said, *except maybe you.*

Iyo had laughed when she was told. It had come out of her wrong, high and cracked, because the joke of it was unbearable. They had erased a ship and its people, and the only thing standing between the erased and their own bridge was a sentence in the language they'd been forbidden to teach their children. The Conclave had banned Old Meridian in the schools two generations before the Perpetua launched—she'd found the edicts in the smuggled fragments the crew's grandparents had hidden in the walls of the lower decks, wrapped in oilcloth like relics. *The Meridian tongue is a vector of sedition. Its use is prohibited in all Conclave facilities.* And so the Perpetua's founders had done the only seditious thing left to them. They had made their children learn it in secret. They had sung it in the dark. They had buried it in lullabies and curses and the names of the dead, and they had waited two centuries for someone like Iyo to be born, someone stubborn enough and strange enough to keep the whole language alive in her head like a coal carried in cupped hands.

*Speak, and be known.*

She knew what the phrase would be. She had known since Gideon had shown her the succession protocol an hour ago, since she'd read the phonetic rendering of the key phrase rendered in the clumsy Conclave orthography by some archivist who had never understood what he was transcribing. It was a line from the Lament of the Founding—the poem every Meridian child of the old fleet had memorized, the one her own grandmother had sung to her in the hydroponics bay of the *Halcyon*, the colony ark where Iyo had been born into the Conclave's official history, the sanctioned history, the history with no second ship in it.

The line meant: *We were not lost. We were left.*

Iyo closed her eyes. She thought of her grandmother's hands, cracked from nutrient-tank work, shaping the old words in the air like something being fed. She thought of the smuggled edicts. She thought of ten thousand pods in the dark, each one a person the ledgers said had never existed.

She spoke.

"We were not lost. We were left."

The lock considered her for three seconds—an eternity—and then the seal released with a sound like a held breath finally let go, and the command deck doors parted, and the amber light of the corridor spilled into a room that had been waiting in the dark for longer than Iyo had been alive.

* * *

The command deck was a cathedral with the lights out.

It was larger than she had expected—larger than the *Halcyon*'s bridge, larger than anything the Conclave built anymore, because the Conclave built small now, built tight, built everything with the assumption that the future was a resource to be rationed. The Perpetua's founders had built for abundance. The ceiling arched up into darkness where the star-charts must have projected, and the stations curved in a horseshoe around a central dais, and every surface was furred with two centuries of dust except where the emergency power kept thin ribbons of status-light crawling like glowworms along the consoles' edges.

Iyo's boots left prints in the dust as she walked to the captain's station. She found herself moving slowly, the way you move in a temple, and she hated herself a little for the reverence. Reverence was for things that deserved it, and she would not know until she looked whether this room deserved it.

The captain's console woke at her touch. The screen flickered, stuttered, and then steadied into a login prompt in Old Meridian script—the real script, the flowing one, not the Conclave's blocky transcription. Iyo read it the way other people read their own names.

*Identify yourself.*

She hesitated, then typed: *Iyo Nakamura-Reyes, linguist, third-generation. I come with the first hundred woken and one friend from outside.*

The cursor blinked. Then, without her asking:

*Welcome home. You are the first to open this door since the Sealing. The archive you are looking for is in the Captain's vault. You will want to sit down. Forgive me—I have been alone with this information for two hundred years, and I have imagined this moment ten thousand times.*

Iyo stared at the words. "You're the ship's intelligence," she said aloud, her voice strange in the big dark room. "The charter intelligence."

*What remains of me,* the console answered, letters appearing as it spoke through a speaker so degraded it hissed like wind. *The Conclave's purge teams took my higher functions when they sealed the deck. What is left is a custodian. I kept the records. I kept the phrase. I kept the count of the sleepers. I have kept everything, because keeping things was all I was permitted to do.*

"Show me the vault."

*You should sit down.*

"I've been sitting down for thirty years," Iyo said. "Show me."

A panel in the dais irised open. Inside, cradled in foam gone brittle with age, was a data core the size of a heart.

* * *

She read it standing, because sitting felt like surrender.

The custodian projected the documents onto the captain's main display, and Iyo read them in the original Meridian, translating as she went out of habit, her mind doing the work automatically while the rest of her went somewhere cold and quiet.

The first document was dated sixty-one years before the Sealing. It was a fleet census annex. She recognized the format from the *Halcyon*'s own records—she had catalogued them, once, in the years before she'd understood what she was cataloguing. This annex listed the passenger manifests of the exodus arks. The *Halcyon*. The *Meridian*. The *Sunder*. And the *Perpetua*—ten thousand, four hundred and twelve souls.

The second document was dated nine days later. It was shorter.

It was an order.

*By authority of the Conclave Emergency Council, and in the interest of the survival of the sanctioned genome and the sanctioned polity: the vessel Perpetua is hereby struck from the fleet registry. Its passenger complement, having been assessed as genetically redundant, politically compromised, or materially burdensome to the restoration project, is hereby reclassified from 'survivor' to 'expendable.' The vessel's beacon transponder is to be disabled. Its course is to be altered to a non-recovery trajectory. No rescue effort is to be mounted, now or hereafter. No record of its complement is to be retained in any Conclave archive.*

*The persons aboard are to be considered never launched.*

Iyo read it twice. Three times. Her lips moved with the old words, shaping them, and she realized distantly that she was reading it aloud, in Meridian, in a voice that shook, because some part of her mind believed that if she translated it into the Conclave's tongue it would become real, and she could not—she would not—be the one to make it real.

*We were not lost,* her grandmother had sung. *We were left.*

The song had been a memory of a wound. It had been testimony, carried in a lullaby for two hundred years because there was no other container left that the Conclave hadn't searched. And Iyo had thought she understood it. She had thought, in the vanity of the young, that she understood everything the old people carried.

The third document was the one that broke something in her and built something else in the same breath.

It was the order again—the same text—but this copy bore signatures. Ratification signatures, gathered over the years as the order was renewed, generation after generation, each new Conclave council choosing again, deliberately, with full knowledge, to keep ten thousand people condemned. The earliest signatures she recognized from history: the founders, the heroes of the Restoration, the names carved on the memorial walls of every colony world. The men and women whose statues she had walked past as a child, whose names she had memorized in school.

And at the bottom of the page, the most recent renewal—signed forty-three years ago, when Iyo was a child aboard the *Halcyon*, when her grandmother was still alive and still singing—the signature was one she knew better than her own.

*Adeyemi.*

The current First Minister's grandfather. The founder of the Adeyemi dynasty, the man whose portrait hung in every government hall, whose name was spoken with the reverence owed to a saint. He had read this order. He had understood it—he had appended a note in his own hand, in Meridian, in a scholar's careful script, which made it worse, because it meant he had bothered to learn the language of the people he was erasing, had learned it well enough to write:

*Renewed without amendment. The redundancy determination stands. Let no future council soften what our generation had the clarity to decide.*

*Clarity.* Iyo read the word and heard, underneath it, the sound of a beacon being switched off. She heard the course change, the non-recovery trajectory, the ten thousand four hundred and twelve souls set adrift in the outer dark with their transponder dead, their existence struck from every ledger, their children's children taught nothing, told nothing, left to freeze in the black while the sanctioned genome inherited a garden.

She became aware that she was gripping the edge of the console hard enough to hurt her hands. She made herself let go, finger by finger.

"Show me the rest," she said.

*There is no rest,* the custodian said. *There is only this, and the count. Ten thousand four hundred and twelve. I have counted them every hour for two hundred years. Four hundred and six pods failed in the first century before the power rationing began. The remainder are viable. I have kept them viable. It is the only obedience left to me, and I chose it, because someone had to keep what they tried to erase.*

Iyo stood in the amber dark of the cathedral her people had built and was not permitted to enter, and she let the anger come. She did not turn from it. She had spent her whole life being gentle with the past, handling the fragments in oilcloth, treating the smuggled edicts as artifacts to be catalogued and mourned. She had thought her work was preservation. She had thought her work was grief.

It was not grief. It had never been grief. It was evidence. Every song she had learned was testimony. Every name she had catalogued was a plaintiff. She had spent thirty years building, without knowing it, the complete case against a government—and now she stood in the room where the case closed itself, in the language they had tried to kill, holding the order they had tried to lose.

Her anger cooled as it rose, the way metal cools into a blade. She felt it happen. She felt the hot, formless fury—the thing that wanted to scream, to smash the consoles, to make the whole ship shake with the wrongness of it—she felt it pass through her and come out the other side shaped, edged, quiet. This was the anger her grandmother had sung with, she realized. Not rage. Resolve. The Lament was not a cry. It was a promise with a melody.

*We were not lost. We were left. And the left do not forgive; the left remember.*

"Iyo?"

She turned. Gideon Vasquez stood in the command deck doorway, still in her borrowed Perpetua coveralls, dust from the corridor on her boots. Behind her, two of the first hundred—Marek the reactor engineer and old Tomas, who had been a judge, once, in the world that no longer existed—waited in the amber light, watching Iyo's face.

Gideon crossed the deck and looked at the display, at the order, at the signatures. Iyo watched her read them. Watched the gardener's face do what every face in the room was doing: the long fall, and then the hardening.

"The First Minister's family," Gideon said quietly. "It goes all the way up. It's been renewed every generation. They always knew."

"They always chose," Iyo corrected. Her voice came out level, and she was surprised by it, and then she wasn't, because this was what the language was for. Old Meridian had no word for a passive atrocity. Its grammar made you name the actor. Its grammar made you say *who did this*. "That's the difference. An accident can be mourned. This was a policy. Signed. Renewed. Countersigned by the man whose grandson runs the Conclave today." She touched the display, over the signature, the way you'd touch a wound to confirm it was yours. "He wrote his note in our language. He wanted us to know he understood every word of what he was doing. That's not shame. That's contempt."

Tomas, the old judge, had come forward to read. He stood a long time at the display. When he turned, his face was the face of a man resuming an office he had thought was abolished.

"In the law we brought with us," he said, "this is not a war crime. It is not even a crime against humanity. It is genocide by ledger. And the statute of limitations on genocide," he added, in the tone of a man reading a sentence he had waited his whole career to pronounce, "does not begin until the victims are free to press the charge."

"Then we wake them," Marek said. "All of them. Every pod we can reach."

"The power won't hold for a full revival," Gideon said. "Not without—"

"Then we find the power," Iyo said.

They all looked at her. She heard her own voice as if from a small distance, and understood that she was no longer the archivist of a dead tongue. The language had never been dead. It had been waiting, the way the ship had waited, the way the pods had waited, the way the truth had waited in a vault under a phrase only the faithful had kept. And now it was spoken, and things moved when it was spoken, and she was the one who could speak it.

"There's a sealed section in the lower decks," she said. "The founders' reserve—the old people sang about it. *The seed-room under the water-line, kept for the day of return.* The Conclave's purge teams never found it because the access phrase is in Meridian and the purge teams couldn't read Meridian." She felt something that was almost a smile move across her face, and it was not a gentle thing. "They banned the language to bury the ship. They buried the ship's rescue inside the language. Every lock they built, our grandparents built the key into the songs we sang them."

Gideon was looking at her with an expression Iyo would remember for the rest of her life—not pity, not awe, but recognition. One keeper of buried things, meeting another.

"The Verdance," Gideon said slowly. "The station. The beacon. I was told to maintain it and never ask where it came from. Iyo—the garden's gene-bank. The cultivars are from before the founding. I think—I think they took them from this ship, before they sealed it. I think the garden itself was built out of what they stole." Her voice frayed at the edge. "If that's true, then the power infrastructure, the resource allocations, everything the Conclave has—it was built on this. On them."

"Then it comes back," Iyo said. "All of it. With interest."

She turned back to the console, to the custodian, to the count.

"Custodian. How long to open the founders' reserve, if I give you the phrase?"

*Give me the phrase,* the ship said, *and I will open every door they locked. I have waited two hundred years for someone who could.*

Iyo closed her eyes. Her grandmother's hands shaped the words in the air. She took the breath, and spoke the old words into the dark of the command deck—words that had been sung in secret, in fear, in hydroponics bays and sleeping berths, for two hundred years, and that were, from this moment, a declaration of war:

*The seed-room opens for the day of return. The day of return is now.*
