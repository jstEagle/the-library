## 19. What Love Costs

The greenhouse at night was a different country.

Gideon had known it for eleven years—the way the grow-lamps dimmed to amber after ship's-night fell, the way the tomato vines went quiet in their trellises, the way humidity beaded on the glass and ran down like slow rain. She had walked these rows ten thousand times alone. Tonight she walked them with Callum Reyes one step behind her, and every plant seemed to know it, seemed to lean toward him the way she was trying not to lean.

"You talk to them," he said. Not a question. He'd heard her do it for three weeks now, since the bargain in this same room, since they'd become co-conspirators instead of interrogator and suspect.

"They're better listeners than most people."

"I'm not most people."

"No," she said, and stopped beside the bean rows, and didn't look at him because looking at him had become dangerous in a way that had nothing to do with Conclave Justice Division. "You're not."

He came around her slowly, the way he did everything—deliberate, courteous, a man who treated even his own hesitation as evidence to be examined. In the amber light his face lost its inspector's polish. She could see the exhaustion under it now, the six weeks of planning carved into the lines around his mouth. The Justice cutter was coming. They both carried that knowledge like a stone in the chest, and neither of them set it down, because setting it down would mean admitting how heavy it was.

"Gideon." Her name in his voice sounded different than it did in anyone else's. Sounded like something he'd practiced saying when she wasn't there.

"Don't," she said softly.

"Don't what?"

"Say whatever you're about to say if you're going to take it back tomorrow. I don't have room in my life for things that get taken back. I've spent eleven years keeping everything alive and nothing close, and I'm very good at it, and I will do it to you too if you give me an opening."

The fans cycled. Somewhere in the canopy above them, water dripped from a leaf onto the soil, a small precise sound, like a clock counting down.

"I don't take things back," Reyes said. "I'm a terrible liar and a worse coward. Ask anyone at the Justice Division." A ghost of a smile. "Actually, don't ask anyone at the Justice Division anything anymore."

She laughed—short, surprised out of her—and hated how good it felt, how much space his presence had made in her chest without her permission.

"The cutter is twenty-two days out," he said. "Maybe less. When it docks, I go aboard as their witness or their prisoner, and either way I don't come back down here. You know the arithmetic."

"I know the arithmetic."

"So I'm not going to spend what's left pretending I feel less than I feel. That's all. That's the whole speech. I just—" He exhaled, and for the first time since she'd met him, Callum Reyes looked unarmored. "I just wanted you to know that the last six weeks haven't been only about the ark. For me."

Gideon looked up at him then, and let herself look, let herself be seen looking. Eleven years of talking to plants because no one else listened, and here was a man who listened like listening was a discipline he'd trained in, like it was the truest thing about him.

"Come here," she said, "before I lose my nerve," and he crossed the row of beans and took her face in both hands like she was something rare and perishable, which, she thought, everyone was, everything was, the whole garden was, and kissed her with the gentleness of a man who knew exactly how much time they had and refused to waste any of it on hurry.

* * *

Reyes lay awake in the narrow bunk of the guest quarters—he'd never gone back to the skiff, some unspoken agreement having moved him into the station's second cabin weeks ago—and listened to the Verdance breathe.

That was the thing nobody told you about a garden station: it breathed. Air handlers, condensers, the slow tidal exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide between the plants and the humans who tended them. On the cutter, the air had been scrubbed dead, recycled through filters until it tasted like nothing. Here the air tasted green. Here the station itself seemed to want its occupants alive.

He thought about the file. He thought about the sub-routine with his own name flagged in it, *asset-implication protocol*, the cold bureaucratic phrase that meant: *if this goes wrong, Inspector Reyes is the designated failure point.* He had read it four times before the horror settled into something usable. Anger was fuel. He'd been burning it for weeks.

But tonight, lying in the dark with the station breathing around him, he wasn't thinking about the Conclave. He was thinking about Gideon's hands in the soil that afternoon, teaching him to graft citrus stock, correcting his grip without embarrassment, without performance. Thinking about how she'd said, *you hold it like it might bite you,* and he'd said, *everything I've held for fifteen years might bite me,* and she'd gone still, and then she'd taken his hand and turned it palm-up and set the graft in it and said, *not this.*

Not this. Two words, and he'd felt something in his chest come unlocked that he hadn't known was locked.

He got up. Padded barefoot through the corridor, past the seed vault with its triple seals, past the observation blister where the dead world turned below them, brown and patient, waiting for the water they were going to steal back from the sky for it. Her door was open a hand's width. He knocked anyway, knuckles on the frame, soft.

"You're awake," she said.

"How can you tell?"

"Your footsteps. Inspectors walk like they're collecting evidence. You were walking like a man with nothing to log."

He slid into the narrow space beside her, and she made room without being asked, and for a while they just lay there in the dark, shoulder to shoulder, watching the stars wheel slowly past the porthole as the station rotated.

"Tell me something true," she said. "Something from before. Something that isn't case files."

He thought about it. That was another thing about him—she'd learned he never answered fast, because fast answers were usually rehearsed ones.

"My mother sang Meridian lullabies," he said finally. "Old ones. She learned them from her grandmother. When I was small I thought they were just songs. Then I joined the Division and found Meridian classified as a restricted linguistic artifact, and I asked my mother where the songs came from, and she went white and told me never to say the word again." His voice stayed level, but Gideon felt him go rigid against her shoulder. "I obeyed. Fifteen years, I obeyed. My own grandmother's language, and I filed it away like contraband."

"That's why you came down here," she said quietly. "Before the orders caught up with you. Some part of you already knew."

"Some part of me has always known. That's the worst part. We always know. We just build the paperwork high enough that we can't see over it." He turned his head toward her. "Your turn. Something true."

Gideon watched the stars. "When I took this posting, the Terraforming Office officer asked me if I understood what solitude does to a person. Eleven years minimum, he said. And I said yes. And I did understand—I thought I did. But understanding loneliness from inside a city is like understanding drowning from a photograph of the ocean." She paused. "The first year, I talked to the plants because it was the only way to keep my voice working. By the third year, I stopped noticing I was doing it. By the fifth, I genuinely believed they answered. Not words. Just—presence. Something leaning toward you in the dark." Her hand found his under the thin blanket. "And then you arrived, and I realized I'd been waiting eleven years for someone to lean back, and I was so angry about it. At you. At myself. Because wanting something is how you lose it."

"Yes," he said simply. "It is."

"And you're staying anyway."

"I'm staying anyway."

Outside, the dead world turned beneath them, and somewhere beyond it, hidden behind the moon, ten thousand sleepers waited in the dark with failing power cells, and twenty-two days away a cutter full of marines burned toward them with sterilization authority. All of it was true. All of it would still be true in the morning. But in the dark, in the breathing green dark, two people chose each other precisely because the choosing couldn't last, and found that the impermanence didn't cheapen it—it distilled it, the way grief distills joy, down to something clear and strong enough to live on afterward.

* * *

They built rituals the way survivors build shelters: quickly, out of whatever was at hand, without illusions about permanence.

Morning tea in the observation blister, before the day's work, watching the terminator line crawl across the planet's face. He learned how she took it—bitter, no sweetener, a habit she'd formed because sugar rations were for the seedlings' growth medium, not for people. She learned that he read case law for pleasure, actual pleasure, and teased him about it until he laughed, really laughed, a sound that seemed to surprise him every time it escaped.

Afternoons in the greenhouse, grafting, transplanting, his hands learning the work under hers. He was careful with living things, she noticed. Careful in a way that suggested he'd spent years being required not to be.

Evenings, planning. Revival schedules, power budgets, descent corridors. The war they were waging against a clock.

And nights—nights belonged to no plan at all.

One night, tangled together in her narrow bunk with the stars turning overhead, she traced the old scar along his ribs and asked, and he told her: a riot in the Meridian quarter of Meridian Station itself, twelve years ago, before he understood what he was policing. A crowd singing those same forbidden songs. His baton. Someone else's knife.

"I reported it as self-defense," he said into the dark. "It was. Technically. But I stood in that square for an hour afterward and I remember thinking: *these people are being punished for remembering.* And then I wrote the report anyway, and went home, and slept fine. That's what frightens me. Not what they made me do. How easily I did it."

Gideon was quiet a long moment. "Do you know what I did my first year here?"

"Tell me."

"There was a blight in bay four. Fungal. It was going to spread to the seed vault through the shared atmosphere, and protocol said total incineration of the affected bays—every plant, everything, burn it clean." Her voice went flat, reciting. "I sealed the bay and vented the atmosphere instead. Killed the fungus with cold and vacuum. Saved ninety percent of the cultivars. Broke about six regulations doing it." She turned to look at him. "I've never told anyone that. There's no record. I falsified the logs myself, the first falsification I ever made, before I knew I'd ever need to make another."

Reyes studied her face in the starlight. "You're telling me you've been a criminal for ten years."

"I'm telling you we're both people who break rules when the rules are wrong. The difference is I've had eleven years to practice being honest about it, and you've had six weeks." She touched his jaw. "You're catching up fast."

He caught her hand and held it against his cheek, and closed his eyes, and for a moment the inspector vanished entirely and there was only a tired man in the dark, holding on.

"When the cutter comes," he said, "whatever happens—if they board, if they take me, if it goes badly—you get the Perpetua behind the moon. You don't wait for me. You don't negotiate. Promise me."

"Don't make me promise that."

"Promise me anyway. Love that costs nothing is just preference. I need this to cost something, or it doesn't count."

She pressed her forehead to his. "Then here's mine: if they take you, I will burn the whole Conclave's house down to get you back, and I will enjoy it, and I will never apologize. That's what my love costs. Are you satisfied?"

His laugh broke in the middle, half grief, half wonder. "Completely."

* * *

On the nineteenth night, Reyes woke to find her side of the bunk empty and followed the sound of her voice to the greenhouse.

She stood among the bean rows in the dim amber light, talking to the plants as she had for eleven years—but differently now. He stood in the doorway, unseen, and listened.

"—and I need you to understand that whatever happens, none of this was wasted," she was saying, her palm flat against the trunk of the oldest citrus, the grandmother tree, grown from a cutting older than the Collapse. "Every hour. Even the lonely ones. Especially the lonely ones. They made me able to do this. So if the station burns, you burn having meant something, and that's more than most of us get—"

"Gideon."

She turned. Didn't startle. Maybe she'd heard him; maybe some part of her had been talking to him all along, the way she'd once talked to the plants because no one else listened.

"You'll wake the whole colony," he said gently, crossing to her.

"They're asleep. All ten thousand of them, plus the ten thousand behind the moon. Everyone's asleep except us." She leaned into him, and he wrapped his arms around her, and they stood together among the sleeping green while the station breathed around them. "Callum. Tell me something true. One more time."

He thought of the extermination order locked in the Perpetua's command deck. Of Okafor, who'd earned his trust and would have to choose soon. Of the cutter burning closer with every rotation, its arrival date a number shrinking in the dark.

"I love you," he said. "Not despite the timing. Because of it. I spent fifteen years waiting for a life worth risking, and then I found yours, and it was."

She pulled back far enough to look at him, and her eyes were wet and fierce at once. "Then we spend what's left like it's everything. Starting tomorrow. Descent simulations at 0600, and I'm putting you on thruster calibration, because your handwriting on the flight plans is a disgrace."

"Aye, Commander Brightwater."

They walked back through the green dark hand in hand, past the seed vaults, past the grandmother tree, past everything they might yet lose—and neither of them saw the comm panel in the corridor flicker once, twice, as an encrypted priority burst from deep space queued itself for delivery, marked URGENT: JUSTICE CUTTER *VIGILANT*, ETA REVISED.

Three weeks had just become nine days.
