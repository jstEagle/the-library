## 20. The Cutter Comes Early

The alarm didn't sound like an alarm. It sounded like a cough — a single dry pulse from the Verdance's long-range array, the kind of automated throat-clearing Gideon had heard a thousand times and never once feared. A transponder ping. A merchant vessel squawking its route. She almost didn't look up from the seed trays.

Then she saw the designation code trailing the ping like a shadow behind a body.

*JCV Halcyon Step.* Justice Division hull prefix. Conclave registry. And beneath it, in the machine language of military traffic control, the number that stopped her heart mid-beat: *ETA 19 hours.*

Nineteen hours. Not six weeks. Not even close.

The tray slipped from her hands. Forty germination plugs fanned across the greenhouse floor in a slow, silent constellation, and she stood there among them, breathing too fast, watching the readout as if staring could change it. The numbers held. The *Halcyon Step* was three weeks ahead of schedule, burning hard on an intercept vector that would put her in high orbit above the Verdance before tomorrow's dawn cycle finished running.

Reyes's voice came through the comm a moment later, tight and stripped of all its usual courtliness: "Gideon. Tell me you're seeing this."

"I'm seeing it."

"Then we're out of time." A pause, and in the pause she could hear him doing arithmetic — the same terrible arithmetic she was doing. "How many of the first hundred are awake?"

"Sixty-one. Iyo has the command deck. Sefa is—" Gideon closed her eyes. "Sefa is arguing with the hawks again."

"Get everyone you can onto the shuttles. I'll start moving the ark." Another pause, longer. "Gideon. Whatever happens in the next nineteen hours — the logs you falsified, the corridor you drew for them — none of it was wasted work. We planned for this."

"We planned for six weeks," she said. "We have nineteen hours."

"Then we improvise," Reyes said, and for the first time since she'd known him, his voice sounded young. "Meet me at the transfer dock at hour sixteen. Bring the seed vaults. All of them."

The line went dead. Gideon knelt among the scattered plugs, pressing them back into their cells one by one with hands that wouldn't quite steady, and somewhere below her, ten thousand sleepers began, without knowing it, to wake.

* * *

On the command deck of the *Perpetua*, Iyo Okonkwo-Meridian felt the ship shudder awake around her like a sleeper rolling over in bed.

She had been reading the old extermination order again. She kept coming back to it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth — the elegant Meridian script, the seal of the First Conclave, the countersignature of Adeyemi's ancestor rendered in ink so faded it might have been apology rather than authorization. She had it open on her display when the proximity klaxon began, soft and apologetic, the way everything on this ship was soft and apologetic, as if two centuries of abandonment had taught even the alarms to expect no one to answer.

"Contact," said the deck, in its patient dead voice. "Justice-class cutter. Designation *Halcyon Step*. Intercept in eighteen hours, forty minutes."

Iyo sat very still.

Around her, the first hundred were scattered through the ship's waking corridors — engineers relearning gravity, dissidents' grandchildren learning they had grandchildren, the whole fragile organism of a resurrected people finding its pulse. Sixty-one souls out of ten thousand. Sixty-one against a warship.

She opened the channel to the Verdance because there was no one else to open it to. "Gideon Vasquez," she said. "This is Iyo. Tell me what you need from us."

There was a silence on the line, and then a voice Iyo had come to think of as the garden's own voice — steady, tired, rooted — said: "I need you to move your ship behind the moon. I need you to do it quietly. And I need you to trust me when I tell you that the Verdance is going to be very loud for the next few days."

"What are you going to do?"

What Gideon said next, Iyo wrote down later, word for word, because some sentences deserve to survive their speakers:

*"I'm going to give them something to look at."*

* * *

Sergeant Adaeze Okafor stood in the *Halcyon Step*'s briefing bay and listened to a man explain why they were three weeks early, and understood, with the cold clarity of twenty years in uniform, that she was being lied to.

"The Division received a partial transmission intercept," said Major Voss, pacing before the tactical display with the unhurried confidence of someone reciting lines he'd rehearsed. "Origin: the beacon station. Content: encrypted, fragmentary, but consistent with unauthorized long-range communication. Justice Directorate assessed the risk profile as elevated and accelerated our deployment accordingly."

Elevated risk profile. Okafor turned the phrase over. In her experience, "elevated risk profile" meant someone senior had gotten frightened, and frightened people in the Conclave did not send cutters early — they sent them armed. The *Step* had come out of dock with full marine complement, breaching charges, and — though it wasn't in any briefing she'd attended — she'd seen the weapons crews calibrating the spinal battery on day two. You didn't calibrate an orbital lance for a gardener and an inspector.

"Sir," she said, "is the mission still apprehension and evidence recovery?"

Voss stopped pacing. He looked at her the way men look at dogs who've caught a scent they shouldn't have. "The mission is resolution, Sergeant. However it needs to look."

"Resolution," Okafor repeated.

"You have a question?"

"No, sir." She straightened. "Just confirming terminology."

He moved on to the approach timeline, and Okafor let her eyes drift to the display while he talked. The Verdance hung in the projection like a green lantern — she'd seen the survey imagery, the impossible garden blooming in vacuum, and had privately thought it the most beautiful thing humanity had ever built, which was presumably why it now needed resolving. Behind it, flagged in amber, was a sensor ghost the analysts had labeled *debris field, unconfirmed* — a mass drifting toward lunar shadow that was much too large and much too regular to be debris.

Okafor had served eleven years. She knew what a hidden ark looked like on a plot, even when everyone agreed not to say so.

She also knew that Inspector Callum Reyes was somewhere on that station, and that Reyes had taught half the Division's junior officers how to build a case that would survive appeal, and that Reyes had gone dark on scheduled check-in four days ago. Four days. Right around, she noted, when the *Perpetua*'s ghost had begun to move.

In the mess hall afterward, she found Lieutenant Brann sitting alone, and sat down across from him without asking.

"You saw the spinal battery," he said. It wasn't a question either.

"I saw it."

Brann turned his cup slowly on the table. "My grandfather came out on the second wave," he said, to the cup. "Meridian family. Old stock. There used to be songs about ships that never arrived." He looked up. "You ever hear those songs, Sergeant?"

"No," Okafor said carefully.

"No," Brann agreed. "Neither did anyone. That's rather the point." He drank, set down the cup, and stood. "Nineteen hours to stationfall. I'll be in the armory if anyone needs me — officially doing inventory. Unofficially, I'll be counting breach charges and thinking about songs."

Okafor watched him go, then pulled up the mission roster on her wrist display and began, quietly, methodically, to note which names on the marine complement had Meridian-family surnames.

It was a short list. But it was longer than zero.

* * *

Hour nine. The Verdance's central hall smelled of crushed basil and ozone, and sixty-one people from a dead century were crammed into it listening to a woman who had spent eleven years talking to plants try to talk to them instead.

"They will offer you mercy first," Gideon said. "Understand that. The Conclave leads with mercy because mercy is cheaper than bullets and better for the record. Some of you will be offered amnesty, resettlement, even citizenship — conditional, of course, on forgetting where you woke up. Do not sign anything. Do not confirm your pod numbers. If they ask where you were born, the answer is here. All of you were born here, as far as anyone knows. You're colonists from the first ark, delayed by drive failure. You've been asleep in the upper holds. I can forge the medical records by morning."

A man near the back — one of the engineers, Kofi, hands still calloused from machinery two hundred years dead — raised a scarred fist. "And when they don't believe it?"

"Then they stop offering mercy," Gideon said, "and I need you off this station before that happens. Shuttles to the surface, dispersal pattern Delta, into the southern valleys. The terraforming seeded native-analog forest cover there two years ago. Nothing on their scopes reads as habitation yet."

"And you?" asked Sefa.

The old woman sat front and center, wrapped in a thermal blanket she refused to surrender, and her voice carried the particular authority of someone who had already died once and found it survivable. "And you, child? You've drawn their fire to yourself twice now. Third time is not charm. Third time is arithmetic."

Gideon looked at her — at the face that had scolded her gently about soil pH, that had sung her fragments of Meridian lullabies in a voice like dry leaves — and told the truth, because Sefa had earned truth the way other people earned medals.

"Somebody has to hold the beam-steering console when the shooting starts. The Verdance's defense grid is a lie, but it's a lie with real emitters, and emitters need hands. If I'm not there, the barrage walks straight down the *Perpetua*'s descent corridor and every shuttle in it dies." She shrugged, and hated how small the gesture felt. "It's my garden. I stay with it."

"It is not your grave," Sefa said flatly.

"That depends," Gideon said, "entirely on their aim."

The room made a sound — not laughter, not quite, but the thing that lives next door to laughter in rooms full of frightened people. Kofi shook his head. Somewhere a woman began, very softly, to hum one of the Meridian songs, and Sefa joined her, and for a moment the hall smelled less like ozone and more like something growing.

Then the lights flickered, and the comm board lit amber, and Reyes's voice came through on the emergency band, and every plan in the room died at once.

"Gideon — new contact. Small craft, launched from the cutter. Fast mover, stealth signature, inbound on direct approach. They're not waiting for stationfall." A breath. "They're sending a boarding party now. Two hours out. Gideon — it's a kill team configuration. I've seen the loadout manifest. This isn't arrest."

* * *

Major Voss watched the boarding craft separate from the *Halcyon Step* and felt nothing in particular, which was how he preferred to feel about most things.

The orders had come down twelve hours ago, sealed, Directorate-level, bypassing the standard chain. *Station assets to be secured or rendered inoperable. Personnel disposition at commander's discretion.* Discretion. He turned the word over as the little ship fell away toward the green light below. In the Directorate's vocabulary, discretion was a scalpel-shaped hole in the paperwork, and everyone involved understood that whatever went through it would never appear in any after-action report.

His second officer, a careful young lieutenant named Ash, cleared her throat beside him. "Sir, Inspector Reyes is stationed aboard. Standard protocol requires—"

"Inspector Reyes's status is under review," Voss said.

"Under review, sir. And the station personnel?"

"One gardener." Voss consulted the file without interest. "Eleven years alone. No family, no faction, no record beyond a handful of procedural irregularities. If she cooperates, she gets relocated with a pension and a confidentiality bond. If she doesn't—" He closed the file. "Discretion, Lieutenant."

Ash said nothing, but she looked at the tactical plot a moment too long, at the amber-flagged mass sliding behind the moon, and Voss noted it, and filed it, and decided she would stand watch during the operation rather than participate in it. Careful people were useful until they weren't.

Below, the boarding craft crossed into the station's shadow, and the Verdance's running lights — warm, domestic, absurdly gentle — blinked once, twice, and went dark.

* * *

"They're ninety seconds out," Reyes said. "South docking arm. Gideon, get to the root cellar. Now."

"I'm at the console."

"I know where you are. I can see the console telemetry from here, which means so can they, which means in ninety seconds there are going to be six armed marines standing in a room with you." His voice was doing something she'd never heard it do — fraying at the edges, the courtesy burned away. "Gideon. Please."

"The emitters don't hold themselves."

"The emitters don't matter if you're dead!"

"Then it's fortunate," Gideon said, keying the first emitter bank live and feeling the station shudder around her like a struck bell, "that I intend to be extremely difficult to kill."

Outside the viewport, the dark above the dead world lit up — not with weapons, not yet, but with the Verdance's own false defenses, decoy flares and spoofed signatures blooming in orchestrated confusion, exactly the chaos she and Reyes had scripted for a battle that was supposed to be three weeks further away. Somewhere behind the moon, the *Perpetua* was burning quiet and desperate for shadow. Somewhere in the black between, a boarding craft was correcting course toward the noise.

And somewhere inside Gideon's chest, beneath the fear, beneath the fury, a thought surfaced with terrible calm:

*They came early because they're afraid. Afraid things move fast.*

*Good,* she thought, hands steady on the beam-steering yoke, the garden blazing around her like a green star refusing to go out. *So do we.*
