## 22. Reyes Stands in the Door

The cutter *Verity's Edge* came out of the dark three weeks early, and Callum Reyes watched it arrive the way a man watches a debt collector's skiff pull up to his door.

He stood on the Verdance's observation deck with Gideon, both of them lit green by the grow-lamps below, the garden breathing its slow wet breath through the vents. The cutter was a splinter of light against the dead world's limb, running cold-dark until it wasn't—running lights flaring all at once, deliberate, theatrical. A statement. *We see you.*

"Six weeks," Gideon said quietly. "They said six weeks."

"They lied about the schedule the way they lie about everything else." He checked his wrist terminal for the fourth time in ten minutes. The partial transmission they'd intercepted—the one that had triggered this early arrival—had been his own encrypted report, sliced and reassembled by an algorithm in Conclave Signals that he'd once helped calibrate. There was a bitter symmetry in that he didn't have time to enjoy. "They'll have maybe forty percent of my report. Fragments. Enough to know something happened here. Not enough to know what."

"Enough to know you're compromised."

"Enough to know I'm *convenient*." He turned from the window. Below them, through the deck grating, he could see the rows of Perpetua seedlings—the first hundred's first crop, pale shoots rising out of Verdance soil under lights Gideon had rerouted without a flicker of hesitation. Somewhere behind the moon, ten thousand sleepers waited in a ship that now had power, heat, and a crew of ninety-one awake people learning to trust each other one shift at a time. "Listen to me. When they board—and they'll board within the hour—I need you somewhere that isn't here."

"I'm not leaving my station."

"Your station is the thing they've come to burn. Which is exactly why it can't be the thing they find you standing in front of." He kept his voice level. He'd learned that in Justice Division training, twenty years ago: the calmer you sound, the more people believe there's something calm to be calm *about*. "Iyo has the shuttle prepped. Sefa signed off on the plan. You go to the Perpetua, you take the secondary gene-bank cores with you, and you let me handle the boarding party."

"And how exactly do you handle a marine assault team?"

"By being exactly what they expect me to be."

She looked at him then—really looked, the way she did when she was reading soil chemistry or a distress signal, looking past the surface to the structure underneath. He felt the look like a hand on his chest.

"You're going to confess," she said.

"I'm going to *manage* a confession. There's a difference." He almost smiled. "They sent me here as a fuse, Gid. Chapter twelve of my own file. If things went wrong, Inspector Callum Reyes takes the fall, cleanly, and the Conclave's hands stay clean. Well. Things went wrong. So I take the fall—but on my terms, at my pace, with a script I wrote instead of theirs. Every hour I keep them focused on me is an hour the Perpetua spends getting further behind lunar shadow."

"That's not a plan, Callum. That's a eulogy with extra steps."

"It's the job." He said it simply, because it was simple, and because he'd known it would come to this since the night he'd sat alone in his quarters and found his own name flagged in a sub-routine designed to bury him. A good man choosing consequences he could see coming from a long way off isn't tragic, he thought. It's just arithmetic. "I swore an oath to Justice Division when I was twenty-three. The oath was to justice. The Division stopped being that a long time ago, but the oath didn't expire. This is me paying it."

Gideon's jaw worked. She was a woman who had spent eleven years talking to plants because no one else listened, and he knew—knew with a certainty that hurt worse than any tribunal—that if he let her, she would stand in that airlock beside him and burn with him, and everything they'd built would die in the same fire.

"Sefa needs you," he said. "Not symbolically. Actually. You're the only person on either ship who understands the Verdance's systems well enough to finish the descent-corridor work, and you're the only person the hawks on the Perpetua council will listen to when they start arguing about seizing the other ark. I can't do that from a cell. You can't do it from a cell either. One of us has to stay free, and we both know which one of us they'll let walk away."

"Why?" Her voice cracked on it. "Why would they let me—"

"Because you're the gardener. Because in their story, you're a victim—a lonely woman deceived by a corrupt inspector. They need that story. It lets them close the file." He reached out and took her hands. They were soil-rough and steady, and holding them was the easiest thing he'd done in weeks. "Let them need it."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The garden hummed beneath their feet. Somewhere in the rows below, an irrigation line cycled, soft as rain.

"If you die in their custody," Gideon said finally, "I will spend the rest of my life making sure everyone in this system knows your name. Not as a fuse. As the man who stood in the door."

"That's the nicest threat anyone's ever made me." He kissed her forehead, brief and hard, memorizing it. Then he stepped back, straightened his jacket, and became, visibly and deliberately, Inspector Callum Reyes of the Conclave Justice Division again—spine straight, face composed, the performance settling over him like a uniform. "Go. Now. Take the long corridor so the hull cameras don't catch you near the airlock."

She went. At the hatchway she stopped, half-turned, and for one second he saw her as she'd been in the greenhouse two nights ago, unguarded, laughing at his terrible attempt to prune a citrus tree. Then she was gone, and the corridor lights cycled after her, and Callum Reyes stood alone in the green light with his whole life arranged neatly around the next thirty minutes.

* * *

The boarding alert sounded at 0340 station time.

He felt the docking clamps before he heard them—a shudder through the deck plates, heavy and certain, the handshake of something much larger than the survey skiff he was authorized to receive. The airlock status board ran its sequence: seal, equalize, cycle. He had time to note, distantly, that his hands were completely steady, and to wonder whether that was courage or shock, and to decide it didn't matter.

The inner hatch opened on six marines in black composite armor, weapons slung but hands near, and behind them a woman in the slate-grey field uniform of Justice Division command—Commander Ansel Voss, tall, silver at the temples, with the patient expression of someone who had already read every file worth reading.

"Inspector Reyes." Her voice was pleasant. It was the same pleasantness he himself deployed, and hearing it from the other side was instructive. "You look surprised."

"I'm not, Commander. Tea's still hot in the mess. I assumed you'd want to talk before you talked."

Something flickered behind her eyes—respect, or its institutional equivalent, which was recalibration. "Walk with me."

He walked. Six marines fell into formation around him with the practiced ease of men who had done this a hundred times and hoped never to do it to a colleague. They moved through the observation gallery, past the viewports where the dead world hung like a closed eye, and Reyes used every step to run the clock in his head. Shuttle departure from the aft bay: nine minutes ago. Perpetua burn initiation: scheduled for twenty minutes from now, on Iyo's mark. Lunar shadow: four hours beyond that. Every minute Voss spent with him was a minute the ark spent falling deeper into safety.

"The Verdance is a remarkable installation," Voss said, glancing down through a floor panel at the green rows. "Eleven years of solo maintenance. Impressive discipline."

"She mostly maintains herself. I just write the reports."

"Yes." Voss smiled faintly. "About the reports."

They reached the mess. She dismissed the marines to the corridor—two remained inside, posted at the hatch—and poured herself tea from the carafe he'd left warming, uninvited, unhurried. He sat across from her because sitting made him look less like a prisoner and more like a colleague, and appearances were the only weapon left in his kit.

"Your last transmission was corrupted," Voss said. "Signal degradation, our analysts think. Solar weather." She sipped. "What we recovered suggests irregularities in station operations. Unauthorized power rerouting. Anomalous traffic between this station and... something in the outer system. Your interim report would clear a great deal up, Inspector."

"My interim report is simple." He folded his hands on the table. Here it was, then. The door. He'd rehearsed this sentence in the shower, in the dark, walking the garden rows at 0200 while Gideon slept, and now that it was time to say it aloud he found it came out clean. "I falsified my findings. All of them. From the beginning of my posting."

Voss set down her cup. Very slowly.

"Go on."

"There is no anomaly in the outer system. There was a sensor ghost—a reflection artifact off the system's Kuiper debris, which I identified, catalogued, and then exploited." He held her gaze. "I used it to mask unauthorized power draws from the Verdance's reactor. I've been diverting station resources for personal projects. Unsanctioned cultivation experiments. Genetic work outside my charter."

"Genetic work." Her voice didn't change, but the temperature of the room did. "On what stock?"

"On archived cultivars I found in the gene-bank. Pre-Collapse lines. I told myself it was research." He let a breath out, shaped it into shame, and hated how easily the shape came. "It was vanity. Eleven years alone out here, Commander—you start needing to build something nobody asked for. Something that's yours."

Voss studied him for a long moment. Outside the viewport, the stars were indifferent. Inside, the two marines by the hatch had gone very still, the way soldiers do when they realize they're watching something historic and aren't sure yet whether it's a fall or a rise.

"You understand," Voss said at last, "that unauthorized genetic manipulation of Conclave-restricted stock carries a mandatory custodial sentence. And that falsifying Justice Division reporting—" she tapped the table, once, "—carries considerably more than that."

"I know the statutes. I wrote half of them into the regional manual."

"Then you understand I have no choice but to place you under arrest."

"I'd be disappointed in you otherwise."

She almost smiled at that. Almost. Then she stood, and the marines came back in, and the cuffs were polymer and cold and lighter than he'd expected, the way the worst things usually are. Voss recited the charges in the flat cadence of procedure—misuse of authority, falsification of records, conduct detrimental to the Conclave—and he listened to each one like a man counting coins he'd saved up over a lifetime, checking them against what they'd bought. Ninety-one people awake. Ten thousand more with power and heat. A descent corridor cleared. A woman on a shuttle behind the moon, safe, furious, alive.

Cheap, he thought. It had all been cheap at twice the price.

"One condition," he said, as they turned him toward the airlock.

"You're not in a position to negotiate, Inspector."

"I'm not negotiating. I'm offering." He paused at the hatch and looked back at her, and let the last piece of the performance show—not the confession, but the loyalty underneath it, the thing she'd want to believe because believing it made her job easier. "Gideon Vasquez knew nothing. Nothing. She's a caretaker, not a conspirator—she logged every anomaly honestly, which is why my falsifications nearly got caught twice. Whatever happens to me, her record comes back clean, or I'll make noise in custody that neither of us wants. I know things about this region's files, Commander. Old things. I'd rather they stayed quiet."

Voss regarded him for a moment with something that might, in a different life, in a different Conclave, have been sympathy.

"Your cooperation is noted," she said, which meant nothing, and he nodded as though it meant everything, which was the job.

* * *

They marched him through the airlock into the belly of *Verity's Edge*, and the last thing he saw of the Verdance was the observation gallery going dark behind the closing hatch—grow-lamps dimming on automated night-cycle, the garden tucking itself in, patient as ever, trusting the morning to come.

The marine escorting him—sergeant's chevrons, name tape reading OKAFOR—was quieter than the others. Watchful. As they crossed the cutter's docking bay, the sergeant glanced sideways at him and said, low enough that only Reyes could hear:

"Genetic work. Personal projects. That's your story?"

Reyes kept his eyes forward. "That's my report, Sergeant."

"Mm." Okafor faced front again. But his stride slowed, fractionally, and his hand drifted—not toward his sidearm, but toward the data-slate on his hip, the one every marine carried, the one that synced, automatically and without anyone thinking much about it, to the cutter's open fleet-relay channel.

And Reyes thought: *they heard the broadcast fragments too. Some of them have been listening.* He filed the thought where he filed all his remaining assets—in a cell, soon, with nothing but time—and let the marines take him down toward interrogation, sealing the door behind him with his own name.

Behind the moon, right on schedule and invisible to every instrument on the cutter, the *Perpetua* began to burn.
