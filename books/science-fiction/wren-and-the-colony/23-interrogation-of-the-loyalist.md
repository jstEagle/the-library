## 23. Interrogation of the Loyalist

The cell they gave him had a window.

Callum Reyes noticed that first, before the cot, before the recycled air with its faint chemical sweetness, before the way the door sealed with a sound like a held breath being released. A window, a full meter of armored glass, looking out onto the black of deep space and, far off, the blue-white crescent of the world everyone aboard this ship was sworn to protect.

Someone had made a choice when they assigned him here. Cells without windows were for men who were already condemned. Cells with windows were for men they still hoped to turn.

He sat on the cot and looked at the planet and did not let himself think about Gideon Vasquez alone on a station that was, by now, either hidden or burning. He thought about procedure instead. Procedure was a rope you could hold onto when everything else was falling. He catalogued what he knew: he'd been in custody thirty-one hours. He'd been read his rights under Article Nine, which meant they intended formal charges, which meant a tribunal, which meant time—days at least, maybe weeks while the cutter's legal officer assembled the record. Time was the only currency he had left, and he intended to spend every coin of it badly, wastefully, gloriously, on the one thing they wanted from him.

The door opened at 0600 ship's time. It would open at 0600 every day; he'd learned the cutter's rhythms during his years as an inspector, and it pleased him in a small, bitter way that his own training was now the thing keeping him steady.

The man who entered wore civilian clothes—a gray tunic, no insignia—and carried a slate and two cups of tea. Reyes recognized him from the personnel files he'd once memorized: Advocate Dain Mercer, Justice Division's senior interrogator, a man whose conviction rate was ninety-four percent and whose reputation rested on never raising his voice.

"Inspector Reyes." Mercer set one cup on the fold-down table. "Or should I say former Inspector."

"Callum is fine."

Mercer smiled like a man appreciating good form. "Callum." He sat opposite, unhurried, and poured nothing—the second cup was theater, a prop for the conversation they were supposed to have instead of the one they were going to have. "You've had a night to sleep on it. I'm told you slept."

"I did."

"No guilt?"

"Some. Not about what you want it to be about."

Mercer nodded, as if Reyes had confirmed something useful. "Let's start simple. The Verdance station. Its keeper, Gideon Vasquez. An unauthorized vessel leaving lunar shadow on your watch. Give me those three things in any order, and we can talk about mitigation. Cooperation is weighed, Callum. You know that better than anyone—you've written the reports where cooperation turned twenty-year sentences into five."

Reyes picked up the tea. It was real leaf, not synth—another calculated kindness. He drank it anyway. There was no virtue in refusing comfort.

"You've read my statement," he said. "I claimed sole responsibility. That hasn't changed."

"Statements change when people understand their situation fully." Mercer produced the slate but didn't look at it; he didn't need to. "So let me make sure you do. Treason under Article One. Sabotage of Conclave assets. Aiding and abetting the theft of state property—because that's what the Perpetua is, legally. State property. Ten thousand units of frozen human capital, misappropriated two centuries ago by criminals, and you helped move them."

*Human capital.* Reyes let the phrase sit between them, let Mercer hear how it sounded in the quiet.

"That's the legal language," Reyes said. "I'm curious what yours is. Off the record. What do you call ten thousand people in pods?"

"I call them a question I don't get to answer." For just a moment, something moved behind Mercer's eyes—not sympathy exactly, but recognition, the look of a man who had spent decades carrying other people's arithmetic. Then it closed over, smooth as water. "That's the difference between us, maybe. You decided you got to answer it."

"Someone has to. The Conclave decided two hundred years ago, and it decided wrong."

"The Conclave decided *survival*." Mercer's voice stayed level, almost gentle. "You weren't there for the Collapse, Callum. Neither was I. But I've read the sealed histories. Half the fleet was dying, the gene banks were contaminated, and a frightened government made a triage decision about who could live on what remained of a poisoned world. Was it ugly? Yes. Was it *theirs* to decide? Also yes. That's what sovereignty means. That's what law means. You don't get to reopen the verdict because you find it distasteful."

"Two hundred years later, the world isn't poisoned anymore." Reyes set down the cup. "Gideon Vasquez terraformed it. Eleven years, alone, with resources the Conclave gave her—resources that came from somewhere, by the way. Have you asked where? Have you looked at the Verdance's founding manifests? Because I have, and the cultivar lines match Perpetua gene-bank records. The garden that feeds the legitimate colony was grown out of the seeds they stole from the people they erased. So when you say the verdict is settled, what you mean is the thieves kept the estate."

Something flickered across Mercer's face then, quick and quickly gone. He'd seen the manifests too. Or he hadn't, and the word *stolen* had landed somewhere he wasn't armored.

"You're good," Mercer said quietly. "They told me you were good. Twelve years in Justice, commendations, the Vasquez file handled by the book right up until it wasn't." He leaned forward. "Here's what I actually came to offer, and I'll only offer it once. The tribunal convenes in four days. Before that, if you give us the Perpetua's position—if you give us *anything*, a vector, a fuel signature, a name among the revived—we can charge you as coerced rather than complicit. Five years, service remission. You're forty-three years old, Callum. Five years is a life. Treason is a wall and a vacuum."

"And the ten thousand people?"

"Are a security problem that predates both of us."

"They're *people*."

"They're a transmission risk!" For the first time, Mercer's voice cracked its casing—not anger, but exhaustion, the sound of a man saying a thing he'd said so many times it had worn smooth and hollow. "Do you know what happens if the Perpetua's existence enters the colonial networks? Do you know what the original ark's descendants learn, overnight? That their government abandoned half the species. That their gardens are grown on stolen ground. That the First Minister's own lineage signed the order. Every settlement, every station, every family with a grandparent in a pod—they will tear the Conclave apart, Callum, and they'll be *right* to, and what comes after won't be justice, it'll be a war over one terraformed world between two peoples who both have nowhere else to go. I am trying to prevent a genocide's sequel. What are you trying to do?"

Reyes held his gaze. This was the moment, and he'd known it was coming since the airlock sealed behind him on the Verdance, since before that, maybe since the night he'd found his own name flagged in a Conclave sub-routine and understood that the institution he'd served had marked him as expendable from the start.

"I'm trying to do my job," he said. "The one I thought I had, before I learned what it was. Justice Division exists to protect people from power. Somewhere along the way it started protecting power from people, and men like you and me were the lock on the door. I'm not giving up the Perpetua, Advocate. Not for five years, not for amnesty, not if you open the window and space me out of it myself. Arrest me. Try me. Put treason on the record in letters a meter high. And then sit in that courtroom and listen to yourself say *human capital* one more time, and ask yourself which side of Article One you're actually standing on."

The silence stretched. Outside the window, the planet turned its slow, indifferent arc, blue and white and utterly unaware.

Mercer rose. He collected his slate, left the tea, and paused at the door with his hand flat against the panel.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "ninety-four percent. You know what the other six percent were? Cases where the accused was right, and I knew it, and the record convicted them anyway." The door sighed open. "Sleep well, Inspector."

* * *

They came for him again at 1400, and this time it wasn't Mercer.

Two marines, escorting him through the cutter's gray corridors to a compartment he knew well—he'd stood in rooms like it a hundred times, on the other side of the table, running interrogations of smugglers and saboteurs and once, memorably, a fourteen-year-old girl who'd broadcast pirate frequencies over a mining settlement. He remembered how she'd looked at him. He wondered if this was what he'd looked like to her.

The compartment held a different advocate this time—younger, sharper, a woman named Okonjo with a scarred knuckle and eyes that never blinked when they should. She didn't bring tea. She brought a holo-display and turned it on without preamble.

"This is a partial transmission intercepted eleven days ago from the Verdance beacon," she said. "Encrypted fragments. We haven't broken it. You're going to tell us what's in it."

"Am I."

"You're going to tell us what's in it, or you're going to be charged under Article Fourteen as well—concealment of evidence material to state security—which carries a separate sentence, served consecutively, in a facility I promise you is less pleasant than this one." She folded her hands. "Inspector. Former Inspector. Whatever you are now. Look at me. You spent twelve years in Justice. You know how this ends. Everyone talks eventually. The only variable is how much of your life is left when you do."

Reyes looked at her. He thought about the girl with the pirate transmitter, and how he'd gotten her to talk in forty minutes, and how he'd felt afterward—clean, professional, correct—and how that feeling had been the first small crack in him, though it had taken years and a dead world and a woman who talked to plants for him to see it.

"I do know how this ends," he said. "Usually with the innocent talking, because the system makes silence more expensive than confession. That's not a flaw in the machine, Advocate. That's the machine working as designed." He leaned back. "I have nothing to say about the transmission. Charge me with whatever fits."

Okonjo studied him for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, hard sound with no humor in it.

"Do you know what my orders are?" she said. "Not the ones in the file. The real ones. If you don't cooperate within seventy-two hours, I'm authorized to classify you as an enemy combatant and transfer you to the cutter's brig population. General holding. No isolation." Her eyes were flat. "You were Justice Division, Callum. You put men in general holding. You know what they do to former inspectors."

He did know. He'd watched it happen through observation glass, and signed the transfer orders, and gone to lunch.

"Then I'll make friends," he said.

* * *

They transferred him that evening. The brig population was forty men and women—smugglers, debt-runners, a pair of dockworkers who'd organized an illegal strike at Meridian Station, a young medic whose crime, according to the manifest, was treating Perpetua sympathizers without reporting them. Word traveled fast in a brig. By the second day, they all knew what he was.

He expected the first attack on the third day. It came on the first night.

A big man with dockworker's hands backed him into the corner by the water recycler, close enough that Reyes could smell engine grease and sweat, and said, very quietly, "They say you know where the lost ark is. They say you're the one who found it."

Reyes didn't flinch. Flinching was a currency too, and he was done spending.

"I'm the one who tried to help them," he said. "And now I'm here, and they're free, or they will be."

The big man searched his face for the lie, and didn't find it, and something in the brig's atmosphere shifted the way weather shifts—imperceptibly, then all at once. By morning, someone had given him an extra ration pouch. By the third day, the medic was checking the bruising on his ribs from the arrest, unasked. By the fifth day, they were asking him questions—not about the Perpetua's location, but about the law. About whether Article One applied to a government that had committed the crime first. About whether a tribunal could convict a man for telling the truth.

He answered as honestly as he could, sitting on a bunk in a Conclave brig, teaching prisoners constitutional law by lamplight, and understood that he had finally, after twelve years, become what he'd sworn an oath to be.

* * *

On the sixth day, they took him to the tribunal's preliminary hearing, and that was where the crack in the world showed itself.

The hearing chamber aboard the cutter was small—six officers behind a bench, a legal clerk, himself standing alone at the defendant's rail because he'd refused counsel. The presiding officer was Captain Voss, a narrow, precise woman whom Reyes had met twice before at division functions, and who now read the charges in a voice like a metronome.

"Treason. Sabotage. Aiding the misappropriation of state property. Concealment of evidence. How do you answer?"

"Not guilty," Reyes said. "To all counts. On the grounds that the laws cited were void from inception, having been enacted to conceal a crime against humanity, and that obedience to them was itself the offense."

A murmur went through the chamber. Voss's metronome voice didn't waver. "The tribunal does not entertain jurisdictional challenges at preliminary hearing. You will answer the charges as filed or be recorded as unresponsive."

"Then record me as unresponsive," Reyes said. "But I want it in the transcript, Clerk, word for word: the defense contends the Conclave abandoned ten thousand citizens to die, harvested their genetic resources to build its colony, and ordered the destruction of the one witness who found them. When this trial is over—and it will be over, one way or another—I want the record to show someone said it out loud, in a Conclave court, before Conclave officers, while it was still dangerous to say."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears. Behind the bench, one of the six officers—young, a lieutenant with a farm-world accent—looked down at her hands and did not look back up.

Voss banged the session closed in nineteen seconds. As the marines walked him out, Reyes caught Mercer's eye across the chamber. The old advocate stood by the wall, arms folded, watching him with an expression Reyes had never seen him wear in twelve years of knowing his reputation.

It looked like doubt.

* * *

That night, in general holding, the lights dimmed to sleep-cycle, and Reyes lay on his bunk listening to forty strangers breathe, and let himself think about Gideon for the first time since the airlock.

He didn't know if she was alive. He didn't know if the Verdance still burned green in the dark, or if the Perpetua had reached lunar shadow, or if the plan they'd built in a greenhouse over shared tea had survived even a single week without him. He knew only what he'd bought with his arrest: hours. Days, maybe. Every hour the cutter spent processing him, assembling his tribunal, arguing jurisdiction, was an hour the Perpetua wasn't being hunted at full urgency. He was a fuse, exactly as the sub-routine had planned—but a fuse burns slow when you make it, and he intended to smolder.

The medic appeared at the edge of his bunk, a thin shadow in the dark, and pressed something into his hand. A scrap of salvaged print-film, folded small. On it, in careful handwriting, was a single line copied from some smuggler's forbidden archive:

*"Law is what the powerful call their fear."*

Below it, a second line, newer, in different handwriting:

*"Then let them be afraid."*

Reyes held it a long time in the dark. Then he folded it away against his skin, next to his heartbeat, and closed his eyes.

In the morning, the alarms began.
