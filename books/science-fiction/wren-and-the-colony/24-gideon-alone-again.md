## 24. Gideon Alone Again

The skiff's running lights died two hundred kilometers out, and Gideon watched the dark swallow them the way water swallows a stone—completely, without comment. Somewhere beyond that darkness, Callum Reyes was being folded into the belly of the Justice cutter *Ardent Meridian*, his wrists in polymer cuffs, his voice steady as he told the marines exactly what he'd told her he would tell them: *I acted alone. The gardener knew nothing. Search her station and you'll find nothing but tomatoes.*

He had smiled when he said it. That was the worst part. He had smiled like a man setting down something heavy.

Now she floated where the Verdance used to be—or rather, where its shattered remains still tumbled, a slow constellation of greenhouse glass and hull plating and one bent solar arm catching starlight like a fishhook. She'd taken the survey skiff out to look at it because she couldn't not look at it. Eleven years of her life hung out there in pieces, turning over and over, showing her every face of itself: the seed vaults she'd welded shut, the root cellar hatch she'd sealed with her own hands while the deck shuddered under the first kinetic strike, the long fracture down the spine of the habitat ring where the air had gone out all at once, taking the smell of wet soil and basil and warm metal with it.

She had loved that smell more than she had ever loved a person.

*Not true,* said a small voice in her head that sounded suspiciously like Reyes. *You know that's not true anymore.*

She killed the thought and turned the skiff away from the wreck, toward the moon-shadowed coordinates where the *Perpetua* drifted with ten thousand sleeping souls aboard and a hull full of patched breaches and borrowed time.

* * *

Inside the ark's dim command bay—the lights at quarter-power, everything rationed now, even light—Iyo met her at the airlock with a data slate and an expression Gideon had learned to read as *bad news delivered efficiently.*

"Power audit's done," Iyo said. "You're not going to like it."

"I haven't liked anything since Tuesday."

"Then this will be consistent." The linguist fell into step beside her, boots silent on the deck plates. Iyo walked like someone who had spent two hundred years asleep and was still negotiating with gravity. "The Verdance gave us what we could strip before the cutter arrived—fuel cells, the backup reactor core, your atmospheric processors' spares. It bought the *Perpetua* another year of cryo draw. Maybe fourteen months if we run the pods at minimum metabolic support."

"Minimum." Gideon tasted the word. "Define minimum."

"Ninety-one percent survival projection instead of ninety-eight." Iyo didn't soften it. She never softened anything; it was one of the things Gideon trusted about her. "Seven hundred people, give or take, who die in their sleep so the rest can keep dreaming safely."

Gideon stopped walking. The corridor stretched ahead of her, lined with pod alcoves behind frosted glass—rows upon rows of them, each one a small lit window with a face inside. She had walked this corridor a dozen times since the first revival wave and it had never once stopped feeling like walking through a held breath.

"And the other option?" she asked, though she already knew. She'd done the arithmetic herself on the flight back from the wreck, scribbled on the back of a seed inventory sheet in pencil, because some numbers you don't want to type.

Iyo handed her the slate anyway. "We divert everything to revival. Full power to waking people in waves, fast, before the cells deplete. We get maybe three thousand up and functional within six months—but then the ark is dead. No reserve for the descent, no reserve for surface infrastructure, no margin for error. And the remaining seven thousand stay frozen in a dead ship until we can build them new pods from scratch, which takes materials we don't have, on a planet whose only industrial base is—" she gestured vaguely at Gideon, at all of her, "—a burned garden."

"There's also the first colony," Gideon said quietly. "Meridian Prime. The descendants. They have reactors. They have manufacturing."

"They have a Conclave that just tried to sterilize this system, and a population that doesn't know we exist." Iyo's voice stayed level, but something moved underneath it, tectonic. "And they have eleven thousand colonists of their own in their own arks upstairs, drawing their own power. Everyone wants the same wattage, Wren. There isn't enough."

Wren. They'd started calling her that aboard the ark too, after the story spread—after Sergeant Okafor's mutineers told it, and retold it, growing a small bird into the telling each time. She hadn't corrected anyone. It felt less like a name than a coat someone had draped over her shoulders, slightly too big, warm anyway.

They reached the observation gallery at the corridor's end. Beyond the glass, the dead world turned—her world, the one she'd terraformed for strangers, its green continents visible even from here, a handprint of life pressed against all that gray. Somewhere down there, in the southern temperate belt, the Perpetua's landing shuttles were supposed to touch ground in five weeks. If there was still power for a descent.

"The council convenes at 0600," Iyo said. "Sefa asked me to tell you she wants you there. Not as a guest. As a vote."

"A vote." Gideon laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Ten thousand people, and my vote counts the same as everyone else's?"

"No." Iyo looked at her, and for once the linguist's precision failed her; she seemed to be choosing words the way you choose footing on ice. "Your vote counts more. You found us. You woke us. You burned your home to shield our descent. Whatever you say at that council, half the room will treat it as law and the other half will treat it as a betrayal. That's not a vote, Gideon. That's a verdict you'll deliver on yourself."

She left Gideon alone with the glass and the turning world.

* * *

She didn't go to her bunk. She went to the small hydroponics bay the woken crew had built her—a gesture, a gift, four racks of greens under grow-lights salvaged from the Verdance's wreckage—and sat on the floor among the seedling trays with her back against the nutrient tank, because it was the closest thing to soil she could reach.

This was where she did her thinking. It always had been. Plants didn't rush you. Plants didn't ask you to be wise by morning.

The choice arranged itself in the dark above the grow-lights, patient as a weed.

Option one: keep them sleeping. Preserve the reserve, protect the ninety-eight percent, wait for a solution that might come from diplomacy, from the fractured Conclave, from the cutter's mutinous crew, from anywhere. But seven hundred would die in their dreams, slowly, silently, and she would know their names—she'd been cataloguing the manifest for weeks, she knew all ten thousand names now, she read them like prayers—and every one of those seven hundred deaths would sit on her ledger beside the garden she'd already burned.

Option two: wake them fast. Save the three thousand who could be saved quickly, spend the ship doing it, gamble everything on human hands being cleverer than arithmetic. But that meant betting seven thousand lives on a planet that had tried to kill them twice in one month, with a Justice cutter still in-system and a First Minister who had ordered orbital fire without hesitation or apparent appetite loss.

And underneath both options, coiled and quiet, the third thing. The thing Sefa had named in her testament, the thing Iyo had found countersigned in the command deck archive: *the other ark.* Meridian Prime, with its eleven thousand colonists and its functioning infrastructure and its population that had been raised on a history with half of humanity deleted from it. The hawks on the newly-woken council—Vashti Oduya and her faction, engineers and soldiers with two centuries of abandonment crystallized into grievance—had already said it aloud in the first session: *their ancestors took our share of the world. We can take it back. Their reactors are right there.*

Gideon pressed her palms flat against the deck and felt the faint hum of the dying reactor through her bones, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Eleven years ago, the Terraforming Office had given her a garden and one instruction: *maintain the beacon, ask no questions.* She had obeyed. She had told herself obedience was neutrality, that tending tomatoes in orbit was a morally weightless act. Then she'd opened one locked file, and then another, and learned that her garden grew in soil enriched by confiscated lives—that every beautiful thing she'd ever raised was fertilized with theft.

She had promised herself, floating in the wreckage of the Verdance while the cutter's searchlights swept past: *never again will I not choose.* Choosing badly was survivable. Not choosing was how the Conclave had happened at all—ten thousand small officials declining to ask questions, each one a gardener keeping her head down among the rows.

But oh, God, the weight of it. This was why the old religions put decisions in the hands of councils and kings and gods—because no single person should have to feel what she felt right now, the specific gravity of ten thousand names balanced on one pair of tired hands. Reyes had understood that. It was the last thing he'd said to her, through the airlock, before the marines took him: *Don't carry it alone, Gideon. That's how they win. One person holding everything is a person who can be broken.*

So she wouldn't hold it alone. But she couldn't set it down either—not tonight, not with the vote hours away and both factions watching her for a sign.

She pulled the seed inventory sheet from her pocket, the one with the pencil arithmetic on the back, and smoothed it on her knee. Seven hundred names' worth of difference between the options. She stared at the numbers until they blurred.

That was when she noticed the tray beside her—the one she'd planted herself, nine days ago, wheat from the Verdance gene-bank. Perpetua-era cultivar, she knew now. Stolen stock, grown in stolen soil, tended by stolen hands. And yet: it had sprouted. Of course it had. Wheat didn't care about provenance. Wheat came up through whatever dirt it was given and leaned toward whatever light remained.

She sat with that for a long time.

* * *

At 0400, unable to sleep, she made her way to the medical bay where Sefa lay in the slow process of full reanimation—muscles relearning themselves, lungs remembering weather, a woman in her nineties thawing back into a body that had been paused mid-century. Gideon expected her to be asleep. Instead the old woman was propped against pillows, awake, watching the pod-status display on the wall with eyes that missed nothing.

"You walk loudly for a ghost," Sefa said. Her voice was still gravelly, still rebuilding itself, but her mind had come back sharp as a blade kept in oil. "Sit. You've been circling this deck for an hour. Iyo told me."

"Iyo tells you everything."

"Iyo is afraid of me. It's efficient." Sefa patted the chair beside her bed. "Tell me what you've decided."

"I haven't. That's the problem. Every option spends someone."

"Yes." Sefa said it simply, without comfort, which was somehow the most comforting thing available. "Welcome to leadership. The Conclave's genius wasn't cruelty—it was making sure no one ever had to feel the weight. Each clerk signed one form. Each guard stood one post. Ten thousand people, none of them murderers, and half of humanity erased." She turned her head on the pillow, and her gaze pinned Gideon the way the old woman's gaze always did, gentle and total. "So. Don't be them. Feel all of it. And then decide anyway."

"What if I decide wrong?"

"Then you decide wrong in daylight, with your name on it, and you spend the rest of your life repairing what you broke. That is the entire difference between us and them." Sefa closed her eyes briefly, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and the century she'd slept through. "But I'll tell you what I see, since you didn't come here to be consoled. You're thinking of the seven hundred who die if we sleep, and the seven thousand at risk if we wake. You're forgetting the eleven thousand upstairs who are already awake and don't know we exist—and the fact that every path forward runs through them. Sleep or wake, fight or share, nothing holds unless the two colonies become one people. So perhaps the question isn't *which ark gets the power.* Perhaps it's *what do we do in the next five weeks that makes the answer belong to everyone.*"

Gideon stared at her. "That's not a decision. That's a miracle with a deadline."

"We were a civilization once," Sefa said, and smiled with old, unhurried amusement. "Miracles were mostly logistics."

* * *

At 0550, Gideon stood outside the council chamber—a repurposed cargo bay, benches scavenged from the crew quarters, the Perpetua's seal painted on the bulkhead by someone with more hope than skill. Through the open door she could hear the room filling: Vashti Oduya's faction on the left, loud and certain; the moderates clustered near the middle; Iyo near the front with her slates and her terrible clarity; Okafor, who had crossed the void to stand with mutineers, sitting with the careful stillness of a man who had already lost one institution and didn't intend to lose another.

On the wall screen, the agenda glowed in Old Meridian script and Common both:

**ITEM ONE: ALLOCATION OF REMAINING POWER RESERVE.**
**ITEM TWO: THE QUESTION OF MERIDIAN PRIME.**

Two items. One word each way. Ten thousand lives in the balance, plus eleven thousand more who weren't in the room and didn't know the room existed.

Gideon stood in the doorway with her speech unwritten and her hands shaking, and understood, finally, what Reyes had meant. One person holding everything is a person who can be broken. But a room full of people willing to hold it together—

She stepped inside.

The chamber went quiet. Two hundred faces turned toward her, and somewhere in the silence she heard the hum of the dying reactor, the heartbeat through the wall, counting down.

"Before we vote," Gideon said, and her voice steadied as she said it, "there's something you all need to hear. A transmission came in four hours ago, on the old beacon frequency. It's addressed to the *Perpetua* by name." She lifted the slate. "It's from Meridian Prime. Someone down there knows we exist—and they're asking to talk."
