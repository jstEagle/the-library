## 25. The Arithmetic of Souls

The council chamber of the *Perpetua* had been a hydroponics bay once. Gideon could tell by the irrigation channels still scored into the deck plating, by the anchor points where grow-racks had hung for a generation before someone stripped them out and welded a horseshoe of tables in their place. Someone had tried to make the room beautiful anyway—strips of salvaged copper ran along the walls in patterns she almost recognized, Meridian knotwork, the same spirals her grandmother had stitched into her winter coat.

Twelve people sat at those tables, and every one of them was looking at her like she was something that had crawled out of the dark between stars.

She understood it. She'd spent eleven years tending a garden built from their stolen inheritance, wearing a Conclave uniform, breathing air paid for with their confiscated power cells. If she sat on their side of the table, she'd look at herself the same way.

"The arithmetic is simple," said Dagny Okonkwo-Hale, and her voice filled the chamber the way cold fills a hull breach. She was broad and gray-templed, an engineer's calluses on her hands, and she hadn't stopped looking at Gideon since the meeting began. "The first ark holds nine thousand colonists in cryo. Their systems are intact because they were maintained—by you, gardener, with power we should have had. Our pods are failing. We've lost four hundred souls since the Collapse. Four hundred people who died in the dark because the Conclave decided we were redundant." She laid one callused palm flat on the table. "The Verdance has fusion capacity to spare. The surface colony below has infrastructure, water rights, arable land already terraformed—at our expense. The solution is not negotiation. The solution is seizure."

Murmurs moved around the horseshoe like wind through wheat. Not all of it agreement. Some of it fear. Gideon had learned to hear the difference over eleven years of listening to plants, which taught you that everything alive makes two sounds: the sound it wants you to hear, and the sound underneath.

"And when you take it," Gideon said, "what then?"

Dagny's eyes narrowed. "Then we live."

"You wake nine thousand people onto a world whose only settlement is three hundred descendants of the people who abandoned you. You hold them at gunpoint—or you don't, and they hate you—and either way you spend the next fifty years farming land you stole, next to people who teach their children your name the way my grandmother taught me the Conclave's: as a warning." Gideon kept her voice level. It cost her. Her hands wanted to shake and she wouldn't let them. "You'd be building the Conclave again. Different flag. Same arithmetic."

"Careful," said a man down the table—Voss, thin-faced, a cryo-tech whose fingers still trembled from two centuries of frozen sleep. "You don't get to lecture us about what we'd become. You weren't in the pods."

"No," Gideon said. "I was alone instead. Eleven years talking to tomato vines because there wasn't another human being within forty million kilometers who would answer." She let that sit. "I'm not here to tell you your anger is wrong. Your anger is the truest thing in this room. I'm telling you what it will cost if you spend it all at once."

* * *

They had woken her at 0400 station time, or what passed for it aboard the *Perpetua*, where the lights cycled on a schedule Iyo had reconstructed from Meridian liturgical calendars because nobody aboard remembered what morning felt like anymore. A young woman named Tamsin—born planet-side? No, born aboard, Gideon reminded herself; there were children on this ship who had never seen a sky—had come to her bunk in the guest compartment with the news that the Council of Woken had convened and her presence was requested.

*Requested.* The word had done its work. Gideon had dressed in the dark and followed Tamsin through corridors that smelled of ozone and old plastic and, faintly, impossibly, of antiseptic and cold—the smell of ten thousand sleepers.

She had walked those corridors slowly, reading the walls. Names, thousands of them, hand-painted in Meridian script in columns that climbed toward the ceiling. She'd asked Tamsin what they were.

"Everyone," Tamsin had said simply. "Everyone who's aboard. We paint them so the ship remembers us even if the Conclave doesn't."

Gideon had stood in front of that wall for a long time. Somewhere in those columns were names of people whose genetic lines now grew in her gene-banks—the Perpetua cultivars, the stolen seeds, the wheat and sorghum and nitrogen-fixing legumes she had pruned and sung to for a decade without knowing she was tending a graveyard.

Now she stood in the converted hydroponics bay, under copper knotwork, before twelve faces, doing the hardest thing she had ever done: defending mercy to people with every reason to hate.

* * *

"We have a tactical assessment," said Okonkwo-Hale, when the murmuring died. "Would you like to hear it, gardener? Since you seem to believe we haven't thought this through."

"I'd like to hear it."

The engineer tapped the table and a holo bloomed above it—the system, rendered in amber wireframe. The dead world. Its moon. The wreck of the Verdance, still tumbling in high orbit, half its spine slagged by the Justice cutter's barrage. The second ark, the original colony ship, hanging in its parking orbit like a lantern no one had lit.

"The first ark's defenses are nominal but unmanned. Its crew complement in cryo includes perhaps sixty personnel with military training, based on manifest estimates. Ours includes eleven hundred. We control the *Perpetua*'s drive, its shuttle fleet, and as of yesterday, thanks to your linguist, its point-defense grid." Dagny's voice was flat, professional, terrible. "We could take both ships inside six hours. We could take the ground settlement inside a week. The Conclave is fracturing—your broadcast saw to that—but fracture takes time to become paralysis, and paralysis is a window. We move now, we consolidate, and when the new order comes asking questions, we're the ones holding everything worth asking about."

"That's not consolidation," Gideon said quietly. "That's just conquest with better paperwork."

"Everything is conquest with better paperwork," Voss snapped. "Your Conclave taught us that. They wrote us out of existence with *paperwork*. Manifests marked lost. Registries purged. Two centuries of bookkeeping, and ten thousand people became a rounding error." He leaned forward, and up close Gideon could see the frost-scarring on his cheekbones, the damage slow thawing leaves on human skin. "Don't stand in our chamber and preach patience. Patience is what killed the four hundred."

The words landed like a blow. Gideon took them, because they were true, and because taking them was the only honesty available to her.

"You're right," she said. "Patience didn't save them. Nothing saved them. And nothing you seize now will unkill them." She turned, slowly, addressing the whole horseshoe. "But listen to me. I have lived among the first ark's descendants' data for eleven years. I've read their logs, their letters home, their children's school records—I catalogued everything for the beacon archive. Do you know what they know? Nothing. They don't know you exist. Their grandparents' grandparents were told the *Perpetua* was a supply hauler lost with all hands. They light memorial candles for empty space." She paused. "When Broadcast Zero hit their receivers, do you know what the first response was? Not fear. A woman named Anouk Vasquez-Terhune—you don't know her, she's a water-systems engineer, third generation—she sent a message to the Verdance wreckage before anyone else did. One line. *How many of you are there, and what do you need?*"

Silence in the chamber. Real silence, the kind that has weight.

"She asked what you needed," Gideon repeated. "Before she asked anything else. That is the seed you'd be planting into, if you chose soil instead of fire. And you want to answer it with boarding parties."

"They'll change their tune," Dagny said, but her voice had lost some of its iron. "Charity is cheap when you're the one holding the stores. Wait until their own pods start failing. Wait until their grandchildren need the water we'd be drinking."

"Maybe," Gideon conceded. "People are people. But here's the difference, and it's the whole difference: if you take it, you'll never know whether they would have given it. You'll live inside your own suspicion forever, and you'll raise your children inside it, and in eighty years there'll be a wall between your settlement and theirs, and in a hundred and twenty there'll be a war, and in a hundred and fifty there'll be a tribunal somewhere writing *lost with all hands* about somebody else." She spread her hands. "I watched the Conclave do this math for eleven years. Trust me—it always balances. It balances perfectly. That's what's wrong with it."

* * *

It was Iyo who broke the deadlock, though not in the way anyone expected.

The linguist had sat silent through most of the session, in the seat furthest from Gideon, her dark eyes moving between speakers like someone translating not words but intentions. Now she raised one hand—a small motion, almost apologetic—and the chamber stilled, because Iyo's silences had already acquired weight in the week since she'd cracked the ark's command deck.

"May I read something?" she asked. "From the record I unlocked. The extermination order."

Dagny's jaw tightened. "Iyo—"

"Not the order itself. The annotations." Iyo rose, and Gideon saw how thin she was, how the long sleep had hollowed her, and how she carried it like a scholar carries a lamp. "There were marginal notes in the original document. Handwritten, scanned in. For years I assumed they were procedural. Yesterday I finished transcribing them." She looked around the horseshoe. "They were written by First Minister Adeyemi's ancestor—the countersignatory. And they are not triumphant. Listen."

She unfolded a flimsy sheet covered in her own careful transcription, and read:

*"Signed under protest. The redundancy determination is politically necessary and morally indefensible, and I record here that history will judge us, whoever survives to write it. May the ones we abandon find better stewards than we proved to be."*

The words hung in the recycled air.

"He knew," Iyo said softly. "He signed it and he knew. And his descendant built a garden station above this world and staffed it with one woman and told her to ask no questions—because he could not bring himself to finish the crime, and could not bring himself to undo it. So he made a compromise. He made—" she turned, and her eyes found Gideon's, "—he made a *wren*. A small keeper of a small green place, positioned exactly where she'd be found, someday, by exactly the right signal."

"That's speculation," Voss said hoarsely.

"It's annotation," Iyo replied. "I deal in texts. Texts testify."

Gideon's throat had gone tight. She had never let herself wonder why her posting had been so strange—why a terraforming office would park a single gardener above a dead world with a beacon pointed at nowhere. She had filed it under *Conclave inefficiency*, the great forgiving category. Now the shape of it stood revealed, and it was worse and stranger than cruelty: it was a guilty man's half-measure, a door left unlatched by someone too cowardly to open it himself.

"So the man who condemned us also built the thing that saved us," Dagny said slowly. "What is that supposed to tell us?"

"That people contain contradictions," Gideon said. "That mercy and complicity can live in the same signature. And that the question in front of you isn't whether the Conclave deserves your forgiveness—it doesn't, and no one's asking for it. The question is what you build on the far side of your anger. You can build a copy of them. Or you can build the thing they were too afraid to build." She looked at each face in turn. "I can't make that choice for you. I only know what I watched happen on the cutter. Half those marines turned the moment they heard the truth—not because they were cowards, but because some part of them had been waiting their whole lives for permission to be decent. People are waiting for that permission everywhere. Including down there. Including in this room."

* * *

The vote, when it finally came, was not the landslide either faction wanted.

They debated for six hours. Gideon gave testimony twice more—once on the Verdance's remaining resources, once on the surface settlement's actual capacity, numbers she provided from memory because her logs had burned with the station and part of her was glad. Voss presented his seizure plan in full detail, and it was good, she hated to admit, clean and fast and cruel as a scalpel. A woman named Ferro who had been a judge before the Collapse argued law. A teenage boy who had been born aboard and would die aboard if the pods failed argued desperation, and his voice cracked on the word *aboard*, and Gideon watched Dagny Okonkwo-Hale close her eyes as if the sound physically hurt her.

In the end the council voted seven to five. Seven for contact and negotiation. Five for prepared seizure—*contingency posture*, Dagny insisted on calling it, and Gideon let her keep the phrase, because a kept phrase is a pressure valve, and pressure valves save lives.

Seven to five. Not victory. A reprieve, purchased at the price of admitting that nearly half the room believed mercy was a luxury the dead couldn't afford.

Afterward, in the corridor outside, Dagny fell into step beside her. They walked past the painted names in silence for a while, the columns of the erased climbing into the dark overhead.

"You're good in a hearing," the engineer said at last. "Eleven years alone and you argue like a diplomat."

"I argued with the tomatoes mostly. They're a tough audience."

Something that was almost a smile crossed the older woman's face and died there. "Understand me, gardener. I voted with you because of the boy. Because if we take this world by force, he grows up a conqueror's son, and conquerors' sons inherit wars." She stopped walking and faced Gideon directly, and up close her eyes were the color of engine coolant, pale and steady. "But if your precious negotiation fails—if the planet-side people hesitate, or haggle, or offer us a refugee camp instead of a homeland—I will bring the seizure plan back to this council, and next time I'll have the boy's cracked voice in my pocket when I argue. So don't fail."

"I'll try not to," Gideon said.

"One more thing." Dagny glanced down the corridor, then lowered her voice. "Medical woke another pod this morning. Full revival, against protocol—they had to, the pod was failing and it was revive or lose her. She's old, gardener. Older than anyone on this ship has any right to be. She was awake for eleven minutes before the Collapse, back in the bad years. She was on the original council. She signed the protest list." Dagny's voice did something complicated. "Sefa Amari. She's awake, and she's asking for the woman from the garden. By name. She says—" the engineer hesitated, clearly quoting, "—*she says the wren should come sing to her, because she has things to say that only a gardener will understand.*"

Gideon stood very still beneath the painted names, ten thousand strong, and felt the floor of the future shift beneath her feet.

"When?" she managed.

"Now," said Dagny Okonkwo-Hale. "She's waiting."
