## 26. Sefa Wakes Fully

The cold left her in stages, like a tide going out.

First the extremities returned — a burning in her fingers, her toes, as if someone had packed them with embers. Then the great dull weight of two hundred years lifted off her chest, and Sefa Okonkwo-Reyes drew a breath that tasted of recycled air and antiseptic and, faintly, impossibly, of green things growing.

She opened her eyes to a ceiling paneled with grow-lights, and a young woman's face hovering above hers, backlit into a halo.

"There you are," the woman said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "There you are. Take it slow. Your heart's been asleep longer than most civilizations last."

Sefa tried to answer and produced only a rasp. The woman — Vasquez, they called her, the gardener, the ghost-tender of the Verdance — held a cup of something warm to her lips. Broth. Salt and fat and heat. Sefa drank it down like a sacrament.

"How long?" she managed, when her throat would obey.

"Two hundred and eleven years since your pod sealed." Gideon Vasquez's eyes were red-rimmed. She had been crying, or had been about to cry for a long time, which amounted to the same thing in a person who lived alone with ten thousand sleepers. "The first time I woke you, you told me what happened. You told me about the schism. Then your heart stuttered and we put you back under for three weeks while we repaired the damage."

"Three weeks." Sefa worked the arithmetic. "And how many of us walk now?"

"One hundred and forty. A hundred from the first wave, forty more this week. Iyo's crew has the command deck half-lit. The engineers say we can hold maybe four hundred awake before life support starts complaining." Vasquez hesitated. "They're arguing again. In the mess hall. About what to do when the Conclave comes."

Sefa closed her eyes. Of course they were arguing. She had spent the last years of the old world arguing too, in rooms much like that one, with people who had every reason to be angry and no idea yet what anger cost.

"Help me sit up," she said. "And find me clothes that aren't two centuries out of fashion. If my people are going to argue, I intend to be there for it."

* * *

Walking the Perpetua's corridors was like walking through a photograph of her own funeral.

She recognized everything and nothing at once. The bulkheads were the same grey composite she'd helped install as a young engineer, though now scarred with micrometeorite pocks and patched with materials she didn't recognize — resin-grown lattice, living sealant, things the gardener's people had clearly improvised across decades of neglect. The gravity plating hummed at a slightly wrong frequency, a half-tone flat, and her inner ear filed a complaint her pride refused to voice.

People stopped to stare as she passed. An old woman, upright, unassisted, wrapped in a borrowed thermal coat. Word had traveled ahead of her: *the one who woke twice. The one who remembers.*

A young man — sixteen, maybe seventeen, born aboard during the long dark from genetic stock kept viable by rotation — fell into step beside her and asked, breathless, if it was true she'd been at the Meridian Accords. She said it was true. He asked what Adeyemi's grandfather had been like in person. She said: *shorter than his statues, and crueler than his speeches.* He laughed like she'd given him a gift.

In the mess hall, the argument was already at a boil.

Sefa stood at the doorway a moment, unseen, and listened.

At the head of the long table sat Dario Venn, who had been a colonel of the Perpetua's defense auxiliary before the Collapse and had apparently never stopped being one, even through two centuries of ice. He was a broad man gone lean in revival, all wire and conviction, and he was speaking with his hands flat on the table the way he'd spoken in the war councils Sefa remembered from the fall of Meridian Station.

"—and I'm telling you, when the cutter arrives, we will have exactly one window. We take both arks' power reserves, we take the Verdance's orbital infrastructure, and we descend with everything. Ten thousand of us on the ground before their marines can spell the word *treason*. Vasquez's gardener friends can file complaints afterward, if there's anything left of them to complain with."

"And if the gardener objects?" That was Iyo — the linguist, sharp-eyed, arms crossed. "If Gideon Vasquez refuses to hand over her station?"

"Then she'll understand eventually. Or she won't, and history will thank us anyway."

"History," Iyo said, "is exactly what the Conclave thought it was writing when it erased us. I've read the extermination order, Colonel. I know how that sentence begins. It begins with *for the greater good*."

Venn's jaw tightened. Around the table, perhaps thirty people — the waking leadership, such as it was — divided visibly into camps. Sefa counted them the way she'd once counted casualties: the hawks clustered near Venn, maybe a dozen; the uncertain in the middle, watching; and a handful around Iyo whose faces said they had already decided and were only waiting for permission.

Sefa stepped into the room.

The noise died the way a fire dies when the air is cut off. Thirty faces turned. Venn rose halfway out of his chair — old habit, respect for rank that no longer existed — and then seemed unsure whether to complete the motion.

"Sefa." His voice was careful. "You should be resting."

"I rested for two hundred and eleven years, Dario. I find I've lost my taste for it." She walked the length of the table, unhurried, letting her cane click against the deck plates — she'd insisted on the cane despite the medics' objections; a woman of ninety-three was entitled to her props — and took the empty seat directly across from him. "Continue. You were explaining why we should become the thing they said we were."

A murmur ran the table. Venn's face went still.

"That's not fair—"

"It's entirely fair." She folded her hands. "Sit down, Colonel. Let me tell you what I remember, since I appear to be the only person in this room who was awake for the relevant parts."

He sat.

"I remember the night the Conclave's fleet sealed the Meridian docks. I remember the broadcasts — not the official ones, the real ones, the ones that came through on the emergency bands. Do you know what they said? They didn't say *surrender*. They said *you are not our people anymore*. That was the whole crime, Dario. Not rebellion, not sabotage — we never fired a shot. Our crime was that Adeyemi's census couldn't fit us inside his story of humanity, so he deleted us instead. Ten thousand souls, struck from the manifest, and a ship pushed into the dark with failing reactors and a prayer."

She let the silence sit. Somewhere in the walls, a coolant pump cycled, patient as a heartbeat.

"Now you propose we do the same arithmetic in reverse. Seize the other ark's reserves. Take the garden station. Because our need is greater and our grievance is older." She shook her head slowly. "Grievance is the oldest fuel in the universe, Colonel, and it burns the hottest and it lights nothing. I watched men younger than you make exactly this argument in exactly this tone, and I watched where it landed. It landed here. It landed in these pods. Two hundred years of ice because somebody's greater good required our deletion."

"The situations aren't equivalent," Venn said. "We wouldn't kill anyone. We'd requisition."

"*Requisition.*" Sefa smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "That was their word too. Requisition the genome banks. Requisition the reactor fuel. Requisition, relocate, reduce. The vocabulary of theft is always administrative, Dario. That's how you know it's theft."

From the middle of the table, a woman Sefa didn't know — one of the revived engineers, face still carrying the waxy look of recent thaw — spoke up quietly. "My grandmother's line was on the first ark. The one they kept. If we take their power, we're taking from her descendants."

"We don't know they're her descendants," Venn said. "Eleven generations—"

"They're someone's descendants," the engineer said. "That's enough."

Sefa turned to her. "What's your name, child?"

"Tomasin. Tomasin Okafor-Ley."

"Tomasin Okafor-Ley just taught this table more than I did," Sefa said. "*Someone's descendants.* Write it on the wall. And now let me tell you about the gardener, since half of you seem to think she's an asset to be managed and the other half think she's a hostage to be reassured."

She looked around the room, meeting eyes.

"I have spoken with Gideon Vasquez four times now. Once when she woke me illegally, against every protocol she was sworn to, knowing it could cost her everything. Once when my heart failed and she sat with me for six hours holding my hand because I asked her not to leave me alone in the dark. Twice since, planning. Do you know what she has asked for? For herself? Nothing. Not once. She has asked for *them*—" she pointed upward, toward the pods, toward the sleepers, "—she has argued for the ones still frozen, including the ones on the other ship she's never met. When I told her what the Conclave did with our confiscated resources — that her beautiful garden, her life's work, was built from our stolen seed banks and our stolen isotopes — do you know what she did? She wept. And then she said, *then it was yours all along, and I've been keeping it for you.*"

Silence. Venn stared at the table.

"She has spent eleven years alone," Sefa went on, softer now, "tending a garden for people who were never coming, on the faith that someone, someday, would come to plant it. And when we finally came, we came out of the dark with our grievances and our seizure plans, and the first thing some of us proposed was to take her home away from her." She tapped the table once with one knuckle. "I will not vote for that. I will speak against it in every hall, on every channel, until my heart gives out again. And I will tell you something else, Colonel, and I want everyone to hear it: the moment this ship becomes a weapon pointed at innocent people, it stops being the Perpetua and starts being the Conclave with our faces on it. And I did not survive two hundred years of ice to be resurrected into *that*."

No one spoke. The coolant pump cycled. Finally Venn exhaled, long and slow, and Sefa saw — not surrender, but the beginning of it, the crack where light gets in.

"You were always better at this than me," he said grudgingly.

"I was always older than you, Dario. It's different." She rose, leaning on her cane. "Now. We have a Justice cutter inbound and a First Minister who would rather burn a garden than confess a crime. So we don't have time for seizure fantasies or martyrdom contests. What we have time for is the truth. All of it, everywhere, at once, so fast and so wide that no one can bury it again. Bring me the comms plan. Bring me the gardener. And bring me tea — whatever passes for tea on this ship, I've earned it."

As she turned to go, Iyo caught up with her in the corridor, falling into step with the careful deference of the young around the very old.

"They'll follow you," Iyo said. "More than they'd ever follow me, and I found the order. Why?"

Sefa considered the question as they walked, past the porthole where the dead world hung below them like a promise, green-veined now at its equator where the terraforming had taken hold.

"Because I'm the past," she said at last. "And the past doesn't get to want anything anymore. That's what makes it trustworthy. When I bless something, nobody wonders what I'll get out of it." She glanced sideways at the young linguist. "But between us, Iyo — I do want something. I want to see a child planted in that garden down there. One child, in open dirt, under open sky. After that, they can do whatever they like with my bones."

Iyo smiled — the first real smile Sefa had seen from her. "Then you should hear what Gideon proposed an hour ago. She wants to hijack the beacon. Broadcast everything — the extermination order, our existence, all of it — to both arks and the cutter's crew simultaneously. Before the Conclave can frame the story."

Sefa stopped walking. Below them, through the glass, the green thread of the world turned slowly in the black.

"She wants to spend her beacon," Sefa said. "Her station's whole purpose. The thing she tended for eleven years."

"She says a beacon that only calls down destroyers isn't worth keeping."

The old woman laughed — a dry, rusted sound, surprised out of her — and wiped her eyes with the back of her hand.

"Oh, I like her," Sefa said. "Get me to the comms deck. If that girl is going to set fire to the sky, the least the past can do is stand beside her when she strikes the match."
