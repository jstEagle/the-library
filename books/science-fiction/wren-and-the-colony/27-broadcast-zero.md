## 27. Broadcast Zero

The Verdance had always hummed. Gideon had lived inside that hum for eleven years—the low chord of the hydroponic pumps, the whisper of the atmospheric scrubbers, the patient tick of the beacon pulse going out every ninety seconds like a heartbeat nobody answered. She had once told a seedling, only half joking, that the station's sound was the closest thing she had to a mother's voice.

Tonight the hum was wrong.

She stood in the command cradle with Reyes's arrest still fresh in her ears—*I'm claiming sole responsibility,* he'd said, standing in the door of the shuttle bay as if he could hold back the Conclave with his body—and she understood, finally and completely, what she had to do. The Justice cutter *Aegis Verity* hung in high orbit like a blade held above a wrist. The Perpetua crouched in lunar shadow with ten thousand sleeping souls and a handful of newly woken ones aboard. And between them, the truth sat in her hands like something molten: the extermination order, signed generations ago, countersigned by the ancestor of the man who now commanded the cutter to burn them all.

Iyo had found it in the ark's locked archives. Sefa had confirmed it from memory, in that voice like dry leaves that had outlived every tree it fell from. And Adeyemi's government had answered the discovery not with denial but with ordnance.

*You cannot unsend a broadcast.* That was what Iyo had said, translating the old Meridian warning carved above the Perpetua's transmitter bay. *That is why they fear it.*

Gideon pressed her palm against the cold console and thought about eleven years of pruning. Every cut she had ever made on the Verdance, she had made for a reason: dead wood removed so the living branch could carry more light. She looked at the beacon array status board—all green, all obedient, all hers—and felt the strange calm of a gardener who has finally identified the disease.

"Okay," she said aloud, to no one, to everyone, to the plants breathing quietly behind the glass. "Okay."

* * *

The Verdance's beacon had one purpose: to pulse a navigation signature at intervals precise enough for Conclave traffic control to log, timestamp, and ignore. It was a lighthouse built to guide ships that were never coming. For eleven years she had maintained it faithfully, oiling its servos, aligning its emitters, logging its pulses in neat columns that said, in effect, *nothing is happening here, nothing has ever happened here.*

Now she opened the maintenance root access—the same buried Conclave keys she had used to find the coordinates in the first place, keys she was never meant to have, keys that had cost Callum Reyes everything to help her keep—and began rewriting the beacon's soul.

The system fought her politely, the way all well-made systems do. Confirmation dialogs bloomed like nettles. *This action will permanently alter primary transmission function. This action requires Tier Four authorization. This action will be logged.* She typed over each objection, feeling the absurd intimacy of it, arguing with a machine the way she argued with blight: patiently, thoroughly, without mercy where mercy wasn't deserved.

"You were never a lighthouse," she told the console. "You were a muzzle."

The plan was Iyo's, refined by Sefa, blessed by the fragile council of the woken. Three targets, three frequencies. First, the original ark—the colony ships above, holding the descendants of the colonists who believed themselves to be all that remained of humanity. They had been lied to longest and most gently; their inheritance had been stolen before they were born. Second, the Perpetua itself—not because its people didn't know, but because they needed to see the proof in the Conclave's own hand, the signature, the date, the clinical language of the order: *assets declared genetically redundant; disposal authorized; records expunged.* Words that turned ten thousand people into a rounding error. Third, the *Aegis Verity*, and every marine and officer aboard her—because a weapon is only as obedient as the hand that holds it, and hands can learn what they're holding.

One transmission. All three. Simultaneous. Unrecalled.

Her fingers hovered over the execute key.

And there it was—the terror she had been outrunning for weeks, catching her at last in the quiet. Not fear of death; she had made her peace with that somewhere around the moment she'd decided to steer a barrage with her own hands if it came to that. The fear was smaller and stranger: that after this, nothing would ever be simple again. That the garden would be gone, the routine gone, the humming gone, and she would be a woman who had burned down her own house to warm strangers. That some part of her wanted, shamefully, to go back to the pruning shears and the soil chemistry and the ninety-second heartbeat, to be small and safe and alone again.

She let the fear sit with her. She had learned that from the plants: you don't fight a hard frost, you shelter what matters through it.

Then she thought of Sefa's face when the pod had opened—a face like paper, eyes like coals—and the words the old woman had rasped: *They wrote us out. Make us written back in.* She thought of Iyo reading the extermination order aloud in Old Meridian, her voice cracking on the word *redundant*. She thought of Reyes in the door, buying seconds with his career, maybe his life, asking for nothing.

Gideon Vasquez had spent eleven years talking to things that couldn't answer. Tonight she would speak to everyone.

She pressed the key.

* * *

The beacon did not pulse. It *sang*.

Full-spectrum burst, raw power drawn straight from the Verdance's reserves, stripping the greenhouse lights to emergency amber as the station gave everything it had to the sky. On her display, the transmission unfolded in three directions at once: up toward the frozen arks, out toward the moon's dark shoulder, across the gulf toward the cutter with its six hundred crew and its orders written in someone else's blood.

The file went out clean. The order, scanned page by page. The Perpetua's registry, her hull markings, her coordinates in lunar shadow—Iyo had insisted on that. *Let them know exactly where we are,* she'd said. *Hiding is how they killed us the first time.* The Verdance's own logs, eleven years of them, showing what the beacon really was, what the Terraforming Office had really asked her to tend. Everything. The whole rotten architecture of two centuries, laid open to vacuum.

There was no encryption to break. That was the point. Encryption could be dismissed as forgery; plaintext in the Conclave's own authentication format could not. She had signed it with the Verdance's official beacon key—the one thing she possessed that the Conclave could not call counterfeit without calling its own voice a lie.

*Truth as a weapon that cannot be recalled.* She watched the range counter climb and understood, with a lurch that was half nausea and half exultation, that she had just done the one thing power cannot forgive: she had made the secret public. Somewhere in the colonial network, relays were already receiving, rebroadcasting, copying. Somewhere a clerk would notice. Then another. You could silence a woman. You could burn a station. But you could not reach across the dark and take the words back out of ten thousand receivers.

"It's done," she whispered. Her hands were shaking. She laughed anyway—a small, cracked sound, the first laugh in days. "It's done. Oh, stars. It's actually done."

The greenhouse glowed amber around her, emergency-lighted rows of greens and grains, the gene-bank vaults humming their cold lullaby. She walked the aisles while the transmission propagated, touching leaves as she passed, an old habit, a goodbye or a blessing or both. *Hold on,* she told them. *Whatever comes next, hold on.*

* * *

The answer came in nineteen minutes.

First, silence—the particular silence of systems straining to listen. Then the Perpetua's channel lit up, Iyo's voice tight and bright through the static: *"Received. Verified. Rebroadcasting on colonial emergency bands now. Gideon—you did it. Every relay in the system is carrying it."*

And then, from high orbit, the other kind of answer.

The *Aegis Verity*'s weapons fire-control pinged the Verdance—once, twice, a targeting handshake as formal as a knock on a door. At the same moment, an open-channel transmission came through, unencrypted, meant to be heard: the sound of a ship tearing itself apart from the inside. Shouted orders. A voice demanding marines stand down. Another voice refusing. Sergeant Okafor's name, spoken like a rallying cry, then like an accusation.

Gideon stood in the amber light among her plants and listened to the law lose its monopoly on legitimacy in real time, and she felt no triumph. Only the terrible, vertiginous freedom of a door that can never again be closed.

"They know," she said softly. "Everyone knows."

Which meant the First Minister knew. Which meant the next transmission from the direction of the capital would not be words at all.

The alert board confirmed it before she finished the thought: a new contact burning hard from Conclave space, weapons-grade emissions, trajectory locked—not on the moon, not on the Perpetua.

On the Verdance.

On her.

Gideon took one long breath, tasting recycled air and green growing things, and reached for the beam-steering console. If the world was going to end tonight, it would have to come through the gardener first.

"Come on, then," she said to the fire falling toward her out of the dark. "I've got a garden to defend."
