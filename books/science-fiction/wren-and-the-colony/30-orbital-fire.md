## 30. Orbital Fire

The first sign was the garden itself.

Gideon had spent eleven years learning the Verdance's moods—the way the wheat rows shivered when the spin ring cycled, the faint hydraulic sigh of the irrigation mains at shift change, the particular quality of silence that meant everything was well. So when the silence changed, she felt it before any alarm could speak.

She was kneeling in the rice paddies on Deck Three, her hands buried to the wrist in warm silt, checking the nitrate levels by feel the way she'd taught herself to do when the sensors went blind in Year Four. The water lay flat and silver under the grow-lights. And then it didn't. A tremor ran across its surface, fine as a spider's leg, and the paddies began to ripple in concentric rings that had no source she could name.

"Reyes," she said into her comm. "Something's wrong with the station."

Static. Then his voice, tight and stripped of all its careful courtesy: "I know. Get to a shelter deck. Now."

"What is it?"

A pause that lasted half a second too long. "The cutter just went weapons-hot."

* * *

She did not go to a shelter deck.

Later—much later, in the ash and aftermath—she would try to reconstruct the decision as if it had been a decision at all, a clean fork in the path with one branch marked *live* and the other marked *die*. It wasn't like that. It was simpler and more terrible than that. She stood up out of the paddy, water sheeting off her forearms, and her body simply turned toward the spine of the station, toward the command blister where the beacon lived, because there was only one thing the Conclave's weapons could be aimed at, and only one thing standing between those weapons and ten thousand people falling through the dark toward the world she had grown for them.

The Perpetua had begun its descent burn ninety minutes ago. Iyo's engineers had coaxed the old ark's failing drives into something like obedience, and now the great scarred hull was committed—a hundred and forty tons of frozen history falling along a corridor that threaded directly past the Verdance, close enough that the station's mass shadow had been part of their plan from the start. Hide behind the gardener. Let the little green station be the shield.

They had always known the Conclave might call that bluff.

Gideon ran the length of Deck Three, boots hammering the grating, past the orchard tiers where the apple trees she'd grafted in Year Two reached their pale branches after her, past the seed vault with its triple-sealed door, up the ladder wells two rungs at a time while the station groaned around her like a ship in weather. The Verdance had never been built for this. It had been built to be beautiful, to be a promise visible from orbit—a lit garden hanging above a dead world, saying *someone is here, someone is waiting, come home.* Nobody had armored it. Nobody had imagined it would need armor.

In the command blister, Reyes was already there, hunched over the tactical display, his Justice Division uniform jacket gone, his shirtsleeves rolled, his hands moving across the console with the fluency of a man who had spent his whole life inside systems like this. He looked up when she came through the hatch, and what was in his face made her stomach drop.

"You should be below," he said.

"So should you." She crossed to the steering console—the old manual array, analog fallbacks, the beam-steering rig the Conclave had installed to keep the beacon's pulse precisely calibrated. Her fingers found the controls without looking. Eleven years. "How long?"

"First rounds are already inbound. Kinetic, mostly—they want deniability, not spectacle. Impact in—" he checked, and closed his eyes briefly, "—four minutes. They're targeting the reactor first, then the comms mast, then the greenhouse decks."

"They're going to burn my garden."

"They're going to kill everyone on this station, Gideon. The garden is just what they'll tell themselves they were aiming at."

She looked at the display. The Verdance hung in its slow rotation, a green-and-silver spindle against the black, and around it, falling in a long glittering arc, the Perpetua—close now, so close she could see the frost patterns on its hull through the optical feed, the Meridian script painted across its flank in letters three hundred meters tall: *WE WERE HERE. WE REMEMBERED.* And beyond both of them, small and cold and patient, the cutter *Vigilant*, opening its weapon bays like a flower that bloomed only for execution.

"The descent corridor," she said. "If the debris field expands—"

"It will. That's the point. Either they shred the Perpetua with kinetic rounds or they shred it with our corpse." Reyes's jaw worked. "Unless somebody keeps the station's attitude locked. Keeps the hull presenting along the corridor axis, keeps the spin stabilized, keeps the debris pattern narrow instead of wide." He looked at her. "That's manual. The auto-systems will try to dodge. Dodging widens the field."

"Then we don't dodge."

"Gideon—"

"We don't dodge." She said it quietly, and heard in her own voice something she'd never heard there before—not bravery, nothing so clean as bravery, just a kind of settled certainty, like soil that has finally been turned enough times to know what it's for. "Eleven years I kept this place alive for people who hadn't been born yet. You think I'm going to step aside now that they're finally coming home?"

* * *

The first round hit at 0347 station time, and the world ended in a way that was almost gentle.

There was no sound in vacuum, of course—what she heard was the station itself, the transmission of violence through structure, a deep bell-note that rose through the deck plates and up through her boots and into her teeth. The lights flickered. Somewhere far below, atmosphere screamed out of a punctured compartment into the dark, and the whole station shuddered like a struck animal.

"Reactor glancing hit," Reyes reported, voice flat, professional, holding himself together with procedure the way a man holds a wound closed with his hands. "Containment holding. Second wave inbound, sixty seconds."

Gideon's hands moved on the steering yoke. The Verdance wanted to tumble—the impact had imparted rotation, slow but growing, and every degree of tumble widened the cone of debris that would sweep the Perpetua's corridor. She countered it, feathering the attitude thrusters, feeling the station's mass respond through the yoke like a living thing, like a horse fighting the bit, like something that trusted her enough to fight her.

"I've got the axis," she said. "Talk to me about the second wave."

"Two rounds, staggered. One for the comms mast, one for—" He stopped.

"For the greenhouse decks." She finished it for him. "For the seed vault."

"Yes."

She thought of the vault then, absurdly, in the last calm seconds she would ever have: the cool dim aisles of it, the thousands of labeled drawers, the cultivars from the Perpetua's stolen gene-bank sitting alongside everything she'd bred herself over eleven years—the frost-hardy barley named for her grandmother, the rice strain that sang in the wind channels, the small stubborn flowers she'd grown from a cutting Sefa had described to her, once, through the glass of a cryopod. *It grew on the balconies of the old arcologies,* the old woman had said. *White petals. We called them morning-stars.* Gideon had grown them for six years without ever seeing one bloom, and then, eight months ago, the first bud had opened, and she had sat beside it for an hour and cried without knowing why.

"They're just plants," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on the word *just*, because it was the most dishonest thing she'd ever said.

"No," Reyes said softly. "They never were. That's the whole crime, isn't it? They knew exactly what the plants were."

The second round took the comms mast off at the root.

Through the forward optic she watched it happen: a flash, a blossoming of vapor and splintered solar film, and then the great slender spire that had been the Verdance's signature for two centuries—the finger pointing at heaven, the pulse that said *come home*—folded, broke, and tumbled away into the black, turning end over end, shedding fragments that caught the light of the distant sun like a handful of thrown glitter.

The station lurched. Alarms she'd never heard before began to sing, layered and discordant. Atmosphere howled somewhere on Deck Six. And Gideon hauled back on the yoke with both hands and all her weight, wrestling the spin, keeping the hull aligned, keeping the corridor clear.

"Hold," she whispered—to the station, to herself, to whatever was listening. "Hold, hold, I've got you, hold—"

"You've got it," Reyes said. "Axis deviation four degrees. Three. Holding." A breath. "Third wave inbound. This is the big one, Gideon. They're done being subtle. Mass driver, full charge. If that hits centerline—"

"There won't be a station."

"There won't be anything to bury."

She looked at the display again. The Perpetua was deeper into the corridor now, committed, its shuttles beginning to separate—she could see them as bright points peeling away from the mother hull, each one carrying two hundred sleepers down toward atmosphere, toward the terraformed valleys she had spent a decade preparing, toward rivers she had never waded and forests she had never walked but had grown anyway, seed by seed, season by season, for people whose names she did not know.

*This is what the garden was for.*

Not the beacon. Not the Conclave's careful pulse. Not the promise made to colonists who were permitted to exist. The garden had always been for *them*—the erased ones, the redundant ones, the ten thousand written out of the record. Every hour of her solitude had been an act of faith she hadn't known she was making. Every conversation with the plants, every log entry read aloud to empty rooms, every night she'd fallen asleep in the greenhouse smelling of loam—it had all been preparation for this moment, though no one had designed it, though no one had intended it. The garden had waited eleven years for its purpose the way seeds wait in frozen ground.

"Reyes." She kept her eyes on the yoke. "Get to the shelter deck."

"No."

"That's an order from the person who owns this station."

"And I'm arresting you," he said, and she could hear the ghost of a smile in it, broken and real. "Obstruction of a lawful bombardment. I'm placing myself under your authority instead. Take the vote."

She laughed—one short, cracked sound, startled out of her—and then the third round came.

* * *

It missed centerline by nine meters, and nine meters might as well have been the width of the world.

The mass-driver slug tore through Decks Four and Five amidships, through the orchard tiers, through the heart of everything green. Gideon felt it as a fist through the station's spine, felt the yoke kick against her palms, felt the attitude thrusters stutter as their feed lines ruptured. On the optical feed, the Verdance bloomed—there was no other word for it. A wound of white light opened along her flank, and out of it poured the garden: atmosphere carrying water, soil, leaves, petals, unripe apples, whole trees torn up root and branch, a blizzard of green and silver expanding into the vacuum in a slow, silent, terrible flowering.

Morning-stars. She saw them even at that distance, even through the smoke and spray—white petals catching the sunlight as they tumbled, hundreds of them, thousands, a snowfall of the flowers Sefa remembered from balconies that had burned two centuries ago.

"Oh," Gideon said. Just that. Just the smallest syllable of grief, wrung out of her like water from cloth.

"Decks four and five are gone," Reyes said, and his voice had gone rough. "Seed vault integrity—checking—vault's sealed, vault's holding, Gideon, the vault is holding." A pause. "But the thrusters aren't. You've lost lateral control. Can you still hold the axis?"

She tested the yoke. The station answered sluggishly, drunkenly, one bank of thrusters dead, the rest gasping. The tumble was starting again, slow and inexorable, and every degree of it swept the widening debris cloud across the corridor where the Perpetua's shuttles fell like a line of embers toward the world.

"I can hold it," she said. "For a while."

"How long?"

"As long as it takes."

He looked at her then, fully, across the shaking blister, across the red wash of the alarms, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop arguing forever. He turned back to his own console. "Then I'm routing auxiliary power to your thrusters. And I'm staying."

"Reyes—"

"Don't." Quietly. "Don't spend your breath on it. We both knew, weeks ago, how this might go. I told you in the greenhouse—I told you what the Conclave does with fuses." His hands moved steadily over the power board. "Let me choose what I burn for."

* * *

The fourth wave never came as a single blow. It came as rain.

The cutter had shifted tactics—or perhaps, Gideon thought later, someone aboard the *Vigilant* had begun to hesitate, because the follow-up fire was scattered, uncertain, rounds falling wide and then closer and then wide again, as though the gunner were aiming at a target that refused to resolve into an enemy. Or perhaps the mutiny Sergeant Okafor had seeded in the cutter's belly was already bearing fruit. She had no way to know. There was no more time for knowing.

There was only the yoke, and the corridor, and the falling ships.

Round after round struck the Verdance or passed shrieking-close through the space she held open for the Perpetua's descent. Each impact was a bell-stroke through her bones. The lights died deck by deck until only the command blister burned, red-lit, running on auxiliary power Reyes fed it hand over hand like a man bailing a boat with his cupped palms. The air grew thin and hot. Somewhere below, fires she couldn't reach ate through the hydroponics galleries, and Gideon steered the burning ruin of her life's work with bleeding hands and did not stop.

She talked to it. Of course she talked to it. She had talked to it for eleven years; she would not fall silent now.

"Hold," she murmured, hauling the nose true as another impact spun the hull. "I know, I know, it hurts. Hold anyway. Almost done. They're almost home. Look—you can see them, see the shuttles? All those people. All those people we grew the wheat for. Hold for them. Please. Please hold."

The station groaned and obeyed, the way it always had, the way a living thing obeys the one who has fed it and sung to it and kept its roots warm through every cold year.

"Deviations holding under two degrees," Reyes said. His face was gray with smoke and blood from a cut above his eye he hadn't bothered to wipe. "Shuttles are entering upper atmosphere. First heat signatures on the descent track. They're through, Gideon. The corridor's clear. The rest of the ark is coming down behind them and the debris field can't touch them now."

She let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for eleven years.

And that was when the final round came—not from the cutter's main battery but from some automated secondary system, some last spiteful subroutine executing itself with mechanical calm, the banal evil of a machine following orders no human present had the courage to give. It struck the Verdance low and aft, where the reactor lived.

The lights went out entirely. For one endless second Gideon floated in absolute darkness, weightless, deaf, her hands still locked on a yoke that no longer answered—and then the emergency cells coughed alive, bathing the blister in amber, and the station spoke to her one last time in a voice she knew better than any human voice: the deep structural moan of a living thing dying slowly, on its own schedule, refusing to hurry.

"Core breach in—" Reyes checked, and laughed, a strange soft sound. "Nineteen minutes. Nineteen minutes. The containment's buying us time. It's actually buying us time."

"Then we have nineteen minutes to get to the shelter deck," Gideon said, "and you're going, and I'm not."

His head came up sharply. "No."

"Somebody has to hold the axis until the last shuttle clears the corridor. The main ark hull is still descending. If the station tumbles into its path—" She shook her head. "You have a trial to stand, Callum. You have testimony the whole future needs. I have a garden." She smiled at him, and found that the smile was steady, that her hands on the yoke were steady, that terror had burned all the way down through her and come out the other side as something else entire—as devotion, plain and total, the devotion of a woman for the thing she had raised from nothing. "Go. That's not a request. That's the last order the owner of this station will ever give."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he crossed the blister, took her face in his smoke-blackened hands, and kissed her forehead like a vow.

"Nineteen minutes," he said. "I'm sending Okafor's shuttle to the aft dock. When the last shuttle clears, you run. Promise me. Promise me and mean it, Gideon Vasquez, or so help me I will carry you out of here over my shoulder in front of your own tomatoes."

"Go," she said.

He went. The hatch sealed behind him, and Gideon Vasquez turned back to the yoke alone, in the amber dark, in the ringing wreck of everything she loved, and held her garden's course against the whole descending weight of the sky.

Below her, through the shattered forward optic, the world turned—green valleys and silver rivers, the terraformed continents glowing in the dawn line, and falling toward them, one by one, the bright shuttles carrying ten thousand waking dreams.

"Come home," she whispered to them, the words she had broadcast to the dark for eleven years, the words the beacon had carried until the Conclave cut it down. "Come home. Come home. I've got you. I've got you."

Behind her, unseen, unheard, the reactor's countdown ticked toward zero—and in the sealed root cellar beneath the greenhouse, three decks below the fires, a door she had forgotten she had built waited in the dark.
