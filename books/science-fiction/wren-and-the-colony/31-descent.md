## 31. Descent

The shuttle's name was *Mercy of Small Hours*, which Iyo had always thought was the most Perpetua thing imaginable—naming an atmosphere-burning coffin after a kindness nobody expected to receive.

She sat strapped into the jump seat behind the pilot's station, her hands flat on her thighs because if she let them go anywhere near a console she would start touching things, and touching things during re-entry was how you died. Through the viewport ahead of her, the world was coming.

Not a planet. Not yet. A curve of darkness with a thin burning seam along its limb, like a coal catching at its edge. The terminator line crawled. And along the night side, scattered in clusters like embers kicked from a fire—lights. Cities, Iyo's mind supplied, and then corrected itself: no. Not cities. Settlement lights. Outposts. The descendants of the first ark, the *Vigil*, the one the Conclave had kept and the histories had kept and the school lessons had kept, while the *Perpetua* and her ten thousand slept in the dark and were written out of the dark.

"Two hundred kilometers," the pilot said. Her name was Dagny Okonkwo-Reyes—no relation to the Inspector, she'd been quick to say when the joke made its first rounds, and slower to say that she'd checked. She flew the way Iyo imagined Gideon Vasquez gardened: total attention, no wasted motion, a kind of tenderness expressed as precision. "Interface in ninety seconds. Everybody breathe like you mean it."

Around Iyo, the cabin held forty-one people. The first landing wave. Chosen in six frantic days aboard a dying ark by a council that hadn't existed a month ago: engineers, medics, agronomists, two linguists—herself and old Tomas Vey—and Sergeant Adaeze Okafor, who had walked off a Conclave cutter with her sidearm surrendered and her spine intact, and who now sat across the aisle in borrowed Perpetua coveralls with her arms crossed and her eyes closed, not sleeping, praying, maybe, to whatever gods marines kept after their institution broke.

The hull groaned. A long, low note, like a whale song played through a ship's bones.

"Plasma sheath forming," Dagny said. "Windows go opaque in ten. Last look is now."

Iyo took it. The night curve of the world, the ember lights, and—high above, at the edge of visibility, a point of green-gold radiance that was not a star because stars did not flicker in rhythms, and this one pulsed like a heartbeat, like a beacon, like a woman.

*Verdance.* The garden station. The gardener.

The windows went white, then orange, then a roaring featureless gold, and the world disappeared, and there was only the sound of their own small ship fighting the sky.

* * *

Sefa had asked to ride in the second shuttle, and the council had told her no, absolutely not, you are eighty-nine years revivified and you are the conscience of the movement, and she had thanked them for their concern and boarded the second shuttle anyway, because she was eighty-nine years old and had been dead for two hundred years and had precisely no remaining interest in being protected from the things she had earned the right to see.

Now she sat in the forward row, the only passenger there, while the plasma fire ate the windows, and she held in her lap a small cloth bundle: a handful of seeds. Wheat from the Perpetua's gene-bank, the old strains, the ones the Conclave had confiscated and "improved" and patented and forgotten. Gideon Vasquez had asked for them by name, in her last transmission before the descent window opened. *Bring the old wheat. I want to see what we're working with.*

*I*, Sefa thought. *We're working with.* As if she would be there in the fields with them. As if she were not, at this moment, alone on a station above them, steering Conclave fire away from their descent corridor with her two hands.

The shuttle bucked. Somewhere behind her, a young engineer began quietly reciting the periodic table, which Sefa understood to be a form of prayer.

She had made this crossing once before, in a way. Two hundred and eleven years ago, she had boarded the *Perpetua* at Meridian Station under protest, her wrists still bruised from the arrest, her daughter's hand in hers, her daughter's daughter in her daughter's arms. *We'll sleep a little,* she had told the child. *And wake somewhere green.* The Conclave's tribunals had called it relocation. The manifest had called it genetic redundancy management. Her own mouth, in those last hours of the old world, had called it what her grandmother's songs called the long dark, and she had sung those songs to the child until the cold took them both, and the child had not woken anywhere green, because there was no green, because the green had been taken from them and given to the other ark, the loyal ark, the ark that got to be real.

And now—

Now a stranger had sung her grandmother's songs back to her through a hull breach, in an accent two centuries drifted, and had opened the pods, and had wept at the sight of them, ten thousand sleepers, and had said: *Your garden was built with your confiscated resources. I'm so sorry. I didn't know. I tended it my whole life and I didn't know whose it was.*

Sefa had looked at this woman—this lone, sun-starved, plant-talking woman who had spent eleven years growing food for strangers she'd been told were colonists and had learned, in one terrible week, were thieves' heirs—and Sefa had seen the only thing that mattered: she had not looked away. She had learned the truth and she had not filed it away, had not buried it, had not done the small comfortable cowardice that the whole Conclave was built on. She had turned her life into a weapon and pointed it at the sky.

The shuttle shuddered hard, and the bundle of seeds shifted in Sefa's lap, and she cupped it with both hands, the way you cup a flame.

*Hold on, gardener,* she thought, to a woman she had spoken to for perhaps four hours total, awake. *Hold on. We are coming to your green. Make it be worth it.*

* * *

In the first shuttle, forty kilometers up and falling, the heat shield's ablation was throwing sparks past the viewport like a forge in a windstorm, and Sergeant Adaeze Okafor opened her eyes and looked at death, professionally.

She had been doing threat assessment since the plasma sheath formed, out of habit, and the assessment was this: the *Mercy of Small Hours* was a two-hundred-year-old atmospheric shuttle that had never once flown, maintained by cryo-sleeping engineers and one miracle-working linguist, descending through an active weapons engagement, toward a surface whose habitability was a promise made by a single woman with a greenhouse and a beacon. By every metric in the Conclave assault manual, this was a doomed operation.

She found, to her own considerable surprise, that she did not care.

Three weeks ago she had been a marine of the Justice Division, and the law had been a load-bearing wall in her soul. Then Broadcast Zero had come through every speaker on the cutter *Halcyon's Fist*—the extermination order, the signatures, the First Minister's ancestor's name at the bottom like a curse—and she had watched the wall come down, and had watched half her squad pick up the pieces and half of them stand there bleeding, and she had chosen, in the end, to walk toward the pieces.

"Okafor." Dagny's voice, calm as still water. "You're my ground coordinator when we're down. Comms will be garbage in the ash interference. You remember the rally protocol?"

"Beacon pulse, three short, one long," Okafor said. "Rally at the tallest green thing."

A few people laughed, the way people laugh when the alternative is screaming. The tallest green thing. The entire surface plan, the whole landing scheme, the whole desperate hope of ten thousand sleepers and one defected inspector and one mutinied marine squad, rested on the premise that a woman named Gideon Vasquez had actually done it—had actually turned a dead world's red dust into something that could feed them, had spent eleven years building a home for people she'd never met, some of whom her own government had ordered her to help erase.

"Forty seconds," Dagny said. "Chute deploy on my mark. Brace positions. And—" her voice did something almost human, almost broke, "—look alive, Perpetua. We're almost home."

*Home.* Okafor turned the word over. She had a home, technically. A housing allocation on Meridian Station, a plant she'd never watered, a life sized exactly to her function. She had traded it, three weeks ago, for a seat on a shuttle full of ghosts.

The ship hit the thick air like a hand slapping water, and the screaming of the hull changed pitch, and the deceleration pressed her into her seat with the weight of every choice she'd ever made, and through the last clear sliver of viewport she saw it:

Green.

Not the green of a screen, not the green of the Verdance's grow-lamps. Green under an open sky, green to the horizon in river valleys and terraced slopes, green with the specific, unmistakable texture of things that had been tended. Someone had fought this world for a decade, alone, and the world had lost.

"Chutes out," Dagny said. "Mark."

The deceleration hit like a fist. Okafor's teeth clicked shut. The cabin filled with the rip and crack of fabric, the lurch and swing of the shuttle under canopy, and then, impossibly, a new sound—thin and high and filtered through the hull, but real, real, real:

Wind. Actual wind, moving over an actual world.

And beneath it, faint, from the shuttle's external pickups, a signal. Three tones. Short, short, short. Then one, long.

The rally pulse. From the surface. From a hand-held transmitter, at ground level, held by someone waiting for them.

"They're down there," the young engineer said, periodic table abandoned. "Someone's *down there*."

"Of course they're down there," Okafor said, and heard her own voice steady and warm and entirely changed. "Somebody had to plant the garden."

* * *

The second shuttle came down hard.

Sefa felt it through the whole frame of the ship—a lurch, a grind, the canopy snapping open with a sound like tearing cloth, and then a drop, a swing, the ground rising up with terrible hospitality—and then impact, a bone-deep slam, the shriek of struts, a long grinding slide through soil and something soft and yielding, and then silence, and then the hiss of the hull cooling, and then, muffled through two centuries of sleep and one terrible minute of falling, birdsong.

Sefa unbuckled her hands first. Then, slowly, everything else.

The hatch cycled. Ground crew—Perpetua engineers, faces gray with shock and joy in equal parts—cranked it open from outside, and daylight poured in, and Sefa stood at the threshold of the ship that had been her tomb and her nation, and looked at the world.

They had come down in a long green valley. The shuttle had plowed a furrow through what the agronomists were already on their knees identifying as a cover crop—clover, vetch, nitrogen-fixers, planted in rows straight as scripture. Beyond the rows, orchards climbed the valley walls in terraces. Beyond them, a river, silver in the late light. And everywhere, everywhere, the smell: soil. Wet, living, mineral, complicated soil. The smell her grandmother's songs had tried to describe and failed, because songs were made for people who had never left it.

"Ma'am," said a young engineer at her elbow, gently. "You should stay back until we—"

Sefa walked out of the ship.

Her boots—boots issued two hundred years ago, boots made for a world that never came—sank half a centimeter into the loam, and she stood there and let the world hold her weight, and the wind moved her white hair off her face, and somewhere in the terraces a bird sang four notes and waited, and sang them again.

She knelt. It took a while, and no one rushed her. She put her bare hand flat on the soil, the way you'd put a hand on a sleeping child's back to feel it breathe.

"We came," she said. Out loud. To the dirt, to the dead, to her daughter, to the ten thousand still frozen in the dark above. "We came after all. Forgive us the delay."

Behind her, the shuttle's ramp was filling with the others, the waking dead, blinking in the first open sky any of them had ever seen. She heard Okafor's voice from the first shuttle's landing site, two kilometers off, crackling through the bad comms: *Beacon pulse confirmed. Rally at the tallest green thing.* And she heard someone answer, laughing and crying at once: *Which one? There's a whole valley of them.*

Sefa stayed kneeling a moment longer. Then she opened the cloth bundle in her lap, and took out a single seed of the old wheat, and pressed it into the soil with her thumb, not deep, just deep enough.

"Your turn," she told it.

* * *

Iyo stood on the ridge above the valley at dusk, when the shuttles had been secured and the wounded were bandaged and the count had come back—thirty-nine alive, two broken legs, one crushed hand, zero dead—and looked up.

The sky was doing things she had no words for. She was a linguist; words were her country; and the sky of this world had annexed her whole vocabulary in a single hour. The sunset was not a color, it was a *weather of colors*, and the first stars were coming out through it, and among the stars—

There. Moving. A spark, too slow for a meteor, too bright for a satellite, crossing the dark from the zenith toward the western horizon, and behind it, a smear of fire, and behind *that*—

She didn't let herself finish the thought.

The Verdance was falling. They all knew it; the last transmission from the station had said it in Gideon Vasquez's voice, calm as a woman reading a planting schedule: *Conclave barrage inbound. I'm steering it off your corridor. Get everyone down. Get everyone down and plant something. That's an order. I've decided I outrank everyone now.*

The spark crossed the sky, and the fire behind it brightened, and around Iyo on the ridge, forty people and then a hundred and then the whole growing camp of the descent wave went silent and still, every face turned up, every person in the first generation of the free Perpetua watching their garden burn.

"Is she—" the young engineer started, beside her.

"Don't," Iyo said.

The spark crossed the whole sky, trailing its long gold ribbon, and did not come down in the valley, did not come down anywhere near them, and then it went behind the western mountains, and the fire went with it, and the sky was only sky.

No one spoke. The wind moved the terraced orchards below, and the leaves made the sound that leaves make, which is the sound of ten thousand small green things saying *here, here, here* to the wind, and Iyo thought: *She made this. Every leaf saying here. Eleven years of her hands, saying here, here, here, to people she'd never met, and we came, and she's not here.*

Old Sefa's voice came up the ridge path behind her, quiet, cracked, certain.

"She's alive."

Iyo turned. The old woman stood with the wind pulling at her hair, looking at the western horizon, at the place where the fire had gone.

"You can't know that," Iyo said gently.

"I held her hand when she woke me," Sefa said. "Women like that don't die with the work unfinished." She looked at Iyo, and her eyes were dry and fierce and two hundred years old. "Get the long-range array up tonight. I don't care about the ash interference. I don't care about the Conclave. Tomorrow at first light, this valley is going to answer that beacon pulse of hers—three short, one long, over and over, until the whole sky hears it." She turned back to the horizon. "She spent eleven years calling into the dark for us. We are *not* leaving her in it."

Below them, in the camp, someone had already started a fire, and someone else was unpacking seed crates, and the first free night on the new world was filling with the sounds of the living: hammering, singing, a marine's laugh, the river going by.

Iyo took out her transmitter and keyed the channel to the array team, and gave the order, and looked west one last time.

Three short. One long. The pulse went out into the dark where the garden had been.

And then—faint, broken, half-drowned in static and ash, but unmistakable, from somewhere under the western mountains—

It answered.
