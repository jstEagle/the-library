## 32. The Burning Green

The first strike took the eastern arm of the Verdance at 0347 station time, and Gideon felt it in her teeth before she heard it—a deep structural groan rolling through the deck plates like the earth clearing its throat.

She was strapped into the beam-steering console in the old relay core, the room she'd spent eleven years walking past without a second glance because it had never mattered. Now it was the only room in the garden that mattered. Around her, screens bloomed with fire.

"Come on," she whispered to the console, to the station, to herself. "Come on, hold."

The second cutter had come out of the dark on a trajectory no honest ship would fly—no deceleration burn announced, no hail, no courtesy. Just the Conclave's answer to Broadcast Zero arriving at four kilometers per second, and then the first lance of plasma shearing through the habitat ring where she'd eaten every meal of her adult life.

The Perpetua's descent corridor glowed amber on her tactical display: a ribbon of safe space threading down toward the southern continent, toward the river valley she'd terraformed one microbial generation at a time, toward forty shuttles full of ten thousand sleeping souls burning atmosphere right now. She could see them on the long-range feed—streaks of orange against the black limb of the world, each one carrying people who had been written out of existence and were, at this moment, writing themselves back in.

And between those streaks and the cutter's guns sat the Verdance.

Her.

*You were always more than a garden,* Sefa had told her, three days ago, gripping Gideon's hands with ice-cold fingers. *You just didn't know what for.*

Gideon knew now.

* * *

The beam-steering array had been built for a purpose she'd only understood after Iyo cracked the command deck: the beacon wasn't just a summons. It was a targeting lattice. The Conclave had installed it so that any future "correction" of the site could be guided precisely—surgical, deniable, clean. The same array that had sung her lonely pulse into the dark for eleven years could catch incoming fire and bend it.

Bend it, if someone held the manual override. The automation had been burned out in the first strike. Every correction from here forward would be hands and eyes and judgment, a human being physically holding the station's death in a chosen direction.

Reyes had wanted to stay. She'd ordered him onto the last shuttle to the Perpetua with the seed vaults and the wounded, and he had stood in the airlock door—the same way he'd stood in her greenhouse door weeks ago—and said, "This isn't how I wanted it."

"It's exactly how you wanted it," she'd said. "You stood in the door. That was always your job." She'd kissed him once, hard, tasting salt that might have been either of theirs. "Go be implicated somewhere else. Go testify. That's the fight you can win. This one's mine—I'm the only one left who knows this station's bones."

He'd gone. She'd watched his shuttle cross the gap and dock, and then she'd sealed the relay core and put her hands on the console, and the war had arrived.

* * *

The third strike hit the hydroponics bay.

She felt it as a scream in the hull and then as silence—the particular silence of a pressure seal doing its job, of a section dying cleanly so the rest could live. On her damage display, Bay Four went from green to grey to a slow spreading red.

Bay Four. The tomatoes. The dwarf wheat she'd coaxed back from two-hundred-year-old stock, generation after generation, selecting for patience. The night-blooming jasmine she talked to when logging reports felt too much like talking to nobody.

"That one was almost ready," she said out loud, and her voice cracked in the middle, and she let it. There was no one left aboard to hear her be strong. "You were almost ready."

The console chirped. Incoming volley, bearing theta-seven, projected impact along the northern spine—if it landed true, it would punch through the core and take the steering array with it, and after that nothing would stand between the cutter's guns and the descent corridor.

Gideon's hands moved. Eleven years of muscle memory—pruning cuts, soil probes, the delicate violence of keeping living things alive—poured themselves into the yoke. The station rolled. Thrusters meant for station-keeping, for gentle orbital housekeeping, fired at emergency tolerances they had never been rated for, and the whole garden groaned like a ship in weather.

The volley came down. She caught it on the array's edge and threw it wide, and the plasma lances skated off into empty space, spending themselves against nothing, and for one heartbeat the tactical board was quiet.

Then the counter-thrust slammed her sideways in her harness, and something in her left side tore with a wet, white heat, and the world went briefly silver at the edges.

* * *

She came back to the taste of blood and the sound of her own breathing, which was wrong—too shallow, catching on something. Broken rib, maybe worse. Her hands were still on the yoke. That was the important thing. Her hands had stayed.

"Systems status," she rasped, and the station answered in its calm machine voice, listing what was dead.

Comms: functional. Steering array: sixty percent. Hull integrity: failing by degrees across nine sections. Life support in the core: forty minutes of atmosphere, less if she kept bleeding.

Forty minutes. The shuttles needed twenty-two more to clear the engagement envelope and enter the corridor's protected approach.

Twenty-two minutes. She laughed, and laughing hurt so badly she saw stars, and then she kept laughing anyway because it was funny—it was genuinely, cosmically funny—that after everything, the arithmetic finally came out in her favor. For eleven years she had been a woman with too much time and nowhere to spend it. Now she had exactly enough.

"Okay," she told the garden. "Okay. We can do this."

* * *

The fourth volley she caught and redirected into the debris field, using a dead cargo module as a shield, buying thirty seconds. The fifth came while she was still recovering from the maneuver, and she couldn't move fast enough—she took it on the western arm instead, sacrificing the arboretum deliberately, choosing which part of her life's work would die so the rest could.

She watched the great trees go. The ironwoods she'd grown from cuttings labeled in Old Meridian, the ones whose tags she'd never been able to read until Iyo translated them: *Property of the Perpetua Expedition. Do not destroy.* Trees planted by the condemned, tended by the unwitting, and burned by the guilty, all inside three hundred years.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'll plant better ones. I promise. Down there. In real dirt."

The sixth volley she caught clean. The seventh clipped the array itself, and her display strobed warnings, and the steering response went mushy in her left hand—she compensated, leaning her whole body into the yoke like a sailor leaning into a tiller, riding the station's wounds.

Blood ran warm down her side and pooled in the seat. She found she wasn't afraid. That surprised her most of all. She had been afraid her whole life, quietly, constantly—the fear of being unnecessary, of tending things for people who would never come, of mattering to no one. And now, hurt and dying in a room full of smoke, holding a burning garden between ten thousand strangers and the guns meant for them, she felt the fear go out of her like water out of a cracked pot, and what remained underneath was simple and enormous.

*This is what I was for.*

Not the pruning. Not the logs. Not the lonely pulse singing to an empty sky. This. The holding. The standing between.

* * *

At minute nineteen, the cutter changed tactics.

She saw it coming on the tactical feed—the ship repositioning, swinging wide to get an angle on the descent corridor itself, abandoning the pretense that the Verdance was the target. If it got that angle, no amount of steering would matter. It would simply shoot past her, into the stream of shuttles, and ten thousand people would become a debris field.

"No," Gideon said. "No, you don't."

There was one thing left. She'd known about it since hour one, filed it away the way she filed everything, and hated it, and saved it anyway. The array's capacitors could overload—dump their entire charge in a single uncontrolled burst, a raw broadcast scream on every frequency, powerful enough to blind the cutter's targeting systems for minutes. Enough time for the shuttles to reach the corridor's shadow.

It would also fry the array permanently. It would end her ability to steer, to shield, to do anything but float here in a dying hull.

And it would broadcast. Everything still in the buffer. Broadcast Zero, again, at full power, punched straight through the Conclave's jamming—not just to the cutter but to both arks, to the colonial relays, to anyone in the system with a receiver. The extermination order. Adeyemi's signature in his ancestor's hand. The truth, one more time, at a volume no one could pretend not to hear.

"They'll hear it," she whispered. "Whatever happens after—they'll all hear it."

Her finger hovered over the override.

On the tactical display, the shuttle streaks crawled toward the amber corridor. Eighteen minutes to safety. The cutter, six minutes from firing position.

She thought of Reyes standing in doors. Of Sefa's cold hands. Of Iyo reading the dead language aloud like a psalm. Of the jasmine, which bloomed only at night, which you had to be awake in the dark to smell.

She thought of the children. Someday there would be children down there, in the valley, running between rows she would never see. They wouldn't know her name. Maybe they'd know a story—a gardener, a bird, a light in the sky that refused to go out. That would be enough. That had always been enough.

"Verdance," she said formally, to the station, the way she'd spoken to it a thousand mornings over coffee. "Thank you for keeping me. I'm sorry we couldn't keep each other longer."

She pulled the capacitor release and held the broadcast key down with both hands.

* * *

Light filled the relay core—not the gentle glow of consoles but a white roar, the station's whole voice screaming at once. The buffer emptied into the dark: the order, the names, the coordinates of the grave that wasn't a grave, the testament of a woman named Sefa who had refused to die quietly for two hundred years. Gideon felt it leave her like something physical, like the last weight lifting off a scale.

The cutter's targeting systems died screaming. On her display, the ship lurched, blinded, its firing solution dissolving into static.

And the Verdance began to come apart.

It happened the way a tree falls—slowly at first, then all at once. The overloaded array blew its housings; decompression took the core ring in a rush of shrieking air and glittering frost; the beautiful, doomed structure she had called home for eleven years folded inward on itself like a flower closing at dawn, except backward, except in fire.

Gideon rode it down.

Shrapnel opened her scalp and she barely registered it. The console died under her hands and she kept holding it anyway, because holding was the job, because someone should be holding when the end comes, because the plants deserved not to burn alone. Through the cracking viewport she could see the planet turning below—green along the river valley now, her green, their green, the first true green this world had worn since before the Collapse—and descending through the clouds, patient as seeds falling, the shuttles of the Perpetua.

They made it. She watched them make it. Streak after streak sliding into the corridor's shadow, into the atmosphere, home, while the station that had sheltered their garden broke into a constellation of burning fragments around her.

"Go," she told them, and coughed blood, and smiled. "Grow."

* * *

The cold came last, after the fire, as the core section tumbled free of the main wreck. Vacuum reached for her through a hundred hairline fractures, and the emergency field flickered, weakened, held—barely, imperfectly, a dying breath of pressure around the little pocket of the relay core where she lay cradled in her harness among the ruins of the console.

Around her, drifting in the low gravity, were the things the explosion had shaken loose from the research lockers: sample vials, data chips, a dried seed packet stamped in Old Meridian. A single cutting from the jasmine, torn from its mount, turning slowly in the air beside her face, impossibly alive-looking, green as a promise.

Gideon reached for it. Her arm obeyed slowly, grudgingly, the way old machinery moves. She caught the cutting and closed her fingers around it and held it against her chest, over her heart, the way you hold a thing you intend to plant.

The beacon was dead. The array was slag. But deep in the wreck's shattered spine, in a sealed root cellar beneath the greenhouse levels—built for quarantine, forgotten by everyone but her—banks of live cultures slept on in the dark, breathing quietly, waiting. And somewhere below the clouds, on the green shore of a brown river, people who had been ghosts were stepping onto soil.

She had done it. Whatever happened in the next minutes—in the next breath—she had done it. The truth was loose in the system and could not be recalled. The ark was down. The garden was passing from her hands into ten thousand pairs of hands, and that was the only immortality she had ever wanted.

"Gideon Vasquez," she said aloud, to the dark, to the fire, to whoever might someday read the black box she could feel still recording against her spine. "Terraforming Officer, Third Class. Keeper of the Verdance. I stayed at my post."

Her vision narrowed to a green-gold tunnel. The cutting pressed against her heart. The planet turned below, vast and patient and newly alive.

Somewhere far away—or very close, it was impossible to tell—she heard a sound through the ruin of the hull: thrusters, small ones, a shuttle flying reckless and low through the debris field where no sane pilot would fly, sweeping the wreckage with searchlights, a voice on an open channel calling her name over and over, getting closer.

She tried to answer. Her lips moved around the words.

The light swept the broken viewport, and held, and did not move on.
