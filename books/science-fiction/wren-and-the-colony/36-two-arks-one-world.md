## 36. Two Arks, One World

The doctors at Meridian Station told her she was lucky. Gideon had decided, somewhere between the third skin graft and the first time she'd walked unassisted down the medbay corridor, that luck had nothing to do with it. She had simply refused to die in a room full of dead plants.

Her hands were what bothered her most. The burns had taken the fine skin of her palms and fingers, and the regrown tissue was new-pink and hypersensitive, so that everything she touched arrived in her awareness a half-second late, slightly wrong, like hearing your own voice on a recording. She kept flexing them anyway. Testing. A gardener's hands were instruments, and instruments could be retuned.

Outside the viewport of her recovery quarters, the world turned. The actual world. Not a simulation, not a projection from the Verdance's observation deck—she'd spent eleven years looking at Meridian through glass and sensors, and now she looked at it through glass again, but this time there were ten thousand people down there who hadn't existed three months ago, planting things in soil she had made.

She still couldn't quite make herself believe it. Some mornings she woke expecting the hum of an empty station, the silence of a garden built for no one, and the absence of that silence was louder than any sound.

A knock. Two soft raps—the rhythm Iyo used, always, as if afraid of startling her.

"Come in."

Iyo entered carrying a slate and a thermos of something that smelled like chicory and, faintly, like hope. She was Perpetua-born, third generation frozen, twenty-six years old biologically and two hundred and forty by calendar, and she had appointed herself Gideon's unofficial keeper within a day of learning what the Verdance's beacon operator had sacrificed. Gideon found this both touching and exhausting.

"Negotiations resumed an hour ago," Iyo said, settling into the chair beside the bed without being asked. "You should know what they're doing with your charter."

"It's not my charter."

"It's your plan. Everyone in that room knows it. Half of them are arguing against it *because* it's yours." Iyo poured two cups and handed one over. "The First Ark delegation wants land apportionment by population share. Straight arithmetic. The Perpetua council wants reparations first—formal acknowledgment of the extermination order before anyone puts a seed in the ground."

"And you?"

"I think both sides are negotiating with ghosts." Iyo blew across her cup. "The First Ark people are negotiating with their grandparents' crime. We're negotiating with our grandparents' grave. Nobody in that room is actually talking to anybody alive."

Gideon sipped. The chicory was bitter and perfect. "What did Sefa say?"

"That's the interesting part." Iyo's mouth curved. "Sefa stood up—stood up, mind you, the woman is a hundred and six years old and was ice for two centuries—and said, 'I did not survive the dark to watch my children hoard the light.' Then she sat back down and let them scream at each other for another hour."

Gideon laughed, and the laugh pulled at the grafts along her ribs, and she didn't care. "That sounds like Sefa."

"There's more. Okafor sent word from the cutter. The tribunal vote went to the floor of the Conclave assembly last night. Broadcast Zero is being read into the official record. Adeyemi has suspended the session—'suspended,' which everyone understands to mean 'ended.'" Iyo set down her cup. "The Conclave isn't falling, exactly. It's... evaporating. Regional councils are declaring interim authority faster than the center can object. Which means, in about a week, nobody is going to be telling either ark what to do."

"And everybody will have to decide for themselves." Gideon looked out at the turning world. "That's the hard part. That's always been the hard part."

* * *

They brought her down to the surface on the ninth day, against medical advice, because the negotiation council had voted—over Iyo's objection—to hold the final session at the site of the first settlement, and because Sefa had sent a message that consisted of exactly one sentence: *"If Gideon Brightwater cannot attend, we will wait until she can."*

The shuttle descent was the first atmosphere Gideon had felt in eleven years. It pressed against the hull like a living thing, shivering and moaning, and she pressed her new-pink palm flat against the viewport and felt the heat of reentry even through the glass, and she thought: *this is what the Perpetua's people never got to feel. This is what was taken.*

The first settlement sat in a river valley on the northern temperate belt—the same valley where, years ago, her automated soil drones had reported the richest loam on the planet, the valley she had seeded with test plots from orbit and then watched, helplessly, from four hundred kilometers up, as green crept across the drone imagery like ink spreading through water. Now there were structures there. Modular habitats, white and utilitarian, arranged around a central commons where someone had already planted windbreak rows of hybrid alder.

Someone had also raised a flag. Two flags, actually—one bearing the First Ark's old colonial sigil, a stylized sun over furrows; the other the Perpetua's mark, which Gideon recognized now from the hull scarred with Old Meridian script: a circle broken by a line, the symbol of a people who had been written out of the record and had written themselves back in.

The flags flew side by side. They were not the same height. Someone had noticed, and someone else was already on a ladder fixing it, and Gideon watched this small correction with her throat tight, because it seemed to her like the whole negotiation in miniature: two symbols, imperfectly balanced, being adjusted by hand.

The council chamber was a converted habitat module, too small for its purpose, benches crowded, the air warm with too many bodies. When Gideon entered, leaning on Iyo's arm, the room did something she hadn't expected: it went quiet, and then it stood. Not all of them. But enough. First Ark delegates and Perpetua survivors rising together, an awkward, unchoreographed gesture, some of them clearly unsure whether standing was right, whether it was owed, whether it was theater.

Sefa remained seated, of course. She beckoned.

Gideon crossed the room and took the seat beside her, and the old woman studied her with eyes that had seen the Collapse and the schism and two hundred years of dark, and said, quietly, "You look terrible."

"I've been told I'm lucky."

"Luck." Sefa snorted. "In my day we called it stubbornness and gave it no medals." She patted Gideon's burned hand, gently, avoiding the new skin. "Now listen. They're about to tear apart your garden plan, and I want you to hear how they do it, because the way people destroy a good idea tells you everything about what they're afraid of."

* * *

They tore it apart for six hours.

The plan itself was simple—Gideon had built it simple on purpose, because complexity was where trust went to die. One territory, not two. Land allocated not by population or by claim but by watershed, each river valley governed jointly, its fields planted from a common gene-bank that merged the First Ark's seed lines with the Perpetua's cultivars—including, though she had not said this aloud, the varieties the Conclave had stolen from the abandoned ark and hidden in the Verdance's vaults. Every harvest surplus pooled. Every child educated together. And at the center of it, the thing both delegations kept circling and refusing to name: the founding charter would open with a formal reading of the extermination order, signed and countersigned, followed by a formal renunciation—not apology, renunciation—of every Conclave edict that had made it possible.

"The order is history," said Delegate Amara Okonkwo of the First Ark, a broad-shouldered woman whose family had farmed the southern plateau for four generations. "My people didn't sign it. My people didn't know it existed until three months ago. You want us to open our founding document with a confession we didn't commit?"

"You inherited the benefit," said Councilor Dagny Voss of the Perpetua, and her voice was iron wrapped in wool. "Four generations of undivided inheritance. The whole planet, Gideon Brightwater's whole garden, built on resources stripped from our hull while we drifted. You inherited the crime's proceeds. The confession comes with the property."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then we take the southern continent and you keep the north, and our grandchildren grow up hating yours across an ocean, and in a century someone writes a new extermination order, and the wheel turns." Voss spread her hands. "I am offering you the cheap option, Delegate. It only costs you your pride."

Okonkwo's jaw worked. Around the chamber, delegates murmured, aligned, realigned. Gideon watched the geometry of it—the way fear moved through a room like water finding level—and understood that neither woman was wrong. That was the trap of it. Both of them were right, and rightness, unreconciled, was just a slower war.

It was mid-afternoon when the argument reached the gene-bank, and that was where it nearly broke.

"The stolen cultivars," said a Perpetua agronomist named Halden, young, precise, furious in the way only the very competent can be furious. "The Conclave took two hundred varieties from our agricultural archive. Some exist nowhere else. The proposal says these go into the common bank. I want to know why the proceeds of theft become community property instead of being returned to their owners."

"Because their owners are dead," said Okonkwo.

"Their owners are *us*. We are the same people. The pods opened. We're here."

"Then take them back. Take them and plant them on your own continent, behind your own border, and let the record show that when this species got its second chance, it reached for the fences first."

Gideon heard Sefa exhale slowly beside her, and knew the old woman was letting it run, letting the room see its own face. But Gideon had spent eleven years talking to plants because no one else listened, and she had learned something in that silence that no negotiation manual contained: that a living thing does not argue about whose water made it grow. It grows. The question of ownership dies at the root.

She stood. Her legs shook. She stood anyway.

"May I?" she said, and the room, which had been waiting for exactly this, went quiet.

* * *

"I'm not a delegate," Gideon said. "I'm not Perpetua and I'm not First Ark. For eleven years I was nothing but a caretaker for people I believed were coming, and it turns out half of you were coming and half of you were already here, and I didn't know about either of you, really. So take what I say for what it's worth—which may be very little."

She held up her hands, the new-pink hands, and let them see.

"These are from the Verdance. From the night the Conclave tried to burn the truth out of orbit. I stayed at the beam-steering console because the barrage had to miss the Perpetua's descent corridor, and staying meant this." She closed her fingers, opened them. "I don't tell you that to claim anything. I tell you because I need you to understand what I learned while the station came apart around me."

She paused. In the quiet she could hear the valley outside—the wind in the alder rows, actual wind, moving through actual leaves.

"I grew every plant on that station for colonists who hadn't been born yet. People in freeze, people I would never meet. I talked to them. I logged the beacon pulse every day for four thousand days and nobody answered, and I kept gardening anyway, and I want to be honest with you: I told myself it was devotion. It wasn't, entirely. Part of it was that a garden for the unborn asks nothing of you. It can't disappoint you. It can't disagree with you. It can't sit across a table and say your grandfather was a thief." She looked at Halden, then at Okonkwo. "You two are doing something harder than I ever did. You're gardening for the born. For people who are here, and present, and inconvenient, and real."

She turned to the chamber.

"Halden is right. Those cultivars were stolen. The record should say so—every variety, its origin, the date it was taken. Write it into the charter's annex. Let the theft be documented forever, so no one can ever say it didn't happen." She paused. "And Okonkwo is right that punishing the descendants of a crime they didn't know about is just a slower way of committing a new one. So don't return the cultivars to the Perpetua alone. Return them to *everyone*—with the theft recorded on the label of every seed packet, generation after generation, so that no child ever plants those varieties without knowing where they came from and what was done. Make the memory part of the planting. Don't fence the past. Compost it."

Silence.

"Compost," repeated Okonkwo, tasting the word.

"Grief and guilt both rot down into the same thing," Gideon said, "if you turn the pile often enough. I've done it for eleven years. It's the only process I fully trust."

Voss laughed—a short, sharp bark, quickly suppressed, but the room heard it, and something in the room's posture shifted, a degree of angle, a loosening. Sefa's hand found Gideon's wrist and squeezed once.

Halden stood. He looked at Gideon for a long moment, and then at his own delegation, and Gideon saw him wrestling with something she recognized: the difficulty of setting down a grievance that had become part of your structure, load-bearing, so that removing it threatened the whole building.

"The annex," he said finally. "Full documentation. Every variety, every date, read aloud at the first planting and at every anniversary planting thereafter. If the charter includes that—if the memory is structural, not decorative—then the Perpetua's agricultural council will release its claim to sole ownership." He sat down heavily, as if the words had cost him blood.

Okonkwo looked at him for a long time. Then she said, "Read aloud. Every year. By alternating readers—First Ark and Perpetua, in the same ceremony." She extended her hand across the aisle. "And the watersheds are joint. All of them."

Halden took her hand.

The chamber did not erupt in cheers. Gideon had half-expected it to, some movie version of reconciliation, but what happened instead was quieter and better: people began to talk. Really talk—delegates leaning across aisles, agronomists pulling out slates and comparing soil surveys, someone from the First Ark asking a Perpetua engineer about cold-climate grain strains and someone else laughing at a joke whose setup Gideon missed. The agreement hadn't ended the tension. It had given the tension somewhere to go.

Sefa leaned close. "Compost," she murmured. "Two hundred years I carried that anger like a stone, and this burned-up gardener tells them to compost it, and by God, I think it might work." She shook her head. "The young ones will ruin it in new ways, of course. That's what young ones are for. But they'll ruin it *together*."

* * *

The charter was ratified at dusk, in the commons, under the two flags—now, finally, at equal height. The text was read in full, including the extermination order, including the renunciation, including the annex of stolen things. When the reader reached the list of cultivars, her voice caught on the entry for a wheat variant designated *Meridian-7*, and Gideon realized the reader was crying, and that half the crowd was, and that crying together in a cold valley at the end of a long day was perhaps the oldest technology humanity had, and the one that had survived the Collapse best.

Afterward, Iyo found her at the edge of the firelight, where Gideon had gone to be briefly alone with the dark and the smell of woodsmoke and growing things.

"You should see this," Iyo said, and handed her a slate.

It was a message relayed from the rebuilt station overhead—the New Beacon, still under construction, its skeleton catching the sunset in orange lines. The message was from the engineering team, flagged routine.

But beneath the routine flag, a single line of sensor data: a long-period echo, faint, repeating, detected at the edge of the system during the previous night's calibration sweep.

Not noise. Not ghost transmission. A structured pulse, in a pattern the Verdance's own beacon had once used—the old summons pattern, the call for ships still drifting.

Something else was out there. Something else had heard.

Gideon stood in the firelight of the world she had helped save, holding the news of the next one, and felt the old loneliness stir and, for the first time in eleven years, find itself surrounded.

"They'll need a garden too," she said.

Iyo smiled. "Then we'd better get planting."
