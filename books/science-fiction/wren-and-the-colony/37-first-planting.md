## 37. First Planting

The soil broke open under Iyo's hands like something waking.

She had spent her whole life in the dark—first the Perpetua's corridors, then its cryo-sleep, then the long weeks of learning to walk again under lights that were too bright and air that was too thick with the smell of living things. She had read ten thousand pages of Old Meridian poetry about earth, and none of it had prepared her for this: the way real dirt resisted, then yielded, warm and damp and full of worms she could not bring herself to look at directly because they were too much like a miracle.

"Deeper," said the boy beside her, one of the Verdance-born, maybe nine years old, born planet-side in the settlement's first fragile months. His name was Tam. He held a seed tray against his chest like a shield. "You have to go deeper or the wind takes them."

"The wind," Iyo repeated. She had never felt wind until three months ago. She still flinched when it touched her face.

Tam grinned and dug his own hands into the furrow to show her, unafraid of anything, this child who had never once been cold in the way the Perpetua's people understood cold. Around them, spread across the terraced valley that Gideon Brightwater had spent eleven years coaxing into existence from orbit, four hundred people knelt in the dirt.

* * *

Sefa could not kneel anymore. Her knees had been old before the ice took her, and the two centuries since had not improved them. So they had given her a chair—a real chair, wooden, made by hands that had learned joinery from Gideon's archived manuals—and set it at the head of the first row, where the ground sloped down toward the river.

From here she could see all of it: the mixed lines of planters stretching down the valley, Perpetua survivors and First Ark descendants alternating in every row because Gideon had insisted on it, drawn up the planting order herself on a slate board while she was still supposed to be resting. *Alternate,* she had written. *Every row. You don't plant a hedge with one species. It falls to the first blight.*

Sefa had watched her say it, thin and gray-faced and propped up on someone else's arm, and had thought: *there is the whole charter, written in gardening advice.*

The old woman's hands rested on the seed tray in her lap. Barley. The label was handwritten, in two scripts—Conclave Standard and Old Meridian—the letters pressed into the tag by some archivist two hundred years dead who had catalogued the confiscated harvests of a murdered people and never known what they were for. Sefa ran her thumb over the Meridian characters. *Hordeum vulgare, line 7.* Her mother's people had grown barley on the northern steppes before the Collapse. She had told Gideon this once, in the greenhouse, in the small hours, and Gideon had gone quiet and then said, "Line 7 is in the gene-bank. It's been in the gene-bank the whole time."

The whole time. While Sefa slept in the dark, her people's seeds had been tended like relics by a woman who didn't know whose they were.

"You're crying, grandmother," said Tam, who had appeared beside her chair the way children did, soundless and sudden.

"I'm watering the seeds," Sefa said. "It's an old technique."

He laughed and wiped her cheek with his sleeve, matter-of-fact, and she loved him with a fierceness that startled her. Two hundred years ago she had buried a grandson in the Perpetua's recycling bay because there was no ground to bury anyone in. Now there was ground. There was so much ground it went on past the river, past the hills, brown and open and waiting.

Down the row, someone started singing. It was a Meridian planting song—Sefa knew it, her bones knew it—and after a moment the Verdance-born picked up the melody without knowing the words, humming along, and the song became a thing with two halves that fit together anyway.

* * *

Reyes stood at the edge of the field because he did not know yet whether he belonged in it.

Six months since the cutter. Six months since Okafor had put a hand on his shoulder in front of the marines and said, "I'll take him down myself, Inspector, no need to trouble your crew"—the most transparent lie ever spoken aboard a Conclave vessel, delivered so earnestly that half the bridge had turned away to hide their faces. Six months since the tribunal, since his testimony, since the word *mutiny* stopped being a threat and became a description of what was actually happening across the colonial network, system by system, council by council.

He had been offered a great many things since. Seats, titles, pardons, commissions. He had declined all of them, which had confused everyone except Gideon.

Now he watched her work her way down the third row, moving slowly, one hand pressed to the scarred side of her ribs where the burns had taken longest to heal. She carried a seed tray in the other arm and stopped at each planter in turn—adjusting a grip here, correcting a depth there, saying something that made an old Perpetua engineer laugh, crouching to let a First Ark girl show her how the local worms worked. She looked, Reyes thought, like a woman walking through her own funeral and finding a wedding instead.

"You're hovering," said Sergeant Okafor beside him.

"I'm observing."

"You're hovering, sir." Okafor had stopped calling him Inspector somewhere over the last month, and had not yet settled on what came next. "Go plant something. That's an order."

"You don't outrank me."

"I don't think anybody outranks anybody anymore. It's wonderful." Okafor shouldered a hoe and walked down into the rows, and after a moment Reyes followed, because the man was right, and because the soil at the edge of the furrow looked soft, and because he had spent forty years enforcing boundaries and wanted, just once, to cross one that mattered.

Gideon found him twenty minutes later, kneeling in the dirt of row three with barley seed under his fingernails, looking faintly astonished at his own hands.

"You're doing it wrong," she said.

"I was told there would be no tests."

"There's always a test." She knelt beside him, wincing, and took a pinch of seed from his palm. "Two fingers deep. Not four. They're not being buried, they're being tucked in." She pressed the seeds into the soil with a gentleness that he had seen her use on exactly one other living thing, and then she looked up at him, and the morning light caught the burn scars along her jaw, and neither of them said anything for a while.

"They gave me a plot," she said finally. "At the end of row twelve. My own. Iyo says if I don't plant it myself she'll consider it a dereliction of duty."

"What are you going to put in it?"

"I don't know." Gideon looked out over the valley—the four hundred kneeling figures, the river running bright, the ruined hulk of the Verdance's descent cradle visible on the far ridge where they'd left it, deliberately, as a monument. "Eleven years I planted for people who weren't here yet. I keep starting to plan a garden and catching myself planning around absence. Around who might need it later. Who might come." She shook her head. "I want to plant something that's just for now. For today. I've never done that."

Reyes reached over and moved her hand aside, and pressed two seeds into the soil of her empty plot, two fingers deep, tucked in gently.

"For today," he said.

* * *

Iyo found the anomaly at dusk, when the planting was done and the light went gold and long across the valley.

She had been walking the rows with a tally-slate, confirming counts for the agricultural council, when she noticed that the seed tags along row seven had been rearranged. Not lost—rearranged. Someone had taken the mixed-line tags and re-sequenced them: Meridian-scripted cultivars paired deliberately with their closest Conclave-standard analogues, barley beside barley, pulse beside pulse, in pairs that any geneticist would recognize as compatibility trials.

Someone was already running an experiment. Someone had used the communal planting, the great symbolic mixing of the two peoples' seed lines, as cover for a private test of hybrid vigor.

Iyo crouched and studied the tags. The handwriting on the re-paired labels was careful, archaic, beautiful—Old Meridian script, but a formal register, a clerk's hand, a registrar's hand. She knew that register. She had spent her life reading it.

There were only a handful of people alive who wrote it that well.

She followed the pattern down the row to its end, where an old woman sat in a wooden chair in the failing light, hands folded on her lap, watching the valley turn violet.

"Sefa," Iyo said carefully, "did you replant row seven?"

Sefa did not deny it. She turned her head, and her eyes in the dusk were sharp and amused and very, very tired. "You noticed."

"It's a compatibility matrix. You're testing whether the seed lines will cross."

"They crossed two hundred years ago," Sefa said. "In the holds of the Perpetua, when we were starving, we grew Conclave grain we'd seized from a supply wreck and our own landrace stock side by side, and the second generation came up stronger than either. I was there. I ate that bread." She looked back at the valley. "Everyone down there thinks today is the beginning. It isn't. Today is the resumption. And I wanted to know—" her voice caught, steadied— "whether the world would still let us grow together. Whether the ground remembers what we survived. So I planted a question at the end of every row, and in the spring, the field will answer."

Iyo sat down in the dirt beside the chair, uninvited, the way you sat with family.

"And if the hybrids fail?" she asked. "If the lines won't take?"

Sefa smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who had outlived an empire's attempt to erase her and did not frighten easily. "Then we'll have learned something true, and we'll plant differently next year. That's all farming ever was, child. That's all peace ever was."

Below them, lamps were coming on in the settlement, one by one, and someone had lit fires at the ends of the rows, and the smell of woodsmoke and turned earth rose together off the valley floor like a single breath.

* * *

Gideon stood at the window of the new station long after everyone else had gone down to the fires.

The rebuilt beacon tower rose behind her, its dish repurposed, its pulse rewritten—not a summons anymore, not a claim, but a welcome, repeating patiently into the outer dark for any other forgotten ship that might still be drifting. Somewhere out there, the arithmetic of souls was not finished. Somewhere out there, other arks might be listening. She had made her peace with that. She had built her life inside that patience.

But tonight she wasn't watching the sky. She was watching row seven.

She had noticed the re-sequenced tags at midday, said nothing, planted nothing, told no one. She recognized a private grief-work when she saw one; she had performed enough of her own. But she also knew what she was looking at, because she had spent eleven years reading soil chemistry the way other people read faces, and what Sefa had planted at the end of those rows was not only a question about seeds.

It was a test of the charter itself. Of the founding agreement signed in the ash rows six months ago, the shared-garden covenant, the whole fragile architecture of two peoples learning to inherit one world. Sefa had embedded the treaty in barley. If the mixed lines thrived, the covenant had roots deeper than paper. If they failed—

Gideon pressed her hand flat against the cool glass, over the dark valley, over the sleeping field.

In the spring, the ground would give its verdict. And whatever it said, she intended to be standing in row seven when it spoke.
