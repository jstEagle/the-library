## 38. Reyes's Choice

The letter arrived the way everything important had arrived in Callum Reyes's life lately: on paper, by hand, carried by someone who wouldn't meet his eyes.

He stood at the edge of the landing field where the Perpetua's shuttles had burned their scars into the new topsoil, reading the message a third time while the wind off the young grass pulled at his collar. *By unanimous provisional resolution, Inspector General Callum Reyes is invited to serve as First Arbiter of the Transitional Council, with authority over matters of justice, restitution, and reconciliation.* Below it, signatures—Okafor's blunt scrawl, Iyo's careful Meridian hand, Sefa's name written in a script so old it looked like weather.

First Arbiter. Six months ago the title would have meant nothing to him. Three months ago it would have meant everything—the summit of a career he'd built one careful, compromised case at a time. Now it sat in his hands like something heavy and warm, and all he could think about was that the greenhouse needed its south panels replaced before the first frost, and that Gideon had promised to show him how to graft the old Verdance cuttings onto Perpetua rootstock this week, and that if he took the seat he would spend the next ten years in assembly halls arguing about jurisdiction instead of kneeling in dirt with her.

He folded the letter along its creases until it was small enough to disappear into his palm.

"You're going to say no," said Sergeant Okafor behind him.

He didn't turn around. "You read my mail?"

"I delivered your mail." She came up beside him, hands clasped behind her back in the stance of a woman who had spent thirty years standing watch and couldn't unlearn it now that there was nothing left to guard against but themselves. "And yeah. I did. You want to know why?"

"Because you're a marine."

"Because I watched you stand in a doorway and tell six armed marines that you alone broke Conclave law." She shrugged. "I've seen men lie under interrogation. I've seen men break. I've never seen a man tell the truth like it was a weapon he'd been saving for exactly that moment. That's not an inspector. That's somebody who already decided who he was before anyone asked him to be it." She nodded at the folded paper. "So whatever you decide, you'll have decided it already too. I'm just here for the paperwork."

* * *

Gideon found him at dusk in the ash rows.

They called them that now—Iyo's word, borrowed from some old Perpetua mourning rite: the long beds where they'd buried what the orbital fire had killed. Charred trellises. The black skeletons of fruit trees Gideon had raised from seed in her first year aboard the Verdance, trees that had never touched soil until the colony planted them planet-side, and had lived eleven weeks in the open air before the Conclave's guns found them. She walked the rows every evening. It wasn't grief anymore, exactly. It was more like accounting. She counted what was dead, she counted what had come up in its place, and the numbers never balanced, and she kept counting anyway.

Reyes was standing at the end of the third row with his back to her, and even from twenty meters she could read his posture the way she'd learned to read weather off the station's hull sensors. Something had landed on him.

"The council voted," she said.

He turned. In the low light his face was doing the thing it did when he was deciding how much truth a moment could hold. She'd catalogued all his faces over these months—the interrogator's mask, the conspirator's half-smile, the one he wore at 3 a.m. when the nightmares about the cutter's brig got loose. This one was new. Or rather, it was the honest one, worn without armor.

"They offered me First Arbiter."

"I heard." She stopped beside him. The dead apple tree between them still smelled faintly of smoke when the wind moved through it. "Congratulations are probably the wrong word."

"Gideon." He said her name carefully, the way he always did when he was about to put weight on it. "I've been thinking about what justice looks like from inside a room where nobody's holding a weapon. And I keep coming back to the same problem. If I take the seat, I become the person who decides things. Restitution schedules. Land apportionment. Who gets tried and who gets amnesty." He looked down the row of burnt trees. "I spent eleven years as the Conclave's instrument. I know exactly how easy it is to confuse procedure with principle. Put me in that chair and in five years I'll be Adeyemi with better manners."

"That's not true."

"It might be true. That's worse." He exhaled. "There's a version of me that takes this job because it's important, and does it well, and comes home to you twice a year looking like a portrait of himself. I've met that man. He signed orders in the Verdance's shadow once already."

She was quiet for a long moment. Above them the sky was doing its evening work—the two arks' salvage hulks in high orbit catching the last sun, the new beacon blinking its slow welcome pulse where the Verdance used to be. She had rebuilt it smaller. One antenna, one transmitter, one light. A garden gate left open instead of a fortress wall.

"What do you want?" she asked. "Not what's right. Not what the council needs. What do you want, Callum?"

He laughed—a short, surprised sound, as if the question had gotten past his security screening. "I want," he said slowly, testing each word, "to be somewhere tomorrow morning. Physically, actually somewhere, watching something grow that I helped plant. I want to learn how to graft. You promised to teach me grafting and I have been thinking about it for nine days, which is either love or a very specific midlife crisis." His voice roughened. "I want to stop being a symbol. Symbols don't get to stay."

"Then say no."

"It's not that simple. Ten thousand people just learned their government erased their grandparents. Half the original colonists' descendants are terrified of the other half. Somebody has to hold the center while it sets, or we get a civil war inside eighteen months, and I'm—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm good at being trusted by people who shouldn't trust me. Okafor trusts me. The cutter crews trust me. Even the hardliners in the Perpetua's council trust me, because I arrested myself before anyone else could. If I walk away from that—"

"If you take it, you'll spend the rest of your life walking away from this." She gestured—not at the ash rows, but at the space between them, the small warm country they'd built out of stolen evenings. "I watched you do the math on the cutter, Callum. You're always going to do the math. But you taught me something on that station, remember? You told me the difference between the Conclave and us was that they optimized for outcomes and we were allowed to optimize for people. Starting with ourselves. You said it like it was obvious."

"I was trying to seduce you."

"It worked. Don't change the subject."

* * *

That night he didn't sleep.

He lay in the narrow cot in the prefab housing block—everyone lived in prefabs now, Perpetua engineers and Verdance colonists stacked in identical rooms by lottery, a policy Gideon had argued for and won—and he turned the decision over the way he'd once turned evidence, looking for the seam.

The truth was that power fit him. That was the ugly, quiet fact underneath everything. He was good at institutions. He understood how they breathed, how they lied, how they could be bent toward mercy without breaking. The new world needed people like him precisely because people like him had built the old one. Refusing the chair wouldn't purify him; it would just leave the chair empty for someone with less scar tissue.

But he thought about his father, who had been a dock clerk in the old Meridian yards and had died believing the Conclave's story about the lost ark, and how the last thing his father ever asked him was whether the work was honest, and how Callum had said yes, and how the yes had taken eleven years and a burning station to make true.

He thought about Gideon's hands in the greenhouse loam, and how she never once talked about legacy, only about next season.

Near dawn he got up, dressed, and walked out into the cold blue morning to find her already awake, because of course she was, because some part of her had never fully left the station's maintenance schedule and probably never would. She was in the nursery bay checking moisture lines, and she saw his face and set down the tool and waited, which was the thing about her that undid him most completely: she always waited, as if whatever he brought her could grow in the space she left.

"No," he said. "I'm going to decline. But not the way you think."

Her eyebrows went up.

"I'll testify. I'll advise. I'll sit with the restitution commission as a witness, not a judge—my testimony on record, my conscience attached, my hands off the levers. And then I'm done. No office, no title, no security detail." He took a breath. "I want to build the arboretum. The one in the charter—the living archive, both seed lines side by side. Nobody's assigned to it yet because everyone thinks it's ceremonial. It's not. It's the whole argument we made, planted in the ground where people can walk through it. Two histories in one bed, growing together, and no one gets to prune either one out." He smiled, crooked and uncertain. "I want a job where the worst thing that can happen is blight."

Gideon looked at him for a long time. Then she reached into the flatbed beside her and held out a pair of gloves, worn soft at the fingers, sized for hands slightly larger than hers.

"Been carrying those around for nine days myself," she said. "In case you decided wrong."

"And if I'd said yes? Taken the seat?"

"Then I'd have kept the gloves and visited you twice a year and hated every portrait." She pulled her own gloves on. "Come on. South bed. I'll show you the splice cut. It has to be angled or it won't take."

* * *

They declined together, formally, that afternoon—his refusal read aloud to the council chamber, her endorsement of it entered into the record, Okafor's grudging nod the only comment from the floor. Sefa, wrapped in blankets in the observer's gallery, caught his eye afterward and inclined her head with the gravity of a woman who had slept two hundred years specifically to see a powerful man give power back.

By evening the letter was already circulating with amendments. Someone had proposed expanding the Arbiter's term. Someone else had proposed making the position hereditary. Reyes read the drafts with a kind of weary recognition—the machine rebuilding itself around the shape of its own emergency—and handed them to Okafor without comment.

"She'll handle it," he said.

"She'll enjoy it," Okafor corrected. "That's scarier."

The frost came early that year, three days after the vote. They spent the night in the arboretum's unfinished frame, wrapping the young Perpetua citrus in cloth while the first cold rain came sideways across the field, and somewhere past midnight, soaked and laughing, Gideon pointed at the horizon where the new beacon pulsed its patient green.

"Signal's clean tonight," she said. "Strong return off the outer dark. Probably just the relay buoys."

Reyes followed her gaze. The pulse went out, steady as a heartbeat, welcoming anything still drifting in the long silence between stars.

"Probably," he agreed.

But the return signal blinked again—twice, then twice again—and neither of them moved to go inside.
