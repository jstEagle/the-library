## 39. The New Beacon

The old beacon had sung for eleven years in a language of summons, and Gideon had spent most of those years pretending not to hear what it was actually saying. *Come and look. Come and check. Come and see if the gardener has gone strange.* Now, standing on the new station's observation deck with a mug of chicory going cold in her hand, she watched the replacement array take its first breath of power and thought: *this one will sing differently.*

Below her, through three layers of transparent composite, the world turned. Not the dead brown sphere she'd arrived to tend from orbit—no, that world had been buried under two centuries of green now, or would be, given time. The terraforming engines had done their slow work even while the Conclave pretended nothing lived down there but dust. The Verdance's gardens had seeded it. Her gardens. The ones she'd grown for colonists who never came, for pods that stayed frozen, for a future that had been stolen and then, impossibly, returned.

She took a sip of chicory. It had gone bitter. Everything did, if you let it sit too long.

"You're brooding," said Iyo from the doorway, not looking up from the tablet cradled in her arm. The linguist had a way of announcing observations like diagnoses. "I can tell because you're holding the mug like it insulted you."

"I'm *supervising*."

"You're supervising badly. The array calibration finished four minutes ago. Reyes has been asking where you are."

"Reyes can ask the plants."

"He did. They said you're avoiding him."

Gideon snorted despite herself. Below, the last of the construction drones detached from the beacon array and drifted back toward their docking clamps, leaving the structure bare against the stars: a slender spire of lattice and emitter vanes, rebuilt from the bones of the old Verdance station, which now orbited as a memorial—a scorched, broken ring that the Perpetua's engineers had voted unanimously to leave exactly as it was. *So we remember what obedience cost,* Sefa had said at the vote. *And what defiance bought.*

"I'm not avoiding him," Gideon said. "I'm thinking about the signal."

That sobered them both. Iyo lowered the tablet.

"Show me."

* * *

They walked down to the listening room together—the room that had once been the Conclave's monitoring station, all hard angles and surveillance architecture, now softened with hanging planters and a rug someone had woven from hydroponic fiber. The walls were still lined with acoustic baffles shaped like folded wings. Gideon had kept them. She liked the idea that the room listened with feathers.

On the main display, the waveform hung suspended: thin, repeating, patient. A pulse pattern that had begun arriving nine days ago from somewhere past the heliopause, faint as breath on glass.

"It's not Meridian," Iyo said, for the fourth time. "It's older. Or it's something else entirely. The modulation structure doesn't match any archive we have—not Conclave, not Perpetua, not pre-Collapse commercial bands."

"But it's structured."

"It's structured." Iyo touched the display and the waveform bloomed into its component layers. "Whoever is sending it wants to be found. That's the part that keeps me up at night. Distress signals beg. This one *knocks*."

Gideon stared at the pattern. Nine days of knocking, from the dark beyond the system's edge, regular as rain on a roof.

When she had first heard the distress call in Old Meridian—her grandmother's songs made sudden and terrible and real—it had felt like the universe reaching through the static to grab her by the throat. This was different. This was quieter. Someone out there wasn't drowning. Someone out there was looking for a door.

"The old beacon," she said slowly, "was built to call the Conclave. To summon oversight. To summon judgment." She remembered Callum's face when he'd told her what his orders really were, in the end—what the Justice cutter had come to do before its crew fractured and its sergeant chose a different law. "We tore that antenna down and melted it into plating for the settlement's water cisterns. Adeyemi's people watched us do it from orbit, and we didn't care."

"And now?"

"Now we build a new one." Gideon set down the mug. "But we get to choose what it says."

* * *

The council session convened that evening in the long hall the colonists called the Seedhouse, because its vaulted ceiling had been framed with timber from the Verdance's original greenhouse and someone had painted seed-pods along every beam. Two hundred people filled the benches—Perpetua folk and first-ark descendants both, farmers and engineers and former marines, Sergeant Okafor near the front with her arms crossed and her skepticism worn openly, like weather.

Sefa sat at the center, wrapped in a shawl despite the warmth, her hands folded atop her cane. She no longer needed the cane, strictly speaking. She kept it anyway. "A reminder," she'd said, "that I died once already and came back with opinions."

Gideon stood at the open space before the benches and laid out the waveform on the great wall display.

"Nine days ago, this began arriving from beyond the heliopause. Structured. Repeating. Deliberate." She let the pattern pulse across the assembled faces. "Iyo's team believes it's a search pattern. A ship—or an ark, or a drifting hulk with survivors aboard—is out there, sweeping the dark for anyone who might answer."

"A trap?" someone called from the back. Gideon recognized the voice: Davorin, one of the Perpetua's security chiefs, who had argued in the early weeks for seizing the first ark's resources and had since become, grudgingly, a farmer. Old instincts died hard. "The Conclave used beacons as bait. We all know that history."

"We do," Gideon agreed. "And if it's a trap, we'll know soon enough, and we'll deal with it the way we dealt with the last one." A ripple of grim laughter. "But here's what I keep coming back to. There were more than two arks. The Conclave's own records—Iyo found them in the command deck archive—list seven launch registrations from the Collapse era. Seven. We've accounted for two. The other five are listed as 'lost with all hands,' and we now know exactly how much the Conclave's bookkeeping on lost ships can be trusted."

Silence. In the back, Okafor uncrossed her arms.

"So my proposal is this," Gideon continued. "The new beacon goes live next week. And when it does, it won't broadcast a summons. It won't call the Conclave. It won't call anyone's masters. It'll send a welcome."

She brought up the message they'd spent six days drafting—translated into fourteen languages, encoded in three protocols, layered so that anything from a primitive radio receiver to a Conclave-grade array could parse it. Simple words, repeated in the cadence of a lighthouse:

*You are seen. You are not lost. This world is green. Come home.*

Davorin read it twice. "You're inviting strangers to our world."

"I'm inviting strangers to *a* world," Gideon said. "One that nearly died twice because people hoarded it. We have soil enough. We have seed enough. What we don't have anymore is any excuse."

Sefa rose, slowly, leaning on the cane. The hall went quiet the way it always did when the old woman stood—half reverence, half the instinct of people who had learned that whatever Sefa said next would matter.

"When the Perpetua launched," she said, her voice thin but carrying, "we believed we were the last of humanity. When we woke and learned of the first ark, we believed we were two halves of a whole. Now we learn there may be five more pieces of us, scattered in the dark, still knocking on doors that were sealed two hundred years ago." She looked around the hall, her gaze settling last on Gideon. "I have spent two lifetimes learning what happens when the powerful decide who counts as people. I will not spend my last one doing the arithmetic again. Light the beacon."

The vote wasn't unanimous. Nothing worth having ever was. But it carried, overwhelmingly, and Davorin himself raised his hand for it in the end—which surprised everyone, including, from the look on his face, Davorin.

* * *

Activation day dawned clear over the settlement, the sky washed pale gold with the high cirrus of the terraforming engines' exhaust. Gideon climbed the new station alone. She'd asked for that—one hour, just her, before the others arrived for the ceremony. Some things you had to say without an audience.

The beacon chamber was small and warm, lined with the same living green walls that threaded through the whole station—mosses and ferns and flowering vines the colonists had insisted on installing, because a machine that only spoke in cold light would sound like the Conclave, and they were done sounding like the Conclave. At the chamber's heart sat the transmitter core, humming at standby, waiting for her hand on the control.

Gideon pressed her palm flat against the warm casing.

"Eleven years," she said quietly, to the plants, to the dead station visible through the viewport as a broken ring against the stars, to the woman she'd been before all of this. "Eleven years I talked to you because nobody else would listen. And then somebody answered, and it nearly killed me, and it was still the best thing that ever happened."

The ferns trembled in the ventilation current. She chose to believe they agreed.

She keyed the sequence.

The beacon spoke.

Not a summons. Not a scream. A steady, patient pulse rolling outward at lightspeed, wave after wave, carrying fourteen languages and three encodings and one simple promise into the dark where five ships might be drifting, might be dying, might be listening. *You are seen. You are not lost.* Somewhere past the heliopause, that unknown signal was still knocking—and now, for the first time in two centuries, something in this system knocked back.

Behind her, the chamber door opened. Callum came in without a word and stood beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, watching the transmitter lights cycle green.

"They'll want to know what it says," he said eventually. "Whoever's out there. They'll be afraid. After everything, they'll hear a voice from a human system and think *Conclave* first."

"I know." Gideon leaned into him, just slightly. "That's why the message repeats every ninety seconds. Fear takes about ninety seconds to talk yourself out of, if the voice is kind."

He laughed softly. "Is that from your grandmother's songs?"

"From gardening. Same thing, mostly."

They stood together while the beacon pulsed, and below them the green world turned, and in the fields the first open-planted crops of both arks' seed lines moved in the wind like a congregation.

* * *

The answer came on the nineteenth day.

Gideon was in the root cellar annex—her favorite place in the new station, cool and earth-smelling, where the rescued seed vaults from the old Verdance now lived in climate-controlled rows—when the station-wide chime sounded. Three tones. Priority. She was halfway up the ladder before the second chime finished.

Iyo met her in the listening room, and the expression on the linguist's face was one Gideon had never seen before: wonder stripped of all its usual irony, raw as a child's.

"It answered," Iyo said. "The signal out past the heliopause. It *answered*."

On the display, the new transmission resolved itself—decoded, translated, unfolding line by line as Iyo's systems worked through the handshake protocol. Not a distress call. Not a knock anymore. Words. Actual words, in a dialect of Old Meridian so archaic that Iyo flagged half of it as uncertain, but the meaning came through clean as water:

*We hear you. We are the Ark Meridian's Daughter, launch registration four of seven. We have 3,112 souls alive in cryostasis. Our power fails within the year. We have been knocking for sixty-one years.*

*Tell us truly: whose garden is this?*

Gideon read it three times. Then she sat down slowly in the listening chair, the one with the woven cushion, and put her hand over her mouth, and laughed, and cried, probably both at once, in front of everyone.

Sixty-one years. Sixty-one years of knocking into a dark that never answered, and the first voice to reply was a gardener's.

Callum arrived, then Sefa, then half the settlement crowding the doorway, and above it all the beacon pulsed on, patient, ninety seconds at a time, saying to the dark what it would keep saying for as long as Gideon drew breath:

*You are seen. You are not lost. This world is green.*

Her fingers hovered over the transmit key. Three thousand souls, failing power, sixty-one years of silence finally broken—and behind them, somewhere deeper in the black, four more registrations still unaccounted for, still drifting, still waiting for a door to open.

Gideon thought of her grandmother's songs. Of Sefa's cane. Of a broken ring orbiting overhead, kept as a scar so no one would forget.

She pressed the key.

"Welcome home," she said.
