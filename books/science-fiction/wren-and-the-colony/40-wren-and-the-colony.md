## 40. Wren and the Colony

The morning comes up gold through the eastern glass, the way it has for six years now, and Gideon Vasquez is in the rows before the sun clears the ridge.

She moves down the bean lines with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, fingers finding the pods by touch before her eyes confirm them. The soil here is real soil—weathered, wormed, alive with the particular smell of things rotting into other things—which still astonishes her some mornings when she kneels and presses her palm flat against it. Eleven years she grew things in nutrient film and engineered loam, everything measured to the milliliter, every death calculated and composted on schedule. Here the ground simply *gives*, extravagantly, without being asked twice.

"Good morning," she says to the beans, because she has never stopped saying it, and because there are children who will come through the gate at midmorning and they have learned from her that you greet what you tend.

Behind her, the settlement is waking. She can hear it: the creak of the well chain, someone laughing near the bread ovens, the high thin argument of goats. Meridian's dawn chorus is not birdsong—the imported avians are still establishing, still shy—but it is close enough. It is voices. It is two hundred people and then some, born here or landed here or thawed here, all of them alive because of choices made in the dark above this valley, years ago, by people whose names are carved into the memorial wall by the meetinghouse.

Her own name is on that wall too. She has asked them, twice, to take it off. They have refused, twice, with the gentle immovability of people who have decided something communally. So instead she comes out early, before anyone can thank her for anything, and she puts her hands in the dirt where thanks belongs.

* * *

The children call her Wren.

It started, as these things do, with one child—Tomas Okafor-Reyes's daughter, actually, though the naming had nothing to do with lineage and everything to do with a story told too many times around too many fires. The story of the Verdance breaking apart in orbital fire, of Gideon strapped into the beam-steering console while Conclave warheads walked their burning line across the sky, of how she kept steering, kept broadcasting, until the last second. And how, three days later, when the landing parties finally cut into the sealed root cellar beneath the ruined greenhouse, they found her barely breathing among the seed vaults—and found something else besides.

A bird.

It had been part of the station's ecological survey stock, a finch-variant bred for atmospheric testing, and it had survived the destruction in the cellar's life-support pocket alongside her, riding out the bombardment in the dark on stored seed. When they carried Gideon up into the light, half-dead, the bird went with her, riding on the stretcher rail, and when she woke weeks later in the infirmary tent, it was the first thing she asked about.

They released it that spring, on the first warm day, from the ruins of the old greenhouse frame. It flew a crooked, astonished circle over the assembled crowd and vanished into the birch stands along the river, and Tomas—who was four—had pointed and shouted, "Wren! Like Wren!" Because in his mind the woman and the bird were the same story, and he was right, and no one ever corrected him.

Now there are forty children on this world and every one of them calls her Wren, and the name has spread to their parents, and even to Iyo, who should know better, and even—once, gravely, at a council meeting—to Sefa, who is ninety-one years old now by body-clock and rules the settlement's memory the way old queens rule: absolutely, and mostly by being right.

Gideon has stopped fighting it. A name given by children is the only kind worth keeping.

* * *

Midmorning brings them, as it always does on planting-days: the school cohort, twelve of them aged five to nine, trailing dust and noise through the garden gate under the long-suffering eye of their teacher, a Perpetua-born botanist named Anselm who once froze for two hundred years and now cannot get children to freeze for two seconds.

"Wren!" they shout, because greeting her loudly is apparently a law.

"Inside voices," says Anselm, hopelessly.

Gideon crouches so she is at their height, which takes longer than it used to, and holds up her hands, which are filthy. "What do we say about the beds?"

"Don't step in them," they recite.

"What do we say about the seeds?"

"One for the row, one for the store, one for the bird."

"And if you don't know what a plant is?"

"We ask before we eat it." This from the smallest one, Dara, who ate an unknown nightshade leaf two seasons ago and spent an instructive afternoon being very sick, and has become the settlement's most cautious forager in consequence.

They work the morning rows together. She shows them how deep to set the maize, how to read the soil's color for moisture, how to tell a weed from a volunteer by the shape of its first true leaves. And as always, working, she tells them stories—not the big ones, not yet; the big ones wait until they're older, the fire and the frozen ships and the men who came to kill the truth. Today's stories are small ones. How beans climb if you give them something to believe in. How her grandmother sang to seedlings in a language nobody spoke anymore until a linguist named Iyo heard her humming it one day and went very still, and said, *That's Old Meridian. That's the tongue of the second ship.* How the first tomato she ever grew aboard the Verdance was pollinated with a paintbrush because there wasn't a single insect in the whole sky, and how she cried when it ripened, and how crying about tomatoes is allowed.

Dara tugs her sleeve. "Wren. Did you really live in the sky alone?"

"For eleven years," Gideon says.

"Weren't you scared?"

Gideon considers this with the seriousness the question deserves. "I didn't know I was lonely," she says. "That's the scariest part. You can be so empty for so long you think emptiness is just what a person is." She brushes soil off her knees. "Then one day a signal came out of the dark, and I found out I'd been waiting my whole life without knowing it. So if you're ever somewhere that feels too quiet—" she taps Dara's chest, gently, over the heart— "remember that the quiet isn't the whole story. Something might be coming."

Dara nods with the ferocious solemnity of the very young, and returns to her maize.

* * *

In the afternoon, Callum Reyes finds her at the irrigation channel, as he always does, as he has done nearly every afternoon for four years, since the tribunal cleared him and he stood in the meetinghouse square with his Justice Division insignia in his fist and asked the assembly—not the Conclave, the assembly—for permission to stay.

He sits on the channel bank the way he sits everywhere: like a man perpetually ready to stand back up. Some habits outlive the institutions that built them. He watches her work for a while, comfortable with silence in a way he had to learn, and then he says, "Beacon's transmitting clean. First cycle since the rebuild."

"You checked it yourself?"

"I let Iyo check it. I held the ladder."

Gideon laughs, wipes her hands, and comes to sit beside him. Below them the valley runs green to the river—barley, orchard, the new paddocks, the roofs of a town that did not exist eight years ago—and above it all, past the blue ceiling of atmosphere, the rebuilt station hangs in its careful orbit, renamed now. Not the Verdance. The Welcome.

"It answered anything?" she asks.

"Not yet. But the listening array caught something last week Iyo wants you to hear. She wouldn't tell me what. She said—" he smiles faintly— "*it's Wren's call to make.*"

Gideon goes still. Out on the water meadow, a flock of the imported finches rises all at once, turns silver, settles again.

"Another ship?"

"I don't know. She said the signature was old. Older than the Perpetua." He looks at her sideways. "There's no hurry. Whatever's out there has been out there a long time."

"No," Gideon agrees softly. "No hurry." But her heart is doing something complicated, the same thing it did eleven years ago when a dead language whispered out of the outer dark: grief and hope in the same breath, because she knows now what drifters cost, and she knows now what finding them costs, and she would pay it again, and again, and again.

Reyes takes her hand. His is scarred across the knuckles—from the interrogation, though he never says so, and she never asks, because some ledgers you keep by not reading them.

"The council session's at dusk," he says. "Sefa wants to talk about the east terraces. Iyo wants to talk about the signal. And Adeyemi's trial verdict comes down tonight, relayed from the coastal court." He pauses. "Whatever it says, half this town will celebrate and half will grieve, and both halves will be right."

"Then we'll plant tomorrow," Gideon says. "We'll plant the day after, too. That's the whole trick of it, Callum. Everything else breaks. Planting doesn't."

* * *

Dusk. The meetinghouse is full, lamplit, smelling of woodsmoke and bread. Sefa speaks from the front bench, wrapped in grey wool, her voice still carrying the measured cadence of a woman who recorded her testament in the dark between stars and meant every word to outlive her.

"The east terraces can carry orchard stock by next spring," she says, "if we take water from the high channel. I have heard every argument against it and none of them survive contact with my patience. We plant. That is what we do. That is what *she* taught us to do." A nod toward Gideon, who studies her boots. "Motion carries. Someone write it down before I die of being agreed with."

Laughter, warm and easy. Then Iyo stands, and the room quiets, because Iyo standing means data, and data means the world is about to get bigger or more dangerous, and on this world everyone has learned those are usually the same thing.

"The Welcome's array isolated the signal three nights ago," Iyo says. She doesn't use the display screen; she never does; she believes important things should be said in a human voice. "It's a repeating pulse in Old Meridian. Simple arithmetic handshake—the same protocol the Perpetua used to hail us. But the source is not a ship in our system. The propagation delay puts it *fourteen light-years out*. Which means the transmission we're hearing left its origin fourteen years ago." She lets that settle. "Which means whoever is sending it started broadcasting before Broadcast Zero. Before any of our signals could have reached them. They aren't answering us." Her voice catches, just slightly, the linguist in her overwhelmed by the grammar of it. "They're hailing into the dark on faith. The way the Perpetua did. There may be another ark. There may be several. We built the new beacon to welcome exactly this, and it has come, and the decision of what we send back belongs to this council—all of us, together. That's the whole point of us."

Silence, full and ringing. Gideon feels every face turn toward her, and this time she doesn't duck it. She stands.

"When I was alone up there," she says, "I used to log the beacon pulse every hour and mark it *no response*. Thousands of entries. Nobody answered, and I kept sending anyway, because a beacon isn't a question. It's a promise. *Someone is here. Someone will help.*" She looks around the lamplit room—at Reyes, at Sefa, at Iyo, at the children asleep on benches against their parents' sides, at the whole improbable congregation of the saved and the forgiven. "We know better than anyone what it costs when that promise gets broken. So we don't break it. We answer. We tell them: come home. There's ground here. There's a garden. We'll plant your rows beside ours."

The vote is unanimous, and it isn't even close, and afterward the town pours out into the star-thick night singing one of Sefa's old songs in a language that died and came back to life, like everything else here.

* * *

Later—much later, when the fires have burned low and the valley is only crickets and wind—Gideon walks out alone to the beacon field on the western rise.

The transmitter array stands slim and white against the stars, humming its patient note into the dark. Somewhere beyond that dark, fourteen years gone, another voice is calling the way she once called: not asking, promising. Soon the Welcome will answer it. Soon there will be coordinates to verify, trajectories to plot, a garden to expand, more names for the school rolls, more children who will need to learn which leaves not to eat. The work is enormous and it is just beginning and she is fifty-nine years old and she has never been less tired in her life.

Something small lands on the fence post beside her.

She turns her head slowly. It's a finch—one of the released line, third generation now, wild and unafraid in the way of animals that have never known a cage. In the starlight she can't tell if it's a descendant of *her* bird, the one from the cellar, the one the children named her for. It hardly matters. Lineage was never the point. Survival was the point, and song after it.

"Hello," she says.

The bird tilts its head, considering her with one bright eye, and sings—a short, rough, glorious phrase of notes that no one taught it, invented fresh out of the dark, offered to the night for nothing.

Gideon listens until it finishes. Then she turns back to the beacon, and together—they and the machine and the sleeping town below and the whole green breathing world—they send the answer outward, into the long dark, where somebody is waiting to hear it:

*Come home. Come home. There is ground here.*

* * *

And in the morning, as always, the gardener goes down to the rows, and the children come running through the gate shouting her name, and the planting begins again.
