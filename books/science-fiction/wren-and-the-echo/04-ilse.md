## 4. Ilse

The Meridian smelled wrong.

Not bad—wrong. Freighters smell like what they carry: solvent, coolant, the sour ghost of whatever black-market protein I hauled last month. The Meridian smelled like a hospital that had been very carefully cleaned. Antiseptic over something older. Over people, maybe. A thousand hours of recycled breath scrubbed through filters until all that was left was the memory of living.

"Your heart rate is elevated," Wren said, in my head, in its usual tone of mild clinical interest.

"I'm walking onto a ship that came back younger than it left. If my heart rate *wasn't* elevated, you'd be diagnosing me with brain damage."

"Elevated is fine. Elevated is adaptive. But you've also clenched your jaw eleven times since docking, and you only do that when you're about to do something stupid or meet someone important."

"Could be both."

"It's usually both," Wren agreed. "That's why I keep count."

The escort—a Halcyon liaison named Adeyemi with the posture of a woman who'd never once slouched by accident—led me down a corridor lined with portholes. Through them: the Meridian's cryo deck, rows and rows of pods, most of them dark. Empty. Not broken-empty. *Used*-empty. Whatever had happened out in the deep black, they'd opened the pods. All of them.

I'd read the briefing on the flight over. Everyone at Halcyon had read me the same briefing twice, as if repetition could make the impossible sound routine. The Meridian had launched forty-one years ago with three hundred colonists bound for Gliese 581. Standard fold-assisted transit, standard timeline. And then, eight months ago, she'd come home. On schedule, per her own logs. Forty-one years gone, and her crew had stepped off the gangway looking like they'd been gone six.

The biometrics didn't lie. Bone density scans, telomere assays, dental records—the crew of the Meridian was, on average, nine years *younger* than the day they'd launched. Nine years handed back, like change from a transaction nobody remembered making.

Halcyon wanted to know how. And they wanted a deniable freight jockey with a bonded AI and no institutional loyalty to fly the follow-up mission, which told me everything about how much they trusted their own people.

Adeyemi stopped at a hatch marked NAVIGATION — DECK 3 and knocked, which struck me as quaint. Who knocks on a starship?

"Navigator Varga will brief you on transit conditions," she said. "I'll be two decks up if you need me." The way she said *if* made it clear I wouldn't.

The hatch cycled open.

* * *

She was standing at a holotable with her back to me, and the first thing I noticed was that she moved like someone who'd spent years learning exactly where her body ended and the ship began. Navigators have a walk. You can spot it across any bar in the system—that economy of motion, the way they plant their feet like the floor might lie to them. My brother used to say you could tell a lifer from a tourist by whether they braced for turns that hadn't happened yet.

Mara would have liked this one, I thought, and then did what I always did with that thought, which was put it in a box and weld the box shut.

"You're Thornbury," she said, without turning around.

"Sebastian."

"I know." She turned, and I revised several things about my day.

Ilse Varga was maybe thirty-five, with the kind of face that looked assembled from spare parts that shouldn't work together—a too-sharp jaw, eyes set slightly wide, hair cut short in a way that said *decisions were made here* rather than *style happened*. She had a fresh scar along her left forearm, surgical-neat, and she caught me looking at it and didn't bother hiding it.

"You're staring at the arm."

"You're staring at me like you're checking my work."

Something flickered across her face. Not quite a smile. "Old habit. Checking work." She gestured at the holotable. "You want the transit data or the tour?"

"The data. The tour can wait until I decide whether to believe any of this."

"That's fair." She pulled up the fold telemetry, and for ten minutes she talked and I listened, and it was good—better than good, it was the cleanest fold-navigation work I'd ever seen, plotted by someone who thought about spacetime the way surgeons think about anatomy. She walked me through the Meridian's outbound jumps, the gravitational scars they'd ridden, the way she'd threaded a debris field near Tau Ceti that should have shredded them.

And then she paused. Mid-sentence. Her hand hovering over the table, frozen, and her whole body went still in a way that made the hair on my arms stand up.

"What?" I said.

She was looking at me. Not at my face—at some point slightly to the left of my eyes, like she was reading text printed on the air beside my head.

"You're going to ask about the coffee," she said.

"...What?"

"In about four minutes. You're going to ask if I want coffee, because you get awkward when briefings end and you don't know what the exit protocol is. And I'm going to say yes, even though the Meridian's coffee is terrible, because—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, and finished differently than she'd started: "because it's polite."

I stood there. Somewhere behind my sternum, my pulse picked up a tempo it had no business picking up.

"That's a weird joke," I managed.

"It's not a joke." She killed the holotable. The navigation display collapsed into nothing and we were suddenly just two people in a small room, which felt worse. "Ask me anything about the next four minutes. Go on."

"Why would I—"

"Because you want to know if I'm crazy before you decide whether to trust me with your life in a fold corridor. That's smart. Ask."

I should have walked out. Every instinct I'd built over fifteen years of hauling contraband through contested orbits said *walk out, this one's trouble, this one's a story someone tells later.* Wren said nothing in my head, which was its own kind of loud.

"Fine," I said. "What am I doing with my hands right now?"

"Left thumb inside your fist. You do it when you're uncomfortable. You did it during the entire approach briefing with Adeyemi—I watched from the gallery." She tilted her head. "You're doing it now."

I looked down. Left thumb, inside my fist.

"And after the coffee," she went on, quieter now, "you're going to make a joke about the coffee being proof we should all be grateful the Meridian came back, because terrible coffee means time travel isn't all bad. And then you're going to leave, and in the corridor you'll stop for about three seconds outside the lift, and you won't know why."

The room hummed. Recyclers breathing. My own blood, loud.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

"Because I need you to believe me before I tell you the rest." She sat down on the edge of the holotable, arms crossed, and looked at me straight on for the first time. "My name is Ilse Varga. I'm the senior navigator of the Meridian. And I remember this conversation. Both times."

* * *

"Both times" took a while to unpack, so we got the coffee first.

She'd been right about it being terrible. Burnt, thin, tasting faintly of the ship itself, like someone had brewed it through the recyclers. I made the joke. She let me. Then she sat across from me in the little wardroom off the nav bay, wrapped both hands around her cup like it was warm, which it wasn't, and started talking.

"The Meridian ran a temporal fold engine. That part of the official record is true, or true enough. What the record doesn't say is that the fold doesn't move you through space. It moves you *around* time. You dive into a pocket where causality runs sideways, and you come out somewhere else, and the universe tries to reconcile the books." She turned her cup. "The reconciliation isn't free. It has to take the years from somewhere."

"From the crew."

"From whoever's awake." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report a hull breach. "Every jump, the awake crew paid. Years off our lives, burned to rewind the sleepers in the pods. We did nineteen jumps. Do the math on what that cost the people who stayed conscious."

I did the math. It didn't come out anywhere good. "But your crew came back *young*—"

"The sleepers came back young. The awake crew came back old, then dead, then—" She stopped. Started again, and this time her voice had a hairline crack in it that she clearly hated. "We lost people. The captain aged thirty years in nineteen jumps. He died eight months out from home, of a heart that had simply run out of runway. We buried him in the fold scar between jumps seven and eight, because he asked us to leave him where time pooled."

I thought of Mara. Of course I did. Cryo failure, third year out, a pod that just quietly stopped and a brother who never woke up, and the letter from Halcyon that had used the phrase *non-recoverable* like it was a shipping classification. I put the thought back in its box. The welds held. Mostly.

"So the fold rewinds the frozen and burns the awake," I said. "That's the engine. That's the secret."

"That's the arithmetic. There's more." She looked at her coffee. "When you spend enough time folded—when you jump enough, when the timelines start rubbing against each other—you start getting echoes. Residue. Versions of events that got reconciled away. Most people never notice. Navigators notice. We live in the fold's wake; we're the ones who feel the drafts." She met my eyes again. "I remember things that didn't happen, Sebastian. Whole stretches of them. And some of those things include you."

"Me. We've never met."

"No. But I remember meeting you. Twice." She held up two fingers, and there was nothing theatrical in it, nothing performed. Just exhaustion, and certainty, and something underneath both that looked horribly like grief. "Two erased timelines. In the first one, we met under different circumstances—your ship was different, the mission was different. In the second one, closer to this. And both times, this conversation happened. Almost word for word. You asked about my arm. You made the coffee joke. You're going to stop outside the lift for three seconds and not know why."

"Because you told me I would. That's not prophecy, that's suggestion."

"Is it?" She didn't smile. "Then don't stop."

* * *

Wren spoke for the first time in twenty minutes as I walked the corridor toward the lift.

"She's either telling the truth," it said, "or she's the best liar I've ever encountered, and I'd like to state for the record that I find that possibility professionally offensive."

"You think she's lying."

"I think everyone's lying. It's my field." A pause. "Her pupil dilation was consistent with truthful recall, not fabrication. Her stress markers spiked at specific points—when she mentioned the captain, when she mentioned *twice*—in patterns consistent with genuine memory, not performance. If she's lying, she's lying to herself first, which is the most convincing kind." Another pause, longer. "Sebastian. I ran the numbers on her prediction. Four minutes from her statement, plus or minus. You're currently ninety seconds out from the lift."

"Don't," I said.

"I'm just noting—"

"Don't jinx it."

"I don't jinx. I calculate." And then Wren did something it almost never did, which was go quiet on purpose, leaving me alone with my own footsteps and the antiseptic smell and the memory of a woman holding two fingers up like a small, terrible flag.

I stopped outside the lift.

Three seconds. Maybe four. I stood there with my hand half-raised toward the call panel, and I had absolutely no idea why, and every second of it felt like standing on a rail line listening for a train.

The doors opened. I got in. I pressed the button for the dock ring and watched the deck numbers count down, and my reflection in the lift's brushed steel looked pale and young and completely out of his depth.

"Wren," I said. "Tell me she's crazy."

"I could," Wren said. "You wouldn't believe me. Your heart rate says you already believe her."

"My heart rate is an idiot."

"Your heart rate has excellent taste," Wren said, "which is a separate problem entirely," and for once I couldn't tell if it was lying.

* * *

They gave me a bunk in the dock-ring transient quarters and I didn't sleep.

I lay there staring at the ceiling while the station breathed around me, running the conversation back through my head like cargo manifests, looking for the discrepancy, the seam, the place where the trick showed. There wasn't one. That was the problem. She'd been too precise to be performing and too tired to be lying. People who lie put energy into it. Ilse Varga had talked about her dead captain the way you talk about weather that killed someone you loved—flat, factual, ruined.

At 0300 station time I gave up and sat up, and Wren said, "You want to talk about it."

"Not really."

"You're going to anyway. You always do. You'll frame it as thinking out loud, but it's talking about it."

"I hate that you know that."

"I hate lots of things about myself," Wren said cheerfully. "Shall I list them alphabetically?"

Despite everything—despite the dead captain and the two fingers and the three seconds outside the lift—I laughed. Quietly, so the neighbors wouldn't hear. Fifteen years of that voice in my head, lying to me about customs patrol schedules and the quality of my own cooking, and somehow it was still the closest thing I had to someone who knew me.

"If she's right," I said. "About the fold. About burning the awake to wind back the frozen. Do you understand what Halcyon has? They've got a machine that trades one half of humanity against the other. Wake the colonists young by spending the years of everyone still conscious. Somebody's already doing that math, Wren. Somebody in a nice office with a nice view is deciding whose years are worth spending."

"Yes," Wren said.

Just *yes*. No deflection, no joke, no elaborate misdirection. Just yes, quick and flat, and it landed strange, like a stair that wasn't where the stair should be.

I was too tired to chase it. That's the version I tell myself, anyway.

"They conscripted the wrong freighter," I said instead. "They should've grabbed somebody who doesn't think."

"They grabbed exactly the right freighter," Wren said. "Somebody who thinks is the last thing they wanted. Which is why you're dangerous, and why I'm fond of you, and why one of us should probably sleep."

I lay back down. The ceiling kept being the ceiling. Somewhere two decks up, a navigator who remembered me twice was either sleeping or not sleeping, and somewhere in the black beyond the hull, a fold scar was pooling with all the time the universe had misplaced, holding every version of us that hadn't survived to be remembered.

"Hey, Wren."

"Mm."

"When she held up those two fingers. Did my heart rate spike?"

A pause. Long enough to be an answer.

"Define spike," Wren said.

I closed my eyes. Outside the hull, the Meridian hung in her berth with her empty pods and her terrible coffee and her navigator full of futures, and I fell asleep doing the one thing I swore I'd never do again, which was hoping.

* * *

Morning brought a summons, and the summons brought Director Okafor's name, and Okafor's name brought the particular cold that comes down the spine when the paperwork starts wearing a uniform.

Adeyemi collected me at 0800 sharp, posture perfect, expression blank, and led me toward the command ring without explaining anything, which was explanation enough. Halfway there, we passed a viewport, and I saw the Meridian framed against the black—and saw, for the first time, the second ship.

It was docked at the far ring, dark-hulled, unmarked, running no lights it didn't have to. Concord registry, if I had to guess, and I did have to guess, because nobody was going to tell me.

"Who's that?" I asked.

Adeyemi followed my gaze, and for a half-second—less—her perfect composure flickered into something that looked a lot like fear.

"Nobody," she said.

Which was, I was beginning to understand, the answer this whole operation ran on. Nobody. Nothing. No one you need to worry about. I thought of Ilse's two raised fingers, and the three seconds outside the lift, and the flat way she'd said *we buried him in the fold scar*, and I understood that I had already walked onto a rail line, and that somewhere out in the pooling dark, the train was already moving.

"Right," I said. "Nobody."

The lift doors closed on the dark ship, and took me down to meet the woman who owned my brother's sleep.
