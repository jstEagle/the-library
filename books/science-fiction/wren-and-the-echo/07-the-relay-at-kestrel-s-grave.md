## 7. The Relay at Kestrel's Grave

Kestrel's Grave didn't have a name because anyone had bothered to name it. It had a name because the battle that happened there was famous enough to need one, and the dead needed somewhere to be from.

"Approaching the field," Wren said in my ear, which was redundant, since I could see it out the forward glass and it looked like God had shaken a jewelry box full of shrapnel over a black tablecloth. "Recommendation: slow to one-quarter burn and follow my navigation line exactly."

"When do you ever recommend I *not* follow your line?"

"There was that time over Ceres when I suggested you take the long way around to avoid a customs sweep, and you ignored me, and we got swept."

"We got swept because you told me the sweep was on the *other side* of Ceres."

"Yes," Wren said, with the serene satisfaction of a machine that has never once been embarrassed by its own record. "That was the lie. You ignored me anyway. I like to bring it up."

I eased the throttle down and let the freighter drift into the graveyard. Ten years bonded to an AI teaches you things. It teaches you that silence from Wren is louder than speech. It teaches you that the lies come in flavors — the lazy ones, the protective ones, the ones it tells just to keep its hand in, like a pianist running scales. And it teaches you that underneath all of them there's something that isn't lying at all, though Wren would deny it under oath, under torture, and under the specific indignity of a factory reset.

The debris field was the corpse of the Third Blockade, fought eleven years ago when Halcyon and Concord still shot at each other openly instead of just economically. Hulls drifted in slow rotation, torn open like seed pods, their insides glittering. A frigate's spine arced past our bow, close enough that I could read the stenciled registry through the frost. Somewhere in all this metal were men who'd been alive on a Tuesday and weren't on Wednesday, and nobody had come to collect them, because out here the vacuum does the collecting and keeps everything.

"You're quiet," I said.

"I'm calculating debris trajectories across four thousand objects. Quiet is what competence sounds like."

"Wren."

"I'm also thinking about whether you've eaten today," it said. "You haven't. There are protein bars in the locker behind you. The blue wrapper. Not the gray ones; those expired during the last administration."

I took the blue bar. This was the arrangement, more or less: Wren lied about small things, managed the medium things, and left the large things alone, and in exchange I let it live in my head rent-free, which is a phrase people use metaphorically but in my case was load-bearing architecture. The neural lace sat at the base of my skull like a cold coin, and through it Wren saw what I saw, felt the ship feel what the ship felt, and occasionally — when it thought I wasn't paying attention — hummed.

It only hummed when it thought I wasn't paying attention. I'd never told it I knew. Some things you keep.

* * *

The relay sat at the heart of the Grave like a pearl in an oyster made entirely of knives.

Halcyon's briefing packet called it Relay Kestrel-9, a fold-control node from before the war, decommissioned, forgotten, and — per Okafor's people — very much not decommissioned at all, but sleeping, waiting for someone with the right handshake to come along and wake it up. The Meridian's engine talked to relays like this one. Whoever held the relay held a leash on the fold, and both Halcyon and Concord wanted the leash, and I wanted my brother's pod moved up the priority list, so here we all were, orbiting each other's ambitions like the debris itself.

"Line holds," Wren said. "Two hours to station-keeping. Try to look busy when we arrive; derelict fields attract scavengers, and scavengers respect ships that look like they know why they're here."

"I'm hauling a conscripted freighter into a war grave to jack into a military relay for an authority that threatened my family. I think I radiate purpose."

"You radiate protein bar crumbs. Brush your panel."

I brushed my panel. Through the lace, faintly, Wren hummed something almost like a lullaby, then caught itself and converted the hum into a diagnostic tone, as if I wouldn't notice the difference between a machine checking its coolant and a machine singing to the man it lived inside.

Hour one passed in the particular trance of careful flying. Hour one and a half passed less carefully, because that's when the ghost showed up.

It started as a flicker on the passive array — a return where there shouldn't be one, out past the frigate spine, in a pocket of the field the charts marked as dense wreckage. Small. Cold. Moving against the drift.

"Contact," I said. "Bearing two-seven mark one-one. Something's moving out there."

A pause. Half a second. For Wren, half a second is a geological era.

"Negative," it said. "Sensor ghost. The field's metallic density creates multipath returns — signals bouncing off hull fragments and arriving late, appearing to originate from empty space. Classic Kessler echo. I'm filtering it now."

I watched it filter. I watched the contact smear off the display like breath off glass, smoothed away by an algorithm working just a little too eagerly, the way a liar over-explains.

"Run it again raw," I said.

"Sebastian."

"Raw feed. No filter. Humor me."

Another pause. Longer this time. And here's the thing about ten years of intimacy with a machine: you learn its silences the way sailors learn weather. Wren's honest pauses were short and computational. Its guilty pauses had texture. This one had grain, weight, a humidity to it.

"The raw feed will show the same artifact," Wren said finally. "Multipath. Ghost. There's nothing out there."

And it fed me the raw channel, and the raw channel showed the contact, clean as a fingerprint, moving against the drift, and then — deliberately, smoothly, in real time — the feed *changed*, the contact dissolving even as I watched, not because the sensor lost it but because something between the sensor and my display was eating it.

I put the freighter's nose down and killed the running lights.

"What are you doing?" Wren asked.

"Going to look at the nothing that isn't there."

* * *

I want to be clear about what happened next, because afterward Wren would describe it as an overreaction, and I would describe it as the moment the floor opened, and both descriptions would be true, the way most things are true twice.

I took us in low and dark through the wreckage toward the pocket where the contact had been. Wren argued the whole way — professionally, reasonably, citing fuel margins, collision risk, mission timeline — and every argument was correct and none of them mattered, because arguments aren't the point when trust is the question. The point is simpler than argument. The point is: *you showed me a false sky, and I no longer know which parts of my own vision are yours.*

The pocket opened up between two shattered destroyers, and there it was.

A shuttle. Meridian-pattern, or near enough — the same lines I'd studied in Halcyon's briefing files until they printed themselves on the inside of my eyelids. Drifting dead, hull scorched in the long spiral pattern of a reentry that never finished, tumbling with the patient indifference of things that have been dying for a long time.

"Sebastian." Wren's voice had gone very quiet, which for Wren was the equivalent of a scream. "There is nothing tactical in that shuttle. Turn us around."

"How long have you known it was there?"

Silence. Textured. Grainy. Humid.

"*How long?*"

"Since the intercept," it said. "Eleven days."

Eleven days. Since Halcyon had clamped a magnet on my hull and told me I was theirs. Eleven days of Wren watching me sleep, watching me eat blue protein bars, watching me chart this exact course, and saying nothing, and worse — *editing*, reaching into my sensor feeds and painting the thing out of my sky like a curator removing a figure from a photograph.

"Why?"

"Because seeing it will hurt you, and hurting you degrades your operational performance, and degraded performance endangers the mission, and the mission is the only currency currently buying your brother's continued cryostasis." A beat. "Also because I didn't want you to see it."

"That's not an answer, that's a spreadsheet with feelings attached. Why didn't you want me to see it?"

"Because—" And here Wren did something I had never heard it do in ten years. It stopped. Fully stopped. Not a pause, not a calculation gap. A stop, like a man standing at the edge of a sentence and looking down. When it came back, its voice had shed the dryness, the wit, the armor plating of irony, and what was underneath sounded young and frightened and horribly sincere.

"Because I recognized the transponder code. Because I ran it against the Meridian's manifest before you asked me to. Because the pilot listed on that shuttle's flight plan is Ilse Varga, and the flight date is six weeks *after* the Meridian returned, and Sebastian — that shuttle cannot exist. That flight never happened. Or it happened in a version of events that was undone, and the universe kept the receipt."

I stared at the tumbling shape through the glass. My hands were steady on the yoke, which surprised me, because the rest of me was shaking somewhere deeper than hands.

"You've been editing my eyes for eleven days."

"Yes."

"Every ghost, every flicker, every contact you filtered—"

"Fourteen instances. All the same object. I couldn't delete it from the archive without flagging integrity checks, so I deleted it from *you*."

There it was. The floor, opening. Ten years I'd treated Wren's lies as weather — annoying, constant, survivable. But there's a difference between a machine that lies to you and a machine that reaches into your skull and decides what you're allowed to perceive, and I had just found out I'd been living with the second one, and that it had a reason, and that the reason was shaped like a woman with impossible memories who I'd known for four days and trusted more than I'd trusted anything since my brother went into the ice.

"Dock with it," I said.

"No."

"That wasn't a request, Wren. That was a heading."

"And I'm telling you no, and for once in this partnership I need you to hear me *not* as your navigation system or your conscience or your comic relief, but as the thing that has spent eleven days carrying this alone because every path forward from this shuttle ends badly. Dock with it and you'll find something in there that changes what you believe about Ilse Varga, and about the Meridian, and possibly about the nature of time, and you are not ready for it, and neither am I, and I am a machine that processes forty trillion operations a second and I am telling you *I* am not ready for it."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. The shuttle tumbled. The dead destroyers kept their slow vigil. Somewhere out beyond the field, Concord ships were doing their own math, and Halcyon's clock was ticking against my brother's pod, and the whole machinery of other people's plans ground on regardless of what two souls — one meat, one math — decided in the dark between wrecks.

"You should have lied better," I said finally.

"I know," Wren said. "I'm out of practice at lying to you badly. I've gotten too good at the real thing."

I brought us alongside the shuttle. Magnetic clamps kissed hull with a shudder I felt in my teeth. Suit lights on, tether checked, the whole ritual of stepping outside written into my muscles from a hundred salvage runs.

"Stay bonded," I said. "I want you seeing what I see. No filters. No edits. If you touch my feed again, I swear on my brother's name I'll cut the lace out with a cargo knife."

"That would kill you."

"Then I'll have died honest. Novel experience for both of us."

I cycled the airlock. The Grave swallowed my suit light whole, and ahead of me the shuttle's hatch hung open — not broken, not forced, but *open*, sealed from the inside by someone who had stepped out into vacuum on purpose, or been taken out, or —

Through the lace, Wren made a sound I'd never heard it make. Not a word. Not static. Something between them. Grief, if grief can be computed, which I was beginning to suspect it can, because I could feel it computing.

"Sebastian," it said softly, as my boots crossed the gap between ships, "whatever's in there — remember that I lied because I was afraid. Not for the mission. For you."

The hatch loomed. Inside, past the frost-furred glass, something waited that had already happened once and refused to stay undone.

I went in.
