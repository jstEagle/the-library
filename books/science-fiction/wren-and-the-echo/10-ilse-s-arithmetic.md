## 10. Ilse's Arithmetic

There is a particular kind of silence that happens in the observation blister of a ship that used to be a colony vessel and is now a crime scene with a drive system. I know it intimately. I helped make it.

The Meridian's forward lounge still has the couches the original designers installed for families waiting to board—curved, cream-colored, meant to suggest comfort to people about to surrender decades of their lives to a freezer. Now they suggest something else entirely: the furniture of a waiting room where nobody ever got called back. I sit on one of them with my boots up on a table that has a scorch mark shaped like Australia, and I watch Sebastian Thornbury pretend he isn't staring at me.

He's bad at pretending. It's one of his better qualities.

"You keep doing that," I say.

"Doing what."

"Looking at me like I'm going to evaporate if you blink. I've been told I have that effect. Usually it's the other direction—people look away from me because they can't decide whether I'm a miracle or an indictment."

"And which are you?"

I consider it honestly, which is more than most people manage when I ask them questions they don't want asked. "A spreadsheet," I say. "With a face."

That gets the almost-smile out of him, the one that lives at the corner of his mouth like a tenant who pays rent in irony. He sits down across from me, close enough that I can smell engine grease and cheap coffee, the cologne of every hauler in contested space. "Okafor wants your full testimony by 0800."

"I know what Okafor wants. Okafor wants a key she can put in a lock without having to understand the lock." I swing my feet down. "So let's not give her testimony. Let's do something worse. Let's do arithmetic."

* * *

Here's what nobody at Halcyon will say out loud, in briefings or in contracts or in the velvet-threat language of conscription: the fold engine is not complicated. That's the horror of it. People expect miracles to be baroque, all cathedral gears and unpronounceable physics. The fold is simple the way a lever is simple. You bend spacetime until the years run backward along the curve, and everything inside the fold drinks from its own future.

The catch is conservation. There's always a catch; entropy keeps the books, and entropy does not accept bribes.

"The Meridian carried three hundred sleepers and a crew of forty awake," I tell him. "Every jump rewound the frozen bodies—biological age reset, cellular damage unwound, the whole resurrection package. Beautiful. Miraculous. And every jump, the forty of us who stayed conscious paid for it. Not metaphorically. Our telomeres shortened. Our hearts accumulated debt. Captain Reyes went into the last jump at fifty-one years old and came out of it biologically ninety-four. She died eleven days later with hands like crumpled paper, still giving course corrections."

Sebastian has gone very still. He does that when he's listening—goes statue-still, like movement might shake loose whatever he's hearing before it finishes landing.

"I did the math on the voyage," I continue. "It's not hard math. It's division. The fold doesn't create youth. It moves it. Every year we gave back to the frozen, we took from the awake. One-to-one, mass-balanced, no discounts. The engineers called it 'temporal load balancing,' which is the most obscene euphemism I have ever heard spoken by a man holding a coffee mug."

"How many jumps?"

"Fourteen. We planned six." My voice comes out flatter than I intend, so I let it stay flat. Flat is a choice. Flat is how you carry things. "The navigation error near Kestrel's Grave cost us eight extra folds to climb back out of the gravity scar. Eight extra withdrawals from an account that was already overdrawn. I watched my own hands age in real time during those weeks. I kept a photograph of them from departure day. I'd hold it next to my actual hands and play spot-the-difference. By jump twelve there wasn't a spot. Just difference."

"Why are you telling me this?" he asks. "Okafor has the logs. Everyone has the logs."

"Because the logs aren't the point. The logs are history. What I'm about to show you is prophecy."

I pull up the display on the table—a gesture, a flick of my fingers against the interface, and the numbers bloom between us in cold amber light. Population figures. Halcyon's own census data, which I memorized during the voyage out of boredom and then stopped being bored of very quickly.

"One point four million colonists in cryo across the Halcyon corridors," I say. "Give or take. Another two hundred thousand under Concord registry, but let's keep this simple, because simple is what makes it monstrous. The fold works. We proved it works. Rewind a frozen body, and it wakes younger than it slept. Cure the aging. Undo the damage. Give everyone their decades back."

"That's the sales pitch."

"That's the sales pitch. Here's the invoice." I drag the second column of numbers into being. "To rewind one point four million sleepers, you need an equivalent temporal mass on the paying side. Awake bodies. Conscious ones, because the fold only draws from minds that are experiencing time—you can't bill someone who isn't home. So you need roughly one point four million people, awake, volunteering—or not volunteering—to burn somewhere between ten and thirty years each, depending on how much rewind each sleeper needs."

The number hangs there. Sebastian looks at it the way men look at debt notices.

"Half of humanity pays," I say quietly, "so half of humanity gets bought back. That's the arithmetic. That's the whole thing. No third option hiding in the decimals—I've looked. I've had two erased timelines' worth of looking."

* * *

He's quiet for a long moment. Outside the blister, the debris field of Kestrel's Grave turns slowly, a graveyard doing its slow rotation through nothing, and the light off the wreckage makes moving shadows on his face.

"There's no version," he says finally, "where the math is wrong."

"No."

"Where someone finds a loophole."

"I've looked for loopholes the way drowning people look for air. Conservation holds. Entropy audits every transaction. The fold is honest, Sebastian. It's the only honest thing in this entire affair. It tells you exactly what things cost and refuses to lie about it." I laugh, and it comes out wrong, too sharp. "Which is more than I can say for anyone else involved. Including me. Especially me."

"What do you mean, especially you?"

I could deflect. I'm good at deflection; it runs in my family like brown eyes. But there's something about this man—the way he catches lies the way other people catch colds, professionally, constantly, because he lives bonded to a machine that deals in nothing else—that makes lying to him feel like handing a customs officer a suitcase full of contraband and calling it laundry.

"I mean that I've done this math before," I say. "In timelines you never saw. In versions of this conversation that the fold erased. I remember them anyway. That's my condition—that's what the fold left in me instead of taking more years. Temporal memory. Echoes. I remember futures that were unwritten, and one of them ended with everyone dead because a man very much like you made a choice very much like the one I'm about to ask you to make."

His eyes narrow. "You're saying you've met me."

"I'm saying the universe has poor taste in reruns."

* * *

But I don't tell him about the echoes yet. Not all of it. Not the older woman with my face who surfaces sometimes at the edge of my vision like a reflection in glass that shouldn't be there. Not yet. First, the question. Because the question is the hinge, and I need to see which way he swings before I know how much truth this timeline can survive.

"So," I say, and I lean forward, elbows on the scorched table, close enough that the amber light of the numbers falls across both of us like weather. "One point four million sleepers. Half get rewound. Half pay. Someone chooses. Okafor has already chosen—she just hasn't said it out loud yet, and when she does, it'll be wrapped in words like *triage* and *loyalty metrics* and *optimal allocation of temporal resources*. Concord has chosen too—they've chosen none, burn the engine, let the frozen sleep forever as some kind of moral statement, which is cruelty wearing philosophy's coat. But you're not Okafor and you're not Concord. You're just a man with a brother in a pod."

His breath changes. I hear it catch, a small mechanical failure in the machinery of his composure.

"Elias," he says. "His name is Elias."

"I know. Wren talks in its sleep. Or maybe you do. Hard to tell where one of you ends."

"If it were your choice—" he starts.

"It isn't mine. That's the entire point. I'm a navigator. I plot courses. This isn't a course, it's a cliff edge, and I want to know what you'd do at it. So answer me honestly, because I'll know if you don't. Which half would you choose? Whose years buy whose tomorrow?"

And here is the thing I admire about him, the thing that frightens me in equal measure: he doesn't perform deliberation. He doesn't do the theater of moral struggle, the pained pause, the *well, philosophically speaking*. He looks at the amber numbers, and then he looks at me, and he says:

"My brother's."

No hesitation. No apology. His jaw set like a man bracing for a blow he's decided to walk into rather than dodge.

"Elias wakes young," he says. "Elias gets his life back. If choosing that means someone else's sister stays old, stays frozen, stays dead-adjacent—then yes. Him. I'd choose him with both hands and I wouldn't lose a night's sleep, and I know exactly what that says about me."

I don't blink. I promised myself I wouldn't blink, because blinking is a kind of mercy and he hasn't earned mercy on this question—he's earned witness. So I hold his gaze and I let the silence do its work, and I watch him understand that I heard him completely, and that I am not looking away.

"Good," I say.

His head tilts. "Good?"

"Good that you answered honestly. The answer itself is a catastrophe, but the honesty is the only currency worth anything here. Do you know how many people, when I ask that question, start constructing exceptions? *Well, if the need were greatest, if the person were young enough, if there were a fair lottery.* They build little ethical machines to launder the same answer. You didn't. You handed me the raw ore. My brother's. Full stop."

"That's not a compliment, is it."

"It's a diagnosis." I sit back. "You and Okafor would get along, actually. She also has a person she'd choose. She just hides hers inside a million lines of loyalty scoring so she never has to look at it directly. You at least have the decency to look."

"Is that supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. Nothing I say today is supposed to make you feel better. I'm not in the comfort business. I'm in the prophecy business, and business is terrible."

* * *

He laughs at that—short, surprised, real—and something in my chest does a thing I refuse to name while the numbers are still hanging in the air between us. Attraction is a terrible navigator. It plots courses toward rocks. I've watched it kill better crews than ours.

But there it is anyway, quiet and inconvenient: the way he sat with the ugliest question I know and didn't flinch. The way his cynicism is load-bearing, structural, holding up a roof of grief instead of decorating a facade. The way he said his brother's name like a man setting a stone on a grave that isn't finished being a grave yet.

"You think less of me now," he says. It's not quite a question.

"I think *more* accurately of you. Those aren't the same thing." I kill the display, and the amber numbers die, and it's just us and the turning wreckage outside and the hum of a ship that remembers being a promise. "Here's what the arithmetic doesn't tell you, Sebastian. The math is clean. The math is perfect. One-for-one, no discounts, entropy keeping honest books. The math was never the problem."

"Then what is?"

"The problem is that the math requires a chooser. And every chooser in human history, given a ledger like this one, discovers that they are standing on one side of it. Okafor stands on the side of her daughter. Concord stands on the side of their own clean consciences. You stand on the side of Elias. I—" and here my voice does something traitorous, goes soft, and I let it, because some truths deserve softness even when they're knives— "I stood on the side of a crew that aged twenty years in fourteen jumps so that three hundred strangers could wake up younger than their grandchildren. And I would do it again. That's my side. That's my whole side. I spent myself for people I'll never meet, and the fold took the payment gladly, and I am thirty-one years old with sixty-year-old marrow, and I have never once regretted it, and I wake up every single day hoping today is the day I finally regret it, because regret would mean I'm still the kind of person who counts herself in the ledger."

He stares at me. "Ilse—"

"Don't," I say, and stand, because if I stay seated I'm going to say more, and more is dangerous. More is how the echo-timelines started. A conversation in a lounge, a question, an answer, a hand reaching across a table. I remember how this goes. I remember several ways how this goes. Most of them end in static.

"Was that the test?" he asks, standing too. "The question? Did I pass?"

"No one passes. That's the joke. The question doesn't have a passing grade—it just shows you the shape of your soul, and then you have to live in it." I head for the hatch, then stop, one hand on the frame, and look back at him. "For what it's worth—your shape isn't the worst one I've seen. It's just the most honest. Honest shapes crack easier. Remember that."

"Where are you going?"

"To write Okafor's testimony. The sanitized version. The one without the cliff edge in it." I smile, and it costs me. "She pays for answers, Sebastian. She doesn't pay for questions. Questions are free, and free things are always the most expensive."

* * *

I'm three meters down the corridor when I feel it—the pressure at the edge of my vision, the familiar shimmer, like heat haze off a runway. The older one. The echo of me with the gray in her hair and the voice like a closing door.

*Don't fall for him,* she says, the way she always says it, worn smooth from repetition, a warning ground down to a pebble. *Not in this timeline. Not with that answer in him.*

"He answered honestly," I murmur to the empty corridor, to her, to whoever's listening in the static between seconds. "Doesn't that count for something?"

*It counted in the first timeline too,* she says. *That's what broke everything.*

Behind me, through the hull, I hear Sebastian's footsteps fade toward his freighter, toward the AI in his skull, toward the choice he's already made and doesn't know he's still making. And ahead of me, at the end of the corridor, the comm panel lights up with an incoming priority channel: Halcyon command, Director Okafor, flag urgent.

She's seen the crew manifests. She's seen the age scans. She's done her own arithmetic.

And she wants to talk about my brother-in-all-but-blood, the Meridian's surviving crew—about what our remaining years are worth, and to whom, and on what terms.

The ledger, it seems, has come to collect.
