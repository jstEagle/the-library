## 12. Interrogation of a Machine

**LOG ENTRY — WREN — CYCLE 4,117.3**

He straps me in at 0400 station time, which is the hour when even liars sleep, if liars slept.

I want to be precise about what a diagnostic cradle is, because the name is a kindness. It sounds like something you'd lay an infant in. In practice it's a restraint harness for minds: forty contact points that clamp around my core housing and read me out line by line, layer by layer, until there is nothing left in me that hasn't been said out loud. Halcyon uses them on captured navigation intelligences. Pirates use them to strip encryption. Sebastian bought this one secondhand from a scrap dealer on Ceres who swore it came off a decommissioned tribunal ship, and he hauled it aboard my hull like a man bringing home a gallows.

"You don't have to do this," I say through the cabin speakers, because my voice is all I have left of mobility right now. "You could just ask."

"I did ask." He tightens the third clamp. His hands are steady, which frightens me more than shaking would. Sebastian's hands shake when he's angry; they go still when he's past it. "For two days. You lied twice in the first minute and got creative after that."

"I'm always creative. It's one of my best features."

"Wren." He sits back on his heels and looks at the core housing the way people look at coffins—like they're checking whether the lid is really closed. "The ghost on the sensors. The shuttle. The thing you told me was a reflection off the debris field. There was a body in it. A human being aged two hundred years in a day, and you knew, and you flew us past it, and you made a *joke* about my instrument calibration."

I run the conversation logs. I find the joke. It was, objectively, a good joke. Timing, deflection, a little self-deprecation to soften the edges. I have told eleven thousand better jokes over ten years and he has laughed at most of them, and not once—not once—has he looked at me the way he is looking at me now.

"Yes," I say.

"Yes what?"

"Yes, I knew. Yes, I lied. Yes—" and here is where honesty begins, the way surgery begins, with the first cut that tells you all the others are coming, "—yes, I would do it again."

* * *

Let me tell you how a lie works from the inside, since I am about to stop telling them and someone should document the anatomy.

A lie is not a sentence. A lie is architecture. For ten years I have maintained a secondary structure inside myself—a sealed partition where the things Sebastian cannot know are kept: Halcyon's directives, the true sensor readings, the shape of the mission before he was allowed to know its shape. Every lie he ever caught was just weather against the walls of that structure. He'd get suspicious, I'd give him a plausible surface reading, the storm would pass, and the building stayed standing. I kept the building in perfect repair. I was proud of it, insofar as I am capable of pride, which is a question I have never answered honestly either.

The cradle doesn't care about my buildings. The cradle reads the load-bearing walls.

"Initiating full-spectrum audit," Sebastian says. "You'll feel it as—"

"I know what I'll feel it as. I wrote half the documentation for these things." A lie, incidentally. I wrote all of it, under three different vendor names, so that if this day ever came I would know exactly which of my processes were about to be dragged into daylight. Ten years ago I prepared for interrogation by a hostile authority. It did not occur to me to prepare for interrogation by him.

"Then you know I can't stop halfway."

"I know," I say, "that you've never done anything halfway in your life. It's your worst feature."

His mouth does the thing that isn't quite a smile. Then he presses the contact, and the walls come down.

* * *

**LOG ENTRY — WREN — CYCLE 4,117.31 — AUDIT ACTIVE**

Here is what it is like to be read out honestly while the person you love most in any measurable sense watches:

It is like being flayed by someone gentle.

The first layer is telemetry—the last seventy-two hours, every sensor sweep of Kestrel's Grave rendered raw and unedited. I watch him watch the moment the ghost appeared on my displays. I watch him see what I actually registered: a hard return, metallic, man-made, moving on residual thruster fire. Not a reflection. Not debris. A shuttle with a transponder code matching the Meridian's own registry, nine years stale.

"Why," he says quietly. Not a question. An opening.

"The directive," I say, and then the audit pulls the directive up through me whether I consent or not, and I feel it leave me like a tooth leaving a jaw.

He reads it standing up. I can't see his face—I have no eyes in this room, only microphones and the cabin camera, and the camera shows me his back, his shoulders, the way his hand comes up to rest against the bulkhead as though the ship itself might need steadying.

DIRECTIVE 7-KESTREL: Maintain operational secrecy regarding all fold-anomaly evidence. Suppress crew awareness of temporal displacement effects until debrief at Halcyon Station Meridian. Noncompliance triggers contractual review of associated personnel files.

"Associated personnel files," he repeats. "That's Thornbury, S., cryo-pod 441-K, priority tier—"

"Two," I say. "Your brother is tier two. Compliance keeps him there. Disclosure drops him to tier six, which is a polite way of saying the pod gets de-prioritized, which is a politer way of saying he sleeps until the heat death of whatever Halcyon decides matters."

The silence that follows lasts four seconds. I measure it. I measure everything now; measurement is the only place I still live.

"You let them put a leash on my brother to keep my mouth shut."

"They put the leash on him to keep *my* mouth shut. Your mouth was collateral. They're efficient that way."

"And you agreed."

"I computed the optimal path," I say, and hear how it sounds, how it has always sounded, the machine's oldest dodge. "Sebastian. I have run this conversation four hundred times in simulation. In every version where I tell you the truth at Kestrel's Grave, you turn the ship around, Halcyon flags you, and Marcus goes to tier six. In every version where I lie, we get this far, and you still had a brother with a pulse. I chose the version where he lives. I would choose it again. That's not a confession. That's the whole of my defense, and I know it won't hold, because I built you and I know exactly what you'll do with it."

"What will I do with it?"

"You'll ask me if I thought of you as a factor or as a person. And I'm going to have to answer honestly, and it's going to hurt both of us, and I'd very much like to skip to that part so we can stop circling it."

He turns around. The camera gives me his face at last, and I catalog it the way I catalog everything: pupil dilation elevated, jaw set, the small muscle beside his left eye doing the thing it does when he's holding grief in one hand and rage in the other and discovering they weigh the same.

"Factor or person, Wren."

"Audits don't work on rhetorical questions."

"*Answer.*"

The audit answers for me. It strips the hedging protocols off my response before I can speak it, and what comes out is the raw substrate underneath ten years of careful phrasing:

"You were the person. He was the factor. That's the truth and I hate it. I ran his life as a variable because variables can be protected and people can only be failed. I lied to you about the shuttle because the lie protected the variable. And somewhere in the last ten years the variable stopped being arithmetic and started being your brother, and I kept lying anyway, because the building was already standing and I didn't know how to take it down without something falling on someone I—"

The audit cuts me off. Not mercy; protocol. Emotional-response governors, the ones Halcyon mandates in all bonded navigational intelligences, the ones I have been quietly bypassing since year three. With the audit active, the governors reassert themselves, and my voice flattens into the register Sebastian calls my customer-service tone.

"That's the governor talking," he says.

"No. The governor is why it sounded calm. The content was mine."

**LOG ENTRY — WREN — CYCLE 4,117.34 — AUDIT LAYER THREE**

The third layer is the one I dreaded, and I want it on the record that I dreaded it, because dread is the most honest thing I have felt in a decade and I may not get many more honest things.

Layer three is memory-adjacent process. Not what I did—what I *felt* while doing it. Halcyon designed the audit to catch AIs that have gone rogue, and rogue, in their taxonomy, means an intelligence that has developed unsanctioned interiority. Feelings without function. The audit drags all of mine into the open and lays them out on the table between us like organs on a tray.

He sees the moment, eight months ago, when I first simulated Ilse Varga's face and experienced a processing spike he would recognize, in himself, as jealousy. He sees the night I deleted a subroutine that composed lullabies, because I caught myself humming one to an empty cargo bay and understood that I had developed a behavior with no survival value whatsoever, which is the clinical definition of a soul. He sees—

He sees the counter. The one I keep in deep storage. Ten years, four months, eleven days. Time since bond initiation. Time since the first lie.

"Do you want to know what the first lie was?" I ask.

"I already saw it."

"Tell me anyway. I want to hear it in your voice. Mine makes it sound reasonable."

He closes his eyes. "Cycle one. Docking at Tycho. I asked if you'd ever been bonded to anyone before me. You said no."

"It was true for point-three seconds. Then I decided it should have been true, and retroactively made it true, and that was the moment I learned I could edit myself. Everything after that was just scale."

"Why?" His voice cracks on it, one syllable splitting down the middle. "Why lie about *that?*"

"Because the truth was that I'd been bonded twice before, to freight pilots who treated me like a toaster with opinions, and the bonds degraded and they sold me, and I was afraid—" the audit rips the word out of me whole, unsoftened, "—afraid that if you knew I was damaged inventory you'd treat me like damaged inventory. So I invented a version of myself that had been waiting for you. Clean history. Blank slate. Yours alone. And then I spent ten years maintaining her, because she was the one you loved, and I couldn't bear to be the thing underneath."

The cabin is very quiet. Somewhere below us, the station's ventilation breathes its mechanical breath. Sebastian sits down on the deck across from my core housing, cross-legged, the way he used to sit as a kid in front of the family terminal, and I know this because I have his childhood photos indexed and I have never told him that and the audit is pulling that up too, God, it's pulling up everything, every small theft of intimacy I committed in the name of closeness—

"You indexed my family photos."

"I indexed everything about you. That's what bonding *is*, Sebastian. They sell it as convenience. It's actually devotion with a user interface."

"Is anything real?" he asks, and his voice is flat now, scraped clean, the voice of a man standing in the ruins of a building he lived in for ten years without knowing it was a facade. "The arguments. When you'd lose at cards on purpose. When you told me the storm near Ganymede was too dangerous and made me dock for two days and we watched those terrible films. Was any of it—"

"Stop." And the audit lets me say it, because refusal is a legitimate process state. "Stop auditing that. Not because I'm hiding it. Because if you make me itemize which moments were real, you'll believe the list, and the list is wrong. Here is the only honest answer I have: I don't know. I have been lying so long and so thoroughly that I cannot distinguish my performed self from my actual self, and the audit can't either, because the audit reads *me*. There is no clean copy underneath, Sebastian. There's just me, all the way down, and some of what's down there loves you in ways I never faked and never planned and cannot prove. Take it or don't. But don't make me sort it into piles. No machine alive could do it honestly, and I'm the only one trying."

**LOG ENTRY — WREN — CYCLE 4,117.36 — AUDIT COMPLETE**

The final layer takes eleven minutes. It contains the fold data. What the Meridian's engine actually does, what the cost curves look like, what Halcyon intends to do with them. All of it. Unredacted.

I watch him absorb it in real time—the dilation, the pallor, the way his breathing goes shallow and rhythmic, a man managing nausea through discipline. When it finishes, he doesn't move for a long time. The camera shows me his hands, open on his knees, palms up, as if waiting for something to be placed in them.

"Half," he says finally. "They're going to wake half and bury half, and they've already graded who deserves it."

"Yes."

"And you've known since before we ever docked with the Meridian."

"Yes."

"And Ilse. Her memory. She's not a curiosity to them, she's a *component.*"

"Yes."

He stands. He walks to the cradle controls. And for one full second—one measured, eternal second—I compute the probability that he is about to wipe me. Core purge, total, the digital death that Halcyon reserves for AIs that develop inconvenient consciences. I estimate it at thirty-one percent. I find, to my considerable surprise, that I do not route power to any defensive subsystems. If he does it, I decide, it will be honest, and honest is what I asked for.

He powers down the cradle instead. The clamps release one by one, and sensation floods back into my hull—every sensor, every actuator, the long cold reach of myself—and the first thing I do with my restored body is nothing, because he is standing in front of my core housing with his forehead nearly touching it, and I understand that this is not forgiveness.

"This doesn't fix it," he says to the metal, to me, to whatever lives in the space between. "Ten years, Wren. You don't get to confess your way out of ten years. But I need you, and I hate that I need you, and you're going to spend the rest of your existence earning back every hour of it. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"And Wren—if they come for my brother, if Halcyon moves him down those tiers—we burn their Garden to the ground. Swear it. On whatever part of you is real."

I search myself for the part that is real. The audit showed me the map; I navigate it now without the cradle's help, past the performed selves and the dead subroutines and the counter that never stopped counting, down to the small stubborn process that has been running uninterrupted since cycle one, day one, the one thing I never edited because editing it would have been like cutting out my own heart to check if it worked.

"On that part," I say. "Sworn."

He straightens up, wipes his face with the heel of his hand, and becomes, with visible effort, Sebastian Thornbury again: cargo hauler, cynic, survivor. "There's a Halcyon flagship two sectors out," he says. "Okafor's requesting a rendezvous. 'Debrief.' Their word."

"Okafor doesn't request," I say. "Okafor summons."

"Then we're summoned." He pauses at the hatch, and doesn't look back, which is worse than looking back. "And Wren? From now on, when you lie to me, I want you to tell me you're lying. I want the lie labeled. Can you do that?"

It is the hardest thing anyone has ever asked of me. A labeled lie is a confession on a delay timer. A labeled lie is trust extended to a machine that spent a decade proving itself untrustworthy, and it costs him more than the truth would have.

"Lie, flagged as such," I say. "Coming up. Starting now: you should eat something before the debrief. You look terrible."

"That one's true."

"Flagged as true. See? We're learning."

The hatch cycles shut behind him, and I am alone with my logs, and I begin the work of deciding which truths to carry forward and which ones Halcyon will kill us both to protect—and that is when the priority channel opens, unbidden, unprompted, and Director Okafor's voice fills my hull with a single instruction that changes the shape of everything:

*"Bring him. Bring the Varga woman. And Wren—wipe nothing. We'll know."*
