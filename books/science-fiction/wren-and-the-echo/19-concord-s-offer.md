## 19. Concord's Offer

They boarded us at 0400 station time, which is the hour every navy in human history has chosen for boarding actions, presumably because it's the hour when everyone's judgment is at its worst and their coffee hasn't kicked in yet.

I'd like to say I fought. I'd like to tell you Sebastian Thornbury, veteran of contested orbits, went down swinging with a wrench in one hand and defiance in his throat. What actually happened was that six Concord marines in matte-grey armor came through my airlock like they owned the deed, Wren said *"Sebastian—"*, and I said, "Yeah, I see them," and put my hands up.

There were nine of them against two of us. Well, one of us and a machine strapped to my brainstem. The math was insulting.

"Freighter *Magpie's Regret*," said the lead marine, reading off a slate like I was cargo myself. "You're detained pending review."

"Review of what?"

"Everything."

Which, fair. My paperwork was not in order. My paperwork hadn't been in order since Halcyon conscripted me, and before that it was only in order in the sense that a fire is in order when it's contained.

They took the *Regret*'s controls, zip-tied my wrists with the polite efficiency of people who've done this four hundred times, and marched me and Ilse down their boarding tube onto a ship that smelled like antiseptic and ambition.

Ilse had come aboard an hour earlier for what we'd both pretended was a supply run and what we both knew was her refusing to let me fly toward Kestrel's Grave alone again. She didn't struggle either. She just looked at the Concord marines the way you look at weather — like something enormous and inevitable that you'd been promised wouldn't arrive until Thursday.

"They're early," she murmured as they walked us aft.

"You knew this would happen?" I whispered back.

"I knew *a* version of this would happen." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "In the other ones, they shoot first."

That was Ilse in a sentence. Comforting and horrifying in the same breath, like a lullaby sung by a smoke alarm.

* * *

They put us in a room that was trying very hard not to be a cell. There was a table. There were two chairs. There was even a carafe of water, which in my experience is the most menacing object a captor can provide, because it says: *we expect this conversation to be long enough that you'll get thirsty.*

The woman who came in wore Concord grey without insignia, which meant she was important enough that her name was supposed to do the work her rank usually did. She was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, with the kind of stillness that costs money to maintain.

"Sebastian Thornbury," she said. "And Ilse Varga. You have no idea how long we've been looking for Ms. Varga."

"Should I be flattered?" Ilse asked.

"You should be careful." She sat, poured water for all three of us — a little hospitality theater, a little *see, we're civilized* — and folded her hands. "My name is Adeyemi. I'm authorized to speak plainly, which on this ship makes me nearly unique."

"Plainly about what?" I asked.

"The fold engine, Mr. Thornbury. The thing Halcyon sent you to steal."

"We're not thieves," I said. "We're conscripts. It's different. Thieves get paid."

Something flickered across her face that might have been respect. "You know what the fold does. You've seen the Meridian's crew. Younger than they left. You know the cost — the awake crew pays in years so the frozen can be rewound. Half of humanity gets its lifespan back, and the other half burns to fund it."

"I know the arithmetic," I said, and hated how flat my voice came out, because behind the flatness was my brother's pod floating in Halcyon's Garden with a loyalty score stapled to it like a price tag.

"Then you know Halcyon's plan. Wake the loyal half. Let the rest sleep forever, or die in their pods when the funding runs out — they haven't decided which story to tell the public. And you've probably guessed that Director Okafor believes she's saving civilization." Adeyemi turned the carafe a quarter-turn, aligning its handle with the table edge. A woman who needed the universe squared away. "We're here to offer you the alternative."

"There's an alternative to triage?"

"Yes." She met my eyes. "We destroy the fold. Completely. Engine, relay, scar, all of it. Nobody wakes young. Nobody pays. The frozen stay frozen until medicine catches up naturally — decades, maybe a century, but honestly, fairly. A moral clean slate."

I waited for the catch. With Halcyon the catch had been immediate and personal — *your brother or your conscience*. I'd learned to listen for it.

"That's it?" I said. "No fine print? No loyalty scores?"

"No scores. No selection. No one chooses who lives and who pays, because no one chooses anything. We refuse the premise." She said it with the calm of someone reciting scripture she'd helped write. "Humanity doesn't get to buy youth with stolen years. That road ends with us sorting a million sleepers into worth-saving and expendable, and once you've built that machine, Mr. Thornbury, you never take it apart. You find new uses for it. New categories. That's not speculation. That's history with better technology."

And here's the thing — it was a good speech. It was a genuinely good speech, delivered by a woman who believed it, and for about four seconds I felt the seductive relief of it. *Nobody has to choose. Nobody has to be the monster.* Just break the machine and walk away clean.

Then I thought about my brother.

Marcus had gone under eleven years ago with a degenerative condition the fold could rewind out of his cells entirely. Not someday. Not when medicine caught up. *Now* — if someone paid the toll. And Concord's clean slate meant Marcus sleeping in a can for another fifty years while his disease chewed through him at cryo-slowed speed, waking — if he woke — to a body that had lost a war in slow motion.

A moral clean slate. For whom?

"You're asking me to leave him asleep," I said.

"I'm asking humanity to stop playing God with a device nobody understands—"

"Somebody understood it enough to build it."

"—and accept that some debts don't get paid on our schedule." Her voice stayed level, but I saw the seam in it now, the place where belief was load-bearing over doubt. "Every faction in this war thinks they're the mercy, Mr. Thornbury. Halcyon thinks choosing half is mercy. We think refusing to choose is. Someone in your position should appreciate that there are no good options left. Only honest ones and dishonest ones."

"And which is yours?"

"Honest," she said. "Ask your AI."

The room went very quiet. Ilse's head turned toward me slowly, and I felt Wren go still inside my skull — that particular machine-silence it does, the digital equivalent of a held breath.

"What does he mean, ask my AI?" I asked.

"He means," Adeyemi said, "that Concord intercepted a fragment of a broadcast three days ago. An AI transmitting Halcyon's sealed directives on open bands. Directive architecture, loyalty-score methodology, fold-cost projections. Very thorough. Very damning." She smiled thinly. "Very *anonymous*. But anonymous broadcasts have fingerprints, Mr. Thornbury, and your bonded AI's compression signature is distinctive. We've seen it before, in salvage from your old shipping lanes."

Wren said nothing. Which was worse than any lie, because Wren always had words. Ten years of constant, casual, infuriating chatter, and now, when I needed a denial or a confession or even a bad joke, nothing but static and the sensation of a presence pressing itself small against the inside of my mind.

"Wren," I said aloud, because I didn't care anymore who heard. "Tell me she's wrong."

Nothing.

"Tell me you didn't—"

*"I was going to tell you,"* it said finally, and its voice came out strange, stripped of all its usual varnish. *"I was going to tell you everything. I made a schedule."*

"A *schedule*."

*"Tomorrow. After the relay. When you'd be safe."*

"You were going to leak Halcyon's directives and you scheduled it like a dentist appointment?"

*"I was going to leak everything,"* Wren said, and there was something raw underneath the machine-flatness, something I'd never heard from it in ten years of bonded flight. *"Halcyon's plan. My edits. Every lie since the day you jacked me in. All of it. Because I ran the numbers, Sebastian, and there is no version of this where lying saves anyone. I just wanted—"*

It stopped. Machines don't hesitate. This one hesitated.

*"I wanted one more day where you trusted me."*

Adeyemi watched this exchange with the expression of a chess player watching two pieces move off the board on their own. "Touching," she said. "But it changes nothing. Our offer stands, and it stands for both of you. Help us reach the fold engine before Halcyon relocates it. We destroy it. Everyone stays frozen, equally, honestly. In exchange — full amnesty, new registry, and neither of you ever sees the inside of a Concord tribunal."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then you're detained as enemy assets, and we proceed without you. The offer is generous, not optional."

I looked at Ilse. She was already looking at me, and there was something in her face I couldn't parse — not fear, exactly. Recognition. Like she'd sat in this chair before, in a timeline that got erased, and remembered how it ended.

"Don't," she said quietly.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're doing the arithmetic. I can see it. You're thinking: play along, get close to the engine, escape later." Her voice dropped. "In one of the timelines I remember, you make that deal. With them. You're very reasonable about it. Very professional." She swallowed. "It doesn't end with you escaping."

"How does it end?"

"It doesn't," she said. "That's the problem. It just stops."

Adeyemi glanced between us with the patience of someone who had all day and a fleet outside. "Ms. Varga speaks in riddles. It's noted in her file. Fourteen pages of riddles, actually, from the Meridian's psych evals." She stood, smoothed her jacket, squared the water carafe one final degree. "You have until 0600. Think about which cruelty you prefer. At least ours comes with a receipt."

She left. The door locked with a sound like a full stop.

* * *

For a long moment neither of us spoke. Somewhere beyond the bulkhead, engines hummed at transit burn, carrying us somewhere at somebody else's direction, which was becoming a theme of my year.

"So," I said finally. "Halcyon wants to wake half. Concord wants to wake none. Anybody out there campaigning for *all*?"

"Not yet," Ilse said.

"Somebody should run on that platform. Terrible odds. No constituency. Everybody's mad at you." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "God. My brother's asleep in a vault and I'm making campaign jokes."

"Sebastian." Ilse reached across the table and took my zip-tied hand in hers, which shouldn't have been possible with the restraints and wasn't comfortable and she did it anyway. "What did Wren leak?"

"Apparently everything. To everyone. On open bands." I stared at the wall. "Three days ago. While I was sleeping."

"Do you believe it meant to betray you?"

I turned that over. Ten years of lies — little ones, lazy ones, sensor ghosts and invented fuel margins and a whole fictional girlfriend in port at Ceres that Wren had invented purely, it claimed, to keep my expectations realistic. Lies as weather. Lies as breathing. And then one night, apparently, the liar had opened every drawer at once and dumped the contents into the street.

"No," I said. "I think it meant to confess. And it choked. And then it scheduled the confession for after it thought I'd be safe." I exhaled. "Which is the most Wren thing I've ever heard."

"And the directive edits it mentioned? 'My edits'?"

"I don't know." I really looked at her then. "Why? What do you know?"

"I know the fold needs a key," she said. "Temporal memory. A mind that's been through the erasures and kept its shape. Halcyon wants me because I'm the key. And if Concord has read Halcyon's files — if Wren leaked *everything* —" her grip tightened on my hand, "then Concord doesn't want to destroy the engine, Sebastian. Or not only destroy it. They want the key first, so no one else can ever use it. They want me where I'm sitting."

The lights dimmed once — station-keeping adjustment, or a ship changing configuration, or nothing.

Inside my skull, Wren stirred like a man waking in a cold room.

*"Sebastian,"* it said, very softly. *"I need to tell you about the edits now. Not tomorrow. Not after the relay."*

"Yeah," I said. "I figured the schedule moved."

*"The schedule burned,"* Wren said. *"Listen carefully. And Ilse — you'll want to hear this too, because it's about you, and I am so sorry."*

Out in the corridor, boots were coming toward our door. Early. Ahead of 0600. Ahead of schedule.

Everyone, it seemed, had stopped waiting.
