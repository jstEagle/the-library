## 20. Escape on Borrowed Time

**Sebastian**

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being captured by a rival interstellar bloc: the coffee is excellent.

I sat in a white room on Concord Station Kestrel with a cup of it going cold in my hands, and I thought about how Wren would have had something to say about that. Something glib. *They torture you with hospitality, Seb. The espresso is how you know you're doomed.* But Wren wasn't in my head. They'd put me in a faraday-lined cell and my neural lace had gone quiet as a grave, and I'd spent six hours learning what ten years of constant company had been papering over: the fact that I don't like myself very much when there's no one to talk to.

The door cycled. Two guards, gray uniforms, faces like unpaid invoices. And behind them, escorted by a woman with the patient posture of someone who'd already won, came Ilse.

My heart did something unprofessional.

"Sebastian Thornbury." The woman extended a hand I didn't take. "Commander Reyes, Concord External Intelligence. We've been having such an interesting conversation with your navigator."

"She's not my navigator."

"No," Ilse said, and her eyes found mine over the guards' shoulders. "I'm really not."

Reyes smiled like she was letting us in on something. "Ms. Varga has been extremely forthcoming about the fold engine. More forthcoming than Halcyon's files suggested she'd be capable of. Which raises questions we're still working through." She turned to Ilse. "You'll have another hour to consider our offer. Your friend here can consider his."

The door shut behind them. The lock made a sound like a full stop.

We stood there for maybe four seconds, two people in a white room, listening to each other breathe.

"You gave them something," I said.

"I gave them something true and useless. The fold's resonance harmonics. Public knowledge if anyone ever bothered to dig through Meridian's maintenance logs." She crossed the room, took the cold coffee out of my hands, drank it herself. "What did you give them?"

"My charming personality and a fake cargo manifest. Also I told them Wren was a navigation assistant, which is technically true the way a knife is technically a spoon."

That got the almost-smile. The one that started at the corner of her mouth and never quite committed. "Are you always like this?"

"Only when I'm terrified. It's a coping mechanism. My therapist was a smuggler named Dov who charged by the bottle."

She set the cup down. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into that register I'd learned on the Meridian—the one underneath the wit, the one where the arithmetic lived. "They offered me asylum. Full citizenship. A lab, a team, protection from Halcyon. All I have to do is help them build their own fold engine."

"And?"

"And Concord doesn't want to rewind anyone, Sebastian. They want to *compete*. A fold of their own, so they can hold the same knife Halcyon holds." She looked at me steadily. "They asked me what I remembered. From the erased timelines. And I realized I could tell them everything, and it would change nothing, because they've already decided what they want the future to look like. Everyone has. Except us."

"*Us* is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"It usually does." She glanced at the ceiling corner where a camera watched us with its red eye. Then she said, clearly, deliberately, in a voice pitched to carry: "I remember this room. I remember sitting exactly here, and I remember that in eleven minutes the station runs a pressure test on the C-ring corridors and every interior bulkhead unlocks for ninety seconds while the system recalibrates. I remember because last time, I didn't use it. Last time I stayed and took the deal and it killed everyone I—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Eleven minutes."

The camera's red light blinked. Blinked again.

And then, faintly, in the bone behind my ear where no sound should be able to reach me inside a faraday cage—a whisper. Thin as smoke. Static-shaped.

*—seb. eleven minutes. she's right. i'm in the station's mail relay, don't ask, i've been routing myself through their laundry scheduling system for six hours, it's humiliating but effective—*

"Wren," I breathed.

*—in the flesh. or whatever i am. listen fast: she's right about the pressure test, i confirmed it in the maintenance queue. corridor nine-C, aft. and seb? when i say trust her, i mean it. i ran the numbers on her twice and both times she comes out as the only variable that makes you survivable. that's not flattery. that's math.*

"What numbers?" Ilse asked sharply, watching my face. "What did it say?"

"That you're right. Eleven minutes." I stood up. "How do we get out of a locked cell during a pressure test?"

"We don't get out of the cell." She was already moving, dragging the table toward the wall, climbing onto it to reach the ventilation grate above. "We go *into* the walls. Concord builds cheap and vents everything through the service channels. It's how they save money on climate control and it's how we're going to save money on funerals."

I laced my fingers for her foot. She stepped into them like she'd known me for years instead of weeks, and hauled herself up, and pried the grate loose with a torque wrench she had somehow concealed on her person, because of course she had. Of course Ilse Varga carried a wrench to an interrogation.

"Nine minutes," she said, dropping into the dark.

I followed her into the guts of the station.

* * *

**Ilse**

There is a particular intimacy in crawling through ductwork with a man, and it is not the one the romance serials promise. It is elbows and knees and the smell of recycled air and the way he pauses when I pause, without being asked, without checking whether pausing costs him anything. He just does it. Twice now I have watched him do things like that—small, uncalculated generosities—and twice I have had to remind myself that I have also watched him do the other thing. The thing in the timeline that no longer exists. The deal. Okafor's hand extended, and his taking it, and the fold tearing open like a wound because its key was spent on a lie.

In that timeline I died angry at him. In the one before that, I died before I met him at all. This is the third version, and I keep catching myself thinking of it as the good one, and I hate that, because hope is just grief that hasn't been informed yet.

"Left here?" he asked, wedged sideways at a junction, breath fogging in the cooling air.

"Left. Then down the vertical run. Don't look down."

"I always look down. Looking down is how you respect the down."

I went first. The shaft dropped eight meters, rungs slick with condensation, and I felt him above me the whole way—not watching, exactly. *Anchoring.* If I fell, he'd fall too, and we'd both die stupidly in a Concord utility shaft, and somewhere in the static between timelines an older version of me would put her head in her hands.

At the bottom, the duct opened onto a maintenance gallery overlooking corridor nine-C. Through the slatted vent I could see it: a long gray throat of a hallway, emergency lights burning amber, and at the far end the airlock to the docking spur where—according to Wren's whispered briefing, delivered in fragments through the wall plumbing like a ghost with a grudge—our ship waited. Not my Meridian. *His* freighter, impounded at the dock, guarded by two bored contractors and a customs drone.

"The pressure test starts in three minutes," I said. "When it does, the bulkheads cycle open for recalibration. Ninety seconds. We drop into the corridor, run the length of it, and we're through the airlock before it seals."

"And the guards?"

"Guards are in the pressurized sections. Nine-C gets evacuated for the test. That's the whole point of the test—to prove the evacuation works."

"And if it doesn't work?"

"Then we're the test."

He looked at me in the amber half-light, and I watched him decide. I have seen men decide many things in many timelines. Most decisions are small and cowardly and dressed up as prudence. His wasn't. He nodded once, like a man signing something.

"For the record," he said, "I want it noted that this plan has no exit strategy."

"Noted. Any objections?"

"Numerous. Overruled by circumstances." He checked the charge on a stolen sidearm he must have lifted off a guard, because of course he had; the two of us were apparently the same kind of disaster wearing different coats. "Wren. You still in the laundry?"

*I'm everywhere,* said the voice in both our ears now, thin and crackling, a signal stretched across improvised relays like a man crossing a river on floating debris. *I'm in the door controls, the dock manifest, and briefly, tragically, the station's cafeteria menu. Two minutes forty. On my mark, move like your lives depend on it, because statistically they do.*

I put my hand on the vent cover. Sebastian put his over mine.

Not to move it. Just—over mine. Warm. Steady. And I thought: *this is the moment.* In the erased timeline, this moment happened differently. He hesitated. He thought about his brother in Halcyon's Garden, about the deal, about the arithmetic of one life against millions, and he chose the one, and the choosing broke him before the fold ever did.

This time, his hand didn't hesitate.

"Ilse," he said quietly. "Whatever happens after this—I need you to know I'm not making any deals. Not with Okafor, not with Reyes, not with God. Whatever the price is, we find out together or not at all."

"You don't know what the price is yet."

"Neither do you. You remember prices. That's not the same as knowing."

I wanted to argue. I had arguments stockpiled like munitions—every erased future, every corpse of a timeline, all of it lined up behind my teeth. But his hand was on mine, and the pressure test klaxon began its low rising note, and somewhere below us the bulkheads sighed and unlocked, and there are moments when argument is just fear wearing a clever mask.

*Mark,* said Wren.

We dropped.

* * *

**Sebastian**

Ninety seconds sounds like nothing until you spend one running for your life down a depressurized corridor with a woman whose memory is older than the universe's patience.

The air was thin—test conditions, oxygen bled down to near-vacuum in sections—and the suit packs were in the emergency alcove exactly where Wren said they'd be, because Wren had read the station's entire safety manual out of spite sometime around hour two of captivity. We grabbed masks, not suits. No time. Half-second seal over nose and mouth, and then we ran.

Running in low pressure is a strange, silent business. Your boots hit the deck and the sound dies instantly, swallowed by the vacuum-hungry air, so you're sprinting inside a bubble of quiet, just your own heartbeat and the roar of your own breath through the mask. Ahead of me Ilse flew—long strides, perfect economy, navigating by some internal map I suspected she'd drawn from a future that no longer existed.

*Forty seconds,* Wren counted. *Also, small problem. The customs drone at the airlock isn't on the evacuation list. Someone flagged it to stay. That someone is Commander Reyes, who is currently smarter than I'd like.*

"Options?" I gasped.

*I overload its optics with the dock floodlights and you walk past it blind. It'll hear your footsteps, but in vacuum-adjacent air, sound propagation is garbage. Stay close to the walls, move slow, and for the love of whatever you pray to, don't sneeze.*

The airlock loomed. Beyond its glass, our freighter sat in the docking cradle like a dog pretending it hadn't chewed the furniture. The drone hung in the middle of the lock chamber, a black teardrop with a sensor array like a spider's face.

The floodlights hit.

Every luminaire in the chamber slammed to full output at once—Wren pulling power from who knows where—and the drone's optics whited out, its array shuttering, and we pressed ourselves along the wall and moved past it at a pace that was technically walking and spiritually screaming.

Ilse reached the airlock control first. Her hands moved over the panel, and I saw her pause—one heartbeat, two—staring at the override sequence like she was reading it off the inside of her eyelids.

"You know this code," I said.

"I knew this code." She entered it anyway. "From a version of tonight where I escaped alone. Where I left you in that cell because taking you slowed me down, and I lived, and the fold got built anyway, and I spent the rest of a very long life regretting a door I opened by myself." The lock clunked green. She looked back at me, mask fogging with each breath. "So this time I'm opening it with you. Call it superstition."

"Call it the smartest thing anyone's ever done on my behalf."

*Fifteen seconds to bulkhead reseal,* Wren said. *Move. Move. Move.*

We came through the airlock and the inner door cycled shut behind us and the freighter's ramp accepted our codes like it had missed us, and then we were inside, in atmosphere, in the sudden deafening ordinary noise of a ship breathing, and I sealed the outer hatch and spun the lockdown and leaned against the bulkhead and laughed—laughed like a madman, high and cracked, because we were alive and it was absurd and the coffee in that cell had actually been excellent.

Ilse pulled her mask down. Her hair was plastered to her forehead and there was a smear of duct grease across her jaw and she was, without competition, the most beautiful thing I had ever seen in thirty-one years of looking at things.

*Course laid in,* Wren murmured, softer now, almost shy. *Kestrel's Grave, secondary relay, fourteen hours if we push. Also, concord patrol cutters are launching. So, you know. Twelve hours if we push harder.*

"Get us out," I said.

The deck pressed up under my feet as the freighter clawed free of the cradle, and Concord Station Kestrel fell away behind us like a bad memory with good coffee.

* * *

**Ilse**

The corridor was ours alone after that. The ship hummed around us, Wren flying some impossible evasion pattern through the patrol net, and Sebastian and I stood in the passageway outside the cockpit, still in our masks hanging loose around our necks, still shaking in the delayed way that adrenaline collects its debts.

"We should strap in," he said, and didn't move.

"We should."

Neither of us moved.

The acceleration built gently, pressing us toward each other the way physics does when it wants credit for human decisions. I could see his pulse in his throat. I could see the exact moment he stopped deciding and simply arrived—he closed the distance and kissed me, and it was clumsy and desperate and tasted like recycled air and fear, and I kissed him back with everything the erased timelines had taught me about wasting time.

When we broke apart, we were both breathing hard, and the masks dangled forgotten, and somewhere in the static Wren said, with elaborate delicacy:

*I'm going to pretend to be very focused on these patrol cutters right now.*

"Thank you," Sebastian said, not taking his eyes off me.

*Don't mention it. Literally. Ever.*

But I wasn't laughing. Because his lips were warm and real and singular, and I knew—I *knew*, with the terrible weight of a witness—that there was a version of this moment in a dead timeline where he never kissed me at all, where he chose the Garden and the guarantee and his brother's name on the waking list, and the fold had eaten itself, and everyone died including the brother, especially the brother, because deals with institutions are just bets placed with other people's chips.

"Sebastian." I took his face in my hands so he couldn't look away. "Okafor will offer you your brother again. She'll make it personal. She'll make it *exact*. And when she does, I need you to remember this corridor. Remember that you chose this. Because I have watched you choose otherwise, and I am the only person alive who remembers what it cost."

His jaw worked under my hands. Behind his eyes I could see the war already starting—the arithmetic assembling itself, one beloved life against the principle, love against the ledger.

"I remember," he said finally. "I promise I remember."

It was the truth. I believed him.

And that was precisely what frightened me, because I had believed him before, in a timeline that no longer existed, and belief had changed nothing.

The ship shuddered through a hard burn. Somewhere ahead, at the bottom of a debris field called Kestrel's Grave, the fold's scar waited with its pooled time and its promises, and behind us Concord's cutters regrouped, and ahead of us Halcyon would be waiting with a devil's bargain wrapped in a mother's mercy.

*Hold course,* Wren said quietly, to no one, to everyone. *Borrowed time is still time. Let's spend it well.*
