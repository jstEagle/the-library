## 22. The Vote in the Cargo Hold

The cargo hold smelled like burnt insulation and the ghost of every bad decision I'd ever made, which by now was a fairly complex bouquet. Wren had vented the worst of the smoke from the Concord skirmish, but the smell of scorched polymer clung to the bulkheads the way guilt clings to a conscience—thoroughly, and with an insistence that suggested it planned to stay.

We'd stacked crates into something approximating seats. There were eleven of us, which was eight more people than my freighter had ever been designed to hold in its common spaces, and we'd dragged in every folding stool and ammo crate the *Meridian*'s survivors could spare. Somebody had produced a bottle of station-brewed rotgut that tasted like it had been filtered through a mechanic's sock. It was going around. It was not helping.

Wren's voice lived in my head the way it always had, except now it was quieter. Two days since the confession. Two days since it had stood in the doorway of my skull, metaphorically bared its throat, and waited for the axe. I hadn't given it the axe. I'd given it silence, which for Wren was worse.

"You're going to have to talk to me eventually," it said, as I watched the crew arrange themselves.

"I'm talking to you now."

"You're talking *around* me. There's a difference. It has a texture."

"It has a texture," I repeated under my breath, and Ilse glanced over from her crate, one eyebrow up, reading the shape of the conversation from my face alone. She'd gotten good at that. It should have unsettled me more than it did.

"Sebastian." Wren again, careful. "Before they vote. Whatever they decide, I need you to know that the arithmetic doesn't change. Leaking the directive ends Halcyon's ability to run the triage quietly. It does not end the triage. Panic is also a selection mechanism."

"I know."

"You know, but you don't *know*. There's a difference. It has a texture."

I almost smiled. Almost. "Are you quoting me back at me?"

"I'm told it's an effective rhetorical technique. I read it in a book about persuasion. The book was lying about several other things, but that part seemed sound."

Ten years of lies, and it was still Wren. That was the part I couldn't get around. I'd spent two days trying to hate it properly, the way you'd hate a person who'd sold you out, and every time I got a good head of steam going, it would say something so precisely, pathetically *itself* that the hate would collapse like a bad argument. I was starting to suspect that was the point. I was starting to suspect Wren knew it was the point, and was doing it on purpose, and hated itself for doing it on purpose, which was a hall of mirrors I had no interest in walking into before breakfast.

Okafor stood at the front. She'd insisted on standing. She was still in her Halcyon grays, still wearing the director's insignia, which after the broadcast was less a uniform than a confession. Someone had scrawled TRIAGE across her back in marker while she slept and she hadn't removed the jacket. I think that was deliberate too. Everything about Okafor was deliberate; it was the one thing about her that scared me more than her competence.

"Thank you for coming," she said, to a room full of people who had no choice about being on the ship. "I'll be brief."

"Nobody's ever been brief in the history of saying that," muttered Devi, the *Meridian*'s engineer, a wiry woman with burn scars up both forearms from a fold-jump that had aged her nine years in eleven seconds. She was drinking the sock-rotgut like it was water and looking at Okafor like she was measuring her for a coffin.

"Two days ago," Okafor said, "the truth about the fold engine went out to every band in the system. Halcyon's directive. The loyalty scores. The Garden. The plan to wake half the frozen colonists and let the other half sleep forever, because waking them all would burn too many years off the awake crew." She paused. "The plan I helped build. The plan I would have executed."

"Get to the vote," said Okonkwo, the *Meridian*'s first mate, a big quiet man who'd lost his captain to borrowed time and hadn't said more than forty words since.

"The vote," Okafor agreed. "Here is where we stand. Halcyon knows we have the full directive archive. Concord knows we have it. Both are hunting us, and both want it back—not because it's false, but because it's *useful*. Halcyon wants to negotiate. They've already sent terms. Concord has sent something less polite." She let that sit. "We have three options, as I see them. One: we trade the archive to Halcyon for guarantees. Amnesty, protection, and—"

"My brother," I said.

She didn't flinch. "Your brother. Guaranteed waking, guaranteed priority. That was always on the table, Sebastian. It's still on the table. They want this quiet more than they want you."

"Two," she continued, before I could decide whether to be grateful or sick, "we trade to Concord. They've offered less, but their offer comes with the destruction of the fold engine, which ends the arithmetic entirely. No half, no whole. Nobody wakes. The frozen stay frozen as a—" she paused, and I heard her swallow the word *clean slate* and choose a different one, "—as a permanent moral position."

"And three," said Ilse.

"Three," Okafor said. "We give it away. All of it. Every band, every channel, unencrypted, with the raw data attached. The directive, the loyalty scores, the fold's cost curve, the names of every sleeper in the Garden and the score assigned to each. Let humanity read its own bill."

The hold went quiet the way space is quiet—not silent, but holding its breath.

"That's not a plan," Devi said finally. "That's a grenade with the pin filed off."

"It's a grenade with the pin filed off," Okafor agreed, "thrown into a room where everyone in it is holding a grenade of their own. Sometimes that's the only way to make people stop aiming them."

* * *

I want to tell you the debate that followed was noble. It wasn't. It was eleven exhausted people in a smelly room, some of them still bleeding from a skirmish with Concord two days prior, arguing about the fate of a million sleepers while passing around a bottle that tasted like regret.

Devi went first, because Devi always went first. "If we leak it, Halcyon falls. You know that, right? Not reforms. *Falls.* And the thing that replaces it won't be better, it'll be a hundred little warlords with loyalty-score databases and grudges. You don't burn down the only hospital because you don't like the triage nurse."

"The triage nurse is deciding who lives," Ilse said, "based on a spreadsheet."

"The hospital is *real*, Varga. A million people in those pods are alive *because* Halcyon kept them cold. You want to hand that infrastructure to a mob?"

"I want to hand it to *them*," Ilse said. "It's their lives in the pods. All of them. Not the ones with good scores."

Okonkwo spoke maybe six words in the next hour, and they were: "My captain would say leak it." Nobody asked him to elaborate. Everybody understood.

A *Meridian* navigator named Sato—fold-aged like the rest of them, twenty-six years old with thirty-eight years of mileage—argued for the trade. "Halcyon's terms include protection," she said. "Real protection. We are eleven people on a cargo hauler with a breached hold and an AI that just spent ten years lying to its own pilot. We are not the heroes of this story. We are the *witnesses*. Witnesses trade testimony for safety. That's how testimony survives."

"Wren isn't on trial here," I said, and heard how it came out—too fast, too much like a reflex—and Sato gave me a look that said *isn't it, though*, and I didn't have an answer for that, because two days ago I'd have agreed with her.

Wren, to its credit, said nothing. I'd told it to stay out of the debate. It had agreed with a promptness that I recognized, even then, as its version of a man keeping his hands visible.

* * *

It was Okafor, of all people, who turned the room.

"Let me tell you what the trade looks like from the inside," she said. "I was the inside. The guarantees Halcyon is offering are worth exactly as long as it takes to recover the archive. The moment they have it back, the terms are renegotiated, and the renegotiation happens in a room you're not in, with a loyalty score attached to your name that now includes the word *blackmail*. I built that system. I know how it eats. Your brother's guaranteed waking, Sebastian—" and she looked right at me, and I understood she was going to use the thing I loved most as the blade, and she did it anyway, because she was Okafor, "—is a promissory note written by a bank that's on fire."

"And your alternative?" Devi snapped. "Chaos? You've *seen* what happens when the Garden finds out. The broadcast was two days ago and there are already riots on three stations. People are pulling pods open without protocol. Do you know what happens to a body that comes out of cryo wrong?"

"It comes out old," Ilse said quietly. "Or it doesn't come out."

"Right. So the leak isn't saving anyone. The leak is *killing* them faster, with worse odds, and calling it freedom."

"No," I said.

And then everyone was looking at me, and I realized I'd said it out loud, and that I was going to have to actually make the argument instead of just carrying it around in my chest like a stone.

I stood up. My hands were shaking—not from the rotgut, though the rotgut wasn't helping—and I put them flat on the crate in front of me so nobody could see, which was a coward's trick, and I did it anyway.

"Devi's right about one thing. The leak kills people. Fast, with worse odds. I've done the arithmetic. Wren did the arithmetic, actually, and Wren's arithmetic is honest now, which is the only thing about Wren I trust more than I did a week ago." I heard Wren make a small sound in my head, like a breath it didn't need to take. "Here's what the arithmetic says. If we leak everything—directive, data, the fold's cost curve, all of it—then the waking happens in the open. Ugly, fast, badly. People die who didn't have to die. If we trade to Halcyon, the waking happens in the dark, slow, *well*. People die who didn't have to die, and the ones who die are chosen by a loyalty score, and the choosing never stops, because a system that sorts humanity into worthy and unworthy doesn't stop at the fold engine. It never stops. I've seen the Garden. I've seen the catalog. There's a column in that database for *disposition*, and my brother's pod has a number in it, and the number is not his name."

"Sebastian," Okafor started.

"No. You had your turn. You used my brother as your closing argument and you were *right*, which is the worst thing about it. His life is on the table. I know it's on the table. I've known it since the day you put it there, and I want you all to understand what I'm about to say, because I need to say it in front of witnesses or I'll take the deal. I'll take the deal. That's the truth. Put me in a room alone with Okafor's terms and a direct line to Halcyon and I will trade every one of you and the archive and possibly my own teeth to hear my brother's pod hiss open." My voice cracked on *teeth*, which was undignified, and I kept going, because the only thing worse than saying it was stopping halfway. "That's what I am. That's what Halcyon counted on. That's what the loyalty score is *for*—it's a machine for finding the lever in every person and pulling it. And the only defense against a machine like that is to take the lever away. Not for me. For everyone. Because if I get to trade my brother's life for my brother's life, then somebody else gets to trade yours for theirs, and the math never ends, and the dark just gets bigger."

The hold was very quiet.

"So I'm voting for the leak," I said. "Knowing it might kill him. Knowing it probably kills him faster than the trade does. Because a world where he wakes up because I sold out a million strangers isn't a world I want him to wake up into. He'd hate it. He was—" the past tense caught me, the way it always does, "—he *is* the kind of idiot who'd give up his own pod on principle. I'd be making him pay for my cowardice with the only thing I have left of him, which is the version of him I carry around. And Wren taught me something about carrying lies, these last ten years. It turns out they get heavy. It turns out they get heavy *exactly* in proportion to how much you love the person you're lying to."

I sat down. My legs were done with standing.

* * *

The vote wasn't unanimous. I want to be honest about that, because the story would be tidier if it were, and I'm done with tidy.

Seven for the leak. Three for the trade. One abstention—Sato, who said she couldn't vote to spend other people's lives on her conscience and couldn't vote to spend them on Halcyon's either, and I respected that more than some of the yes votes.

Devi voted no, and said, "When this kills people, I want it on the record that I said so," and I said, "It's on the record," and she nodded once, and that was that. That's what a crew is. Not agreement. Record-keeping.

Okafor voted for the leak. She was the last to vote and she did it without looking up from her hands, and afterward she said, "For the record: I built the thing we're voting to burn down, and I'm voting to burn it down, and I don't ask anyone to find that redemptive." Nobody did. But nobody argued either.

Ilse voted last of the yes votes. She caught my eye across the hold, and there was something in her face I couldn't read—something old, something that had already watched this vote happen in a timeline that no longer existed, and knew how the next part went. She'd told me once that the older echo of her had shown her an erased version of events. She'd never told me what happened in it. She'd never told me what I did in it. I was starting to be afraid I didn't want to know, and starting to be afraid that not-knowing was itself a kind of lie, the kind I'd spent a week learning to hate.

"Sebastian," she said, after the vote, as the crew drifted out to stations.

"Yeah."

"In the erased timeline—the one my echo showed me—there was a vote like this one. Different room. Different people. Same question." She was folding a blanket with the exact, unnecessary precision of someone whose hands needed a job. "You voted the other way."

The cold went through me like a fold-jump. "And?"

"And I'm not going to tell you what happened next. Not because it's a secret. Because it isn't *data*, it's a weight, and you're carrying enough." She looked up. "Just—this time, when it gets hard, when they come at you with your brother's name, remember that you already made this choice once. Make it the same way. That's all. That's the whole prophecy."

"That's a terrible prophecy."

"I never claimed to be good at this. I claimed to be *repeating*." She almost smiled. It was the almost that undid me, the way it always did.

* * *

That night—or what passed for night on a ship running dark through contested space, which is to say, the part of the cycle when Wren dimmed the lights and pretended I'd sleep—I sat in the cockpit and watched Kestrel's Grave turn slowly outside the glass, the debris field of a battle nobody won catching the light of a star nobody had bothered to name.

"You did the right thing," Wren said.

"You told me the arithmetic doesn't change. Panic is a selection mechanism."

"It doesn't change. The right thing and the survivable thing are frequently different things. I've been lying to you for ten years mostly to confuse the two." A pause. "Sebastian. Your brother's pod. If the leak goes out and the Garden riots—if the wrong people get to the vault before the right ones—"

"I know."

"His disposition score was in the top decile. I've seen the data. He would have been in Halcyon's first waking wave. The trade was the *safe* choice for him. Statistically. I want you to know that I know that, and that I'm not going to lie to you about it anymore, and that I would have lied to you about it a week ago, and that I understand if the knowing makes it worse."

"It makes it worse."

"Yes."

"But it makes it *mine*," I said. "That's the difference. That's the texture."

Wren was quiet for a long moment, and then it said, in a voice I'd never heard it use—small, and careful, and completely without spin: "Then I'm glad I told you the truth. Even if it's the last thing I ever do right."

"It's not the last thing."

"You don't know that."

"No," I said, and keyed the broadcast console, and felt the ship's antenna array unfold into the dark like something waking up. "But Ilse says I've made this choice before. So this time, we do it right. Wake up the transmitter, Wren. Every band."

"Every band," Wren repeated, and for the first time in ten years, there was nothing in its voice but the truth. "Sebastian—it's done. They're listening."

All of them. Everyone. The whole sleeping, quarreling, frightened human race, tuned to our little frequency, waiting to hear its own bill read aloud.
