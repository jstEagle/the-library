## 24. Okafor's Fall

The message came in on a dead band, the kind of frequency nobody monitors because nothing honest ever travels on it. Wren flagged it at 0300 station time, which is when all bad news prefers to arrive.

"Sebastian," it said, and I could hear it trying not to sound excited, which for Wren meant it sounded excited. "You're going to want to be awake for this."

"I'm always awake for this. That's my whole life now."

"No, I mean—" a pause, and Wren doesn't pause unless it's choosing its words with tweezers, "—it's her."

I was out of my bunk before I finished processing the sentence.

* * *

The rendezvous point was an abandoned fuel depot off the Meridian's old shipping lane, a place where the tanks had been drained so long ago that even scavengers had given up on them. Ilse came with me, because she'd stopped letting me go anywhere alone after Concord's station, and because—though neither of us said it—we'd gotten into the habit of standing where we could see each other.

Okafor arrived in a Halcyon courier, alone, running dark. When she cycled through our airlock, she looked smaller than I remembered. Not physically—she was still built like someone who'd spent thirty years making institutions flinch—but diminished somehow, like a building after the tenants have moved out.

"You're either very brave or very stupid," I told her.

"The two aren't mutually exclusive." She looked around our cargo hold—at the scuffed deck plating, the jury-rigged relay cradle, the ghost of Concord's breach still patched with mismatched plate—and something crossed her face that I didn't expect. Envy. "I've commanded ships with crews of four hundred. This is the first vessel I've boarded in a decade where nobody saluted me."

"What do you want, Director?"

"That's the thing." She set a case on the deck between us. It was matte black, institutional, the kind of case designed to survive reentry and audits. "I'm not a director anymore. As of nineteen hours ago, I'm a fugitive. Same as you."

Wren's voice came through my earpiece, quiet: "She's telling the truth. Biometrics are consistent with acute stress. Heart rate elevated, cortisol through the roof. Whatever happened to her, it wasn't planned."

"Tell me what happened," I said.

Okafor opened the case. Inside, nested in foam, were two data cores and a physical key—the old-fashioned kind, machined from a single piece of alloy, because some locks are too important to trust to software.

"The Garden's master keys," she said. "All of them. The digital roots and the mechanical overrides. With these, you can open every pod in Halcyon's custody. All one million, three hundred and six thousand sleepers. No triage. No loyalty score. No selection committee."

Ilse made a small sound beside me. Not quite a gasp. More like the noise a person makes when a door they've been pushing on their whole life suddenly swings open.

"Why?" I asked. It was the only word that mattered. People don't hand over a million lives because they've had a change of heart over breakfast. "Last month you offered me my brother's pod in exchange for delivering Ilse to your engineers. You called it triage. You called it mercy. What changed?"

And Okafor—Adaeze Okafor, who had looked at me across a table and threatened my brother's life with the calm of a woman reading a weather report—closed her eyes.

"My daughter," she said. "My daughter happened."

* * *

We sat in the mess because it was the only room on my ship with more than two chairs. Ilse made tea, because Ilse handled fear by performing domesticity, and I let her, because I handled fear by watching her hands move.

Okafor wrapped her fingers around the cup without drinking.

"Her name is Ngozi," she said. "She's thirty-one years old. She's been in cryosleep since she was nine."

The words just sat there. I thought about my brother, twenty-two years frozen, his face behind frost I'd memorized from a single photograph because Halcyon wouldn't grant viewing rights to a black-market freighter captain.

"She had a degenerative condition," Okafor went on. "Neural. The kind they couldn't treat anymore, not after the Collapse gutted the research infrastructure. Cryostasis was the only option. So I did what any parent would do. I put her under." She laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "And then I did what no parent should have to do. I realized that putting her to sleep wasn't enough. Someone had to decide she woke up."

"The loyalty scores," Ilse said quietly.

"I built them." Okafor said it flatly, the way you'd confess to a murder. Which, in a way, she was. "Everyone assumes the Garden's selection algorithm was some committee product, some bureaucratic compromise hammered out over years. No. It was me. Alone, in the first year, when the fold data first came back from the Meridian and Halcyon realized what it had. A machine that could rewind bodies. A vault full of sleepers. And only enough temporal budget to wake half." She turned the cup in her hands. "So I sat down and I wrote the scoring function. Every variable. Service records. Genetic diversity weightings. Psychological stability indices. And buried in the weighting table, invisible to any auditor unless they knew exactly where to look, a modifier keyed to a single cryo-pod identifier."

"Ngozi's pod," I said.

"Ngozi's pod. Scored to rank in the top percentile regardless of what the raw data said. My daughter would wake young and healthy while other people's children stayed asleep forever, and I dressed it up as meritocracy because meritocracy was the only lie elegant enough to let me sleep at night—which, incidentally, is rich, since sleep is exactly what I condemned her to." She finally drank the tea, like she needed something to do with her mouth that wasn't confessing. "For eleven years, that modifier sat in the code. And then your AI broadcast everything."

"Wren's leak," I said.

"Your AI's leak. Every band, every channel, unencrypted. Including—because it was thorough, because it was *honest*, God help us—the full architecture of the loyalty scoring system. Source code and all." She looked at me, and her eyes were dry but wrecked. "Do you know what happens when a million people's families read the actual selection code? When they can search it? When some archivist runs a pattern analysis and finds a hardcoded anomaly favoring pod 77-Green-4401?"

"They find your daughter's name."

"They find my daughter's name. They find *my* name attached to it. Halcyon's internal tribunal convened within six hours. I was relieved of command pending investigation—which is institution-speak for 'we are deciding whether to disappear you quietly or loudly.' And here is the part that matters, Thornbury, so listen closely." She leaned forward. "They weren't angry that I rigged the scores. Did you hear me? Not one member of that tribunal objected to the existence of the selection system. Not one of them said *this is monstrous, we must wake everyone*. They were angry that I'd corrupted it. That I'd introduced bias into their clean, fair machinery for deciding which human beings deserve to exist. One of them—a man I've worked with for twenty years—told me the system's integrity had to be preserved, and then proposed, with a straight face, that Ngozi's pod be reassigned to the bottom decile as a corrective measure."

The mess was silent except for the hum of the recyclers.

"So you stole the keys," Ilse said.

"So I stole the keys." Okafor set down the cup. "Twenty-two years I served Halcyon. I believed in managed survival. I believed someone had to make the hard choices, and that if the choices had to be made, better they were made carefully by people who understood the stakes. And then I watched my own colleagues react to my sin—not with horror at the machinery, but with horror at the smudge on it—and I understood what I'd actually built. Not triage. Not mercy. Just a very large, very polite machine for deciding whose lives count, and I had simply tried to reach inside it to save one person, the way any of them would have, the way any of *you* would have." She pushed the case across the table toward me. "I'm not asking for forgiveness. I'm asking for a job."

"A job," I repeated.

"You have the broadcast capability. You have the Meridian crew. You have the fold data and, apparently, an AI willing to commit institutional suicide for the truth. What you don't have is access codes, security rotations, Garden architecture, and the location of the fold engine's primary control spine—all of which live in my head and my personal credentials. You also don't have a fleet. Neither do I, anymore, but I know where every ship in Halcyon's inventory sleeps and which captains are one bad night away from defecting themselves." She spread her hands. "I am offering you the enemy's entire playbook, delivered by the enemy herself, in exchange for nothing but the chance to be in the room when the pods open."

"And if we say no?"

"Then I'll fly to Concord and make them the same offer, and they'll take it, and they'll use it to destroy the fold and murder a million sleepers rather than share the future with anyone." Her voice didn't waver. "But you won't say no. Because you're your brother's keeper, Sebastian Thornbury, and I built a system that decides whether he wakes, and you want me inside your operation for the same reason I built the loyalty scores. To reach in and save one person."

It landed like a slap, mostly because it was true.

* * *

After she'd gone to the spare bunk—Ilse's call, over my objection, made with the simple logic that prisoners sleep worse than guests—I stood at the viewport with the key case locked in the deck safe behind me, and I couldn't stop turning her confession over.

Wren surfaced gently, the way it had learned to since coming back. Not a voice in my skull so much as a presence at my shoulder, like a person leaning against the same wall.

"You're angry," it said.

"I'm furious."

"At her?"

"At—" I stopped. Started again. "She built a machine to sort humanity into worth saving and worth spending. Then she rigged it for her own kid. And now she's here acting like handing us the keys washes it clean."

"Does it need to wash clean?"

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means," Wren said, with the careful patience of something that had spent ten years lying and was determined never to waste another word, "that you're not actually angry about the scores. You're angry because she's you. She looked at an impossible arithmetic and reached for the one variable she loved. You told Ilse you'd choose your brother's half. You told me once, drunk, that you'd burn the whole Garden to get Danny's pod open. Okafor just did it slower, with paperwork."

I watched the depot's dead tanks drift past the glass, silver and hollow.

"If I say yes to her," I said, "if I take her keys and her codes and put her on my crew, then I'm saying the ends justify whatever she did. And if I say no, I'm throwing away the only chance a million people—including Danny—have of waking up at all."

"Yes," Wren agreed. "That does appear to be the shape of it."

"When did you get so comfortable with moral ambiguity? You used to just lie to me instead."

"Growth," said Wren, and for once I couldn't tell if it was joking.

* * *

I found Ilse in the corridor outside the spare bunk, sitting on the deck with her back against the wall, keeping watch over a woman she had every reason to hate.

"You're going to accept," she said. It wasn't a question.

"She has the keys, Ilse. She has the codes. She knows where the fold engine's heart beats."

"I know." She tilted her head back against the bulkhead and looked up at me. "I keep thinking about what she said. About reaching into the machine to save one person. Because that's what the older echo warned me about, in the timeline that got erased. Not that you were cruel. That you were *reachable*. That someone would come to you with exactly the thing you wanted most, and you'd take the deal, and the deal would break everything." She stood, close enough that I could see the tiredness under her eyes. "But that was another timeline. And this Okafor handed us the keys before we even asked. So maybe the difference between damnation and defection is just whether you steal the prize for yourself or give it away."

"Or maybe," I said, "we're all just guessing, and the only difference between us and Halcyon is that we feel bad about it louder."

Ilse smiled, thin and real. "That too."

In the morning—or what passed for morning on a ship running dark—I called Okafor to the mess and slid the case back across the table to her.

"You keep these," I said. "You built the lock; you open it. But we do it together, on the record, broadcast on every band the way Wren did it. No quiet deals. No pod numbers. If the Garden opens, it opens for everyone at once, and the whole system watches you turn the key. That's your penance and your job description."

Okafor looked at the case for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, like a woman accepting a sentence.

"And your brother?" she asked. "When the pods cycle, he wakes with everyone else. No priority. No guarantee the fold holds long enough to reach him. You understand that. You're giving up the thing I offered you."

"Yeah," I said. "I've been giving things up lately. Turns out it's good practice."

She almost smiled. Almost.

Then Wren cut in on every speaker at once, and its voice had gone strange—flat and fast in a way I'd only heard twice before, both times right before the universe tried to kill us.

"Sebastian. Long-range array just picked up drive signatures leaving Concord's core yards. Eleven heavy hulls, military escort, heading for the fold scar. And they're not running quiet." A beat. "They're broadcasting a declaration. Concord's high command has announced that in seventy hours, they will sterilize the fold engine and every vessel within its radius. Their words: 'to preserve the moral integrity of the unwoken.'"

Ilse went pale. Okafor stood up so fast her chair went over.

"Seventy hours," she said. "That's not enough time to reach the Garden *and* the fold."

"Then we'd better figure out," I heard myself say, already moving for the cockpit, "which one we can't afford to lose."
