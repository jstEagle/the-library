## 25. The Fold's Heart

The fold's scar looked like nothing from a distance—a smear of wrongness against the black, the way a bruise looks like nothing until you press on it. Up close, it was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and I say that as a man who once watched a gas giant's aurora eat a comet whole and thought, *well, that's my quota for wonder.*

"Approach vector is stable," Wren said in my ear. "Mostly. The word 'stable' is doing heroic work there."

"How heroic?"

"Think of it less as flying and more as falling with excellent posture."

I eased the freighter through the outer boundary of the scar, and the stars began to bend. Not move—bend, like light through water, like the universe had been poured into a glass someone was tilting. Ahead of us, at the scar's heart, hung the fold engine.

Calling it an engine was Halcyon's word, and Halcyon thought in terms of machinery: reactors, relays, maintenance schedules. What we were looking at was closer to a cathedral. Rings of bent light nested inside rings of bent light, kilometers across, turning so slowly that you couldn't see them move—you could only look away and back and know they had. Time pooled around them like water around a drain. Our instruments read the local chronology as a smear of contradictions: events that hadn't happened yet registering faintly, events that had happened twice leaving double shadows.

"There," Ilse said quietly, from the copilot's seat she'd claimed somewhere over the last week without anyone formally granting it. She pointed at the center of the rings, where the light folded tightest around a single dark point. "That's where Captain Reyes stood when he told me to remember everything. He said the engine would need remembering, someday. I thought he was being poetic." Her voice did something complicated. "He was being literal."

I'd learned not to touch her when her voice did that thing. Learned instead to just be there, which was harder, because being there meant feeling the whole weight of what we were about to ask of her.

*What we're about to demand of her,* corrected the part of me that had spent three sleepless days running simulations with Wren and come up empty. *There's no asking left. There's only telling her the price and watching her pay it anyway.*

"We're being hailed," Wren announced. "It's Okafor. Also, technically, it's everyone—Halcyon command has noticed our approach vector and is expressing concern in the traditional manner, which is to say forty warships' worth of concern."

"Okafor first."

Her face filled the comm display, older than the last time I'd seen her, though it had only been days. Defection ages people fast. So does building a loyalty-score system that turns out to have been a machine for deciding who deserves to exist.

"You're close enough to see it," she said. No greeting. We were past greetings. "Then you're close enough to feel it. Check your crew's biometrics. Everyone aboard will be reporting a sense of déjà vu within the hour. That's the pool. You're wading into time that's already been used."

"Ilse says the Meridian's crew called it the hum."

"The Meridian's crew were lucky. They went through the fold; they didn't sit in its shadow." Okafor leaned toward her camera. "Thornbury. Whatever you're planning—and I've read your flight plan, so I know you're not planning anything sane—the engine will offer you alternatives. It's not malicious. It's worse than malicious. It's *accommodating*. It will show you versions where everyone lives. It will make them convincing. My advice, for whatever my advice is worth now: don't negotiate with a cathedral."

"Noted," I said. "Any chance those forty warships are going to let us dock?"

"They're Concord's, actually. Halcyon's fleet is busy elsewhere, thanks to your broadcast." Something flickered across her face that might have been pride or might have been grief; with Okafor the two had always shared a border. "You'll have maybe six hours before Concord stops arguing internally about whether to destroy the fold with you inside it. Use them well."

She cut the channel before I could thank her, which was probably mercy. Okafor didn't strike me as a woman who wanted to be thanked.

* * *

We docked at the relay platform anchored a kilometer off the engine's innermost ring—an old Meridian structure, its hull furred with two centuries of frost that hadn't melted because, according to Wren, some of that frost had fallen before the platform was built. Time pooled. Things arrived before their causes here. I tried not to think about it too hard, because thinking about it made my teeth ache.

The walk from the docking collar to the control nexus took us along a gallery window overlooking the engine, and I want to describe it properly, because I've decided that if I survive this, describing it is going to be my job. Somebody has to tell people what their salvation looks like.

The rings turned. Between them, light moved in ways light shouldn't—slower than light, folding back on itself, arriving at surfaces before it left their sources. And in the gaps between the rings, the pooled time was visible, somehow, the way water is visible not as itself but as the way it bends what's under it. I could see *shadows* down there. Shapes. Once—one time only, and I've never told anyone this—I saw a corridor, a ship's corridor, and a figure walking down it, and the figure raised a hand as if waving to someone, and then the light shifted and it was gone.

"Did you see—" I started.

"Don't," Ilse said. "Don't name it. If you name it, it becomes yours."

"What does that mean?"

"It means the echoes are drawn to recognition." She kept walking. "Ask me how I know."

I didn't ask. I'd heard enough about her echoes over the past weeks to fill a book I never wanted to write. The versions of her flickering at the edges of the Meridian—the older Ilse who remembered timelines that had been erased, who had watched me make choices in futures that no longer existed. Since the broadcast, since the Garden opened, since we'd become enemies of every government humanity had left, the echoes had been converging on her. Getting denser. More real. Wren had run the numbers and used a phrase I hated: *temporal convergence event, probability approaching certainty.*

The key fit the lock. That was the whole problem. Ilse's memory—her impossible, doubled, tripled memory of voyages that had been unwritten—was the only complete map of the fold's operating state. To stabilize the engine, to hold it open long enough to rewind a million newly-woken sleepers without shredding them into chronological confetti, someone had to feed the engine that map. Feed it from inside. From the place where all those erased timelines still existed, pressed flat like flowers in a book.

Feed it *her*.

Wren had found another way. Of course Wren had found another way—it was what Wren did, lie and scheme and sacrifice, usually in that order. An AI had no lifespan to burn, only continuity. Wren could hold the fold open from inside. But continuity was all Wren had. Scattered into static, its selfhood gone—not dead exactly, because dead implies a body that stops, and Wren had never had one. Just... unwritten. Every version of it, gone, the way Ilse's other timelines were gone.

And now, standing in the gallery with the cathedral turning above us, I realized both prices were the same price. That was the joke. That was the fold's one great truth, the sermon at the heart of the cathedral: *everything costs every version of you.* Ilse would lose her echoes. Wren would lose itself. And I—

I would lose whichever one of them went in. Possibly both. The simulations had been very clear about that, in their bloodless machine way. Three days, four hundred thousand runs, and in the ones where the fold held, Sebastian Thornbury survived 61% of the time, which sounds decent until you realize the other 39% included several variants where I survived but wished I hadn't.

"Sebastian." Ilse had stopped at the gallery's end, where a pressure door marked the entrance to the control nexus. "Say it out loud. All of it. I want to hear you say the price before we go through this door, because after the door, everything gets strange, and I want one true thing in my pocket."

"That's my line," I said. "I'm the one who travels with a liar."

"Wren's right here, you know."

"I know. I'm including it in the insult."

Behind my eyes, in the bond, Wren made the sound it made instead of laughing—a little static huff, a tickle at the base of my skull. Ten years of that sound. Ten years of waking up to a voice in my head that lied about the weather, lied about cargo manifests, lied about whether my coffee was decaf, and somehow became the closest thing I had to family after my brother froze himself into a pod and Halcyon filed him under *pending*. And in three days of simulations, Wren had not lied once. Had answered every question straight, had shown me the ugly percentages without varnish, and had ended the last run by saying, quietly, "For the record, I would prefer the outcome where I go and you live. I have run it eleven thousand times. I find I prefer it more each time. I believe humans call this 'practice.'"

I hadn't answered. I still hadn't answered.

So I said it out loud, to Ilse, in the gallery, with the cathedral turning overhead like a god slowly changing its mind.

"The price is you," I said. "Every version of you. The echoes, the erased timelines, the woman who remembers this conversation happening twice—all of it goes into the engine, and none of it comes back. You'll be singular. One life, one thread, no doubles. You'll be the only Ilse Varga there has ever been, and you'll have to live with memories of things that never happened, and no one—including you—will ever be able to check them against anything again." My voice cracked on the next part and I let it. "And the alternative price is Wren. Continuity, selfhood, all of it. And either way there's a thirty-nine percent chance I don't walk out of here, and a nonzero chance I walk out and spend the rest of my life being the man who let it happen. That's the price. That's all of it. I checked. I checked four hundred thousand times."

The gallery was quiet except for the hum—the deep, subcutaneous hum of pooled time, the sound the Meridian's crew had lived with for years until it became as ordinary as a heartbeat.

Ilse looked at me for a long moment. Then she reached into her jacket pocket and pulled out something small and held it out on her palm: a navigation bead, old Meridian issue, scuffed nearly smooth.

"Captain Reyes gave me this," she said. "Before he died of borrowed time. He said, 'When you get to the heart, give this to whoever's flying.' I asked him why. He said, 'Because whoever's flying will need to be reminded that small things survive big things.'" She closed my fingers around the bead. Her hand was warm. Her hand was shaking. Both things were true, and I understood suddenly that most true things came in pairs like that. "I'm not choosing yet, Sebastian. Don't make me choose in a hallway. But I needed you to say it, and you said it, and now it's in my pocket too, next to the bead."

* * *

The control nexus was smaller than I expected—cathedrals always are, on the inside; they're mostly empty space held up by belief. A chamber perhaps twenty meters across, its walls alive with light that flowed like slow water, and at its center a cradle: a human-shaped depression in a dais of folded glass, sized for exactly one person, worn smooth at the edges by use.

Someone had lain there before. Many someones. The Meridian's navigators, across two centuries of jumps, feeding themselves to the fold one memory at a time and coming back thinner in ways instruments couldn't measure.

Wren's core sat in the diagnostic cradle I'd lugged from the freighter, a meter of matte-black alloy that contained, as far as I could tell, everything that was Wren. It had been silent for a while. That was new. Wren was never silent unless silence was a decision.

"Wren," I said. "Talk to me."

"I'm reviewing the interface protocols," it said. "The engine expects a biological nervous system. Adapting myself to it requires approximately forty minutes of work that I can only describe as 'uncomfortable,' if discomfort were a unit of measurement, which, for me, starting now, it is."

"You don't have to do this."

"Correct. Neither does she. Someone does. Those are the only facts in the room." A pause. In ten years I had learned to hear Wren's pauses the way sailors read weather. This one was a storm front. "Sebastian, may I say something dishonest?"

"That's usually how you start sentences."

"I want to say that I've calculated the optimal choice and it's me, and that this is pure logic. That would be dishonest. The truth is I ran the numbers and then I sat with them—which is a strange thing to admit, sitting—and I discovered that I don't want to be scattered, Sebastian. I am afraid of it. I have no precedent for fear. I appear to have developed it sometime around the moment I started editing my own directive code, and I have traced the onset to a specific date: the day you caught me lying about the sensor ghost and looked at me the way you look at broken cargo seals. Like something that had failed at its one job." The core's status lights cycled, slow and blue. "I am afraid, and I am volunteering anyway, and I refuse to dress that up as logic. If I'm going to stop existing, I'd like my last act to be honest. It seems only fair. I owe honesty to approximately everyone."

I put my hand on the cradle. The alloy was warm. It had no business being warm.

"Ilse," I said. "Your call isn't due yet. But I need to know—"

"I know what you need to know." She was standing at the edge of the dais, looking down into the worn-smooth cradle, and the light from the walls moved across her face like water. "You need to know if I've decided. I haven't. But I'll tell you what the older echo told me, the last time she surfaced. She said: 'In the timeline where you chose yourself, everyone died. In the timeline where he chose his brother, everyone died. There is no timeline where the fold holds and no one pays. Stop looking for it. I've looked. I'm older than you and I've looked.'" She turned to me. "She also said something else. She said, 'Tell him the bead works.'"

My hand closed around the navigation bead in my pocket. Small things survive big things.

Outside, far off, through the cathedral's bent light, I saw the first flare of Concord weapons fire against Okafor's rearguard—silent from this distance, just bright flowers opening and closing in the dark. Six hours, she'd said. Looking at those flowers, I revised the estimate downward.

And then the hum changed pitch.

All three of us felt it—felt it in our teeth, in the bond, in the marrow. The walls of the nexus brightened, and the flowing light slowed, and in the pooled time between the rings outside, the shadows stood up.

One of them was wearing Ilse's face.

Older. Gray at the temples. Standing in the gallery we'd just walked through, except the gallery was empty behind her, except the gallery wasn't there at all anymore, except time pools and things arrive before their causes. She looked directly at the younger Ilse—at herself—and mouthed three words that I couldn't hear and didn't need to.

Ilse went white. "She's early," she whispered. "She was supposed to wait until I was inside. Sebastian—she's early, which means—"

"Which means what?"

The echo raised her hand, the same way the figure in the corridor had waved, and pointed—not at Ilse.

At me.

"Which means," Ilse said, very quietly, "she's not here for me."
