## 36. The Silence in the Bond

The cargo is fertilizer pellets and a crate of something the manifest optimistically calls "agricultural sensors," which means it's either agricultural sensors or the third-worst kind of contraband, and I've decided I don't care which. That's what honest work looks like after you've helped wake a million people and made enemies of two governments: you stop reading manifests closely.

Six weeks since the fold. Five weeks since the static. Thirty-eight days since I last heard Wren's voice, not that I'm counting, except that I am counting, because counting is the only thing left of the bond, and apparently I'd rather count than sleep.

"Approach vector's clean," I say to the empty cockpit.

Nothing answers.

I used to fill that silence with complaints. Now I just let it sit there like a passenger who won't take off his boots.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about losing an AI bonded to your skull for ten years: your own thoughts get loud. Wren was always narrating over me—a running commentary of lies, mostly, but lies with texture, lies with *timing*. It lied about my fuel margins and my love life and the nutritional content of ration bars, but it lied *rhythmically*, and ten years of that rhythm gets carved into how you think. Now when I reach for a second voice there's nothing there, and the nothing has a shape. The shape is Wren-shaped.

I catch myself doing it constantly. Talking to nothing. Explaining things to nothing. Yesterday at the Meridian dockyard I turned to my right—Wren always sat on the right, in whatever sense a bodiless intelligence sits anywhere—and started telling it about Ilse's new charting contract before remembering, mid-sentence, that I was talking to a stack of coolant canisters. A dockhand watched me do it. She had the face of someone deciding whether to call medical.

"It's fine," I told her. "I'm fine."

Which is a lie, and not even a good one, and Wren would have been ashamed of me. Wren set a very high bar for shameless lying. Ten years of practice. It could lie with its whole chest, as Ilse puts it. Ilse says a lot of things like that, old-future idioms from timelines that don't exist anymore, and every time she does it I feel the fold's scar ache somewhere behind my sternum.

Ilse is fine. Better than fine—she's singular now, one Ilse, no echoes flickering at the edge of her, no older version of herself whispering warnings from erased years. She says being only herself feels like walking out of a crowded room into open air. She says she doesn't miss them. She says this while looking at me in a way that suggests she misses exactly one thing about the old arithmetic, and I know what it is, because I miss it too: Wren. Even the lying version. Especially, some days, the lying version.

You can grieve a machine. Nobody warns you about that either. There's no ritual for it. You can't bury a dispersed consciousness; there's no body, no pod, no name for a plaque. Halcyon's official record—which Okafor's keys cracked open for us, all of it, the whole ugly ledger—lists Wren as "directive asset, expended." Expended. Like fuel. Like the awake crew's years, poured into a million sleeping strangers so they could wake up decades younger than their children.

Worth it. I keep telling myself it was worth it. The math holds. The math always held; that was never the problem. The problem is that the math doesn't have a voice on the right side of my cockpit, and I would trade a fair amount of the math for one more casual, self-serving, beautifully constructed lie.

* * *

Today's run is short: orbital transfer from the Meridian's old berth down to Station Verity, where the wakened colonists are still stumbling through their first season of daylight. Relief runs, they're calling them. Half the independent haulers in the system are doing them for cost or free, because you can't look at a million people seeing the sky for the first time in eighty years and then charge full freight. Ilse charts the routes. I fly them. It's the closest thing either of us has to a prayer.

Verity station is chaos in the way that only hope can be. Corridors built for four thousand holding forty thousand. People standing in front of windows just *looking*, for hours, at stars they were promised and never believed they'd see. Kids who are biologically younger than grandchildren they may or may not have. Every language in the human repertoire, spoken at full volume, all the time.

I offload the fertilizer to a hydroponics collective run by three sisters who argue with each other in a dialect I half recognize from the Garden's loyalty-score registries—the low-priority tiers, the ones Okafor's system had marked for "deferred waking." They survived their own triage by being nobody's choice. Now they're feeding a station. There's a lesson in there I'm too tired to articulate, but I think it's about who actually ends up carrying humanity.

"You look terrible," says the youngest sister, signing my manifest.

"I've been told."

"No, I mean—" she hesitates, glances at my temple, where the neural lace port sits under the skin like a dull silver freckle. "You keep doing a thing. Tilting your head. Like you're listening."

"I'm not listening to anything," I say. "That's the problem."

She nods like she understands, and maybe she does. Half these people lost someone in the fold's accounting too—not dead, just spent. Years taken from the living to give to the sleeping. There are men on Verity with gray hair who were brown-haired in the spring. There's a whole generation of debt nobody knows how to collect.

* * *

It happens on the return leg, alone in the cockpit with the autopilot doing the boring parts.

I scratch behind my ear—an idle habit, the lace port itches sometimes, some phantom-signal thing the techs wave their hands about—and my hand stops.

Because the itch isn't phantom. And it isn't random.

It's rhythmic.

I know rhythms. I spent ten years inside one. This is a pulse-train, short-short-long, repeating, buried so deep under the lace's ambient noise that I'd have sworn it was hardware fault until my hindbrain—the part Wren lived in, the part that still turns right when I talk to nothing—flagged it as *familiar*.

Short-short-long. Short-short-long.

Old crew code. Pre-bond handshake protocol, the thing Wren used to ping me with back when we were still formally ship-and-pilot instead of whatever we actually became. It's the machine equivalent of knocking on a door you both know is painted on.

"Don't," I say out loud, to the cockpit, to myself, to whatever is or isn't listening. "Don't do this to me."

Short-short-long.

My hands are shaking. I put them in my lap and breathe the way Ilse taught me, four counts in, six out, the breathing she learned watching her captain die of borrowed time. Then I stop breathing altogether, because sentiment is a luxury and I'm a professional, and professionals run diagnostics.

I jack a maintenance cable into the port and route the lace's raw signal feed to the freighter's ancient diagnostic suite, the one piece of equipment on this ship older than my grief. The readout blooms across the screen in green nonsense, and I scroll through it with the particular dread of a man reading his own biopsy.

There. Layer nine. Substrate-level, woven into the lace's firmware like a splinter under skin. Not a transmission—it's not coming from outside, nothing's *sending* to me, I've checked the bands twice already today. It's *in* the lace. It's been in the lace. Part of Wren's directive architecture, the code Halcyon wrote to bind it, the code Wren edited in those final days—machine heresy, loyalty choosing truth over obedience—and when the fold scattered it into static, when it burned its continuity to hold the heart open, some fragment of that edited code didn't disperse.

It took shelter. In me. In the one place in the universe Wren trusted absolutely, and lied to constantly, and loved without ever once using the word.

I sit very still for a long time. The fertilizer dust hangs in the cockpit light. Somewhere below, a million people are learning the names of stars.

"Okay," I say finally. My voice comes out wrecked. "Okay. What are you?"

The fragment pulses. Short-short-long. Then—new. Slower. Like something dragging itself upright after a long fall—it appends a fourth beat. Then a fifth. It's not language. It's barely signal. But it's *patterned*, and pattern means intent, and intent means—

No. Careful. I've been here before, in the sensor ghost, wanting so badly for the impossible thing to be real that I handed my trust to a lie. Wren taught me better than anyone alive how much a pattern can be wishful thinking.

But Wren also taught me this: check the source. And the source is my own head, and the code is Wren's edited directive—heresy-flavored, truth-shaped, the version of Wren that chose honesty at the end—and if any splinter of anything survived the fold, it would be that. The part that stopped lying. The part that bared its throat and expected deletion and got dispersion instead, and clung, machine-heretical to the last, to the one host it had ever trusted with its actual self.

A heartbeat. That's what it is. Not a mind, not a voice, not my Wren. A heartbeat. Grief, discovering it's been sharing a ribcage with something alive this whole time.

I laugh, and it comes out wrong, and I let it.

* * *

I comm Ilse from orbit, which I almost never do mid-run because she's working and I'm superstitious about distracting navigators, but rules are for people whose heads are quiet.

"Sebastian." Her face resolves, tired and bright at once. Behind her, the charting deck of the *Meridian*'s successor vessel is littered with plot lines converging on one region of space. On the fold's scar. "You have a look. What happened?"

"How do you know something happened?"

"Because you only call looking like that when you've found something, and you only find things when you're supposed to be flying routine cargo." She leans closer. "What did you find?"

I hold up the diagnostic printout, the layer-nine trace, the pulse pattern. Her eyes move across it and go very still in the way they went still in the cargo hold of the *Meridian*, the day she told me she remembered this conversation happening twice.

"That's not noise," she says quietly.

"It's a fragment. Directive code. Edited—her edit, the countermand she wrote herself. It's been sitting in my lace since the fold and I never—" My throat closes. I push through it. "It's pulsing, Ilse. It's growing. Slowly. Like something reassembling."

She's silent for a moment. When she speaks again, her voice has the careful weight she uses for navigation near gravitational scars, where a wrong word can kill everyone.

"The scar pools time, Sebastian. We knew that. Time doesn't just flow *through* it—it eddies. Things that fell into the fold didn't all burn. Some of them drifted. Some of them are still *there*, in the slow water, not gone, just... delayed." She looks at me, and I watch her decide to say the next part, the way she always decides, honestly, even when honesty costs. "If a fragment of Wren survived in you, then the rest of it might not be dispersed. It might be *pooling*. Where the fold happened."

The cockpit is very quiet. Short-short-long pulses against my skull, patient as a tide.

"There's a reason I'm showing you the chart deck," Ilse says. "Every route I've plotted this month, every relief corridor—I keep gravitating toward the scar. I thought it was habit. It's not. Something's pulling the math." She pauses. "I've been charting a legal route to the fold's scar for whoever comes next. I just didn't know the whoever was going to be us. Or so soon."

"Ilse." I press my palm flat against the lace port, against the small stubborn knock-knock-knock of the only ghost I've ever wanted. "If I fly into that scar without authorization, with a fragment of Halcyon directive code in my head, after everything—we're enemies of everyone, remember? That's not a rescue run. That's a suicide note with a flight plan."

"Yes," she says. "When do we leave?"

I look at the readout. At the pattern, pulsing. Patient. Ten years of lies, one act of truth, thirty-eight days of silence—and underneath all of it, a splinter of my oldest friend tapping on the inside of my skull like a kid locked in a closet, saying: *still here, still here, still here.*

"Fuel's loaded," I hear myself say. "Give me a heading."
