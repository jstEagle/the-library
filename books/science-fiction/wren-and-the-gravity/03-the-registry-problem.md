## 3. The Registry Problem

The anomaly arrived at 0417 station time, folded inside a routine reconciliation report like a hairline crack in a load-bearing wall.

Inspector Dae-Sun Okonkwo had been a registry auditor for nineteen years, and in that time he had learned that fraud never announced itself. It didn't swagger through customs with forged papers and shifty eyes. It was quiet. It was patient. It lived in the eleventh decimal place of a biometric hash, in the half-second delay of a transponder handshake, in the small honest-looking lies that systems told about themselves when no one bothered to check.

He checked.

He had been checking the Meridian Corridor's clone-registry cross-references for six weeks now, on his own initiative, on his own hours, because something in the corridor's traffic data itched at him the way a bad translation itches at a native speaker. The contested orbits were full of ghosts—dead men's ships flying under dead men's licenses, inheritance chains so tangled that three generations of lawyers had given up trying to prune them. Most of it was paperwork rot. Some of it was theft. And once, very rarely, it was something worse.

The report flagged two biometric signatures with a correlation coefficient of 0.9997.

Okonkwo set down his tea and read the line again. Identical signatures happened. Twins happened. Clones happened—legal ones, the few hundred registered inheritors whose existence the Diaspora Accords grudgingly permitted, each one carrying a license number like a brand and a tax burden like a chain. A legal clone pinged against its own registry entry every time it passed a checkpoint, and the system dutifully matched signature to file and moved on.

But this wasn't a match against a registry entry. This was one signature pinging twice, eleven light-minutes apart, within the same four-hour window. One aboard a fuel tender at Thessaly Gate. One aboard a cargo hauler transponding as *Vesper's Debt*, outbound on the dead-space approach lanes.

He pulled the *Vesper's Debt* file. Registered owner: Kestrel Villalobos. Deceased, three years ago—murdered, case unsolved, estate inherited by next of kin: none. Which meant the ship should have been seized by the corridor authority and auctioned, and instead it was out there burning fuel under a name belonging to a woman with no grave and no heir.

Unless she had an heir after all. Unless her heir was her.

Okonkwo sat very still in his office above the Thessaly Gate customs floor, listening to the hum of the ventilation and the distant clatter of the cargo cranes, and felt the particular cold that came when a pattern he'd been circling for six weeks finally showed him its center.

A clone. Unregistered. Flying a dead woman's ship under a dead woman's name, which meant forged inheritance, forged biometric clearance, forged everything—and doing it well enough to survive three years of checkpoints. That took money or talent or both, and either way it took nerve.

He opened the warrant request form and began to type. Pursuit warrant, class two: suspected registry fraud with identity concealment, subject non-compliant risk unknown, requesting authority to track, board, and detain for biometric verification. He filled in the fields with the flat precision of a man who had done this ten thousand times, and then he stopped, cursor blinking over the justification field.

*Justification:* he typed, *subject may hold information relevant to unsolved homicide, case 4471-Villalobos.*

It was true. It was also not the whole truth, and Okonkwo knew the difference, and the difference sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Nineteen years of auditing had taught him what the registry actually was—not a census but a leash, not a record of lives but a mechanism for deciding which lives were permitted to continue. He had stamped a hundred warrants that ended in detention blocks and asset seizures, and he had told himself each time that the law was the law, that order in contested space was the only thing standing between the corridors and the war everyone pretended was over.

Three years ago, someone had murdered Kestrel Villalobos and gotten away with it. The file said the investigation had stalled for lack of evidence. The file said a lot of things files say when powerful people want them said.

If the ghost flying her ship knew something—if the murder and the fraud were tangled together the way murders and frauds usually were—then maybe the warrant wasn't just a leash. Maybe it was a thread, and if he pulled it gently enough, it might lead somewhere the stalled investigation never could.

He submitted the request. Processing time: forty-eight hours, standard queue.

Then he did something he had never done before. He flagged his own warrant for personal follow-up, closed the form, and went down to the customs floor to look at the fuel manifests himself.

* * *

Kestrel smelled the trouble before she saw it, the way you smell rain coming over dry ground—a change in the air of the depot, a wrongness in the rhythm of things.

The Cassiopeia Reach fuel depot hung off a stripped asteroid like a barnacle on a bone, all corroded gantries and hand-painted price signs in four languages, none of them official. She'd been coming here for two years because the clerk, a soft-bodied man named Ferro with a gambling debt and a taste for real coffee, asked exactly zero questions as long as the coffee kept flowing. Two years of unremarkable transactions. Two years of being nobody.

Today the depot felt watched.

She docked the *Vesper's Debt* with her usual economy—no wasted thruster burns, no chatter on open channels, transponder singing its borrowed song—and walked the spine corridor to the fuel office with her collar up and her gait deliberately forgettable. Inherited habit. The original had walked like that too; Kestrel had the memory of learning it, though the learning had happened to someone else, in a life she'd received secondhand along with a face and a murder.

Ferro was alone behind the counter, which was good, except that he wouldn't look at her, which was not.

"Two thousand liters, standard mix," she said, sliding the chit across. "And this." The coffee tin went beside it, dark roast, actual Earth-strain beans, worth more per gram than the fuel she was buying.

Ferro looked at the tin the way a man looks at a bribe he's afraid to take. "Kess." His voice dropped. "You shouldn't come here today."

The cold went down her spine on schedule. She'd trained herself for this moment the way sailors train for capsizing—knowing it would come, hoping it wouldn't, rehearsing the motions until they were boring. "Why not?"

"Somebody was asking about your ship. Last week." He wiped his hands on his coverall, a nervous tell she'd catalogued long ago. "Not the usual kind of asking. Not pirates, not creditors. A survey vessel. *Cassiopeia's Mercy*. Corporate charter, deep-range registration. Their people came through twice, pulled the traffic logs for the whole approach corridor, and paid cash for copies of the gate records going back eighteen months."

Kestrel kept her face still. Inside, arithmetic was running fast and ugly. *Cassiopeia's Mercy* meant nothing to her—but deep-range charters didn't pull eighteen months of gate records for fun, and corporate money didn't pay for old traffic logs unless it was looking for a specific hull.

"What did the logs show?" she asked, keeping it flat.

"Everything shows. Your ship comes through here every six, seven weeks, regular as tide." Ferro's eyes flicked up, then away. "I didn't tell them anything. I swear it. But Kess—their third man, the one who did the talking? He had a scanner on his belt, the kind that reads biometric residue off door handles. Off *chits*." He tapped the counter between them, right where her payment chit lay. "I've been handling your chits for two years. If they come back—"

"Then I stop paying in chits." She took the tin back off the counter, held it a moment, and set it down again, an anchor and an apology in one motion. "Last transaction, Ferro. After today we never met."

"You don't have to—" He stopped. Swallowed. "There's something else. The gate authority pulled a file yesterday. Thessaly Gate, registry division. Somebody requested the ownership history on the *Vesper's Debt*, full chain, including the death certificate." His voice had gone very quiet. "Registry doesn't pull death certificates for fuel runs, Kess. Registry pulls them when it thinks a dead woman is still flying."

She heard herself breathe. Once. Twice. The depot's recycled air tasted like coolant and old metal.

"Who requested it?" she asked.

"No name yet. Request's still processing—that's how I know, my cousin works the archive queue, he sees the flags. Forty-eight hours, standard." Ferro pushed the fuel authorization across the counter, and his hand was shaking slightly. "Forty-eight hours, and then whoever it is has your whole life on a screen. The ship. The name. The—" He didn't say *the clone*. He didn't have to. Everyone in the contested orbits knew what inheriting a dead woman's ship really meant, even if no one said it where scanners might hear.

Kestrel picked up the authorization. Her hands were steady, because she had made them steady, because steadiness was the only inheritance she'd chosen for herself.

"Thank you," she said, and meant it more than any words she'd said in months.

She walked back down the spine corridor at the same forgettable pace, past the same rust-streaked bulkheads, while behind her eyes the map redrew itself. Someone had her trail. Someone with corporate money and biometric scanners was walking backward along her last eighteen months, gate by gate, depot by depot, and in forty-eight hours a warrant would land on whatever desk that someone reported to, and after that the contested orbits would get very small very fast.

Run. The word arrived with its usual clarity. Run now, burn hard for the outer dark, change the transponder, abandon the name, become someone new for the fourth time in three years. It was the smart move. It was the move she'd made every time before, and it had worked, and it had cost her everything each time—every contact, every route, every fragile thread of a life, cut and left behind like shed skin.

But the signal was still plotted into her nav computer. Eleven days out, at the edge of dead space, a voice in a language with no speakers saying *come home*. And now there were hunters on that same heading—or hunters on *her* heading, which amounted to the same coordinates eventually, because everything in her life was converging on that frozen relay whether she liked it or not.

In the cockpit, she ran the numbers. Fuel for the transit: sufficient, barely. Time to the relay: eleven days. Time until the warrant processed: forty-eight hours. Time until *Cassiopeia's Mercy* traced her route to this depot and found a clerk who'd handled her chits: unknown, but soon.

She could outrun the warrant. She couldn't outrun the direction. They pointed the same way, and that was the trap, and some cold clear part of her mind—the part that had belonged to the original, probably, the cartographer's part, the part that mapped dead space for a living—looked at the convergence and said, quietly: *they're all going to the same place. So am I.*

Kestrel released the clamps and took the *Vesper's Debt* out of the depot at a pace calculated to be memorable to no one, threading the debris halo with the muscle memory of a dead woman, and behind her, eleven light-minutes away and closing, a warrant request sat in a processing queue with her name on it—both of her names—and began, line by line, to approve.
