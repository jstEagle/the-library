## 4. Cargo of Ghosts

The *Wren and the Gravity* ran dark and quiet through the long middle of nowhere, and Kestrel Villalobos ran quiet with it.

Deep transit had a texture she'd come to know the way you know the inside of a coffin. No traffic. No checkpoints. Just the hull's slow tick as it shed heat into the absolute cold, and the drive's subsonic hum that she felt in her teeth more than heard. Eleven days to the coordinates. Eleven days of nothing but herself and the ship and the signal looping in her head like a hook she couldn't spit out.

*Kestrel, come home.*

She'd tried to delete it four times. She'd gotten as far as the confirmation dialogue each time, cursor hovering over the red field, and each time her hand had drifted away from the console as if it belonged to someone else. Which, in a sense, it did.

She made tea in the galley because making tea was something to do with her body while her mind did something it had been avoiding for three years. The water heater was temperamental; she thumped it the way the original used to, then caught herself doing it and stopped, cup halfway to her mouth.

That was the thing nobody told you about being a clone. It wasn't the memories. It was the habits. The memories you could interrogate, file, distrust. But the habits came up out of the dark unasked—the way she cracked her knuckles left to right, the way she liked her tea bitter enough to strip paint, the way she said *sorry* when machines failed instead of cursing at them. Three years of careful, constructed selfhood, and her own hands kept betraying her with another woman's gestures.

She carried the cup to the cockpit and strapped into the pilot's chair, and she looked at the navigation plot, and she admitted what she'd been admitting since day two: she wasn't going to find out who sent the signal by refusing to think about it. Whoever had spoken Old Meridian into the dark had known her original. Known her name. And the people who killed the original had spent three years making sure that name stayed dead.

Which meant Kestrel needed to know what the original had known. And there was exactly one place to look.

The memory archive sat in a shielded compartment under the cockpit deck, a black slab the size of a hymnal, legally speaking a piece of contraband more damning than any weapon she carried. Clone inheritance was supposed to stop at assets. Ships, debts, contracts, enemies—the law was very clear that a clone received the estate and none of the essence. The archive in her deck was a registry violation stacked on top of a registry violation, a copy of a copy of a woman's recorded life, and Kestrel had never once plugged it in.

Not once in three years.

She set the tea down where it wouldn't spill, and she unlocked the compartment, and she held the slab in both hands for a long moment. It was warm. Not physically—it had been powered down for years—but her hands made it warm, and she thought: *this is the closest thing I have to holding a ghost.*

"Okay," she said to the empty cockpit. Her voice sounded wrong in it. "Okay. Coat's yours. Let's see if it fits."

She slotted it in.

* * *

The archive opened the way a drowned ship opens—slowly, in layers, pressure equalizing. First the index, a lifetime of timestamps scrolling past like sediment. Birth. Schooling. First ship, first license, first solo survey. The original had been a cartographer for the Thessaly Combine, mapping contested orbits for a living, drawing the lines that decided who got to claim what. Kestrel had known that from the estate paperwork. What the paperwork hadn't conveyed was how much the original had *loved* it.

Kestrel surfaced from a memory of a nebula the color of a bruise and found her own face wet.

That was the trap of it. She'd braced for the murder, for the fear, for the last day. She hadn't braced for the ordinary joy. The original—*Villalobos, K., registry designation original*—had loved her work with a whole-heartedness that Kestrel had never managed for anything. She'd loved the math of orbits, the patient triangulation, the moment when a blank region of a chart filled in and a piece of the universe became legible. She'd loved bad coffee in a cold cockpit. She'd loved—

Kestrel paused the memory.

She'd loved a lullaby. There was a child in the memories—not hers, a sister's kid, back before the Diaspora orphaned that whole branch of the family—and the original had sung to the child in Old Meridian, a nonsense song about a wren who carried the moon in a basket, and the child had laughed, and the original had laughed, and Kestrel sat in her cockpit three years and eleven light-years away from any of it, crying over a stranger's happiness that was also, by every biological measure, her own.

*Wearing someone else's coat,* she thought. *And it fits. That's the worst part. It fits.*

She wiped her face, drank the cold tea, and went looking for the last day.

* * *

The final timestamp was eleven hours of recording, and Kestrel made herself watch it straight through, the way you'd make yourself read a dead friend's last letter.

The morning was mundane. The original had filed a survey report from a relay station at the edge of dead space—Kestrel recognized the relay now, recognized it with a jolt that went through her like current: it was the relay the signal was coming from. The original had logged routine readings, eaten a ration pack, complained to the station log about the coffee. She'd been planning to take leave. There was a draft message to a booking agent on Meridian Station, half-written: *thinking maybe the southern arcs this year, I want to see open water—*

Then, at 1400 station time, the day bent.

The original had picked up an anomaly. Kestrel watched her own hands—another woman's hands—run the readings three times, four times, watched her own face go from frown to stillness to that specific blankness Kestrel knew from her own mirror, the face she made when something was too big to show on. The anomaly wasn't a rock or a wreck. It was a *pattern*. Mass signatures in dead space, dozens of them, arranged in a distribution that no natural process produced. Hulls. The original had found hulls—ships, hundreds of them, drifting silent in a region every chart marked as empty, and the registry markings on the nearest one, when she enhanced the image, belonged to a convoy that had officially vanished during the Diaspora Wars with eleven thousand souls aboard.

Eleven thousand people hadn't been lost. They'd been *put* there. And the original had found them.

Kestrel watched her original do the bravest and most foolish thing Kestrel could imagine: she didn't report it. Not to the Combine, not to the registry, not to anyone. She watched the original sit very still for a long time, and then she watched her open a children's lullaby—the wren and the basket and the moon—and she watched her begin, with a cartographer's precision, to hide a massacre inside a song.

The method was beautiful. That was the horror of it, how beautiful it was. The original had encoded the coordinates of the hull field into the melody itself—each note a bearing, each rest a distance, the song's structure a star chart you could hum. Any cartographer who knew Old Meridian and knew what to listen for could reconstruct the field from the tune alone. Anyone else would hear a nursery song. She'd buried the truth so deep in innocence that only grief could dig it out.

The recording's last hour was the original singing the lullaby to herself in the empty cockpit, once through, slow, committing it to muscle memory. Then she'd encrypted the archive, sealed the cache, and logged one final entry to the station's outbound queue—a message Kestrel had never found, addressed to no one Kestrel had ever identified. The recording ended with the original looking directly into the camera, and Kestrel felt the look go through her like a harpoon.

"If you're watching this," the original said, "then I didn't make it, and you're me, and I'm sorry. Not for the danger. For the loneliness. I remember how it feels to be the only one who knows a thing." A pause. Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "The song is the map. Don't sing it where anyone can hear. And Kestrel—when it gets too heavy, put it down and pick it up again. That's all anybody does. That's all I ever did."

The archive went dark.

* * *

Kestrel sat in the humming cockpit for a long time after, the tea stone cold, her hands flat on her thighs.

So that was why. Not piracy, not debts, not some lover's quarrel. A cartographer had found eleven thousand murdered people floating in a hole in the map, and someone with enough power to fill that hole with corpses had decided she couldn't be allowed to hum. They hadn't killed her for what she stole. They'd killed her for what she *knew*—and then spent three years interrogating a copy of her, trying to find out whether the knowledge had survived the copying.

And the signal, the impossible signal in a dead language, coming from the relay where she'd died—

*Kestrel, come home.*

Someone knew about the song. Someone was sitting at the site of the murder, in the dark, calling her back to the place where the original had hidden the map inside a melody. It could be a trap. It was almost certainly a trap. The people who'd killed the original had had three years to learn everything she loved, everything she'd sing, everything that would make a clone come running.

Kestrel pulled up the lullaby file, and her own voice—her original's voice, there was no difference and all the difference in the world—filled the cockpit, quiet and steady, the wren and the basket and the moon.

She listened to it the way she'd been trained to listen: not as music, but as data. And there, under the melody, she heard it—the bearings, the distances, the shape of a mass field that no honest chart had ever shown. The massacre, humming.

Her hands were shaking when she killed the playback. She plotted the intercept instead, refined it, checked it twice. Nine days out. And somewhere out there, at the end of a song about carrying the moon, sat the truth that had killed her, and whoever had sung her name back across the dark.

She keyed the log with a steady hand she didn't feel.

"Day four of transit," she said. "I know why she died now. I'm going to go stand where she fell."

Outside the viewport, dead space turned, patient and black, full of things the maps refused to name.
