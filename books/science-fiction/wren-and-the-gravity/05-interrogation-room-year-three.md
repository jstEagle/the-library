## 5. Interrogation Room, Year Three

The room had no windows, but the second Kestrel had learned to tell time anyway.

It was in the way the ship breathed. The *Cassiopeia's Mercy* ran on a day-cycle calibrated to some dead world's rotation, and every twenty-six hours the ventilation system cycled down for maintenance—a four-minute hush in the hum that lived in the walls. She counted the cycles. She had counted eleven hundred and forty of them since they'd pulled her out of the relay wreck, half-frozen, her lungs full of vacuum-burned tissue, and put her in this chair.

Eleven hundred and forty days. Three years and change.

She sat with her hands flat on the table because they liked it when she did that. It made them feel in control. Her wrists were bare—no restraints anymore; she'd stopped trying to kill anyone around day six hundred, or rather, she'd stopped letting them see her try—and the table between her and the door was scarred polymer, gray as old ice.

The door opened at the usual interval. Warrant Officer Halloway came in first, carrying his slate like a hymnal, and behind him came the one whose name she'd never been given—the tall man with the surgeon's hands and the voice like warm oil poured over a wound. He'd introduced himself once, three years ago, with a name she'd deliberately forgotten. Names were gifts. She didn't accept gifts from men who kept her in a box.

"Good morning," he said.

"It isn't," she said. "But it's morning."

He smiled the way he always did, settling into the chair across from her, arranging himself with the patience of a man who had all the funding in the world and nowhere else to be. Halloway took his position by the wall. The recorder light above the door glowed amber.

"You slept well," the tall man observed.

"I dreamed about drowning."

"That's new."

"It's not." She let a small silence stretch. "You just haven't asked before."

He opened his slate. On it, she knew, was the same file they'd shown her on the first day: orbital telemetry from the Meridian Relay, shipping manifests, a dead woman's biometric profile. A cartographer named Kestrel Villalobos who had mapped something in the dark and died for it, her body left where the vacuum would keep it honest. And beside that file, another one—hers. Clone registry designation redacted, growth-acceleration records sealed, legal existence: none.

They had never explained why they'd taken her alive. They didn't have to. A clone inherits the original's memories up to the point of decanting. Whatever Kestrel Villalobos had hidden, her copy might carry it somewhere behind her eyes, in some fold of inherited memory they hadn't managed to cut out of her yet.

"The charts," the tall man said, as he said every day. "Where are they?"

"I don't know."

"You understand my difficulty."

"I understand you've asked me that question four thousand times, and my answer has a perfect service record."

He turned the slate toward her. On it: a star chart fragment, the edge of dead space, the relay's position marked with a patient red cross. "Your original came here. She spent nine hours aboard this station before she died. Nine hours, doing something. We recovered her tools. We recovered her personal effects. What we did not recover is whatever she built, copied, hid, or transmitted during those nine hours. You were grown from her. Somewhere in your head is a map of what she knew."

"And if there were," she said, "what makes you think I'd give it to you?"

"Because we've been very good to you."

The laugh came out of her before she could stop it—short, ugly, real. Three years of a cell the size of a cargo container. Three years of nutrient paste and recycled air and questions. *Very good to you.* She watched his face absorb the laugh without changing, and she understood, again, that nothing she felt would ever move him. He was not cruel the way sadists are cruel. He was cruel the way auditors are cruel: everything was a line item, including her.

"Let's try something different today," he said.

Her stomach tightened. He only said that when he'd found something.

He set the slate down and slid it across the table. On the screen was a waveform—a signal, rendered in silver lines against black. Repeating. Old.

"Do you recognize the language?"

She looked at it and kept her face still, which was the hardest thing she had done in three years, harder than the cold extraction, harder than the first year's beatings. Because she recognized it instantly. Of course she did. It was stitched through her inherited memories like a thread through cloth—lullabies sung in a dialect that died with the Diaspora, a mother's voice murmuring words that meant *come home* in a grammar no living throat used anymore.

Old Meridian. Their original's private language. *Their* language—the one the two of them had been grown knowing and almost no one else alive could read.

Someone was broadcasting in it.

*Someone answered,* she thought, and had to fight the wave of heat that rose in her chest. *Someone finally answered.*

"No," she said.

"It's transmitting from a relay near the Thessaly corridor. Encrypted, low-power, repeating on a schedule. Our linguists can't parse it—the language has been extinct for two centuries. But our cryptographers noticed something interesting." He tapped the waveform. "The transmission pattern matches a legacy authentication protocol. Pre-war Meridian survey code. Whoever is sending it is using a cipher that was only ever taught to—" he paused, savoring it, "—cartographers. Licensed ones. There were perhaps forty people in contested space who knew this protocol, and thirty-nine of them are accounted for."

She said nothing. Her heart was a fist against her ribs, and she made herself breathe slowly, the way you breathe when you're pretending to be bored.

"So either a ghost is transmitting," the tall man continued, "or someone out there has access to a dead cartographer's cipher keys. Someone like, say, a person who inherited them." His eyes came up from the slate, mild and terrible. "Or someone who *is* her."

"There were three of us," she said. "You know that. You grew us."

"We accounted for two. You, and the one who burned in the escape attempt on Callisto." He folded his hands. "Which leaves an interesting arithmetic problem, doesn't it? Either the third copy survived and has been hiding for years—which would be a remarkable feat for someone with no legal identity, no credits, and no transponder—or someone has stolen her keys."

"Maybe she's just better at this than I am."

"Oh, I don't doubt it." He smiled again. "Whoever she is, she's stayed invisible for three years in a surveillance lattice that catches everyone eventually. That takes discipline. Paranoia. A certain kind of temperament." He leaned forward slightly. "Here's what I think. I think she's been running from us since the murder. I think she knows exactly what happened at that relay, because she remembers it—we both know how inheritance works. And I think that signal is her finally making a mistake."

*No,* the second Kestrel thought, with a ferocity that surprised her. *That signal is her answering me.*

Because now she understood. The transmitter wasn't a ghost and it wasn't a thief. It was the third one—the one who'd gotten out, the one whose file they'd never closed, the one who'd spent three years being careful while this woman sat in a box and bled information she didn't have. The signal was in their language. The signal carried their original's cipher. And buried in its repeating pattern, if the linguists' instruments were even a tenth as sensitive as hers, was a phrase she'd decoded from the waveform in the four seconds it had been on the slate:

*Kestrel, come home.*

She had sent it. Three weeks ago, through a maintenance channel she'd spent eight months cultivating—a smuggled pulse routed through a decommissioned buoy, timed to the ventilation cycle so the guards' monitors would read it as system noise. A message in a dead language, flung at the dark, addressed to whoever might still be listening. *Come home. Come to the relay. Come to where she died. I'll leave the rest where she left it.*

She hadn't known if anyone would hear it. She hadn't known if the third one even existed, or if she'd escaped, or if she was ash in some unmarked grave. She'd sent it anyway, on the theory that hope was the only weapon they couldn't confiscate.

And now the tall man was holding her own signal in his hands, calling it a mistake.

"When did you first detect the transmission?" she asked, keeping her voice flat.

"Eleven days ago."

Eleven days. The buoy route took a signal that long to propagate through the dead relays. Which meant the message had arrived. Which meant that somewhere out past Thessaly Gate, a ship with a forged transponder and a borrowed name had received a summons written in a mother-tongue shared by exactly three people, two of whom were dead or caged—

—and she was coming.

The knowledge settled into the second Kestrel like ballast. Not hope, exactly. Hope was soft. This was colder and more useful: *certainty*. Somewhere out there was a woman with her face and her memories and three years of practice at surviving, and she was plotting a course toward a trap neither of them fully understood, because that was what their original would have done, and they were, for all their differences, still made from the same clay.

"They'll come to the relay," the tall man said, watching her with those auditor's eyes. "Signals like this are bait, or they're beacons. Either way, whoever sent it expects a response. And when that response comes—if it comes—we will be waiting. So I'll ask you one more time, and then we'll move to less pleasant methods." He turned the slate back toward himself. "The charts. Where did she hide them?"

And here was the thing—the thing she'd been carrying for three years like a stone swallowed whole, the thing that had kept her sane through every session in this room:

She didn't know.

Not truly, not precisely. But she remembered, from the inherited tangle of their original's last months, a lullaby their mother had sung—four verses, simple, the kind of tune a child could hold onto forever. And she remembered that in the final week of the original's life, the lullaby had changed. An extra phrase woven in. A fifth verse that didn't belong, that mapped, if you sang it right, if you'd been raised on Old Meridian navigation songs the way all three of them had been—a course. Coordinates dressed as comfort, hidden in plain sound.

Their original had hidden the charts inside a children's song. And she had given that song to all three of her copies, because she'd known—one of them, any of them, all of them—might need to find it someday.

The tall man was watching her face. She gave him nothing. Three years of practice: a face like a locked room.

"I told you," she said. "I don't know."

He studied her for a long moment. Then he stood, gathered his slate, and paused at the door—the ritual closing he always performed, the small theater of mercy.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I hope your sister-copy is as stubborn as you are. It'll make her easier to respect and harder to break. We'll see which matters more."

The door sealed behind him. Halloway followed. The recorder light went dark.

The second Kestrel sat alone in the humming room and allowed herself, for exactly sixty seconds, to feel everything: the grief, the fury, the wild terrified joy of it. *She's coming. She heard me. She's coming.*

Then the ventilation cycle began its four-minute hush, and she went to work.

There was a panel in the cell wall, loose since month nineteen, that she'd never pried open because prying it open would accomplish nothing except getting her sedated and restrained again. But the panel wasn't the point. Behind the panel was a junction of coolant lines, and wrapped around the junction, held by thermal tape she'd stolen in thumb-width pieces over fourteen months, was a coil of salvaged wire. And connected to the wire, spliced into the ship's internal sensor loop at a point too trivial for anyone to audit, was a transmitter the size of her thumbnail.

Eight months to build. Eleven days since the signal went out. If the third one was inbound—and she was, she *had* to be—she would pass through sensor range of the *Cassiopeia's Mercy* long before she reached the relay. And when she did, the second Kestrel intended to be ready with the only gift she had left to give:

A warning. In Old Meridian. *They know you're coming. They're waiting at the relay. Change your approach.*

Her captors thought the signal was a mistake.

She pressed her palm flat against the cool wall, over the hidden coil, and smiled for the first time in three years.

It was not a mistake. It was a door, and she had just heard footsteps on the other side of it.

* * *

They came for her early, off-cycle, which had never happened before.

Two guards instead of one. Halloway absent. The tall man waiting in the corridor outside her cell, and behind him, visible through the open door, a woman in fleet grays with a courier's case handcuffed to her wrist.

"Change of venue," the tall man said pleasantly. "We're done asking questions. The relay's gone active—power draw on a station that's been dead for three years. Someone's aboard." He stepped aside to let her pass, gesturing down the corridor with something that was almost courtesy. "You're going to walk me through what she finds. Every cache, every chart, every secret your original died keeping. In real time."

The second Kestrel fell into step between the guards, her heart hammering a rhythm she refused to show on her face.

*Hurry,* she thought, at the dark beyond the hull, at the woman with her face somewhere in the black. *But not too fast. And not the way they expect.*

Somewhere ahead, a dead station was waking up. Somewhere ahead, her sister was walking into the arms of the people who had murdered the woman they both used to be.

And somewhere in the wiring of the *Cassiopeia's Mercy*, a thumbnail-sized transmitter waited for its moment to speak.
