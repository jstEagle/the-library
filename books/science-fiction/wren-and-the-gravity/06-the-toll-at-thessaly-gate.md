## 6. The Toll at Thessaly Gate

Thessaly Gate hung in the throat of the corridor like a fishhook—curved, patient, and designed to catch anything that swallowed too fast. From a distance it looked almost gentle: a ring of docking arms wrapped around a spinning habitat core, floodlights soft as candle-glow against the black. Up close you could see the truth of it. Every arm bristled with interdiction batteries. Every light was a lens pointed inward.

Kestrel had passed through the Gate eleven times in three years under four different names, and she had never once been asked to dock for inspection. That was the arrangement. The Gate took its toll—a percentage skimmed from every cargo manifest, untraceable, unremarked—and in exchange it stayed incurious. Incuriosity was the most expensive commodity in contested space, and Kestrel had learned to buy it by the ton.

Today the toll booth's channel opened before she'd even finished her approach burn.

*Freighter* Vela's Mercy*, this is Thessaly Gate Control. You are directed to berth nine for routine customs verification. Acknowledge.*

Her hands went still on the console. *Vela's Mercy* wasn't her ship's real name any more than Kestrel Villalobos was her real name—the hull had been born the *Cartographer's Daughter*, which was a joke someone else had made at her original's expense, and she'd repainted the transponder twice since. But no one had ever made her dock. Not once. Not in three years.

She acknowledged anyway, because refusing would be worse, and began the slow, stomach-tightening spiral toward berth nine while her mind ran through everything that could have gone wrong: the fuel-depot clerk at Calloway's Rest who'd gone quiet when she mentioned her ship; the manifest discrepancy she'd papered over two months ago; the coffee bribe to the dock inspector whose name she couldn't now remember, which meant he might not remember her either, which meant he might sell her instead.

Or—and this thought arrived cold and complete, like ice water finding a seam—it was the signal. The Old Meridian broadcast she should have deleted and hadn't. Someone had triangulated it back to her rig.

Berth nine's clamps closed around the *Vela's Mercy* with a sound like a jaw setting.

* * *

They didn't send soldiers. They sent a man with a tablet and a coat that had been good five years ago, standing alone on the docking apron with his hands folded behind his back like a professor waiting out a late student. Medium height, gray at the temples, dark skin going ash around the eyes from too many years under artificial light. He looked tired in the specific way that people look when they've stopped expecting their work to mean anything.

"Kestrel Villalobos," he said, when the airlock cycled. Not a question. "I'm Inspector Dae-Sun Okonkwo, Registry Audit Division." He held up his credentials long enough for her to read them and not long enough to photograph. "Thank you for complying."

"I wasn't aware I had a choice."

"Nobody ever is. That's rather the point of choices." He gestured down the apron, toward an observation room with a window overlooking her ship. "Walk with me? It's about your file."

My file. Two words, and her pulse went up through her teeth. Everyone in contested space had a file; most files were thin—a name, a debt, a bribe or two. But there were files and there were *files*, and hers, if anyone had ever pulled the thread all the way down, ended at a cloning vat in a facility that had burned its records and half its staff to cover what came out of it. A clone registered as a natural-born heir. A person who existed only because a dead woman's biometrics said she did.

She followed him into the observation room because running was impossible and because some animal part of her brain insisted that if she could see him, he couldn't see all of her.

Okonkwo didn't sit. He stood at the window, looking down at her freighter with something like sympathy. "Do you know how many ships pass through Thessaly Gate in a standard cycle?"

"No idea."

"Eleven thousand, give or take. And do you know how many of those crews I could describe, right now, from memory?" He turned. "Four. You're one of them."

"That's either flattering or terrifying."

"It's arithmetic." He set the tablet on the table between them, face up but dark. "Three years ago, a cartographer named Kestrel Villalobos was murdered aboard a relay station at the edge of dead space. The case has never been solved. The estate passed to—" he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over an incision, "—a next of kin. You."

"The registry says so."

"The registry says a great many things." He tapped the tablet awake. It glowed between them, and though she couldn't read it upside down, she watched his eyes track across whatever was there. "Two weeks ago, a biometric signature matching yours pinged a customs scanner at Calloway's Rest. Standard enough. Except that eleven minutes later, the same signature—not similar, *identical*, down to markers we don't publish—pinged a sensor on a survey vessel operating out of Meridian Deep. Two bodies, one biometric profile, eleven light-minutes apart."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere below them, a cargo loader beeped its slow idiot rhythm.

"Sensor error," Kestrel said.

"Almost certainly," Okonkwo agreed, and the ease of it frightened her more than accusation would have. "Ghost echoes happen. Calibration drift happens. But I'm an auditor, Ms. Villalobos, and my entire profession consists of noticing when the numbers are almost certainly wrong and asking why anyway. So I asked. And I found that the second signature belongs to a passenger listed aboard the survey vessel *Cassiopeia's Mercy*—a vessel with no published owner, no port of registration, and a crew complement that officially does not exist." He looked at her then, fully, and his eyes were not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "Someone is holding a woman with your face, and I don't think she's a passenger."

Kestrel had prepared lies for this moment without knowing it—three years of rehearsing answers to questions no one had asked. She reached for the best of them. "Cloning fraud is a serious allegation, Inspector. If you're implying I'm—"

"I'm not implying anything. I'm offering." He slid the tablet across the table. On it was her file—or the public shape of it, which was thin and clean and boring, exactly as forged documents should be. His finger rested beside a single line item: *AUDIT PENDING*. "This warrant processes in six hours. When it does, my division takes your biometrics, runs them against the deep registry, and follows wherever they lead. It will lead somewhere ugly. It always does. People don't forge registries for innocent reasons."

"And if I run?"

"Then you confirm everything, and I stop being polite." He said it without heat, without threat, like a man reading a weather report. "But here's the other thing. The thing I actually came to say." He drew the tablet back and killed the screen. "I've spent four years auditing the margins of this war, Ms. Villalobos, and I have learned that the worst things out here wear official signatures. Whoever killed the original Kestrel Villalobos did it thoroughly—scrubbed the relay logs, bought off the first responders, buried the case under three layers of jurisdictional nothing. Bureaucracy doesn't scrub itself. Someone with access did that. Someone above me."

He let that sit. Outside the window, her ship sat clamped in the Gate's jaw, small and alone.

"So here is my offer, and I'll make it once. Help me find who killed her. Give me something real—a name, a vector, a reason a cartographer dies in dead space—and when this audit closes, your file gets lost. Misfiled. Corrupted beyond recovery. I've done it before." A ghost of a smile, gone quickly. "You walk out of Thessaly Gate as whoever you claim to be, permanently."

"And if I say no?"

"Then in six hours I do my job, and we both find out what's in your blood."

* * *

She made herself take the time. Asked for water, drank none of it. Walked the observation room's perimeter twice, feeling his gaze follow her without pressure, without hurry—he had six hours and a warrant; patience cost him nothing. She stood at the window and looked down at the *Vela's Mercy* and understood, with terrible clarity, that she was already caught. The only question left was what being caught would cost, and to whom.

If she gave him something—if she told him about the signal, the coordinates, the lullaby with charts folded inside it like a seed inside a fruit—she'd be handing the last secret of her original's life to a man she'd known for twenty minutes. And if the murderer wore official signatures, as Okonkwo himself believed, then telling him anything might be the same as telling the murderers everything.

But if she refused, the audit would crack her open in six hours anyway, and the *Cassiopeia's Mercy*—the woman with her face, the sister-copy she hadn't known existed until four days ago and now could not stop thinking about—would stay in whatever hold they kept her in, forever.

There was a third option. There was always a third option, and it was the one her original would have taken: lie.

"I don't know who killed her," Kestrel said, turning from the window. "I inherited a ship and a name and a stack of debts. That's all inheritance means, where I come from. If you're looking for a murderer, Inspector, I'm the wrong door."

It was a bad lie. She heard it go bad as it left her mouth—too flat, too rehearsed, the pauses in the wrong places. Okonkwo heard it too. She watched him hear it, watched something shift behind his tired eyes: not triumph, not anger. Something closer to disappointment, and beneath that, faintly, a kind of respect. He knew she was lying. He also knew why, and he let her do it, and that courtesy was the most frightening thing in the room.

"All right," he said quietly. "The wrong door."

He gathered his coat. At the observation room's door he stopped, hand on the frame, and spoke without turning around.

"For what it's worth—when the audit clears, and it will clear, because I'm going to write it that way—I'd take the long route home, Ms. Villalobos. Whatever's calling you out past the Meridian line, it isn't worth what's waiting there." A beat. "And if you change your mind about the offer, Thessaly Gate Control can reach me faster than you'd believe."

Then he was gone, and the door sighed shut, and Kestrel stood alone with the hum of the station and the sound of her own heart doing arithmetic.

* * *

The warrant processed in five hours and forty-one minutes, and it cleared her.

She watched it happen from the cockpit, transponder handshake accepted, departure vector approved, clamps releasing with a shudder she felt in her spine. *Audit closed: no anomaly found.* Okonkwo had written it exactly as promised. He had looked at a lie so bad a child could see through it, and he had signed his name under it anyway, and she could not decide whether that made him an ally, a fool, or a fisherman letting out line.

She brought the *Vela's Mercy* around, fed power to the drive, and threaded the Gate's toll lane with the careful, measured speed of a ship with nothing to hide—which was itself a performance, because everything in her was screaming to burn.

Behind her, Thessaly Gate receded into a hook of light. Ahead, the corridor to dead space opened like a held breath.

Six hours ago she'd been invisible. Now she was a file with a friendly hole burned through it, a signature on a warrant, a name spoken aloud by a stranger who knew she was lying—and somewhere out along that vector, a woman with her face was waiting to learn whether any of it had been enough.

Kestrel pushed the throttle past prudent and ran for the dark.
