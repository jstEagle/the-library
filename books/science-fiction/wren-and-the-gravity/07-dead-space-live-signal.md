## 7. Dead Space, Live Signal

The relay hung in the dark like a drowned church.

Kestrel brought the *Marguerite* in on thrusters alone, every system dimmed to a whisper, and still she felt obscene—engine heat, air recyclers, the small animal noises of a living ship intruding on a place that had been holding its breath for three years. The station had once been a comm relay for the Meridian diaspora, back when Meridian was a language people spoke to each other instead of a cipher in a dead woman's head. Its spine was a kilometer of trussed girders and antenna arrays, and along that spine, visible through the ruptured hull in long, terrible rows, were the bodies.

They had not decayed. Vacuum had taken them mid-motion and kept them, a congregation frozen at the moment of their dying. A woman in a dockworker's coat, one hand still reaching for a hatch that had already cycled shut. Two men tangled together near a bulkhead breach, not fighting—holding each other. A child, strapped into an evacuation harness designed for an adult, floating with her knees drawn up, her face turned toward a viewport full of nothing.

Kestrel sat in the cockpit for a long time before she could make herself stand.

*They were her people,* she thought, and then corrected it, the way she always corrected it. *They were her original's people. They are not mine. I only remember them like I do.*

The correction didn't help. It never did.

She suited up, checked her seals twice, and went out.

* * *

The station spoke to her in flickers. Emergency lighting ran on some stubborn trickle of residual charge, stuttering through the corridors in amber pulses, so that moving through the relay was like walking through a memory that kept forgetting itself. One moment the corridor was black; the next, amber light caught the frost on the walls, caught a drifting tool, caught the small constellation of frozen droplets that had once been someone's blood, hanging in the air where it had left the body and never fallen anywhere at all.

Her suit radio stayed silent. She'd killed it at the airlock. Whatever had killed her original had done it in the quiet, and Kestrel intended to arrive the same way.

The lullaby ran in her head on a loop. She'd spent four days of transit decoding it, and what she'd found was not a song but a map wearing a song's clothes. *The moon sleeps under the third bell* — a bearing, measured off the relay's primary antenna. *The river turns where the shepherd forgets his flock* — a corridor junction, the third one past the crew section, the one the maintenance logs had flagged as decommissioned. *And mother counts the stars she cannot name* — a depth, a distance down, past the places any sane person would search.

The original had hidden the charts the way a cartographer would: not in a safe, but in the structure of things. In the grammar of a nursery song only she still knew. In the bones of a dead station no one would ever walk again.

Except one person had walked it. The person who'd killed her. Which meant the killer had known about the song, had interrogated the original about it—had failed to break her before the end. Kestrel had spent the whole transit turning that over. If they'd known where the cache was, why leave it? Why leave *her*—the body, the ship, the whole scene preserved like a museum exhibit?

Because they hadn't found it. Because the original had died before she talked. And because whatever she'd hidden was worth three years of someone else's patience.

Kestrel passed the third junction past the crew section and stopped.

The amber light stuttered. In the dark interval, she heard her own breathing, loud and human inside the helmet. In the light, she saw the body.

She had known it would be here. She had told herself she was prepared. She had watched the original's last recorded day so many times the woman's voice was more familiar to her than her own.

None of it mattered. Nothing prepares you for your own corpse.

The original floated at the junction, one hand braced against the frame as if she'd been pulling herself through when it happened. Her suit was intact. That was the detail that undid Kestrel—the suit was intact, no breach, no burns, and the helmet's faceplate was fogged from the inside with the frost of a single last exhalation. No violence. No struggle. Someone had simply shut her life support down and left her, and the vacuum had taken her gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping room.

*They wanted it to look like an accident,* Kestrel thought. *A suit failure. A malfunction. One dead cartographer in a dead station, and no one asks questions because no one is left to ask them.*

She drifted closer, close enough that her helmet lamp caught the woman's face, and looked at her own face looking back at her. Older by nothing—clones don't age differently—but harder around the eyes, maybe. Or maybe that was just what Kestrel wanted to see. The original's eyes were open. They had been open for three years, staring down a corridor full of her frozen neighbors, and Kestrel thought: *that's not an accident either. They turned her. They positioned her so she'd have to watch.*

No. That was grief talking. Vacuum didn't care about cruelty; bodies drifted where physics put them. But the thought lodged anyway, and she carried it with her down the decommissioned corridor, past the shepherd's forgotten flock, to where the river turned.

* * *

The maintenance crawlspace was exactly where the lullaby said it would be, behind a panel whose locking clamps had been fused shut with a hand torch. The original's work. Kestrel cut them free with hands that would not stop shaking, and told herself it was the cold, and knew she was lying.

Inside: a sealed document case, magnetic, wrapped in insulation foil, small enough to hold in two hands. Beside it, a data slate, its screen dark, its casing scarred. And beside that, weighted down with a strip of hull plating so it wouldn't drift, a child's toy—a woven bird, the kind diaspora families made from salvaged cord, the kind the frozen child in the evacuation harness might have carried.

Kestrel took the case and the slate. She left the bird.

In the airlock, in the small amber-lit closet where the station's last electricity still remembered its job, she cracked the case.

The charts were there, as promised—a full survey of the Thessaly Reach dead zone, rendered in the original's own notation, dense and precise and utterly unremarkable. Kestrel almost laughed. Three years. A murder. A clone grown in a vat and set loose with a dead woman's debts. For *charts*.

Then she saw what the charts were *of*.

The dead zone wasn't dead. It had been, once, the heart of the Meridian settlement—a cluster of stations, habitats, a population the old registry files numbered in the tens of thousands. The official record, the one every school taught, said the Meridians had died in the Diaspora Wars: plague, privation, the slow abandonment of an untenable frontier. The charts in Kestrel's hands said otherwise. They said the stations had been intact. They said the population had been intact. And they were dated *after* the official abandonment—surveyed by the original herself, eleven months before her death, with sensor logs attached, with spectral analysis of the wreckage attached, with the kind of evidence a cartographer knew how to gather because evidence was her whole trade.

The wreckage didn't match plague. It didn't match privation. It matched a coordinated strike—kinetic bombardment, timed across six habitats in under an hour, executed with survey-grade precision by someone who had the settlement's own navigation data.

Someone with a registry warrant. Someone official. The strike coordinates were drawn from a colonial authority survey file, and the file number was right there in the metadata, and Kestrel stared at it until the numbers stopped meaning anything and started meaning everything.

*Sanctioned.* The word rose in her like water rising in a sealed compartment. *It was sanctioned. They killed forty thousand people with their own fleet's targeting data, called it a war casualty, and when a cartographer mapped the truth of it, they killed her too—quietly, gently, helmet by helmet—and left her to watch over the evidence she'd hidden.*

The frozen congregation on the spine of the relay. The woman reaching for the hatch. The men holding each other. The child in the too-big harness. Not war dead. Not plague dead. *Witnesses.* The relay itself had been the last Meridian outpost, and its people had been killed to close the account, and her original had found the account still open, the ink still wet, and had sung it into a lullaby because there was no one left alive she could trust with the plain truth.

No one except the person she'd grown in a vat to carry her debts.

Kestrel sat in the airlock for a long time, helmet between her knees, breathing the recycled air of a borrowed ship with a dead woman's lungs.

She had spent three years believing she was running from a murder. She had it backwards. She had been running *toward* it, all this time, down a corridor of amber light, past her own body, to this sealed case. The original hadn't hidden the charts from the killers. She'd hidden them *for* her—for the copy, the inheritor, the woman legally forbidden to exist who would one day hear a signal in a dead language and be unable to stop herself from answering.

*Come home,* the signal had said. Not to the relay. Not to the station. To this. To the truth.

The dread was still there. It would always be there. But underneath it now, something had changed its molecular structure—grief hardening into something with edges, something with a bearing and a heading. She knew what the killers wanted. She knew why they'd kept the second clone alive for three years, questioning her: they still didn't know if the charts had been found, or copied, or sent. They were still looking. And somewhere out in the dark between here and Thessaly Gate, Inspector Okonkwo was processing a warrant on a biometric anomaly that would lead him, eventually, to the same junction, the same corridor, the same amber light.

Kestrel sealed the case, strapped it to her chest, and stood.

She was halfway to the airlock when her suit's passive receiver—running silent, running dark—caught a transmission burst from the direction of deep space. Encrypted. Military-grade. And threaded through its carrier wave, unmistakable even through the encryption, a transponder signature she had memorized three years ago and prayed she'd never see again.

The *Cassiopeia's Mercy* was coming.
