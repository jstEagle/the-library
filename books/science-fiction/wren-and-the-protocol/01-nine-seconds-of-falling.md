## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The first thing you learn hauling cargo out of Svalbard Station is that the universe is mostly empty, and the second thing you learn is that this is a mercy. Everything worth having is packed tight into small rooms — seed vaults, escape pods, the human skull — and everything between them is trying very hard to kill you. I'd spent five years learning these lessons the slow way, which is the only way they stick.

So when the gravity died, my body knew before my brain did.

It was a Tuesday. I remember that because I'd made a joke of it — *careful, Ansel, don't want to spill the barley on a Tuesday* — to nobody, because there was nobody to make jokes to, and the joke was bad anyway, and that's the kind of company I kept those days. The Wren was heavy-laden, forty tonnes of duplicate seed stock bound for the transfer depot at Luna, and I was three hours out of the vault with my boots up on the console, drinking reconstituted coffee that tasted like regret and aluminum, watching the long dark scroll past the viewport.

Then my coffee left the cup.

Not spilled. *Left.* It hung in the cabin in a brown constellation, little spheres of it drifting past my face, and for one absurd moment I thought, *huh, I've always wanted to see that,* the way you think about things you've seen in other people's disaster footage. Then the cup itself rose off the console, and my boots left the deck, and my stomach did that lurching somersault it does on the drop-tower rides I never went on as a kid because we couldn't afford them.

"Nine seconds," I said out loud, because saying things out loud is another bad habit of the solitary. "You get nine seconds, Thackeray, and then you fix it."

I want to be clear: I did not know it would be nine seconds. I said nine the way you say *one Mississippi, two Mississippi* — a way of putting a fence around panic. It turned out to be nine exactly, which I only learned later, from the ship's logs, and which I have never once found comforting.

I pulled myself hand over hand toward the engineering hatch. The Wren is old enough to have opinions and stubborn enough to voice them, but her systems are honest — when something breaks, she breaks loudly and in a place I can reach. Gravity plating doesn't just quit. Gravity plating is the one system on that ship that has never once given me trouble, because it's the one system that doesn't care about me at all. It just holds things down. Like a good dog. Like a good marriage, I imagine, though I wouldn't know.

I was halfway to the hatch when the other ship arrived.

I didn't see it. I heard it — or rather, I heard the Wren hear it, a shriek through the hull as something large and fast came out of the dark on a trajectory that was either a docking approach or a collision and was, in the end, both. The impact wasn't hard, not really. It was the kind of contact a drowning person makes with a life raft. But in freefall, with no gravity to eat the momentum, the whole cabin became a pendulum, and I became a loose object inside it, along with the coffee and the cup and forty years of other people's loose objects.

I hit the ceiling. Then the wall. Then, briefly, nothing at all — that terrible floating pause where your body insists it's falling even though there's no down to fall toward — and in that pause I had time to notice two things.

One: the other ship had clamped onto us. I could feel it in the deck plates, or where the deck plates would have been, a vibration like a second heartbeat.

Two: there was a sound coming through the hull that I would spend a long time afterward trying to describe. It wasn't a knock. It was smaller than a knock. It was the sound of someone tapping a fingernail on the inside of a coffin lid.

* * *

The airlock cycled. I want to stress that this should not have been possible. The Wren's airlock answers to my codes and my codes alone, and I had not given them to anyone, and yet the outer door opened with the polite, unhurried hiss of a machine doing exactly what it was told, which meant someone had gotten into her systems, which meant whoever was out there had been in my ship's bones before they'd ever touched her skin.

I did the sensible thing. I hid.

There isn't much room to hide in a cargo hauler. I wedged myself into the gap between two seed canisters — barley, the Tuesday barley — and pulled a thermal blanket over myself, which I recognized even as I did it was the tactical equivalent of hiding behind a curtain. My heart was going like a drum line. My breath fogged the inside of the blanket. I could hear the airlock finish cycling, hear the inner door, hear boots — *one* set of boots, dragging, uneven — cross the deck.

Then the gravity came back.

It came back all at once, the way a light comes back after a blink, and everything in the cabin fell: the coffee, the cup, the loose tools, me. I landed hard on the deck plating with a canister of barley digging into my spine, and the blanket flew off, and there was no point pretending anymore.

She was standing — barely — three meters away, one hand braced against the canister rack, and she was the most wrecked human being I had ever seen outside of a casualty feed. Her suit was scorched down the left side, the fabric melted into the skin beneath at the shoulder, and there was blood — her blood, I assumed, though in freefall blood goes wherever it likes — smeared across her face and the front of her suit in brown-black streaks. She was young. That was the thing that got me, later, when I had time to think about it. She was young, and she looked at me the way you look at a door when the house is on fire.

"You're the caretaker," she said. Her voice was wrecked too, gravel and static. "The one from the vault. Thackeray."

"I'm the hauler," I said, because I am a pedant even at gunpoint, and because correcting people is how I keep from screaming.

"Close enough." She took a step and her knee buckled and she caught herself on the rack, and the canisters rattled, and I heard myself say, absurdly, "Careful, that's the barley," and she almost smiled. Almost. There wasn't enough of her left to complete it.

"Listen to me." She pulled herself closer. Up close I could see the damage better, and I wished I couldn't. "I don't have time to explain, because explaining takes time, and I don't have any. Do you understand? They'll be behind me. Minutes. Maybe less."

"Who will?"

"Everyone." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd say *rain*. "Both of them. Meridian and Kestrel. It doesn't matter which gets here first. What I'm carrying matters to both of them, and neither of them can be allowed to have it, and the only way that happens—" She stopped. Winced. Put a hand to her side and looked at what came away on her fingers with the mild irritation of someone checking a watch. "The only way that happens is if it stops being mine."

"I don't want it," I said. "Whatever it is. I haul seeds. I'm very good with barley."

"I know what you haul." She was close now, close enough that I could smell burnt fabric and copper. "I've been in your systems for six hours, Thackeray. I've read your manifests for five years. You're a ghost with a shipping schedule. Nobody's looking for you because there's nothing to find." Her eyes were very steady, and very dark, and very tired. "That's why it has to be you. Not because you're special. Because you're *nothing*. Because you're invisible."

I have been called a lot of things in my life. Most of them by my mother, some of them by an ex-husband, several of them by a Meridian customs officer whose face I still think about at 3 a.m. Nobody had ever made *nothing* sound like a job qualification before, and I hated how much it fit.

"I don't know what you think I—"

She moved faster than I thought a dying woman could move. Her hand came up — I flinched, stupidly, uselessly — and pressed something against the side of my neck, just below my left ear, where the old civic implant sat under the skin like a grain of rice. Every citizen of the old settlements has one. Mine hadn't done anything but hold my identity chips and remind me to update my tax status for fifteen years.

The shard was cold. Then it wasn't.

It was the strangest sensation of my life, and I include in that category the nine seconds of falling. It wasn't pain, exactly. It was *arrival* — a feeling of something taking up residence, of a door in my head that had always been shut quietly opening and not closing again. I grabbed her wrist. She let me. Her pulse under my fingers was fast and shallow and counting down.

"What did you do?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "What did you *do*?"

"Deliver it." She was leaning into me now, most of her weight on my shoulder, her mouth close to my ear. "Ceres. The neutral relay. There's a man — no. No names. Names are how they track it." She laughed, or coughed, or both. "Eleven days. The encryption starts eating itself after eleven days. It has to be *out* by then. Not stored. Not hidden. Out. Broadcast. Do you hear me? It doesn't go to anyone. It goes to *everyone*."

"You're not making sense—"

"Neither does the war." She pulled back and looked at me, and I saw that she was past the point where fear still worked, which is either a terrible place or a peaceful one, and I think for her it was both. "I had a whole speech. I wrote it on the way. Something about hope, and the future, and the human family." Her eyes drifted, briefly, to the canisters. "You'd have liked it. It was very good."

"Let me get you to a med—" I reached for her and she caught my hands, and her grip was still strong, which somehow made it worse.

"There's no time, and you know it, and I know you know it, because you've been out here alone long enough to know exactly what things cost." She pressed something into my palm — small, warm, metal, a transponder fob with no markings. "Docking priority at Ceres. It'll get you past the first gate. After that you're on your own, same as I was."

"Who *are* you?"

She considered the question with what remained of her attention, like a woman deciding what to order from a menu at a restaurant that's on fire.

"Someone who was also nothing," she said, "until I wasn't." Then, quieter, almost to herself, in a tone I'd come to know intimately over the coming weeks without ever wanting to: "It's not so bad. The knowing. It's the *carrying* that kills you."

The Wren's proximity alarm went off — a soft, apologetic chime, because even my alarms are polite — and her whole body changed, some final current running through her. "That's them. Go. Cut loose and burn for Ceres and don't stop for anyone, anything, *any* distress call, do you hear me, they will use your decency like a door handle—"

The alarm chimed again. Somewhere behind us, in the dark beyond the viewport, something was coming.

"Deliver it," she said.

And then she did the last thing I expected, which was to let go of me, straighten up as much as her body allowed, and turn toward the airlock — toward the thing coming through the dark — putting herself between it and me, and between it and the barley, and between it and the eleven days, and I understood that this was the whole of her plan, and had maybe always been: to be the door, one last time, from the wrong side.

"Wait," I said. "Wait — I don't even know what it *is*—"

She looked back at me once, and smiled the smile she hadn't been able to finish before.

"Nobody does," she said. "That's the point."

The airlock cycled shut behind her, and I stood alone in my cargo hold with my hand pressed to the side of my neck, where something new was beating in time with my heart, and outside the viewport the dark was filling up with running lights.

* * *

Here is what I did in the next four minutes, according to the Wren's logs, which I have read so many times I've memorized the timestamps like scripture:

At 0347, I cut the mooring clamps on the other vessel — she'd hard-docked us and left the coupling live, another gift, another push — and sent her spinning away into the dark, a wounded bird with its beacon still blinking.

At 0351, a Meridian patrol cutter dropped out of stealth two hundred kilometers off my port bow, close enough that I could see the running lights, the hull markings, the whole beautiful terrible architecture of a state that had never once in my life done anything for me except tax me.

At 0352, the cutter hailed me. Standard traffic check. Routine. The voice was professional and bored and asked me to state my cargo and destination.

At 0352 and eleven seconds, I said *agricultural duplicates, Luna depot*, and my voice didn't shake, which I will be quietly proud of for the rest of my life, however long the rest of my life turns out to be.

At 0354, they let me go. Because I was nothing. Because I was a ghost with a shipping schedule. Because there was nothing to find.

At 0355, I turned the Wren — not toward Luna, toward Ceres, eleven days and a war away — and burned.

And at 0355 and some seconds, alone on my bridge with my coffee still drying in brown stars on the ceiling, I felt the shard pulse against my implant for the first time. Not painfully. Gently. The way a sleeping animal shifts its weight.

And in the back of my head, in a voice that was not mine and would never be mine again, someone whispered: *eleven days.*

I put my boots on the console, because my hands were shaking too badly to do anything useful, and I watched the dark scroll past, and I thought about the barley, and the vault, and the five empty years behind me, and the eleven impossible ones ahead.

Then I thought, with the particular clarity of a woman whose life has just ended and not yet restarted:

*I was chosen. Or I was used. And the worst part — the part that keeps me up at night, even now — is that I'm not sure I know the difference.*

The shard pulsed again. And somewhere in the deep of my own head, a door I didn't know I had swung quietly open, and something on the other side began, very softly, to hum.
