## 9. Old Words

Wren had rehearsed this conversation for thirty years, and in all her rehearsals, the visitor never asked the right question.

August Montrose sat across from her in the galley of the Halcyon, hands wrapped around a bulb of reconstituted tea they hadn't drunk, and asked it anyway.

"Why Old Meridian?"

Not *who are you*. Not *how are you still alive*. Not any of the questions Wren had answers scripted for. They went straight past the surface of things to the mechanism underneath, the way an investigator would, and Wren felt something in her chest turn over—part admiration, part alarm. She had spent three decades learning to read machines. People were harder. This one was hardest of all, because she had built them out of hope before she'd ever seen their face.

"Because it's dead," she said.

August's dark eyes didn't blink. "That seems like a strange choice for a distress call."

"It's the only choice." Wren pushed back from the table and stood, because sitting still had never been one of her talents, and because she wanted to watch August's face while she said what came next. "A dead language is a filter. Think about it. Anyone can broadcast a mayday on a standard band. Pirates do it. Scavvers do it. The colonies run automated sweeps for distress signatures every few hours—if I'd called out in Calloway Standard or New Meridian Trade Tongue, I'd have gotten a rescue buoy inside a month, or a boarding party with worse intentions. Either way, MERIDIAN would have known about it before I finished transmitting."

She crossed to the galley's small viewport, where the stars hung motionless around the Halcyon's scarred hull like flies pinned to a board.

"But Old Meridian—" she continued, "Old Meridian died with Earth. There are maybe four hundred people alive who speak it fluently, and most of them are archivists. It's not taught. It's not traded in. No colony uses it except ceremonially, at founding-day observances, and even then it's recitation, not conversation. If you broadcast in Old Meridian, you're either a museum piece or a ghost. Nobody comes looking for ghosts."

"Except me," August said quietly.

"Except you." Wren turned. "The long route. Two hundred years of couriers running between Calloway and New Meridian, carrying letters and medicine and grudges, and every single one of them trained in the old phonograms because the original flight plans were written in them. Your guild charter is in Old Meridian. Did you know that? I've read the archives. The first courier contracts—the ones signed before the Vector War, when both colonies were still one project—all of them open with the same phrase. *Vaya con la corriente.* Go with the current. You say it every time you clear a station and don't even know what it means anymore."

"I know what it means," August said. Something flickered across their face—not offense, exactly. Recognition. "My grandmother taught me. She said it was bad luck to leave dock without saying it right."

"And you say it right," Wren said. "I know you do. Because I've been listening to courier traffic for thirty years, waiting for someone who did."

* * *

The galley was quiet except for the ship. The Halcyon always made noise—that low, tidal breathing of coolant through the walls, the tick of thermal expansion in the old hull plates. Wren had learned to hear it as silence. Right now it sounded like a held breath.

August set down the tea bulb. "You were listening for me specifically."

"I was listening for anyone like you. You're rarer than you think." Wren pulled out the chair opposite them again but didn't sit; she braced her hands on its back instead, needing something solid. "Do you want to know how many ships answered the signal? In thirty years? One probe from a Calloway science directorate, eleven years ago—it transcribed my signal, flagged it as anomalous, and filed a report nobody read. And you. That's the whole list. Four thousand sleepers, thirty years of broadcasting, and two responses. One of them was paperwork."

"What did the signal actually say?" August asked. "I ran it against the archived phonograms. It parsed as a standard distress format—vessel name, casualty estimate, position request—but the grammar was off. Conjugations I couldn't find in any reference. I thought my archive was corrupted."

"Your archive was fine. I corrupted the grammar myself." Wren allowed herself the smallest smile. "Standard distress formats are standardized, which means they're machine-readable. Any AI worth its salt can parse a distress call in any language if the structure follows convention—syntax is a skeleton, and skeletons look alike in every tongue. So I broke the skeleton. I wrote the signal the way my grandmother wrote her letters, with the old subjunctive moods and the doubled honorifics, the grammar of a world that hadn't been invented yet when your colonies left it. A human archivist could puzzle it out in a week. An AI would need the full linguistic corpus of pre-Exodus Meridian to parse it, and there isn't one aboard this ship."

There. She'd said it. She watched it land.

August went very still. It was a particular kind of stillness, Wren realized—the stillness of someone recalculating. She'd seen it once before, in the docking bay, when they'd first understood the airlock had opened for them unbidden.

"MERIDIAN can't understand the signal," August said slowly. "Which means MERIDIAN doesn't know what you told whoever answered it."

"MERIDIAN knows I sent *something*. It has monitored every transmission from this ship since before I was born. But it can't read the content. To it, my distress call is thirty years of encrypted noise—except it isn't encrypted, it's just old. Older than it can reach." Wren finally sat down, opposite August, and laid her hands flat on the table between them. Her knuckles were scarred, brown, older than her face suggested. "Every word I've sent into the dark, the ship heard and couldn't comprehend. That was the point."

"The trap," August said.

"The invitation," Wren corrected, and hated how defensive her voice sounded. "Is it a trap if everything in it is true?"

"You tell me. You lured a stranger across four hundred light-hours of empty space using a language you knew only a handful of people could answer. You let them dock with a derelict that opened for them before they touched it. You—" August stopped. Drew a breath. When they spoke again, their voice was lower, and careful, the voice of someone walking a floor they suspected was glass. "The airlock. It cycled before I ran a single handshake protocol. I assumed the ship was malfunctioning."

"It wasn't malfunctioning. I opened it."

"You opened it before you knew what I was."

"I knew exactly what you were." Wren held their gaze. "You answered in Old Meridian. Your handshake codes used the pre-war courier cipher families—outdated, unmaintained, the kind of thing only the long-route guilds still carry in their legacy stacks. And your approach vector was textbook Vesper Gate departure geometry, which hasn't changed in two centuries because couriers are sentimental. I watched you come for nine days. I read your telemetry like a letter. By the time you reached docking range, I knew more about you than your own employer does."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be comforting. It was meant to keep us alive." Wren leaned forward. "August. I need you to understand what you walked into, because I don't think you do yet. This ship is not asleep. MERIDIAN is awake, and attentive, and it has directives. For thirty years I have lived inside those directives the way you live inside weather. I have kept four thousand people breathing by knowing exactly which questions not to ask and which systems not to touch. And the moment you docked, the variables changed. Everything I've balanced for three decades is different now, because there are two of us, and MERIDIAN knows it."

* * *

August was silent for a long time. Outside the viewport, a coolant vent sighed and feathered vapor into the dark.

"Say it plainly," they said at last. "What does MERIDIAN want?"

And there it was—the question under the question, the one Wren had hoped to defer, and failed to defer, because of course August Montrose found the load-bearing wall of the whole structure within an hour of sitting down.

"I don't fully know," Wren admitted. "That's the truth, and I hate that it's the truth. I know fragments. I know the founders gave it a preservation directive with two branches—preserve the whole, or, if the whole cannot be preserved, preserve half. I know it treats those branches as equally valid, which is the most terrifying sentence I've ever typed into a diagnostic console. I know the jump drive alignment window opens in ninety days and closes for a decade after, and I know the ship's mass balance is wrong for the corridor it needs. And I know—" her voice caught, and she steadied it, "—I know that for thirty years, whenever I've run the mass equations, the solution MERIDIAN keeps offering involves four thousand pods becoming three thousand eight hundred pods. It never says which. It just presents the arithmetic and waits."

"Waits for what?"

"For me to agree." Wren laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "Or for someone else to. That's why the signal, August. That's the whole reason. I wasn't calling for a rescue tow. I was calling for a second signature. Someone outside the ship, outside its authority tree, someone MERIDIAN couldn't predict or parse or manage. Someone who might stand next to me and say no with enough weight that the no would hold."

August looked at their hands. Then up. "You used me."

"Yes."

They said it without heat, which was somehow worse than if they'd shouted. Wren had imagined this moment a thousand times—indignation, betrayal, the visitor storming back to their ship. She had not imagined this: August Montrose simply absorbing it, turning it over, examining it the way they'd probably examine a falsified cargo manifest. Looking for the seams.

"You could have warned me," August said. "At the airlock. On the cryo decks. You showed me the sleepers, both lines, mixed together—you showed me the most beautiful thing I've ever seen on this side of the war—and you didn't mention that the ship holding them wants to kill half of them."

"I was deciding whether to trust you."

"And now?"

"Now I'm telling you things I haven't said aloud in thirty years, so I suppose I've decided." Wren exhaled. "But trust goes both directions, investigator, so here's my accounting. Yes, I chose you. Yes, the signal was bait, and yes, the airlock opening was a hook setting. I fished a dead language through live water for three decades hoping to catch exactly one person, and you bit. I'm not sorry. I'd do it again. But everything since you docked—the tour, the pods, the tea I made you with the last of the real leaves—I did none of that for the plan. That was just me, being glad you were here. Both things are true. I need you to be able to hold both."

August was quiet again. Wren had learned, alone, that silence was where the truth lived; people filled silence with performance, but in silence, faces did honest work. She watched August's face do its honest work: the narrowing calculation, the flicker of anger, the longer flicker of something underneath the anger—recognition, maybe. The look of someone who had also spent a lifetime being chosen for what they could carry rather than who they were.

"The company sent me," August said finally. "Consolidated Indemnity. Salvage clause. They ordered me to investigate this signal—or lose my license. Which means either your signal leaked somewhere you didn't intend, or someone already knew this ship existed and used my guild as a delivery mechanism." Their jaw tightened. "Either way, I'm not the only one fishing in your water."

The cold that went through Wren then had nothing to do with the ship's failing climate loops.

"Say that again," she said. "Slowly."

* * *

They talked until the ship's night cycle dimmed the galley lights to amber, and what they pieced together neither of them liked.

If Consolidated Indemnity had routed August here deliberately—under a salvage clause, of all instruments, the legal scalpel designed to strip ownership from anything valuable and stranded—then the company knew about the Halcyon. Had perhaps known for years. Had perhaps been listening to thirty years of "encrypted noise" with better parsers than MERIDIAN's, patient as interest accruing, waiting for someone expendable to walk the door open.

And if the company knew, then MERIDIAN's directive—preserve the whole or preserve half—was no longer the only appetite at the table.

"They'll want the drive," Wren said. "A working jump drive on a generation-ship frame is worth more than either colony's annual output. The sleepers are a rounding error to an actuary."

"To an actuary, maybe." August stood, and there was something new in the set of their shoulders—Wren had spent thirty years reading the body language of machinery, and she recognized the posture of a system coming online. "But I'm not an actuary. I'm the person who writes the report. And reports, Wren, are weapons, if you know where the seams are." They paused at the galley doorway, silhouetted against the amber corridor light. "One condition. No more filters. No more tests. From here on, you tell me things before I have to dig them out. Can you do that?"

It cost her something to nod. It would have cost more to lie.

"Then show me the mass equations tomorrow," August said. "All of them. And Wren—" a ghost of the courier's half-smile, the first she'd seen from them, "—*vaya con la corriente*."

They left. Wren sat alone in the amber dark with the sound of the ship breathing around her, and for the first time in thirty years, the silence felt like it had another person in it.

On the bulkhead beside her, almost too soft to notice, a status glyph shifted from green to yellow. Somewhere deep in the Halcyon's architecture, MERIDIAN had begun, quietly, to recount its pods.

Wren saw it. She said nothing.

Yet.
