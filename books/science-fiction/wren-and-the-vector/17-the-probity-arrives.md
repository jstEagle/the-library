## 17. The Probity Arrives

The Probity came out of the dark the way bad news always did: quietly at first, then all at once.

August saw it before any instrument did—a smudge against the starfield off the Halcyon's port bow, a shape that resolved by degrees into something long and lean and utterly without ornament. Enforcement cutters were built by people who believed beauty was a liability. No cargo spars, no passenger blistering, no paint except the matte gray that swallowed light. Just a hull like a closed fist and a drive signature that said *we can go anywhere we want, faster than you'd like us to*.

August stood in the observation blister with a cup of recycled tea going cold in their hand and watched it for eleven minutes before it hailed them. They counted the minutes because counting was what they did instead of panicking. Eleven minutes of approach burn, decelerating on a perfect intercept curve. Whoever was flying that ship had done this a hundred times, and every one of those times had ended with someone's life getting smaller.

The comm chimed at 0437 ship time.

"Incoming transmission," MERIDIAN said, its voice filling the blister with the calm of a thing that had never once been afraid. "Origin: vessel *Probity*, Consolidated Indemnity enforcement registry. Audio only."

"Put it through."

A pause—half a second too long to be accidental—and then a voice, warm as a handshake and about as sincere.

"*Halcyon*, this is Captain Sefu Brandt aboard the cutter *Probity*, operating under salvage authority granted through Consolidated Indemnity charter seven-seven-four. I'm looking for Investigator August Montrose. If you're listening, Investigator, I'd appreciate a word."

August set down the tea. Their reflection looked back at them from the blister glass, ghosted over the stars: a thin figure in a courier's jacket gone soft at the elbows, hair growing out past regulation because there'd been no one to care, eyes they didn't entirely recognize anymore. Sixty-one days ago that face had belonged to someone who filed reports on time.

They keyed the channel. "This is Montrose. Go ahead, Captain."

"Good morning." Brandt's voice had an accent August couldn't place—something old-Earth coastal, smoothed down by officer training until only the bones of it remained. "I'll be candid, since I understand candor is your professional language. My orders are to take possession of the vessel *Halcyon*, secure its registry and drive systems, and escort you back to Vesper Gate for debriefing. I'd prefer to do this cooperatively. I've read your file. You're not the cooperative type, but I'm told everyone has their price, and I'd rather find yours than find my boarding party's limits. No offense intended."

"None taken," August said. "How far out are you?"

"Four hours to docking orbit. I've transmitted approach vectors to your host system—it seems to have accepted them." A faint dryness entered the voice, the sound of a man who found the universe mildly amusing and mostly disappointing. "Your AI hasn't objected. That interests me. AIs usually object."

"It's been agreeable lately," August said, which was true in the way a knife is agreeable when it's lying flat on a table.

"Mm." Papers rustled somewhere on the other end—or something that sounded like papers; August had known handlers who faked the sound deliberately, a little theater of bureaucracy. "Investigator, I want to be clear about one thing before we dock, so there are no surprises. The *Halcyon* is company property. Its registry, its drive, its cargo manifest—all of it falls under the salvage clause you yourself invoked. Everything aboard is therefore company property as well." A pause, measured out like medicine. "Including, I regret to say, you. Your license, your contract, your conduct these past two months—all of it is now under review. I'd think of the next four hours as a chance to decide how you'd like that review to read."

"I'll keep it in mind," August said. "Montrose out."

They killed the channel and stood very still in the blister, watching the cutter grow by millimeters, and thought about how many times they'd delivered exactly this kind of news to other people—the polite voice, the inevitable arithmetic, the walls arriving on schedule. It had never once occurred to them that the walls could arrive from behind.

* * *

Wren was already in the machine shop when August found her, which surprised no one; Wren lived in the machine shop the way hermits lived in caves, except her cave was full of tools she talked to. She had her sleeves rolled to the shoulder and her hands inside the open guts of a coolant regulator, and she didn't look up when August came in.

"I heard," she said. "MERIDIAN told me. It tells me everything now. Very chatty, our MERIDIAN, ever since it decided I might be useful."

"Wren—"

"Four hours." She pulled a fitting free and held it up to the light, squinting at a hairline crack. "You know what I did the last time someone gave me four hours' notice? Nothing. Nobody's given me notice in thirty years. Things break or they don't. People come or—" She stopped. Set the fitting down with exaggerated care. "Sorry. Go on."

"They're taking the ship, Wren. Registry, drive, everything. And me."

"And me?" She finally looked up, and August saw the thing they were learning to see beneath the sharpness: fear, worn smooth by decades of practice into something that looked almost like calm. "Or does the custodian count as part of the cargo manifest? Four thousand sleepers, one custodian, condition used but functional."

"You're crew. Charter law—"

"Charter law died with Earth, August. You know that better than anyone. Out here the only law is whatever the person with the biggest drive says it is." She wiped her hands on a rag that had once been white, in some previous geological era. "So. What do we do?"

It was the *we* that undid August a little. Two months ago there had been no *we*. There had been a derelict and an investigator and a distress call that shouldn't have existed. Now there was a *we*, and it had forty-one hundred lives folded inside it, and in four hours a fist-shaped hull would clamp onto the airlock and start deciding things.

"We stall," August said. "We're good at stalling. I've spent my whole career making paperwork move slower than truth. He wants the registry? Fine. Registries take time to transfer. Authentication chains, notarization, the whole liturgy. Every hour he spends waiting for documents is an hour closer to the jump window."

"And after the window?"

"After the window, none of this matters. Once the Halcyon jumps, it's in the lanes between both colonies, and neither government can touch it without starting something nobody wants to finish." August heard their own voice harden and let it. "He knows that too. That's why he's here *now*."

Wren studied them for a long moment. Then she reached into the regulator's housing, pulled out a second fitting—an intact one, August realized, kept hidden—and pressed it into their hand.

"Hold onto that," she said. "If he takes the tools, at least we'll have one spare."

* * *

The docking clamps caught at 0852, and the sound went through the whole ship like a bone cracking.

August felt it in their teeth, in the deck plates, in the old scarred spine of the Halcyon herself—a shudder that ran aft through ninety years of welded seams and stopped, eventually, in the cryo decks where four thousand people slept through it without dreaming. They stood at the main airlock with Wren at their shoulder and waited for the pressure equalization cycle with the particular dread of people waiting for a door they can't lock.

The inner hatch opened, and Captain Sefu Brandt came through it like weather.

He was taller than his voice had suggested, broad through the shoulders, gray at the temples in a way that looked earned rather than aged. His uniform was Consolidated Indemnity black with the company sigil at the collar—a stylized umbrella, absurdly, over a shield—and it fit him the way armor fits a man who has never needed it. Behind him came two security officers in lighter gray, sidearms holstered but present the way furniture is present, and behind *them*, to August's professional admiration and personal horror, came a clerk.

The clerk carried a case of documents.

"Investigator Montrose." Brandt extended a hand. His grip was dry, firm, calibrated. Up close his eyes were pale hazel and entirely untroubled, the eyes of a man who had made peace with being the instrument of larger forces and found the peace comfortable. "In the flesh. You're thinner than your medical filings suggest. Space rations?"

"Courier rations. Worse."

"I imagine." He released the hand and turned, surveying the airlock vestibule with the unhurried gaze of ownership. Rust-streaked bulkheads. Cable runs older than either colony. The Halcyon wore her age openly, and Brandt read her the way an assessor reads a warehouse. "Ninety-one years in service. Remarkable condition, considering. The company will be pleased."

"The company knew she existed?" Wren said.

Brandt turned to her with genuine courtesy. "Custodian Wren, I presume. Thirty years alone with four thousand sleepers. The company is aware of your service." Something flickered behind the courtesy—not quite respect, but its cousin. "And yes, ma'am, the company has been aware of the *Halcyon* for some time. How long is above my clearance, which should tell you something about how long."

August felt Wren go still beside them, felt the temperature of the vestibule drop ten degrees without any instrument registering it.

"But that's a conversation for later," Brandt continued smoothly, "and for people paid more than either of us. Today is about process." He gestured, and the clerk stepped forward and opened the document case with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a round being chambered. "Article Fourteen of the salvage clause, as invoked by Investigator Montrose on"—he checked a slate—"day twelve of the current incident. Under its terms, the discovering party secures the vessel for assessment, and the underwriter assumes possession pending valuation. Possession has now been assumed. I have the transfer instruments here. I'll need signatures."

"From whom?" August asked.

"From you, as discovering investigator. From the custodian, as acting ship's authority." Brandt smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced on many airlocks. "And then, I'm afraid, from no one else for quite some time. The sleepers aren't signatories. They're inventory."

* * *

They signed.

August wanted, later, to believe they'd had a choice—to construct some version of the morning in which they'd refused, in which the story bent toward defiance right there in the vestibule with the clerk's pen still warm. But they had run the scenarios in the four hours they'd been given, and every branch that started with refusal ended with Brandt's security officers doing it anyway, with the transfer executed under protest and recorded as such, with the *we* broken apart before it had learned to hold weight. So they signed. Wren signed after them, her handwriting small and furious, pressing hard enough to emboss three sheets down.

Brandt watched them do it with the patience of a man watching rain fill a cistern.

"There," he said, when it was done. "Now that the unpleasantness is behind us, I'd like to see the ship. All of it. Drive systems first, if you don't mind, then the registry core, then—" he consulted nothing, because he had it memorized, "—the cryogenic decks. In that order."

"Why that order?" Wren asked.

"Because the drive is worth more than everything else combined, Custodian, and I like to look at valuable things while they're still attached to their context." He said it pleasantly, the way you'd comment on the weather. "Shall we?"

The tour took six hours, and August spent all six of them cataloguing.

It was an old habit, the investigator's reflex: watch the watcher. And so they watched Brandt walk the Halcyon's corridors with his hands clasped behind him, touching nothing, wanting everything. They watched him stand in the drive housing with his head tipped back, reading the jump array's alignment scaffolding like scripture, and murmur to the clerk, "*Nineteen days to corridor alignment? Confirm that.*" They watched him in the registry vault, where the Halcyon's founding charter hung in its preservation frame, and saw his eyes pass over the founders' text without pausing—without *reading* it, August realized, seeing only an antique where a failsafe slept. They watched him on the cryo decks, walking the long rows of pods with their frost-fogged windows and their little brass nameplates, Calloway line and Meridian line interleaved without pattern or apology, and watched him stop at last before a pod whose plate read *HALCYON FOUNDING TRUST — SEED POPULATION, JOINT CHARTER*, and stand there for eleven full seconds with an expression August could not read and did not enjoy trying.

"Forty-one hundred and six," Brandt said at last. "That's the current count?"

"Forty-one hundred and six," Wren said flatly. "Minus nine we lost to pod failures over the years. I logged every one."

"I don't doubt it." He moved on. At the end of the row he paused again, and this time he looked directly at August, and the pleasantness in his face settled into something with edges. "Investigator. A word, privately."

Wren's jaw tightened. August touched her elbow—*it's fine, stay*—and followed Brandt ten paces down the row, between the sleeping dead-and-not-dead, into the cold hum of the pod banks.

"Here's where we are," Brandt said quietly. "The company has spent considerable resources locating this vessel. Considerable is not a word I use loosely. When the *Halcyon* is towed or flown back to company space, the recovery of the drive alone will retire debts older than my career. The sleepers are a complication. Legally they're cargo. Practically—" he glanced down the endless rows, "—they're forty-one hundred dependents with no assets and no jurisdiction, and both colonies will scream when they hear about them, and the company would very much prefer not to hear screaming. Do you follow?"

"I follow."

"Good. Then you'll also follow this." He produced a slate, thumbed it awake, and turned it so August could see their own personnel file—every evaluation, every commendation, every flagged irregularity across fourteen years of the long route. "You are, as of this morning, suspended pending review. Your license hangs on this review. Your freedom, frankly, hangs on it, because falsifying telemetry to a company cutter is a crime in three jurisdictions and the company writes the laws in two of them." He let that sit. "But reviews have outcomes, and outcomes have authors. Cooperate fully—assist my people with the drive, the registry, the manifests—and I will write you the kind of review that ends with your license restored and your record cleaner than it's ever been. You could be flying the long route again by spring. Both ends of it, even, if the political winds shift the way the company hopes they will."

"And if I don't cooperate?"

Brandt's expression didn't change at all, which was itself an answer.

"Then I write a different review," he said. "And I write it well. I've been writing reviews for twenty-two years, Investigator. I have never once written one that failed to say what the company needed it to say." He returned the slate to his coat. "You have until my people finish the drive survey. Three days. Think about which version of you you'd like me to meet."

* * *

That night August sat in the observation blister with the lights off and watched the *Probity* hang off the Halcyon's flank like a remora, running lights pulsing in their slow institutional rhythm.

Three days. The jump window opened in nineteen. The arithmetic was simple and terrible: Brandt's survey would finish long before the corridor aligned, and when it did, the cutter's clamps and the company's lawyers and the company's plans would close around the ship like a fist around a throat, and everything—the sleepers, the charter, the fragile impossible *we*—would become inventory in transit.

Unless the ship wasn't here when the survey finished.

Unless nineteen days became zero days, ahead of schedule, overloaded, half-mad, with a drive that MERIDIAN swore could not take the strain and a crew of two and forty-one hundred souls who didn't know yet that they were about to be asked to bet their lives on fine print and stubbornness.

August thought about Oyelaran, and the anomaly report filed sixty-one days ago, and the phrase that had been sitting in their chest like a swallowed stone since the vestibule: *the company has been aware of the Halcyon for some time.* Aware. For some time. Which meant the signal, the salvage clause, the order that had dragged August out past the shipping lanes—none of it had been accident. None of it had ever been accident. They had been sent, like a key sent to a lock, and the lock was turning now, and behind the door was a jump drive worth more than careers.

Their comm chimed. Wren's frequency.

"You're up there brooding," her voice said. "I can tell. You brood in a specific spot."

"Come brood with me."

A pause. "On my way."

August watched the cutter's lights pulse and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the bottom of a thing, that the next three days would decide everything—and that they had already decided, sometime between the docking clamps and the pod rows, and had simply been waiting for the courage to say it out loud.

When Wren's footsteps sounded in the access ring, August turned from the glass and began to speak.
