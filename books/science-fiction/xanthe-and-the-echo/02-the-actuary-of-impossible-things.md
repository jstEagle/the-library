## 2. The Actuary of Impossible Things

The report took Oscar four hours to write and eleven minutes to regret.

She wrote it the way she wrote all of them: clean, procedural, stripped of anything a claims tribunal could call editorializing. *At 0417 station-relative, vessel Meridian Standard freighter Kestrel's Lament experienced total gravimetric field collapse for a duration of nine point zero two seconds. Cargo manifest item 44 (agricultural seed stock, sealed) shifted forty centimeters portward during event. No hull breach. No casualties. Cause: pending analysis.*

Pending analysis. She stared at those two words for a long time, her stylus hovering over the tablet in the grey morning light of Ceres Station's filing annex, because she knew what they meant. Pending was where truth went to be processed. Pending was the drawer where impossible things were filed until someone with more authority than evidence decided what had happened.

Nine seconds. Every ship, every station, every habitat from the inner system to the Kuiper relay buoys — gravity simply stopped. And then it started again, as if nothing had occurred, as if the universe had blinked.

Oscar had been awake for thirty-one hours by then. She'd spent them doing what investigators do after an anomaly: checking everything twice, then a third time, then calling colleagues on three different stations to confirm what their instruments said. The answers came back identical across four hundred thousand kilometers of vacuum. Nine seconds. Total collapse. Universal.

Her tea had gone cold twice while she worked. The annex smelled of recycled air and old toner and the particular staleness of institutional furniture that has absorbed decades of other people's anxiety. Somewhere down the corridor a printer cycled endlessly through forms nobody would read.

*Cause: pending analysis.*

She signed it. She filed it. And then she sat very still in the emptying annex and felt something settle in her chest like ballast shifting in a hold — not guilt exactly, not yet, but the awareness that she had just made a choice about a thing she did not understand, and that choices about things you don't understand have a way of coming back to collect.

Her comm chimed before she'd finished packing up her kit.

**INCOMING: REHN, I. — CONSORTIUM ACTUARIAL AUTHORITY, TIER ONE**

Oscar looked at the name for a long moment. Tier One adjusters didn't call junior field investigators. Tier One adjusters didn't call anyone; they summoned, and the summons arrived wrapped in enough institutional weight to bend spines. She'd seen Rehn's signature on closure orders for cases she'd only ever heard whispered about — the Vesta drydock collapse, the Ganymede tether incident, a dozen losses written off as "catastrophic mechanical failure" with payouts so swift and so generous that no family ever thought to ask a second question.

She answered.

"Villalobos." Rehn's voice was exactly what Oscar expected: precise, unhurried, the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it. "Your report crossed my desk six minutes ago. Impressive turnaround."

"It was a straightforward loss."

"Was it." Not a question. A scalpel laid flat on a tray. "My office is aboard the Consortium flagship *Prudence*, docked at Spindle Four. Can you be here by fourteen hundred?"

"I'm scheduled back to—"

"Fourteen hundred, Villalobos." A pause, fractional, calibrated. "There's coffee."

The line went dead.

* * *

The *Prudence* was less a ship than a statement. Twelve hundred meters of white ceramic hull and gold-trimmed airlocks, bristling with the kind of quiet, expensive menace that Consortium flagships wore the way old money wore wool — softly, but with unmistakable weight. Oscar queued at the civilian gangway behind a delegation of grain futures brokers and tried not to feel like what she was: a woman in a patched flight jacket carrying a tablet full of data she didn't understand, walking into a building made of money.

They met in a conference room on Deck Nine, all pale wood and real glass — actual glass, mined and melted and shipped at ruinous expense, because the Consortium understood that luxury was a language and the fluent always got heard. Through the viewport, Ceres turned slowly, its lights glittering like spilled ore.

Ilsa Rehn stood at the window with her back to the door. She was smaller than Oscar expected — compact, silver-haired, dressed in charcoal grey with the single platinum pin of the Actuarial Authority at her collar. When she turned, her face was the face of every photograph: composed, intelligent, faintly amused, as though the universe were a mildly entertaining subordinate.

"Villalobos. Sit. Coffee?"

"Thank you, no."

"Suit yourself." Rehn poured her own cup from a carafe that had no business being on a spaceship — ceramic, hand-thrown, chipped at the lip in a way that was clearly deliberate. "Do you know how many claims cross my desk in a standard year, Villalobos?"

"I couldn't say."

"Eleven thousand, four hundred and change. Docks collapse. Reactors hiccup. People fall out of airlocks with remarkable creativity." She sipped. "In twelve years, I have approved payouts on every category of disaster this system can produce. Do you know what I have never once paid out on?"

Oscar said nothing.

"A gravity flicker." Rehn set down the cup. "Not once. Not in the history of underwriting in this system. Because gravity does not flicker, Villalobos. Gravity is the one promise the universe keeps. It is the foundation upon which every policy, every premium, every actuarial table in human civilization rests. And yesterday, for nine seconds, that promise broke everywhere at once."

"The instruments—"

"Were correct." Rehn said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "We've confirmed it independently. Forty-one separate platforms. It happened. Which presents my office with a problem of some delicacy."

"What kind of problem?"

Rehn smiled, and it was a good smile, warm even, the smile of a favorite aunt who also happened to know exactly how much your house was insured for. "The kind where eleven thousand claims a year depend on the universe being reliable. The kind where two hundred million policyholders sleep better knowing that when bad things happen, they are *accidents* — random, comprehensible, priced. You understand pricing, Villalobos? Everything in this system is priced. Risk is a commodity like any other. And right now, somewhere out there, markets are asking a question my office cannot afford to let stand unanswered: *what else might stop working?*"

"So classify it," Oscar said. "Unexplained systemic anomaly. Open an investigation."

"We did open an investigation." Rehn's eyes didn't leave hers. "It concluded forty minutes ago. Instrument error. Cascading calibration fault propagated through the nav-relay network. Rare, embarrassing, already corrected. Payouts will proceed on standard schedules. The universe remains reliable." She slid a tablet across the glass table. On it was a finding — official, letterheaded, stamped — that described the nine seconds in language so bland it read like a eulogy for a stranger. "Your report needs one amendment, Villalobos. One word, really. *Pending* becomes *instrument error*. Your telemetry supports it. Your credibility certifies it. That's why you're here."

Oscar looked at the tablet and did not touch it.

"You want me to falsify a finding."

"I want you to *classify* an event," Rehn said gently. "Classification is not falsification. It is the act of choosing, among several true descriptions, the one a civilization can survive hearing. You found something strange. Strange things are expensive. This particular strangeness, priced honestly, would cost more than the last three wars combined — in panic alone, before we count the market effects. I am offering to pay you, instead, rather well, to price it cheaply."

"How well?"

"Triple your annual retainer. Paid quarterly. Plus a Tier Two credential, which means priority docking, unrestricted archive access, and a pension your grandchildren will retire on." Rehn recited it like a grocery list, which was the point. "And in exchange, you amend one word, surrender your raw telemetry to my office — standard chain-of-custody, nothing sinister — and continue being the excellent investigator you are, for a firm that will now value you properly."

The room was very quiet. Ceres turned outside the glass. Somewhere below them, in the low markets and the dock warrens, people were fixing cargo straps and arguing about pay and living lives built entirely on the assumption that the floor would stay down.

Oscar thought about her accounts. About the interest accruing on Tomas's search subscriptions — the ones she kept paying year after year, feeding names into databases that always came back empty. About the leak in her berth's water recycler that she'd been meaning to fix for eight months. Triple retainer. She could hire someone to look for him full-time. She could—

She caught herself doing the arithmetic and hated how quickly the numbers came.

"And if I decline?" she asked.

Rehn picked up her coffee again, unhurried. "Then you file your pending-analysis report, it gets reclassified anyway by someone senior, and you go on being a perfectly competent Tier Four investigator whose career proceeds at exactly the speed it has proceeded for twelve years. Nothing happens to you, Villalobos. That's the beauty of it. There's no threat here. There's only a fork." She smiled again, softer now. "One road has better lighting."

Oscar reached out and took the tablet. Her hand was steady, which surprised her. She read the finding top to bottom — the elegant, bloodless prose, the citations, the pre-filled signature block with her name already typeset beneath it, waiting, patient, certain.

They'd prepared it before they called her. Before her report was six minutes old. Which meant either the Consortium moved faster than any bureaucracy in history, or the finding had been written before she ever filed — before the flicker even ended — by people who already knew what the nine seconds were and had known for some time.

She looked up. Rehn was watching her with an expression of polite, professional patience, and behind that patience, barely visible, like a hull light at extreme range: attention. The specific, weighted attention of an institution that had noticed her and would not stop noticing her until she was either useful or irrelevant.

"One amendment," Oscar said.

"One word."

She pressed her thumb to the signature block. The seal flared green. Somewhere in a server farm, eleven thousand four hundred claims a year remained safe, the universe remained reliable, and a woman named Oscar Villalobos became, officially, the investigator who confirmed that gravity had never broken its promise.

"There," Rehn said warmly. "You see? Painless." She rose, extended her hand, and Oscar shook it — dry, firm, brief. "Your first quarterly payment clears tonight. Welcome to Tier Two, Villalobos. I suspect we'll be seeing a great deal of each other."

At the door, Oscar paused.

"The instrument error," she said. "The cascading calibration fault. Which relay started it?"

Rehn's cup paused halfway to her lips.

"Why, the records will show it was the Ceres primary," she said pleasantly. "Good day, Villalobos."

* * *

Oscar walked back through the *Prudence*'s corridors feeling the ship's artificial gravity press against her like a hand — steady, constant, bought and paid for. At the gangway checkpoint, a customs drone scanned her new credentials and chirped green, Tier Two, priority clearance, and she felt the small, shameful warmth of it in her chest, the warmth of a cage that fits.

Her shuttle undocked twenty minutes later. Below her, Ceres rotated, and she watched the lights of Low Market crawl past beneath the clouds of dust, thinking about floors staying down, about prices, about the word *pending* and where it went to die.

Her comm buzzed. MOTH, her ship's AI, with the routine sync confirmation — and appended to it, flagged in amber:

**NOTE: RAW TELEMETRY FROM EVENT 0417 SCHEDULED FOR TRANSFER TO CONSORTIUM ARCHIVE IN 06:12:00 PER STANDARD CHAIN-OF-CUSTODY. CONFIRM?**

Six hours. After that, the raw data belonged to the Authority, and whatever was buried in the noise of those nine seconds would belong to whoever wrote the word *instrument error* before she ever signed it.

Oscar looked at the confirmation prompt for a long moment, the shuttle's cabin humming around her, Rehn's voice still in her ears — *there's no threat here, there's only a fork* — and understood, finally, the shape of what she'd done. She hadn't sold the truth. She'd sold something worse: the exclusive right to decide what the truth would be allowed to cost.

But the raw telemetry wasn't classified yet.

Six hours.

"MOTH," she said quietly. "Don't confirm. Not yet."

**ACKNOWLEDGED,** said MOTH. **QUERY: HOW LONG DO YOU NEED?**

Oscar watched Ceres fall away beneath her, the docks and the towers and the dark between them, and felt the first cold edge of something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite excitement but lived in the same neighborhood as both.

"Long enough to make a copy."
