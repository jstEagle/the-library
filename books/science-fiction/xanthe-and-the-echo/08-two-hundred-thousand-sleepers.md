## 8. Two Hundred Thousand Sleepers

The summons came at 04:12 ship-time, which was how Oscar knew it wasn't a summons. Real invitations arrived during business hours, wrapped in courtesy the way cargo arrived wrapped in foam. This one arrived in the dead hours, when the towers of Ceres Station turned their faces away from the Belt and pretended to sleep, and it did not ask. It stated.

*Ms. Villalobos — please present yourself at Consortium Adjustments, Spire Level 40, at your earliest convenience.*

Her earliest convenience. She read the phrase three times and heard, underneath its blandness, the click of a lock.

MOTH's voice filled the cabin of her ship, the *Ledger*, soft as dust settling. "You could decline."

"I could." She pulled on her jacket, the good one, the one with the lining she'd stopped wearing after her third year investigating contested orbits because it made people think she had money. "Declining is a thing licensed investigators do exactly once."

"And then?"

"Then we find out what they do with the once."

She took the lift up through forty levels of sleeping station, watching the deck numbers scroll past like a countdown she hadn't agreed to. The Spire levels were a different country. The air smelled different up here — filtered twice, perfumed faintly with something green that didn't grow anywhere within four hundred million kilometers. The floors were real wood, or something engineered to remember being wood. Her boots, scuffed from the docks, left small confessions on the polished surface.

Ilsa Rehn's office occupied the corner of the fortieth floor, and its windows looked down on the rotation of Ceres itself, the cratered gray face turning slow and patient below like something dreaming. Rehn stood at those windows when Oscar entered, hands clasped behind her back, a slim woman in a charcoal suit cut so precisely it seemed less tailored than grown.

"Villalobos." She didn't turn. "Do you know what I see when I look out this window?"

Oscar said nothing. In her experience, questions like that were not questions.

"Two hundred thousand tons of rock," Rehn said, "and underneath it, eleven million people who believe the gravity holding them to the floor is a law of nature rather than a service we provide. Belief is infrastructure, Villalobos. It has to be maintained." Now she turned, and her face was pleasant in the way of well-maintained machinery. "Sit, please. Would you like tea? It's actual tea. From Earth stock."

"No, thank you."

"Suit yourself." Rehn sat behind her desk and folded her hands. On the desk's surface, a display woke without being touched. "Let's talk about instrument error."

* * *

The footage began without preamble.

It was grainy — dock-level surveillance, low light, the kind of camera that lived in the seams of places that didn't want to be seen. Oscar watched herself walk into frame, twelve days younger, wearing the same jacket. She watched herself stop at a table in the back of a noodle bar two levels under Low Market, where the air tasted of steam and solder. She watched Dov Kessler rise to meet her, his coat too good for the neighborhood, his smile too easy.

The audio was clean. That was the part that made her stomach drop — not that they'd found her, but that they'd been close enough to hear every word. Kessler's voice, warm and conspiratorial: *Three other ships recorded the same handshake, Ms. Villalobos. All three crews are dead.* Her own reply, flat with the effort of staying flat: *Dead how?*

Rehn let it play. She didn't watch the screen; she watched Oscar, and her attention had weight, the way a hand on the back of your neck has weight.

On the screen, Kessler leaned forward. *The Meridian Compact would very much like to compare notes.*

The footage froze there, both their faces caught mid-conspiracy, lit blue by the noodle bar's cheap signage.

"Twelve years," Rehn said. "That's how long you've been with us. Twelve years, four commendations, a closure rate in the top decile. You closed the Halcyon drift case. You closed the Vesta rail incident that three other investigators called unresolvable." She paused. "You have a reputation for finding truth in noise. It's why this is so disappointing."

"Is it?" Oscar kept her voice level. "Because from where I'm sitting, you've been surveilling an independent contractor in a public establishment, which suggests you already knew what I'd find before I found it. Disappointment usually comes as a surprise."

Something flickered across Rehn's face — not anger. Appreciation, maybe, thin and cold as frost on a viewport. "There it is. That's the mind I hired you for." She tapped the frozen image. "Here's what happens now, and I'll be precise, because precision is a courtesy I extend even to people who don't deserve it."

She stood and walked around the desk, perching on its edge, close enough that Oscar could smell her cologne — expensive, mineral, like rain on stone that had never known rain.

"The event of nine seconds ago — and yes, we're calling it an event, because calling it anything else invites questions — has been classified as cascading gravimetric sensor failure across no fewer than forty platforms. Instrument error. A mundane, embarrassing, forgettable malfunction. Your report will reflect that classification. Your raw telemetry will be surrendered for archival verification. And your recent social activities—" she gestured at the frozen screen, "—will be understood, going forward, as what they appear to be: an insurance investigator enjoying a bowl of noodles."

"And if my report reflects something else?"

Rehn smiled. It reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Then we arrive at the second item on today's agenda." She touched the desk again, and the display changed.

It was Oscar's license. Her investigator's credential, rendered in official gold-and-slate: *VILLALOBOS, O. — CLASS ONE ACCIDENT INVESTIGATOR — CONSORTIUM CHARTER AUTHORITY.* Below it, a status field. Currently it read ACTIVE. Rehn's finger hovered over it.

"Do you know what you were before you were this?" Rehn asked softly.

Oscar knew. She'd been a girl on Mars with a brother who counted stars and a mother who worked triple shifts in the atmosphere processors. She'd been hungry in the specific way that orbital kids were hungry — surrounded by wealth moving overhead in tin cans, none of it touching down. The license hadn't just been a career. It had been a door out of a life where doors opened outward into vacuum.

"A person," Oscar said. "But go ahead."

"A person," Rehn conceded. "And this—" she tapped the credential, "—is what that person became. Class One. Do you know how many Class Ones there are in the system? Forty-one. Forty-one people trusted to say what really happened when impossible things happen. You spent twelve years earning a place in a room with forty chairs, Villalobos. I'm offering you the chance to keep it."

"For a signature."

"For a signature," Rehn agreed pleasantly. "The cheapest thing you'll ever sell and the most expensive thing anyone will ever buy."

* * *

Oscar stood and walked to the window. It was a move she'd learned from watching executives, and it worked: it put distance between them and gave her somewhere to put her eyes that wasn't Rehn's face. Below, Ceres turned. The lights of the docking rings glittered along the equator like a string of cheap beads — Auntie Vex's market down there somewhere, the noodle bar, the welders and salvagers and stevedores whose accidents she'd spent a decade honestly cataloging while the towers above her quietly decided what honesty was allowed to mean.

"Tell me about the other ships," she said.

"There are no other ships. There is instrument error, and there are conspiracy theorists, and one of them bought you noodles."

"Three crews are dead, Rehn. The *Cormorant*'s pilot burned up in a reentry window that had been clear for six hours. The *Meridian Rose*'s engineer died in a coolant flash that the maintenance logs say couldn't happen. And the third—" she'd spent two sleepless nights pulling the records, "—the third doesn't even have a name attached anymore. The registry entry was scrubbed. I checked. Someone got to the archives."

For the first time, Rehn's composure showed a seam. Small. A hairline crack in good porcelain. "You've been busy."

"That's what you pay me for."

"What I pay you for," Rehn said, and her voice cooled by degrees, "is closure. Clean endings. The system runs on certainty, Villalobos — do you understand that? Eleven million people on this rock alone need to believe that the floor will hold, that the air will stay, that the accidents we investigate have explanations and the explanations have ends. If I let one investigator tell them the sky flickered for nine seconds because a ghost ship eighty years dead sent us a handshake—" she shook her head slowly, "—you wouldn't be starting a scandal. You'd be starting a religion. And religions burn things."

"You think I haven't thought about that?"

"I think you've thought about everything except what you actually saw." Rehn came to stand beside her at the window, and for a moment neither spoke. The planet turned beneath them, indifferent and gray. "Nine seconds. You know what I did during those nine seconds? I was in a meeting on the thirty-eighth floor. My tea floated out of my cup. A pen drifted past my face. And then it ended, and everyone laughed nervously, and someone made a joke about budget overruns in the gravitics division, and we went back to work." She turned to look at Oscar directly. "I felt it too. I'm not asking you to pretend it didn't happen. I'm asking you to help me make sure it never happens again."

"That's not what you're asking."

"No," Rehn admitted. "It isn't. What I'm asking is smaller and larger. I'm asking you to choose which world you live in. The one where the signal means something and every faction in the system tears itself apart fighting over it — or the one where it meant nothing, and two hundred thousand people keep sleeping soundly, and you keep your chair among the forty-one." She held up a hand before Oscar could speak. "Before you answer — and you don't have to answer today — consider what's actually at stake. Not your license. Consider this."

The display shifted again. Oscar braced herself for more surveillance, more leverage, another dead crew.

Instead: a manifest fragment. Partial, corrupted, watermarked with the archive stamp of a shipping company that had ceased to exist before Oscar was born. Most of the lines were static. But one line resolved clearly enough to read:

*VILLALOBOS, T. — cryo berth 7-C — STATUS: ACTIVE*

The floor of Oscar's world tilted, though the gravity field in the office never wavered.

"Tomas," she said. Her voice came out strange, distant, like it belonged to someone else standing in the room.

"We pulled the Echo's partial manifest from the same telemetry you've been hoarding," Rehn said, and there was no cruelty in her tone, which was somehow crueler than cruelty would have been. "We know what it means to you. Your brother disappeared when you were nineteen. You filed four missing-person inquiries over six years. You stopped filing." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Why did you stop, Villalobos?"

"Because hope hurts," Oscar said, before she could stop herself, and hated the words the instant they left her mouth.

"Yes," Rehn said quietly. "It does. So here is my offer, and understand that it is the only offer, and that it expires." She returned to the desk and sat, folding her hands, the picture of reasonable authority. "Classify the event. Surrender the telemetry. And I will personally guarantee that when — *if* — the Echo is ever recovered by legitimate authority, Tomas Villalobos's pod moves to the front of any revival queue that exists. He wakes first. He wakes healthy. He wakes into a system that still functions, because you helped keep it functioning." She tilted her head. "Or refuse. Keep your principles. Chase your ghosts. And when they revoke your license — and they will, because I will recommend it — remember that revoked investigators lose more than credentials. They lose dock access. Financial accounts. Legal standing to board vessels, subpoena logs, open cases. Everything you've built for twelve years, gone in an afternoon. And your brother stays asleep in the dark, in a ship that may already be a tomb, and no one — no one — will ever go looking for him but you. Alone. Unlicensed. Unfunded."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, a freighter fired its thrusters, and the window shivered with a vibration too faint to feel and too deep to ignore.

"You've done this before," Oscar said finally. "This speech. This exact architecture of threat and gift."

"Variations on it. Yes."

"How many times?"

Rehn considered her, and something almost human passed behind her eyes — fatigue, maybe, or memory. "More than I'd like. Fewer than you'd think." She slid a data chip across the desk. Blank, unlabeled, innocent. "The report template is on there. Take a week. Think about your brother's face. Think about whether he'd want you to be right or whether he'd want you to be smart." She stood, signaling the audience was over. "He's been asleep eighty years, Villalobos. He can wait a little longer. Can't he?"

Oscar took the chip. It weighed nothing. Things that weigh nothing are always the heaviest.

At the door, she stopped. "One question. Off the record."

"If you like."

"The third ship. The one with the scrubbed registry. Did you scrub it, or did someone above you?"

Rehn's expression didn't change, but the temperature of the room dropped several degrees. "Get some rest, Ms. Villalobos. You look tired."

* * *

The lift down took eleven minutes. Oscar spent all of them staring at her own reflection in the brushed-metal door, seeing a woman she half-recognized: twelve years of small compromises layered over a girl from Mars who used to count ships leaving for the outer dark and wonder if any of them carried her brother.

Tomas. Berth 7-C. Active.

*Active.* Eighty years in the cold and the word still meant alive, meant possible, meant that somewhere in the black between here and nowhere, her brother's heart was beating sixty times a minute inside a machine that had promised his mother — had promised *her* — a new world.

They'd handed her the one thing in the universe she wanted most, wrapped around the one thing she couldn't do, and called it a choice.

The lift reached the dock levels. The doors opened onto noise and steam and the smell of hot metal and frying onions, the honest stink of the working Belt, and Auntie Vex's market humming two corridors down. Oscar stepped out, the blank chip burning in her pocket like a coal.

A week, Rehn had said.

She gave herself until nightfall. Because MOTH had flagged something else in the handshake protocol — a repeating structure, buried deeper than coordinates, deeper than the manifest — and if the Echo was talking, then somebody aboard was awake enough to listen.

And Oscar Villalobos had never once, in twelve years, let a witness die unheard.
