## 10. The Betrayal Option

The call came at 4:17 in the morning, which told Sebastian everything before Marsh said a word.

Marsh never called early. Early was for emergencies, and Marsh did not believe in emergencies — he believed in schedules, in contingencies, in the slow grinding machinery of the Consulate that turned crises into paperwork and paperwork into silence. A call at 4:17 meant the machinery had jammed somewhere, and someone above Marsh wanted it unstuck by breakfast.

Sebastian sat in the kitchen of the Bratislava safehouse with his phone face-down on the table, watching it buzz against the wood like something trapped. Across the room, Ilse slept on the couch under a wool blanket, one hand curled near her mouth, her breathing even. She'd decoded the last of the photographs at midnight — bank routing numbers threaded through shell companies in Nicosia and Tbilisi, all of them terminating in an account whose beneficial owner was redacted in every document except one, where the initials *M.C.* appeared like a signature.

Meridian Consulate. His own house, selling access to men who moved rifles in crates marked as agricultural equipment.

The phone stopped buzzing. Started again.

He picked it up on the third ring because not answering would be its own answer.

"Blackwood." Marsh's voice was exactly what it always was: dry, unhurried, faintly amused, the voice of a man who had already read the ending of whatever story you were about to tell him. "You're up."

"I'm up."

"Good. I hate leaving messages." A pause, the sound of paper turning. "I have good news. I'm authorized to offer you full reinstatement. Records restored. The incident expunged — properly this time, from the top down. Your Vienna flat reactivated, your accounts unfrozen, your standing returned to what it was two years ago. Everything you've spent the last twenty-four months trying to buy back with compliance."

Sebastian closed his eyes. In the dark behind them he saw the ledger page Ilse had shown him, the shipment manifest, the eleven names of the crew, ten of which had lines drawn through them in some analyst's careful hand. "That's generous."

"It's earned," Marsh said. "There's a condition, and you're going to pretend to be surprised, so let's skip that part. Varga. Deliver her by dawn. There's a location — I'll text it after we speak. A debriefing suite, very comfortable, medical staff on site if she needs calming. You bring her in, you walk back into your life. You don't—" the paper turned again, "—and I'm authorized to make certain things official that are currently only true in unofficial ways."

"Such as?"

"Your deviation. Bratislava. The forged papers. The fact that you've been sitting on evidence of an active operation while drawing a Consulate stipend." Marsh let that sit. "None of that exists yet, Sebastian. Files are written by people. I'm offering you the chance to be the person who writes yours."

* * *

Afterward he sat with the phone in both hands, staring at the message that had arrived: an address in Petržalka, a timestamp of 06:00, a single line beneath it — *Alone means alone, Blackwood. We'll know.*

We'll know. Three syllables doing the work of a surveillance apparatus.

He got up quietly and stood at the window. The street below was wet with pre-dawn rain, orange sodium light pooling in the gutters. Somewhere out there Lark was watching — he could feel it the way you feel weather coming, a pressure behind the ears. And somewhere beyond Lark was whoever Lark answered to, the unnamed principal whose patron's name sat buried in Ilse's photographs, the man who needed a dead courier to pin a live scandal on.

They weren't going to debrief Ilse. They were going to bury her twice — once in a file, once in a grave — and they were going to use Sebastian's hands to do it, because a betrayal committed by the man she trusted would close every question forever. No inquiry. No journalist asking how a courier died in custody. Just a clean narrative, authored by the same institution that had erased him.

And they were offering him his life back as payment.

The obscene thing — the thing he made himself look at directly, because Marsh had taught him that lies only worked when you audited your own first — was that part of him wanted it. Not the reinstatement itself. The *ending*. Two years of sleeplessness, of checking mirrors, of being a man with a hole where a memory should be. All of it could stop. He could hand over a woman who trusted him, walk into a building, and be whole again by lunchtime. The arithmetic was almost beautiful in its simplicity.

Almost.

He thought about her brother. Tomas Varga, twenty-six, radio operator, dead somewhere along the Danube bend with nine other people whose deaths had been converted into a budget line. He thought about the way Ilse had said his name last night — not grieving anymore, past grief, into something harder. *Resolve*, he'd have called it in a report. He wasn't writing reports anymore.

He went back to the table and did what he did best. He prepared.

* * *

Marsh called again at 5:02, punctual as a train.

"You haven't confirmed receipt of the address."

"I received it." Sebastian kept his voice level, slightly tired, the voice of a man wrestling with himself. That was the performance — not confidence, but conflict. Marsh distrusted easy yeses; they smelled of rehearsal. "I'm looking at her right now."

"And?"

"She sleeps like someone who hasn't slept in years," Sebastian said. "Do you know what she was doing before that? Decoding your operation. Your routing numbers, Marsh. Nicosia. Tbilisi. She has copies in three places I don't know about, and probably a fourth I do. If she wakes up in a room with medical staff instead of walking into it on her own feet, those copies move. Hague, Brussels, wherever journalists still answer their phones at dawn. You get a corpse and a headline. Is that what the principal wants on the morning news?"

Silence on the line. Long enough that Sebastian heard the office behind Marsh — a chair creaking, distant traffic on some capital street.

"You've been reading my files," Marsh said finally, and there was no anger in it, which was worse. There was something almost like pride.

"I've been reading *our* files. That's the problem, isn't it? They're the same thing until they aren't."

"Don't philosophize at me, Sebastian. It doesn't suit you. You were built for execution, not commentary." A breath. "The copies are a solvable problem. People who hold copies are also solvable problems. What isn't solvable is the clock. Dawn is dawn. After six, decisions get made above my level, and the people making them are less patient than I am, and considerably less fond of you."

"Then give me until eight."

"No."

"Seven."

"What is this?" Marsh asked, and now there was genuine curiosity under the dryness, the sound of a man leaning forward. "You've never negotiated in your life. You've never stalled, never bargained, never once asked me for time. Twenty years of clean execution and suddenly you want seven hours. Either you've grown a conscience overnight, or she's already gone and you're managing me."

Sebastian felt the trap close around the sentence and stepped through it the only way left. He laughed — short, bitter, real enough to cost him something.

"She's here, Marsh. Asleep on my couch. I could put a bag over her head and be at your address in forty minutes, and we both know it, and that's exactly why I'm not going to do it that way." He let the laugh die. "Here's what I know about debriefings with medical staff on site. I ran extractions for fifteen years. I know what a suite with a nurse looks like versus what it looks like when the nurse is there to certify sedation levels. So I'm going to bring her in — I said I would consider it and I'm considering it — but I need the morning because she won't come scared, and a scared courier burns documents. Give me until seven and she walks in calm, hands over everything voluntarily, and your principal gets a cooperative witness instead of a martyr. That's worth two hours. Even to you."

Another silence. When Marsh spoke again, his voice had changed register — softer, almost gentle, which from Marsh was the most frightening sound available.

"You're lying to me."

"I'm negotiating with you. There's a difference."

"There used to be." Papers shuffled. "Seven o'clock. Not a minute past. And Sebastian — I've known you since you were a young man learning which end of a lie to hold. You were never any good at deceiving me. Don't start now; you'll only embarrass us both."

The line went dead.

* * *

Sebastian set the phone down and discovered his hands were shaking — not from fear but from the sheer unfamiliarity of it. Twenty years of telling the truth to power, ugly truths delivered cleanly, and it had taken him one phone call to discover that lying fluently was a skill he'd been carrying unused all along, like a blade sealed in grease.

He'd lied four times in five minutes. Ilse was asleep — false, she was awake, he'd seen her eyes open a slit when the second call came, listening, trusting him to shape the morning. Copies in three places — invented, though she'd fix that within the hour. Voluntary surrender — fiction. And underneath all of it, the largest lie, the load-bearing one: *I'm considering it.*

He wasn't considering anything. He'd decided somewhere between the first ring and the second, in the space where the machinery offered him his old life priced at one woman's pulse, and he'd found the price obscene.

But Marsh knew him. That was the wound in all of it. Marsh had trained the truthfulness into him, and Marsh would be listening for the exact frequency of its absence. Seven hours wasn't a concession. It was a leash measured to the length of Sebastian's known habits — long enough to feel free, short enough to hang him.

Which meant the seven hours were worthless unless he used them to become someone Marsh didn't know.

He crossed to the couch. Ilse's eyes were open now, fully, watching him through the dimness.

"He offered you your life back," she said. Not a question.

"He offered me a receipt for a life I sold them two years ago." He sat on the arm of the couch, careful, the way you approach something that might bolt. "Dawn. Petržalka. Alone means alone."

"And you told him—"

"I told him I'd deliver you by seven. Calm and voluntary." He met her eyes. "It's the first lie I've ever told him. I intend to make it expensive."

Ilse studied him for a long moment, and he braced for the question he deserved — *why should I believe you now, when everyone else who promised me safety ended up dead or bought* — but what she said was quieter and worse.

"The address he sent. Petržalka." She pushed the blanket back and reached for the photographs spread on the table, her finger landing on one he'd looked at a dozen times without seeing it: a loading dock, gray and anonymous, timestamped fourteen months ago. "I know that building. Tomas described it once. It's not a debriefing suite, Sebastian. It's a transfer point. Whatever they're calling it, nobody walks out the far side."

Outside, the rain had stopped. The sky over the Danube was going the color of gunmetal, and somewhere in it — on a roof, in a stairwell, behind glass — Lark was already moving toward the same arithmetic.

Seven hours. Less, now. Sebastian picked up the phone and began deleting the morning.

* * *

By 6:40 they were gone — not fled, nothing so legible as fleeing, but dispersed into the city's early mechanics: Ilse on a tram heading east with the dossier split across her coat lining and a bakery bag, Sebastian on foot toward Petržalka, visible, findable, exactly where Marsh expected him to be. Bait walking willingly onto the hook, buying her the one commodity dawn wouldn't otherwise allow.

His phone buzzed at 6:52. Marsh, presumably, savoring the punctuality.

It wasn't Marsh. The number was unknown, the message four words long:

*He knows about the split.*

Sebastian stopped in the middle of the bridge, the river sliding gray beneath him, and understood that somewhere between the couch and the tram, the machinery had already swallowed his seven hours whole — and that the person warning him was the last person alive he'd ever expected to warn him at all.
