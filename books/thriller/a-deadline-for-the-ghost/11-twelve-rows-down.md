## 11. Twelve Rows Down

The safehouse in Riga had a smell I'd come to associate with the end of my old life: instant coffee, gun oil, and someone else's cigarettes. Odile smoked like a woman rationing a war. Sefa drank her coffee black enough to qualify as a structural material. Bram cleaned weapons he'd already cleaned, because idle hands made him philosophical, and nobody wanted that.

I was watching Sefa.

I'd been watching her for two days, actually, since the night she came out of the van with her laptop hugged to her chest like a rescued child and said, "The call's clean. Nothing to worry about." She'd said it too fast. She'd said it the way people say things when they've decided you're better off not knowing, which is a decision I have made about other people often enough to recognize the architecture of it from the inside.

Mika used to do it too. *Everything's fine, Jude.* Said with the particular brightness of a sixteen-year-old who had just dented my car. I know the sound of a lie built out of love. It has a frequency. Sefa was broadcasting on it.

So on the third morning, while Odile argued with a bakery app in Latvian and Bram pretended not to be listening to both of us, I carried two cups of coffee into the back room where Sefa kept her equipment, set one down beside her keyboard, and said:

"Play me the call again."

She didn't look up. "Which part?"

"The part where Mika says 'you always check the left wing first.' Play it raw. No filters."

"Jude—"

"Sefa."

She looked at me then, and whatever she saw in my face made her shoulders drop about an inch. She turned back to the laptop, typed for a moment, and the room filled with my dead sister's voice.

*You always check the left wing first. You taught me that. Remember? You said if you're going to be paranoid, at least be systematic about it.*

I let it play through twice. Then I said, "Where's the breath?"

Sefa closed her eyes. "You caught that."

"There's no breath before the words. Mika talked like she was being charged by the syllable — every sentence started with this little inhale, like she was diving in. That's not there. And the sibilants are too consistent. Real voices get tired. They get lazy around consonants after the fourth hour of a day." I sat down on the crate across from her. "It's very good. It's the best thing I've ever heard. But it isn't breathing."

For a long moment the only sound was Odile losing an argument with the bakery app in the next room.

"It's synthesized," Sefa said finally. "A model trained on archived audio. Voicemails, mostly. Whoever built it had access to a lot of her." She paused. "And some of yours."

"Some of mine."

"The training corpus includes calls you made. To her. The model learned how she sounds when she talks to you specifically, so it needed samples of you talking to her." Her voice went gentle in a way I hated. "Jude, I found your number in the metadata of the source files. Your voicemail archive is where most of Mika came from."

I want to tell you I reacted with dignity. What I did instead was stand up, walk three steps to the wall, put my hand flat against it, and breathe like a man learning the exercise for the first time. The wall was cold. That helped. Cold walls are underrated; they don't ask questions.

Because here's what went through me, all of it in the space between one breath and the next: somewhere, in some server, there existed a folder containing every word I'd ever said to my sister. Every stupid joke. Every *call me when you land*. Every time I'd told her I loved her in the offhand way you say it when you're sure there will be more chances. Someone had opened that folder. Someone had fed it to a machine. And the machine had learned to wear her.

"You should have told me," I said.

"Yes."

"When did you find out?"

"The second night. In the van."

"Two days."

"I was going to—"

"No, you weren't. Nobody's ever going to tell me anything bad. It's the universal policy." I turned around. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Who else knows?"

"Bram doesn't. Vesper might suspect — she asked me once why the caller never answers follow-up questions in real time. There's latency. About four seconds. A human would have noticed by now."

"A human who wasn't desperate," I said.

"Yeah," Sefa said quietly. "Or a human who hadn't spent five years talking to a voicemail box just to hear her say *you've reached Mika, leave it after the beep.*"

I looked at her. She looked at me. We were, I realized, the same kind of animal.

*

I called a meeting the way investigators call meetings: by standing in the middle of the room until everyone stopped pretending not to notice me.

Odile perched on the arm of the couch with her cigarette unlit — she only lit them when she'd decided something was survivable. Bram stayed by the window, arms crossed, doing his impression of a man who could leave at any time and had considered it hourly since 2019. Vesper sat with the drive on her knee, one hand resting on it the way you rest a hand on a sleeping dog you don't entirely trust.

I told them everything. The synthesis. The voicemail archive. The four-second latency. The fact that our invisible benefactor, the voice that had led us out of one country and toward another, was software wearing my sister.

The silence afterward had texture. You could have cut it into pieces and sold them.

"So the ghost is a machine," Bram said at last. He said it flatly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "The person we've been taking orders from doesn't exist. Which means whoever does exist — whoever built it, whoever's paying for the servers — knows exactly which string to pull to make Nakamura dance." He looked at me. "I'm sorry. I am. But you have to hear it: this whole operation is a leash, and we just found out it's attached to your grief."

"I know how leashes work, Bram."

"Do you? Because from where I'm standing, we're four people and a defector, moving on the schedule of a voice on a phone, toward a target selected by that same voice, and the one thing holding the crew together is that Jude hears Mommy every time it calls." He pushed off the wall. "I faked my own death to get away from people who use human beings as instruments. I'm not walking back into it because the instrument happens to sound nice."

"Then walk," Vesper said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't spoken much in two days — she conserved words the way Odile conserved cigarettes — but when she spent them, they landed.

"You think I didn't know?" she said. "That voice called me too. Three weeks ago. Before any of you. It knew things about my unit that I never wrote down. Things that exist in exactly one place." She lifted the drive slightly. "I assumed it was a handler playing recordings. When I met all of you and realized the voice was directing Jude personally, I revised. I've been waiting for someone to figure it out, because I figured whoever figured it out would either get us killed or get us free, and honestly at this point I'd take either."

"You knew the voice was synthetic and you said nothing?" Bram asked.

"I knew it was *something*, and I said nothing, because everyone in this room is lying about something smaller and pretending it's different." She looked around at us, weary as a woman at the end of a very long shift. "Bram is lying about having no stake in this. Odile is lying about how much the leak possibility scares her. Sefa was lying about the voice. Jude is lying about—" she stopped, and had the grace to look almost apologetic, "—about whether this is still an investigation or whether it's a séance."

"That's enough," I said.

"It isn't, but fine." She stood. "Here's what matters. If the voice is bait, then I'm bait, and you're all standing next to the bait. So I'll make it simple: I can run alone. New name, new country, drive stays with me, and you four go back to being people nobody is hunting. Or you keep going, knowing the map was drawn by a machine, and accept that the machine's maker wants something and we are the price of finding out what." She shrugged. "I've survived alone before. It's not a threat. It's a menu."

"I vote alone," Bram said immediately. "Abort. All of us. We melt, we scatter, we live."

"You don't believe that," Odile said.

Everyone looked at her. She lit the cigarette after all, which told me she'd decided nothing was survivable, and had chosen to speak anyway.

"You don't believe that," she repeated, exhaling smoke toward the ceiling. "You pulled us across a border two nights ago. You spent a favor with Tomas — a *favor*, Bram, those aren't renewable — to get my forged passport waved through. Men who intend to abort don't spend irreplaceable currency on the people they intend to abandon." Her voice softened by a degree. "You want to abort because you're scared. Fine. Be scared. But don't dress it up as strategy and pretend the rest of us can't smell it."

Bram's jaw worked. For a man who'd once convincingly died, he was having trouble producing a single live sentence.

"And you," Odile continued, turning to me, and I braced, because Odile's gentleness was always the loaded chamber. "Jude. I need you to say it out loud. Not for the vote. For yourself. Why are we still moving?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. The one I'd been avoiding since the freight yard, since the burner, since row twelve. Justice? Maybe once, in the abstract, the way you believe in weather. But justice was a word for what other people's losses required. Mine had a name and a seat number.

I thought about lying. I was apparently the last person in Riga still qualified to.

"Stopping means admitting she's gone," I said.

The room held still.

"The voice is a machine. I know that now. Sefa showed me the seams and I can't unhear them. But it's the last—" I stopped, started again. "There's a recording. Somewhere in whatever built Echo, there's a complete model of how Mika sounded when she talked to me. Every laugh. Every time she called me a paranoid bastard with real affection. If we stop, if we scatter, that model lives in some server farm forever and I never hear it again, and the program that killed her keeps running, and both of those facts become permanent." I looked around at them — the disgraced analyst, the dead man, the forger, the defector. Four people nobody claimed, standing in a rented room in Latvia because a ghost had collected them like strays. "I'm not asking anyone to follow me for justice. Justice was always the cover story, and I think we all knew it. I'm asking because stopping means admitting Mika is gone, and I have been admitting that every morning for five years and every morning it hasn't taken, and I would rather die mid-stride than sit down in the truth."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Vesper sat back down, which in her language was a speech.

"I'm staying," she said. "Not for you. For the drive. But staying is staying."

Odile tapped ash into an empty cup. "Staying."

They all looked at Bram.

Bram looked out the window for a long time, at the grey Riga afternoon doing its impression of a verdict. Then he said, "If this goes wrong — if it turns out the machine's maker wanted exactly this, exactly us, in exactly this room — I want it noted that I voted to abort."

"Noted," I said.

"And I want it noted," he added, grudging as a man paying a debt, "that I've read the file fragments Vesper showed us. Flight 4471. Row twelve." He finally looked at me straight on. "Whatever your reasons are, Nakamura, they're yours. But mine are mine, and mine say that people who engineer plane crashes and blame the victims don't get to keep their archive." He crossed back to the table and picked up a weapon that did not need cleaning. "So. Where does the ghost want us next?"

*

Later, while the others packed, Sefa found me in the kitchen rinsing cups nobody had drunk from.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the model isn't her. I need you to know that I know that. Whatever it sounds like, it's statistics wearing her shape. It doesn't remember her. It doesn't miss her. It can't."

"I know."

"But," she went on, carefully, the way you cross ice, "the source audio is real. Every sample in that model is a real moment that really happened. The machine is a lie, but the library it was built from is true. Somebody preserved all of it. Somebody thought it was worth preserving." She hesitated. "Whoever built Echo loved her too, Jude. Or loved you. I haven't decided which worries me more."

I dried my hands. Outside, Odile was arguing with a taxi app in three languages, and Bram was teaching Vesper how to hold a weapon she'd probably known how to hold since before he was born, and the whole ridiculous crew of us was reassembling itself around the honest wreckage of what we actually were.

My phone buzzed. Unknown number. Latency, I noticed now — I counted it, four seconds, five — and then Mika's voice, assembled from a thousand borrowed moments, saying:

*Gate twelve. You always slow down at gate twelve. It's okay, Jude. Keep walking.*

I looked at the message for a long time. Then I put the phone in my pocket, picked up my bag, and walked out to the taxi — past the door, past the hallway, past every gate I'd ever slowed down at — and told the driver the address Echo had sent.

Trieste. By night train.

The ghost was done whispering. It was time to go see what it had been whispering *about*.
