## 6. Internal Affairs Smells Smoke

The email arrived at 7:42 in the morning, which was how Ada knew it was bad.

Corliss didn't send early emails. Corliss sent emails at ten, after his second coffee, when the building had warmed up and the day had revealed its shape. An email at 7:42 meant he'd been at his desk since six, which meant something had kept him up, or something had woken him, and both possibilities pointed like a compass needle at the same account number.

*Subject: Halden & Roe — Ref. 4471-C. My office. 0900.*

No pleasantries. No "when you have a moment." Ada read it twice, set her phone face-down on the kitchen counter, and looked out the window at the gray light coming up over the rooftops of her neighborhood. Somewhere down there, people were starting their days without knowing that a single line of text could reorganize a life.

She made coffee she didn't drink. She dressed with the care of someone arming herself — the charcoal blazer, the flat shoes, nothing memorable, nothing vulnerable. Fifteen years of practice at looking like exactly what her file said she was: Ada Eastwick, senior investigator, financial crimes division, twenty-two commendations, zero disciplinary actions, a woman whose reputation was built on the simple, brutal proposition that rules were rules no matter who broke them.

Especially me, she thought, and locked the door behind her.

* * *

Corliss's office smelled of leather polish and old ambition. He stood at the window when she came in, hands clasped behind his back, watching the traffic on the avenue below as if it were a tide he personally commanded.

"Eastwick." He didn't turn around. "Close the door."

She closed it. Sat without being asked, because sitting uninvited was a small assertion of equality and she wanted every millimeter of ground this morning.

He turned. He was sixty-one and wore his age like a uniform — silver at the temples, reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, a face built for committee rooms. People underestimated him because he was polite. Ada had spent fifteen years not underestimating him.

"Tell me about account 4471-C," he said.

"Halden & Roe, private client division. Flagged in my routine audit of their correspondent flows. The funding source is a burner mobile registered to a shell — Ashgrove Holdings, incorporated in Malta, dissolved in—" She stopped herself from reciting the whole chain. Reciting too fluently was its own kind of tell. "It's in my preliminary memo."

"I've read your memo." He sat down across from her, folding his hands on the blotter. "What I'm asking is why you flagged it."

Ada let a beat pass. Not long enough to seem evasive. Long enough to seem thoughtful. "Because the funding pattern matches a legacy typology from the Vann case. Prepaid instrument, layered through three jurisdictions, dormant for years, then activated within ninety days of a summit-related transaction. It's either coincidence or it's Meridian rehearsing."

"And you recognized the pattern." His eyes were mild. Mildness was his weapon; she'd watched him end careers with the gentle tone of a man discussing weather. "From memory. A case file fifteen years old."

"The Vann case was my case. I don't need the file."

"No," Corliss agreed softly. "You wouldn't."

The silence stretched. Outside, a siren rose and faded somewhere below, a small drama neither of them turned to watch.

"You've been carrying that case alone for a decade and a half," he said. "Every time Meridian surfaces — a fragment here, an echo there — it's your name on the referral. Your analysis. Your instinct. Do you know what that looks like from the outside, Ada?"

"It looks like persistence."

"It can look like obsession. Or ownership." He removed his glasses, cleaned them on his tie with slow precision, put them back. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I want to be clear about that. I'm telling you how it will look if this goes where I think it's going."

"Where do you think it's going?"

"The summit." He said it plainly, and the plainness landed harder than any accusation would have. "Eleven days out, and a dormant account tied to a dead man's case wakes up inside the host bank's clearing corridor. If that's Meridian, we get one shot at it, in front of forty delegations and every camera on earth. And if we miss—"

"We won't miss."

"You said that fifteen years ago." He held up a hand before she could answer. "That wasn't fair. But you know why I said it."

She knew. Everyone in the division knew. Elias Vann had walked out of a briefing room on a Tuesday afternoon with a folder under his arm and had never been seen again, and the investigation had died with him, and Ada Eastwick had spent the rest of her career becoming the kind of investigator who could never be accused of walking out of anything.

"I'm assigning support," Corliss said.

Something cold moved down Ada's spine, slow as syrup. "Support."

"A junior analyst. Priya Nair. Top of her cohort, clean security history, no prior exposure to the Vann material." He slid a single sheet of paper across the desk. Her photograph was clipped to it: a young woman with dark hair pulled back, dark eyes that even in a personnel photo seemed to be measuring something. "She'll shadow your work on 4471-C. Independent verification. Fresh eyes."

"Fresh eyes," Ada repeated. "Or a witness."

Corliss smiled, and it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "In this building those are usually the same thing."

* * *

Priya Nair was already at the desk they'd given her — one row over and slightly behind Ada's own, close enough to read her screen over her shoulder if she leaned, far enough to pretend she wasn't — when Ada came back down at nine fifty.

"Ms. Eastwick." Priya stood, hand extended, and her grip was firm and brief. Up close she was younger than her photo, mid-twenties, with the particular alertness of someone who had never yet learned to hide how much she noticed. "Deputy Director Corliss briefed me. I'll try to stay out of your way."

"That would defeat the purpose," Ada said.

"Yes, ma'am." No flinch. Just the acknowledgment, filed away. "I've read your memo on the Halden account. The burner registration is interesting — the activation date falls eleven weeks before the summit's host-bank contract was signed. That's not rehearsal timing. That's positioning."

Ada looked at her. Eleven weeks. She'd noted the same interval herself at two in the morning, alone, and had told no one, because the interval meant the account hadn't been opened in response to the summit. It had been waiting for it.

"You did that math in four hours," Ada said.

"It's not hard math once you see the shape." Priya hesitated, then, with the directness that was clearly going to be either her best quality or her fatal one: "Can I ask you something? Off the record, which I know doesn't exist, but — off the record anyway."

"Ask."

"Why does everyone go quiet when the Vann case comes up? I've been here eight months. I've heard the name maybe five times, always in lowercase, like people are afraid of waking it up."

Ada turned back to her monitors. On the screen, the ledger of account 4471-C glowed in neat columns, every decimal accounted for, every figure innocent except for what it meant.

"Fifteen years ago," she said, "an investigator named Elias Vann disappeared during a Meridian Trust inquiry. No body. No defection on record. No explanation. Half the division thought he ran. Half thought he was killed. Nobody thinks about it anymore because thinking about it doesn't help."

"And you were his partner."

"For two years before that."

"What was he like?"

Ada's hands were perfectly still on the keyboard. She had rehearsed this answer for fifteen years in front of mirrors, in front of review boards, in front of herself at three in the morning, and it had worn smooth as river stone.

"He was the best investigator I ever worked with," she said. "And the worst liar. Which is probably why he's gone."

Priya wrote something down. Ada didn't turn to see what.

* * *

The day went sideways at 2:17 in the afternoon, when Ada requested the archived Vann files through the proper channel and watched the request bounce back in ninety seconds.

*ACCESS RESTRICTED — AUTHORIZATION LEVEL: DIRECTORATE.*

She sat very still. She had pulled those files a hundred times over fifteen years. They lived in deep storage, catalogued, dusty, hers by right of authorship. Now they required directorate sign-off — meaning Corliss, or someone above Corliss, had sealed them sometime between last night's email and this afternoon.

Someone had moved first.

She walked the request upstairs in person, because paper trails cut both ways and she wanted Corliss to feel her footsteps. His assistant waved her straight in.

"They're my files," Ada said, standing, because he gestured at the chair and she declined it. "I wrote half of them."

"And now they're evidence," Corliss said, not unkindly. "Precautionary integrity hold. Standard procedure when an active case touches a historical one. You taught the division that rule, Ada. Chapter and verse."

"My own rule."

"Your own rule." He laced his fingers on the desk. "You see the problem. Everything anomalous about this account routes through you. Your audit found it. Your memory contextualized it. Your history frames it. In eleven days, if this goes public, the first question every journalist and every delegate is going to ask is: who is Ada Eastwick, and what does she know, and when did she know it? I'd rather answer that question with a clean process than a wounded reputation."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning Nair verifies your findings independently. Meaning the archive stays sealed until the verification's done. Meaning nobody — including you — gets to touch the old case without a second signature." He paused. "Including me. I signed the order myself so you'd hear it from me and not from a memo."

There it was. The courtesy was the cage. He was being gentle with her the way you're gentle with a suspect you like.

"Is there anything else?" he asked.

Ada thought about a midnight phone call from a dead man. About a voice fifteen years unchanged saying *eleven days, Ada, give me eleven days.* About a ledger in a floor safe under her sister's name.

"No," she said. "That's everything."

* * *

She worked late. The floor emptied around her in stages — the nine-to-fivers, then the ambitious, then the true believers, until only the hum of the servers and the blue wash of her monitors remained. Priya left at seven, but not before Ada caught her photographing the whiteboard with her phone, quick and casual, the way people photograph things they intend to study later.

At 9:40, Ada opened a blank document and began writing the memo she would never send: a private chronology, dated, precise, the habit of a lifetime. *Request for archive denied. Integrity hold ordered by D.D. Corliss, same day anomaly reported upward. Analyst assigned without consultation. Timeline of seal unknown.*

Then she stopped, cursor blinking, and faced the sentence underneath all the others, the one she'd been stepping around all day:

*Either Corliss is protecting the case, or Corliss is protecting himself, and I no longer have a way to tell the difference.*

Fifteen years ago she'd have known. Fifteen years ago she'd have walked into his office and read his face like a balance sheet. But fifteen years ago she'd had a partner to compare notes with, a second set of eyes, a man who was the worst liar she'd ever met and therefore the easiest person in the world to trust.

Her burner phone sat in her bag, silent, inert, a dead thing that had spoken to her once at midnight.

She didn't take it out. There were cameras in the ceiling corners and a junior analyst who photographed whiteboards, and whatever happened next, it would not happen inside these walls.

At 10:15 she saved nothing, printed nothing, and went home the long way, changing trains twice, watching reflections in the dark windows for the shape of anyone following.

Behind her, on the eleventh floor, the lights over Priya Nair's desk stayed on past midnight.

And in the morning, pinned under the wiper blade of Ada's windshield, in handwriting she hadn't seen in fifteen years but would have known blindfolded, was a note on a card the color of old paper:

*They sealed the archive because I'm in it. Fish market, Saturday, dawn. Come alone — they'll be counting on you not to.*
