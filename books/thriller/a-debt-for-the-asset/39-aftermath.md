## 39. Aftermath

The apartment smells like Nadia's cooking, which is how Ada knows she's been here long enough to stop being a guest.

Three weeks since the summit chamber, since the ledger flooded forty delegate screens in a cascade of white text that made grown men in ten-thousand-dollar suits look like they'd been caught in their underwear. Three weeks since Corliss was walked out of the chamber in handcuffs, his face doing something complicated between disbelief and arithmetic — she'd watched him calculate, even then, how many years, which wing, whether his pension survived. Three weeks since Elias.

Ada has stopped measuring time in weeks. She measures it in the small units now: the time it takes the kettle to boil. The time it takes Nadia to stop pretending she isn't watching her sister across the table. The time it takes for the notebook on the shelf to stop being a thing she looks at and becomes a thing she looks through, the way you stop seeing a picture that hangs in the same place every day.

She has not stopped seeing it yet.

"More?" Nadia asks, ladle hovering over the pot like a question mark.

"I'm fine."

"You said fine an hour ago. You've been saying fine since 2004." Nadia sets the ladle down and sits across from her, folding her hands the way she does when she's about to be unbearable, which is to say, right. "You cleared your desk out of a federal building. You gave up your badge, your clearance, and your entire identity as a person who catches bad guys. You did it in a room full of international delegates with cameras rolling. And now you're sitting in my kitchen eating my soup and saying fine."

Ada looks at the soup. Orzo, lemon, chicken. Nadia has made it since they were children, since the winter their mother worked doubles and Nadia learned to feed them both out of necessity that hardened into habit. The surgery money had come from somewhere else. They had never talked about that, and then, in the hospital corridor two months ago — the second dinner, the one where Nadia had said *I always knew something saved my life, I just never asked what it cost* — they had started to.

"I'm not fine," Ada says. "But I'm not not-fine either. There should be a word for it."

"There is. It's called Tuesday."

Ada laughs. It surprises her, the laugh, coming up out of her chest like something that had been holding its breath. Nadia smiles like she's been waiting three weeks for exactly that sound and would have waited three more.

The kettle clicks off. Nadia gets up, makes tea the way Ada likes it — too strong, milk first, an abomination their mother had defended to her grave — and sets the cup down in front of her without asking. This is what Nadia does. She has spent her whole life doing the next small necessary thing while Ada did the large impossible ones, and only in the last month has Ada understood which of them had the harder job.

"Priya called," Nadia says, settling back down. "She's been promoted."

"I heard. Section chief. Thirty-one years old."

"She said you didn't answer her call."

"I texted. Congratulations, don't let them make you Corliss."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Ada turns the cup in her hands. "She followed the money when I told her not to. She went rogue when going rogue could have ended her career, or worse. She did it because the evidence told her to and because — I think — because she decided I was worth the fall. You don't thank someone for that with a text message. You thank them by getting out of their way while they do the job better than you ever did."

Nadia is quiet for a moment. Then: "You know that's not how she sees it."

"I know how she sees it. That's the problem. She sees it as a debt. I spent fifteen years learning what debts do to people."

* * *

The hearing had lasted four days.

Ada had gone in expecting to be dismantled. She had laid it all out in her opening statement anyway — the account in 2009, the cartel payment, the nineteen-year-old with a sick sister and a ledger she'd kept for fifteen years like a splinter working its way toward the skin. She had said the words *I laundered money* in a formal inquiry room with her old colleagues watching, and the strange thing, the thing she still turns over at three a.m., is how light she had felt saying it. Fifteen years of carrying it, and the carrying had been the weight. The confession was just setting it down.

They had cleared her. *Cooperation in extremis, disclosure voluntary, no ongoing liability.* The lawyer had translated it afterward: they couldn't prosecute her without prosecuting the institution that had missed it for fifteen years, and institutions do not commit suicide for the sake of a nineteen-year-old's sister. Justice, Ada had learned, is mostly bookkeeping.

She had resigned the same afternoon. Corliss's deputy — a gray, decent man named Okafor who had spent the inquiry looking like a man finding water damage in a house he'd just bought — had offered her Corliss's chair. The irony was not lost on her. She'd declined in writing, one paragraph, no adjectives.

Priya had come to the apartment that evening, still wearing the lanyard of someone who belonged to an institution, holding a bottle of wine that was too good for the occasion.

"You're really leaving," Priya had said. Not a question.

"Someone should be on the outside watching the inside. It's the only seat I've never had."

"You could have had any seat you wanted."

"That's what Corliss thought too." Ada had opened the wine. "That's the whole disease, Priya. Everyone thinks the seat is the point."

Priya had stayed until midnight. They had not talked about the case. They had talked about Priya's mother's garden, about a man Priya was seeing who cooked, about whether Ada would finally learn to rest. It was only at the door, coat on, that Priya had stopped and said, quietly, the thing she'd clearly come to say:

"He bought you the minutes. Everyone in that chamber knows it. The record shows a service corridor breach, an armed response, a delay of eleven minutes and forty seconds. Eleven minutes and forty seconds." She had looked at Ada with those clear analyst's eyes. "I ran the numbers. That's not a delay. That's a man spending himself."

"I know," Ada had said.

"I just wanted you to know that someone counted."

* * *

The notebook sits on the shelf where she can see it from the kitchen table. Elias's notebook — no. That's not right anymore. Her notebook, the one that vanished from her desk fifteen years ago along with the man, returned to her at a fish market by a dead man who looked older and kept his weight on his left leg. It had come back with pages she never wrote, forged entries building her into Meridian's mole, and for one terrible week she had believed the whole object was a weapon aimed at her.

Then, in the safehouse, in the arithmetic of each other's lies, Elias had shown her the truth of it: the forged pages were the frame, and beneath them, in a pocket sewn into the back cover by someone who'd had fifteen years to think about it, were the pages he truly wrote.

She has read them eleven times now. She knows she should stop counting. She counts anyway.

They are not what she expected. She had expected — she doesn't know what. Confession. Tradecraft. A dossier. What she got was a man's handwriting getting worse over fifteen years, entries that start as surveillance logs and slowly, entry by entry, become something else. Dates she recognizes now: the day Meridian's accountants moved money through a hospital charity in Zurich, and beside it, in his hand: *She would have caught this. Check if she caught it.* The day he learned her sister's name from the old file. The day he wrote, in a hand pressed so hard into the paper it nearly tore: *I did not fake my death to save myself. I did it because they had her ledger and I could not take it from them and live. I chose the version where she stayed free.*

And near the end, undated, the ink faded as if written and rewritten and written again:

*If you are reading this, I ran out of time. Don't carry me like a case. Carry me like a Tuesday.*

She had laughed when she read that — a wet, cracked sound in an empty apartment — because he had known. He had known her well enough to know that grief would try to make him a case file, an unsolved problem, a thing to be worked. And he had left instructions against it.

She gets up, takes the notebook down, and reads the last entry again. It's dated the morning of the gala. It says only:

*Whatever happens in there, the eleven days were the best ones I've had since before. That's not a line. Check my other entries. I don't write lines.*

* * *

The anniversary of nothing in particular arrives on a Thursday, which is to say, an ordinary day, which is to say, the hardest kind.

Nadia has started staying over more. She doesn't ask. She just appears on Thursdays with groceries and an opinion about Ada's curtains, and Ada has stopped pretending she doesn't notice the pattern. Her sister, who spent her whole life not asking what her life had cost, has decided that the price is paid now and the account is closed, and the only thing left to do is show up.

They are on the couch. The television murmurs the summit inquiry's closing summary — Meridian Trust dissolved, assets frozen in eleven jurisdictions, Aurel Kastel still unlocated, a warrant with his face on it in every airport that matters. Sable Renner's name has not appeared in any of it. Ada thinks about that more than she should. Women like Sable Renner do not vanish; they relocate.

"Turn it off," Nadia says. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're reading a room that isn't there."

Ada reaches for the remote. Before her hand gets there, the burner phone rings.

Not her personal phone. The burner. The one from the fish market, from the eleven days, the one that rang at midnight fifteen years' worth of grief ago. It sits in the drawer by the door where she put it after the funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral, and it is ringing now, in the drawer, in the quiet apartment, on a Thursday.

Nadia goes still beside her. "Ada."

"I hear it."

"Is that—"

"It's the burner."

They look at each other. The phone rings. It does not ring the way phones ring in movies, urgently, portentously. It rings like a phone. That's what makes it terrible.

Ada crosses the room. Opens the drawer. The screen says UNKNOWN, and beneath that, a number she does not recognize, and she stands there with her thumb over the answer icon while Nadia watches from the couch with her whole heart in her face.

She thinks about Elias's last entry. *Don't carry me like a case.* She thinks about Sable Renner, relocated but not gone. She thinks about a wrong number, a ghost, a dead man's network dissolving into the world one burner at a time, someone's phone plan auto-renewing into oblivion.

She answers it.

There is a silence on the line. Not dead air — a silence with a person's weight in it, the particular quality of someone breathing carefully, deciding. Ada says nothing. She has spent thirty years knowing that whoever speaks first in a silence has told the other person something.

Ten seconds. Fifteen.

Then the line clicks, and it's gone, and the screen says CALL ENDED, and Ada stands in her kitchen holding a dead phone on a Thursday afternoon while her sister watches her with her whole heart in her face.

"Wrong number," Nadia says, but it's a question wearing a coat.

"Maybe." Ada sets the phone on the counter, screen up, where she can see it. "Maybe not."

She looks at it for a long moment — the small dark rectangle, the ordinary object, the possibility humming in it like a wire. Then she turns back toward the couch, toward her sister, toward the soup and the tea and the Tuesday of her new life, and she finds — and this surprises her more than the call did — that she is smiling.

Not because she believes he's alive. She's an investigator; she believes evidence, and the evidence says eleven minutes and forty seconds, and she counted too.

But because whoever it was — a ghost, a stray, a wrong number, a man who doesn't write lines — had let it ring long enough to be heard. And somewhere in the last three weeks, without noticing the exact moment it happened, Ada Eastwick had stopped carrying a secret and started carrying a memory, and a memory can survive a phone call. A memory can survive almost anything.

"Come eat," Nadia says. "It's getting cold."

Ada goes. The phone stays on the counter, screen up, quiet, patient.

She leaves it there.
