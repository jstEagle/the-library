## 26. Betrayal Signal

I have a rule about gifts: nobody gives you something for free unless they want something, and nobody leaves equipment behind unless they want you found. It's the same instinct that keeps me from picking up coins on the sidewalk and from trusting men who say "trust me." Twenty years of forensic accounting will do that to a person. You learn that every number has a motive.

So when we swept Sable's recovered gear — the tactical vest, the suppressed pistol, the comms unit she'd abandoned in Rotterdam when she switched sides — I was the one who insisted we do it properly. Not a pat-down. A teardown. Every seam, every lining, every battery compartment opened like an oyster we suspected of hiding a pearl.

Tomas did the vest. Gideon took the pistol. I took the comms unit, because electronics are just money with wires, and money is my native language.

For three days, nothing. We sat in the staging site — a defunct fish-packing warehouse outside Ghent that smelled like brine and old decisions — and I picked the unit apart under Tomas's magnifying lamp while he argued with hospital Wi-Fi from his bed forty kilometers away and Gideon paced the perimeter like a man auditioning for the role of Worried Leader.

Then, on the third night, I found it.

* * *

It was wedged inside the housing of the GPS module, a component so small and so ordinary that even I almost logged it as factory hardware. But factory hardware doesn't have hand-soldered joints. Factory hardware doesn't hum at a frequency that makes your fillings itch. And factory hardware definitely doesn't come wrapped in a Faraday sleeve made of foil and electrical tape — which meant someone had built this thing to sleep until it was unwrapped.

"Tomas," I said, very calmly, because calm is what I do instead of screaming. "Come look at this."

"I'm in a hospital bed, Mara, not a taxi—"

"Come look at this."

The screen went quiet for four seconds, which for Tomas is roughly the equivalent of a fire alarm. Then: "Photograph it. All sides. Don't touch the contacts. And Mara?"

"Yes?"

"Tell no one else yet. Not even Gideon. Especially not Gideon. He'll want to confront her immediately, and if we're wrong about who planted it, we'll be showing our whole hand to whoever's watching."

I looked down at the little silver parasite in my palm and felt the temperature of the room drop ten degrees.

Because there was only one "her" to be wrong about.

* * *

Sable slept in the loft above the loading office, alone, by choice, with a knife under her pillow and her boots within reach. She'd been with us eleven days. Long enough to save our lives twice — once in Lisbon, once in the corridor outside Metz — and not nearly long enough for anyone to stop flinching when she moved quickly.

I climbed the ladder at two in the morning without announcing myself, which in retrospect was either brave or suicidal, and found her already awake, already sitting up, knife loose in her hand before my head cleared the floorboards.

"You should make noise," she said. "If I'd been asleep, you'd be missing an arm."

"If you'd been asleep, you wouldn't have heard me anyway." I held up the evidence bag with the transmitter inside. "We need to talk."

She looked at the bag for a long moment. Her face did something complicated — a flicker of recognition, then something colder underneath it, something that might have been fear if fear were allowed to live on that face.

"That's not mine," she said.

"It came out of your gear."

"No." She set the knife down deliberately, a gesture I recognized as calculated but appreciated anyway. "It didn't come out of my gear. It came out of the gear they gave me after Rotterdam. There's a difference."

"Explain the difference slowly, because I'm an accountant and I audit everything twice."

She swung her legs off the cot. "My original kit was mine. Bought, assembled, maintained by me, over nine years. Nobody touches my kit. When Wray's people pulled me off the Rotterdam job — or thought they did — I went dark and left most of it behind. What I carried out was a go-bag I grabbed from a cache in Liège. A cache I didn't stock."

"You're saying Wray resupplied you."

"I'm saying someone did. The rifle was a different caliber than I use. The comms were a model I've never liked. I noticed, and I assumed it was budget cuts, because contractors always assume budget cuts first and betrayal second." Her mouth twisted. "Occupational habit. Apparently I have the same blind spot you do."

"So you're telling me you suspected nothing."

"I'm telling you I noticed everything and rationalized all of it, which is worse, and which I would have flagged days ago if I weren't busy trying to convince three people who hate me that I've changed employers." She stood, crossed to the window, checked the yard out of pure reflex. "Where exactly was it?"

"GPS module. Comms unit. Wrapped in a Faraday sleeve so it wouldn't transmit until we powered the unit up."

Something shifted in her posture. "When did you power it up?"

"First night. Standard check."

"And it's been transmitting since."

I hadn't let myself do that math yet. Now I did it, and hated every digit. Three days. Three days of a beacon singing our location into the dark, and us sitting in one building, sleeping in shifts, believing the walls meant something.

"We move tonight," I said. "Now, actually. Now works too."

"Mara." She turned from the window. "I need you to hear this, and I need you to believe it, because in about six hours Gideon is going to ask me the same question and I'd rather rehearse on you. I did not plant that device. I have never planted a tracking device in my career, for the simple reason that trackers get found, and found trackers point back at the finder. If Wray wanted me dead, he had cheaper options than a beacon. If he wanted *you* found, he didn't need me — he had my contract file, my last known movements, my psychological profile. Planting hardware on me is the clumsy option. Wray doesn't do clumsy."

"Everyone does clumsy eventually."

"Not him. Not this." She paused. "But I know whose work this is."

The words landed in the loft like dropped glass. "Whose?"

She was quiet long enough that I counted her breaths. Four. Five. Six.

"There's another contractor," she said finally. "Works the same circuit I do, different client base. Calls himself Meridian — no real name anyone's confirmed. He specializes in containment jobs. Not elimination — containment. He doesn't kill witnesses; he herds them. Funnels them somewhere specific, usually toward a jurisdiction where they can be arrested quietly, or a location where someone else can do the killing deniably." Her jaw tightened. "That solder pattern on the module. That's his signature. I've seen it on two previous jobs. He builds his own hardware because he doesn't trust commercial suppliers, and he wraps everything in tape because he's superstitious about RF. It's a quirk. People remember quirks."

"Why would Wray hire a herder? He wants us dead."

"Does he?" Sable's eyes were flat and thoughtful. "Think like an accountant, Mara. Wray offered Gideon a pardon. A *pardon*. You don't offer pardons to people you intend to bury. Wray's plan B isn't corpses — it's custody. Get Dunmore into a cell, discredit him officially this time, permanently, and the ledger becomes the rantings of a convicted murderer. Dead men make martyrs. Convicted men make footnotes."

I stood there holding the evidence bag and felt the shape of the trap reorganize itself around us. For eleven days we'd assumed we were running from a killer. Killers you can hide from. But a herder — a herder needs you to run. A herder *wants* you moving, because movement is predictable, and every safehouse you choose tells him where the next one will be.

"He's not chasing us," I said slowly. "He's driving us."

"Now you're thinking like me. Congratulations. It's a terrible way to live."

* * *

Gideon took it better than Tomas predicted, mostly because I told him before Sable could deliver her rehearsed speech, and hearing it from me first stripped the confrontation out of it. He listened, asked Sable three questions — where she cached in Liège, who signed her Rotterdam extraction order, whether she'd ever met Meridian face to face — and then stood at the whiteboard for a full minute writing nothing.

"She's telling the truth," he said finally.

"You believe her?" I kept my voice neutral. Belief wasn't the issue. Belief was cheap. The issue was whether belief survived contact with three a.m. and no sleep and the memory of a man who'd once been family turning out to be the leak.

"I believe the solder pattern," Gideon said. "And I believe the logic. Wray doesn't pay for redundant solutions." He capped the marker. "Which means we have a second hunter, and this one is smarter, and we've been broadcasting our address for seventy-two hours. So the question isn't whether Sable betrayed us. The question is how fast we can leave."

Tomas's voice crackled up from the laptop on the workbench, thin and furious through the bad hospital connection. "Then leave. I've rerouted the copies — you know the protocol, three splits, three couriers, no two of you carry the same route. Take the vehicles, burn the warehouse, and Mara—" a cough, wet and ugly, "—Mara, check the vehicles too. Before anyone gets in them. Check *everything*. If Meridian tagged the comms, he tagged whatever else he could reach, and he reached your gear the same way he reached hers."

"How?" I asked. "Our gear never left our sight."

"Didn't it?" Tomas said gently. "Rotterdam. The raid. You ran out with what you wore and recovered the rest later — from the hotel, from the cars, from places other people had access to for hours. Think about it. Think about every hour anything you own spent out of your hands."

I thought about it. I thought about the duffel bags in the rental car while we sat in the police station giving statements. I thought about the hotel luggage room. I thought about the twenty-minute gap in Metz when Gideon went for medical supplies and the rest of us waited in the café, bags at our feet, while a man in a gray jacket read a newspaper two tables away.

A newspaper. In this century.

"Oh," I said.

"Yeah," said Tomas.

"Oh, *hell*."

* * *

We were loaded and rolling within forty minutes — two vehicles, routes split, Odile riding with me because her cover documents were thinnest and her nerves, despite everything, were made of some alloy I couldn't identify. She sat in the passenger seat with the decrypted ledger copies strapped against her chest like a grandchild, and watched the dark fields slide past, and after twenty kilometers of silence she said:

"You checked the car twice."

"Habit."

"You found nothing the second time."

"No."

"But you'll check again at the next stop."

"Yes."

Odile nodded, unsurprised. Seventy-one years old, buried a son, decrypted a state secret, and still the calmest person in any vehicle I'd ever occupied. "In Lyon," she said, "after Henri died, I used to check the locks every night. Fourteen times. My therapist said it was grief looking for a door it could actually close." She smoothed the strap across her chest. "Check the locks, Mara. It costs nothing, and sometimes the grief is right."

I checked the locks.

* * *

The call came at 4:17 a.m., forty minutes after we'd gone to ground in a farmhouse outside Aalst belonging to a cousin of Tomas's cousin, a man who asked no questions because Tomas owned his mortgage outright. Gideon took the call on the burner, listened for ninety seconds, and I knew before he lowered the phone, because his face went through the same sequence mine had gone through three nights earlier — recognition, arithmetic, dread.

"The warehouse," he said. "They hit it at three. Twelve men, professional entry, surgical search. In and out in under half an hour."

"They found nothing," I said. "We took everything."

"They found nothing," Gideon agreed. "That's the problem. Meridian's team searched for four hours' worth of material in twenty-five minutes. They weren't looking for the ledger, Mara. They already knew we'd moved it. They were confirming we'd moved it — and checking whether we'd left anything behind that would tell them where we went." He set the phone down on the table between us, screen dark, reflecting all five of our faces in miniature. "They didn't find anything. Which means by now they know exactly how good we are at covering tracks."

"And how good we are at covering tracks determines what they do next," Sable said from the doorway. She'd dressed without being told to. Some instincts don't sleep. "A herder who loses the herd doesn't quit. He changes methods. Beacons are passive. His next tool won't be."

"Which is?"

"Human beings," Sable said. "Informants. Faces. Someone who approaches you offering help — a journalist, a fixer, a friendly cop, a grieving mother. Meridian's specialty is making the target open their own door." Her gaze moved around the kitchen, touching each of us, and stopped, briefly, on Odile. "From here on, assume every stranger is bait. Assume every coincidence is engineering. Assume—"

The burner rang again.

Gideon answered. Listened. And this time I watched the color leave his face entirely, because the second call wasn't about the warehouse at all.

"That was the courier for split two," he said quietly. "He was supposed to confirm delivery at four o'clock. He hasn't checked in. And the drop address — the bookshop in Antwerp —" He exhaled. "It burned down at midnight. Two hours before the courier arrived. Gas explosion. No casualties reported."

No casualties. Because the shop had been empty, and the courier had known to stay away, and the only people who could have arranged both facts were people who had learned the details of a route that three people in this room and one man in a hospital bed had designed together.

Five of us knew split two. Five.

I looked at Sable. Sable looked at me. And neither of us said the word, because the word was *again*, and because this time the math didn't spare anyone — not the new ally, not the wounded man in the hospital bed, not even the dead handler whose ghost had started all of this.

Someone was hunting us from inside the shape of our own trust.

And they were faster than we were.
