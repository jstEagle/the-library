## 3. Ilse Answers at 3 A.M.

There is a particular kind of silence that lives inside a control tower at three in the morning, and if you've worked the night shift long enough, you learn to hear it the way sailors hear weather. It isn't empty. It's *full* — full of everything that isn't happening. The freighters out of Istanbul stacked in their holding patterns like dishes on a shelf. The red-eyes from New York fat with sleeping businessmen. The whole invisible architecture of the European night, humming along at thirty-seven thousand feet, and me with a headset on one ear and a cold coffee going stale on the console, listening to the nothing between transmissions the way other people listen to music.

My name is Ilse Brandt. I have worked Frankfurt Approach for eleven years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things worth passing on: never trust a pilot who says "no problem" twice; the machine that fails will always fail during handover; and when something feels wrong on a scope, it's wrong nine times before anyone admits it out loud.

The thing about Flight 4471 was that it felt wrong for four hours before I said anything to anyone.

* * *

It started, as these things do, as a smudge. A blip without paperwork. At 23:40 I had a contact squawking a transponder code that my system insisted did not correspond to any filed flight plan. That happens, occasionally — a data glitch, a code entered wrong by some exhausted first officer over the Atlantic, a filing that fell through a crack between two national systems. You flag it, you query the aircraft, you get an embarrassed voice saying "say again the code, please," and life goes on.

Except this aircraft didn't answer. Not on frequency, not on CPDLC, not anywhere. And it didn't deviate either. It flew its line like it was drawn on glass — steady, purposeful, heading northeast across our sector at a speed and altitude that suggested a very expensive aircraft flown by people who knew exactly where they were going.

I flagged it. The supervisor, Vogel, came over, looked at the scope for maybe ninety seconds, and said the sentence I would think about for the rest of my life:

"That's probably a sensor artifact. Log it and move on."

A sensor artifact. Right. A sensor artifact that maintained a consistent track for four hours, responded to mode-S interrogations with a full data burst — I had the hex code, the altitude report, the position reports, all of it, crisp and alive — but which existed nowhere else. Not in the flight plan system. Not in the CFMU. Not in any arrival manager, any airline feed, any database my screens could reach. I typed the hex code into every query box I was allowed to touch, and the machines looked back at me with the digital equivalent of polite blankness.

The aircraft was transmitting. Therefore it existed. Everything else insisted it didn't.

You want to know what that does to a person who has spent eleven years believing that the sky is a place of perfect records? It does something structural. Something load-bearing gives way. Because my entire profession rests on one assumption: that every object in my airspace can be accounted for. Strip that away and I'm not a controller anymore. I'm a woman watching lights move in the dark and telling herself stories.

At 02:15 I did something I'm not proud of and have never regretted. I pulled up the maintenance log for the radar head at Kelsterbach — because if the system wouldn't tell me what the aircraft was, maybe it would tell me what it wasn't, and there's a difference between those two questions that matters. Maintenance logs are the honest part of aviation. Nobody writes poetry in a maintenance log. It's all serial numbers and fault codes and the initials of tired technicians.

And there, wedged between an entry about a cooling fan and an entry about a replaced coaxial cable, was a line that did not belong:

*Ref. anomaly queries re: unscheduled tracks — contact K. Amberdale, +31 6 [number].*

No ticket number. No work order reference. Just a name and a Dutch mobile number, sitting in a German radar maintenance log like a key left under a doormat. Somebody had put it there deliberately. Somebody who knew that the only people who read maintenance logs at three in the morning are the ones who've run out of somewhere else to look.

I wrote the number on my hand. Then I sat with it for forty minutes, which tells you something about how far gone I already was, because Ilse Brandt does not call strange numbers. Ilse Brandt follows procedure. Ilse Brandt has a laminated card of emergency contacts and a drawer of annotated checklists and a reputation — earned, I'll admit, at some cost to my social life — as the controller who reads the fine print.

But here is the thing you need to understand, the thing that made the decision for me before I'd admitted I was making one.

Tobias was on that plane.

* * *

My brother flies more than is good for him. He's a renewables consultant, which is a phrase that means he gets paid to fly to places with wind and argue with men in bad suits about turbines. He'd texted me from the gate — he always texts from the gate, it's his little ritual, *wheels up soon, save me some of that terrible Christmas stollen* — except it wasn't December, so the stollen reference was a joke between us that had outlived its season, the way jokes between siblings do. Flight 4471. He'd sent it with a row of airplane emojis, because Tobias communicates primarily in emoji the way earlier generations communicated in Morse code.

Then, somewhere over the night, the flight stopped existing.

Not the plane. The *flight*. His airline's app showed a blank where the booking used to be. His confirmation number returned "record not found." When I called the airline's service line — I called four times, escalating each time, using the tone that makes airline staff transfer you upward — a woman in a call center somewhere east of here told me, with the gentle patience reserved for the confused, that she had no record of any passenger named Tobias Brandt on any flight, past or present, and would I like help booking a new trip?

I said: he's in the air right now.

She said: ma'am, we have no flights currently airborne under that number.

I said: then whose plane is it?

She said: ma'am.

And I hung up, because I could feel myself becoming the kind of person who shouts at call centers, and I needed to be the kind of person who thinks instead.

Here is what grief does that fear doesn't: grief makes you methodical. Fear wants to run. Grief sits down at the table, takes out a pen, and starts making lists. By 01:00 I had screenshots of everything — his booking email, the boarding pass PDF, the gate text with its timestamp, the app showing the flight in his trips folder at 21:12 and showing nothing at 01:03. I had the airline's own schedule archive, cached on a third-party site, still listing 4471 as scheduled, departed, en route. I had a photograph of him at the gate, grinning, holding up his passport like a trophy, sent to our mother, who had forwarded it to me with the caption *your brother is finally coming to visit properly.*

The plane was real. My brother was real. The record of both was being eaten, quietly, while everyone around me smiled and said *ma'am*.

So when I found that number in the maintenance log, I didn't hesitate because of protocol. I hesitated because calling it meant admitting, out loud, to a stranger, that I believed the impossible thing. That I believed someone had reached into the world's records and taken a plane out of them — mid-flight, with two hundred and change souls aboard, my brother among them — and that the machinery of my profession, my beautiful, precise, rule-bound profession, was either complicit or blind.

At 03:07, I dialed.

* * *

It rang four times. Long enough for me to compose three different opening statements and abandon all of them.

Then a voice answered, and I want to be fair to that moment, because everything that came after started there. The voice was awake. Fully awake, no sleep-slur, no "hello?" with the rising offended note of a person dragged from a dream. Just: "Yes."

Neutral. Watchful. Neither male nor female in the way that voices are when their owners have trained themselves out of it.

I said, "I got your number from a maintenance log. Kelsterbach radar head. The entry about unscheduled tracks."

Silence. But a specific silence — the silence of someone recalculating. I've spent eleven years reading pauses off the radio; pilots pause differently when they're scared than when they're lying, and this pause was neither. It was the pause of someone deciding how much truth a stranger could carry.

"You're a controller," the voice said finally. Not a question. "Frankfurt?"

"How did you—"

"The log entry is only in the Hessen maintenance archive. It's a filter. Anyone who finds it is either a technician in Darmstadt or a controller who's run out of official channels." A breath. "Which are you?"

"Ilse Brandt. Frankfurt Approach." And then, because the professional introduction had spent itself and there was nothing left between me and the true thing: "There's an aircraft in my sector right now with a live transponder and no existence. No flight plan, no booking, no crew roster, nothing. It's been flying clean for five hours. And my brother is on it, and every record of him being on it has been deleted, and your name was in a log where names don't go, so please — please — tell me you know what's happening."

The silence this time lasted long enough that I checked the screen to see if the call had dropped.

"You're the first person to call," the voice said at last, and there was something underneath the control now, something that sounded almost like relief wearing a disguise. "Do you understand that? Four days I've been pulling threads on this flight, and you're the first person who's called the number."

"Four days? The flight departed six hours ago."

"Yes," the voice said. "That's the interesting part."

I gripped the edge of the console. Below me, Frankfurt sprawled in its orange sodium haze, runways blinking, the night traffic flowing through my sector like blood through something patient. On the scope, the ghost track ticked another mile northeast, punctual as sin.

"Who are you?" I asked. "What's your name?"

Another pause. Shorter. "That's complicated."

"It usually is, at this hour."

"I work with records," the voice said. "Disappeared ones, mostly. Someone hired me to find out why Flight 4471 was vanishing from databases while it was still in the air. Which means, Ms. Brandt, that as of six hours ago, I am probably the only person on earth being *paid* to remember your brother exists."

I laughed. It came out wrong — cracked down the middle, half sob — and I pressed my hand over my mouth like I could stuff it back in. Eleven years of composure, undone by one stranger's terrible, perfect sentence.

"I'm sorry," I managed. "That's not — I'm not laughing at you. It's just. Everyone keeps telling me there's no record. The airline, the system, my own supervisor. 'Sensor artifact.' And you say it like it's a job listing."

"It is a job listing," the voice said, and now I caught it — dry, wry, a person who coped by being sardonic, which is a coping mechanism I respect deeply because it's mine. "Badly paid, cash only, client smells like a trap. Standard rates for the end of the world."

"What's your name?" I asked again. "Your actual name."

A longer pause this time. When the voice came back, it had dropped the dryness, just for a moment, the way you take off a coat indoors.

"Tonight? Tonight I'm whoever answers this phone. Ask me again when your brother lands."

"If he lands," I said, and hated myself for it, and said it anyway, because I was done lying to myself in the dark. "If they let it land. If whatever ate the records doesn't decide the plane is easier to lose than the paperwork."

The voice was quiet for a moment. Then: "That's exactly the right question, and I hate that you asked it, and I need you to keep asking it. Ms. Brandt — Ilse. Can you keep the track on your scope? Can you keep it visible, logged, timestamped, somewhere outside the systems that are eating everything else?"

"I can print raw radar returns," I said. "Paper doesn't have a delete function."

"Paper," the voice repeated, and I swear I heard something like hunger in it. "Ilse Brandt, you beautiful procedural dinosaur. Print everything. Every return, every interrogation response, every second you can capture. And don't file it anywhere the system can see. Don't tell your supervisor. Don't tell anyone."

"Why not?"

"Because whoever deleted a flight from every database on earth did not forget to delete the people who noticed. They just haven't gotten to you yet."

I looked at the scope. The ghost track, ticking northeast. Somewhere up there in the cold dark, Tobias was presumably asleep in seat 14C, trusting the machinery of the world to deliver him home, unaware that the machinery had begun quietly disowning him at altitude.

"Keep it in the air," I said. "Whatever you're doing — whatever this is — keep my brother's plane in the air until you figure it out."

"I intend to," the voice said. "One more thing. The hex code on that transponder — read it to me. Slowly."

I read it. There was a sound on the other end — keys, fast, urgent.

"That registration block belongs to an airframe that was scrapped in 2019," the voice said softly. "Your brother is flying on a dead man's tail number, Ilse. Somebody built this flight to be deniable from the rivets up. I have to make some calls. Don't contact me — I'll contact you. Keep printing."

The line went dead.

I sat there in the humming tower with the receiver warm against my ear and my heart going like a departure board, and then I did the only brave thing available to a woman at three in the morning with a printer and a grudge.

I hit print. And print. And print.

Outside, over the black fields northeast of the city, a plane that did not exist carried my brother toward a dawn that somebody, somewhere, was already trying to erase — and for the first time in eleven years, Ilse Brandt broke every rule she had, and kept the only record that mattered.
