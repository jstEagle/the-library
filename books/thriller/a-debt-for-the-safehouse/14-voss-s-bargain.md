## 14. Voss's Bargain

The summons came the way Voss always delivered them: no phone call, no message, nothing so crude as a traceable word. Instead, at nine minutes past six in the evening, a black sedan with diplomatic plates that belonged to a country that did not exist idled outside Hazel's flat on Rue Cler, and a man in a grey overcoat stepped out, checked his watch once, and stood beside the passenger door without speaking.

Hazel watched him from her window for eleven minutes. She counted the passersby, noted the delivery van that made two loops of the block, catalogued the woman with the pram who never once looked down at the child. Standard coverage. Meridian's own. Which meant this was not an ambush; it was an audience.

She dressed plainly—dark trousers, a coat with deep pockets, flat shoes she could run in—and went down.

The man in the grey overcoat opened the door for her himself. "Madame Lockridge." His accent was northern European, sanded smooth by years of saying very little to very important people. "Mr. Voss is waiting."

"Where?"

"You'll see."

* * *

They drove forty minutes out of the city, past the ring road, into a landscape of wet fields and bare poplars, and stopped at last before a shuttered restaurant on the edge of a village whose name Hazel had already forgotten. The kind of place Meridian kept the way other organizations kept safe houses: unremarkable, paid for in cash decades ago, invisible unless you were meant to see it.

Voss sat alone at a table by the window, a pot of tea and two cups before him, reading something on a tablet he set face-down as she entered. He looked, as always, like a man who had been assembled from spare parts of other, more distinguished men—the shoulders of a professor, the hands of a surgeon, the eyes of someone who had signed things. He was perhaps sixty. He had been perhaps sixty for as long as Hazel had known him, and she suspected he would be perhaps sixty at his funeral.

"Sit," he said. "The tea is actually good here. That's the only reason I chose it."

Hazel sat, but didn't pour. "You had my credentials revoked."

"I had your credentials suspended. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm offering to erase it."

"Six days ago you told me to stand down while the Office assessed exposure."

"The Office has now assessed exposure." He poured tea for both of them anyway, an old habit of hospitality she'd seen him perform across negotiating tables in four capitals, always with men who were about to lose everything. "Would you like the summary? It's short."

"Go ahead."

"Weller ordered the listing. Without authorization, without oversight, and without telling anyone above him—including me." Voss said it flatly, without theater, which was how she knew it had cost him something to verify. "He used a cutout estate agency, a shell buyer, and a photographer who is either dead or very well hidden. His intention was to force the ledger into the open so that internal security could seize it under emergency protocols—protocols, incidentally, that he drafted himself three years ago and that no one thought to question, because no one imagined they'd be used against the Office's own interests."

"And the Director signed off on the cleanup team."

Something flickered behind Voss's eyes—not surprise, exactly, but the recalibration of a man discovering his student had done her homework. "You've been busy."

"Milo's been busy. I just pay for the coffee."

"Hazel." He set down the teapot with a small, precise click. "I'm going to say something I have said to perhaps five people in thirty years of this work, and I will not repeat it. Weller went rogue. Not in the romantic sense—in the bureaucratic one. He built an apparatus inside the Office for his own protection, and when the ledger threatened that apparatus, he moved to destroy it using the Office's own hands. The cleanup team currently preparing to move on your safe house is operating on his authority, dressed up as mine. If it proceeds, everyone in that house becomes collateral in a cover story. You know how those stories end. You've written enough of them yourself."

Hazel looked at him. Outside the window, the poplars moved in a wind she couldn't feel, and somewhere behind the kitchen door she registered the faint presence of at least two more of Meridian's quiet men, breathing.

"And you?" she asked. "Where do you sit in this story?"

"On the side of the institution," Voss said simply. "Which is the only side there is. The Office made a mistake letting Weller build what he built. I intend to correct that mistake. But I can only correct it cleanly if the ledger comes back inside the walls where it belongs. Quietly. Completely. No copies, no leaks, no auction, no archivists authenticating fragments for journalists." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Yes, we know about the archivist. We know about Rue Cler. We know about Reyes. Hazel, we know about the dog Milo feeds behind the bakery. What we don't know—what nobody knows but you—is whether you're going to do the sensible thing."

"And if I do?"

"Full reinstatement. Your credentials restored, your file sealed, the suspension expunged as though it never happened. Weller goes down—not quietly, but legally, internally, in a way that protects the institution rather than bleeding it. And you come back inside. Your work, your standing, your—" he gestured vaguely, encompassing a decade of her life in the gesture, "—your place."

"My place," Hazel repeated.

"You are one of the best fixers this Office has ever produced. You know that. I know that. Throwing that away over one house and three strays would be—" he caught himself, and Hazel watched him decide, deliberately, to be crueler than he'd planned, "—sentimental."

There it was. She'd been waiting for it, the way you wait for the second shoe, and hearing it aloud still landed like a blow beneath the ribs. *One house and three strays.* As though Ida were a piece of furniture. As though Ruth's twenty-two years as a police officer could be summed up as *stray*. As though Milo—who had found in six days what Meridian's analysts hadn't found in six weeks—were a rounding error.

"They're not strays," Hazel said. "They're the only reason I'm still alive to be sitting here."

"I understand that you feel that way."

"Do you?" She leaned forward. "Because here's what I hear, Voss. I hear that Weller ordered a hit on my house dressed as a cleanup, and your response is to offer me my old job back if I hand over the ledger—which means the ledger matters more to the institution than the people it was supposed to protect. I hear that you'll punish Weller, eventually, in some committee room, after the fact, while his team is moving on Ida and Ruth *now*. Tonight. Tomorrow morning at the latest. And I hear that if I refuse, you'll let it happen, because the institution's arithmetic doesn't include them."

Voss was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had lost none of its calm, which was somehow worse. "If you refuse, I can't stop what follows. I want to be honest with you about that. My authority extends to suspending Weller's operation—but only once the ledger is secured. That's the sequence. That's the price. Secure first, save second. I didn't write the rules, Hazel. I've just survived them longer than most."

"And if I give you the ledger and you secure it—what happens to the copy Milo made?"

The silence stretched.

"Ah," Hazel said softly. "You didn't know about the copy until just now."

"I suspected."

"You hoped. There's a difference, and the difference is that you're asking me to bet three lives on your hope." She stood, buttoning her coat with steady fingers. "Here's my counteroffer, and it's the only one I'll make. Call off Weller's team tonight. Not after the exchange—now, before, on trust you haven't earned. Then we talk about the ledger."

"That's not how institutions work."

"No," Hazel agreed. "It isn't. That's rather the problem."

She walked to the door. Behind her, Voss said, without heat, without pleading, in the voice of a man laying a card face-up on a table:

"The team moves at dawn, Hazel. Whatever you're planning—plan faster."

* * *

The sedan dropped her at the corner of Rue Cler at half past eight. The market was closing, stalls folding into darkness, the smell of fried onions and wet stone hanging in the cold air. Hazel walked slowly, letting the familiar rhythm of the street settle her nerves, running the conversation back through her mind the way she'd run surveillance routes a thousand times.

*Secure first, save second.*

It was the oldest lie in the institutional repertoire, and the cruelest part was that Voss probably believed it. Probably believed he was offering her salvation—her real salvation, the only kind that counted, the warm interior of the machine that had shaped her whole adult life. Ten years ago, half-dead in the back of a smuggler's van, she would have taken the deal without blinking. Ten years ago the Office was the only family she had.

But ten years ago she hadn't yet learned how Ida took her tea. She hadn't heard Milo laugh—really laugh, the startled bark of it—at two in the morning over a keyboard. She hadn't watched Ruth reorganize the pantry by some classification system known only to ex-cops and generals, humming something tuneless and content.

*One house and three strays.*

Her phone buzzed against her hip. A number with no name, a text with no signature:

**Team mobilizing early. Not dawn. Midnight. — R**

Ruth. Inside whatever channel she still had left from her old life, she'd heard the same whisper Voss had tried to dress up as mercy.

Hazel stood very still in the middle of the closing market, vendors calling final prices around her, and felt the last thread of her loyalty to the Meridian Office go taut, then snap, clean as wire under load.

Midnight gave her five hours. Five hours to move a deaf landlady, a retired cop, and a prodigy who couldn't drive. Five hours to decide, finally and completely, which side of the wall she lived on.

She turned toward home and began, quietly, to run.
