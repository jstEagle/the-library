## 16. The Devil's Ledger

Trieste in February is a city that has given up on being noticed. It sits at the top of the Adriatic like a retired accountant, drinking its coffee, minding its ledgers, watching empires come and go with the mild disapproval of someone who has seen too many balance sheets to be impressed by any of them. It was Habsburg once. It was Tito's window to the west. Now it is ferries and wind and a sea the color of dishwater, and it is the kind of place where two people who should never be in the same room can sit at the same table without anyone looking twice.

I arrived by train from Venice, having flown into Venice under a passport that would not survive a second glance, and I spent forty minutes in the station café watching the departures board and the reflections in the window behind it, which between them covered every approach. Sarn had given me the meeting protocol in his encrypted message with the same unhurried precision he presumably applied to everything, including, I assumed, the killing of the auditor in Rotterdam. Platform six, the waiting room, the bench farthest from the door, ten minutes past the hour, and a signal: he would be reading a newspaper folded to the shipping pages.

I told myself the whole way down the platform that I could still walk away. That was the lie I carried like a talisman, the same lie I'd carried through every bad decision of my life: *I can still leave.* I never leave. Leaving is a skill I lost somewhere around the time I buried my real name.

He was there. Of course he was there. He'd been there for an hour.

He looked smaller than I expected, which is a stupid thing to think about a man who has ended lives with the efficiency of a filing clerk, but there it was. Grey jacket, reading glasses, a newspaper folded to the shipping pages, and a face you would forget before you finished describing it. He had the gift I'd spent nine years cultivating and he'd apparently been born with: he was nobody. He was a man waiting for a train he had no intention of catching.

"Casper Villalobos," he said quietly, not looking up. "You're early. That's either discipline or fear."

"Which would you prefer?"

"Neither. Both are exploitable." He folded the paper with the care of a man putting away a surgical instrument and gestured to the bench beside him. "Sit. Not across from me—beside. Two people facing each other is an interview. Two people looking the same direction is a conversation. Cameras read posture before they read faces."

I sat. Up close he smelled of nothing at all, which unsettled me more than any cologne could have. A man with no scent, no shadow, no edges. I had spent weeks studying his kills, reconstructing his movements from bank transfers and hotel keycard logs, and I had built, in the privacy of my own head, a monster. The man on the bench was worse, somehow. The monster would have been easier to hate.

"You sent me a message that said the tape is incomplete," I said. "That there's a section the fund doesn't know you have. That it names the principal."

"It does."

"And you want out."

"I want," he said, "to stop being a line item." He said it without drama, the way a man complains about weather. "Do you know how the fund works? Not the money—the machine. The algorithm generates a list. The list is reviewed. The review is approved. The approval is executed. I am the execution. I have been the execution for six years. And in six years I have never once seen the face at the top of the chain, because the architecture was built by someone who understood that a chain with no visible top cannot be cut."

"Wren," I said.

His eyes came to me for the first time, and there was something in them—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. "You knew."

"I suspected. I needed to hear it from inside the machine."

"Then hear this too." He reached into his jacket and produced a slim drive, matte black, unmarked. He held it between two fingers like a cigarette he'd decided not to smoke. "The board meeting. The widow's fragment ends at the vote on the termination protocol. Mine doesn't. Mine runs eleven more minutes. In those eleven minutes, the board discusses implementation, and the principal signs off by voice, and a name is said aloud that your three governments would each burn a city to keep out of their newspapers."

"A sitting official."

"A *deputy secretary-general*," he said, and let the title sit in the cold air between us like a coin on a table. "The fund isn't a rogue operation. It never was. It's an instrument of state, laundered through enough shells that even the state that built it has lost track of which crimes it's committing. And the man who built the architecture—the man who taught you to read money like a confession—approved the protocol personally. His voice is on the tape, Casper. Giving the authorization. Using words I could recite in my sleep."

The waiting room was nearly empty. A cleaner pushed a mop across the tiles forty feet away. Somewhere above us a departure board clattered through its changes. Ordinary sounds. I sat inside them and felt the last warm room in my heart go dark.

I want to be honest about what I felt, because honesty is the only currency I have left. I did not feel shock. Shock is for people who still believe things. What I felt was *confirmation*—the dull, grinding click of a lock finally turning, the one I'd been jiggling for weeks. Wren had built the fund. Wren had kept my voiceprint as insurance. Wren had told me to ignore Malta. And now the machine he'd built had a voice on it, calm and familiar, approving murder in the vocabulary of compliance. I had spent my whole adult life learning to hear the lie inside a clean ledger, and I had failed to hear it in the one man I'd let close.

"He'll have an explanation," I said. My voice came out flat. "He always has an explanation. Containment. The lesser evil. He'll say the protocol was theoretical."

"It was theoretical for eleven months," Sarn said. "Then it wasn't. I was the theory's hands." He turned the drive over between his fingers. "Before I give you this, we should be clear about what we are to each other. I am not your ally. I am a man negotiating for his life, and you are the currency. The moment I'm safe, I will trade everything I know about you to whoever pays. I want you to understand that going in, because I've read your file, and I know you prefer betrayal you've priced in advance."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in nine years."

"I know. I read that too." Almost a smile. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, but it left the station of his mouth, which for him was presumably a journey of some distance. "Verify it. I insist. A gift you can't verify is a hook, and you'd know that better than anyone."

* * *

We did it in the station's business lounge, which Sarn had access to because Sarn had access to everything, purchased in advance through a shell travel agency that had existed for exactly one booking. He bought two coffees from the machine and we sat at a corner workstation with our backs to the wall and he watched me work with the attentive stillness of a man studying a lock he might one day need to pick.

My fragment from Malta—the widow's drive—had decrypted to sixty percent. Audio, fragmented, the board meeting's first half: agenda items, fund performance, the vote on the "termination protocol," the phrase that had haunted me for weeks, spoken in the bored cadence of men discussing a quarterly report. What I'd never had was the second half. The implementation. The names.

The two fragments were cut from the same master. That was the first thing I verified—hash structure, encoding artifacts, the room tone underneath the voices, the particular hum of the ventilation system in whatever room they'd met. Identical. The seams matched. When I laid his eleven minutes against my sixty percent, the audio knitted together the way a torn photograph knits when you find the missing corner: suddenly there was a whole face where before there had been paper and guesswork.

"Play it," Sarn said. "From the vote."

I put in one earpiece and gave him the other, because he asked, and because there was a test in it—his or mine, I couldn't tell—and I have never been able to resist a test.

The tape hissed. Then the room came alive.

I had heard the first half a dozen times, but hearing it whole changed it. The vote passed. There was a pause, the sound of water being poured, someone laughing at something inaudible. Then a voice I knew—not from the tape, but from a decade of dictation, of phone calls, of *Casper, walk me through the flow again*—began speaking in the measured, reasonable tone of a man designing something he believed in.

Wren. Reading the implementation terms. Not objecting to them. *Reading* them, the way I had once read terms aloud in another life, in another voice, in a recording I thought was ash. The parallel was so exact it felt like a joke someone had written for me. He read the scope. He read the conditions. He read the deniability architecture—the layers, the cutouts, the way each approval would be fragmented across jurisdictions so that no single government could assemble the picture. And then he said the sentence that ended the last illusion:

*"Approved. Meridian Sovereign, protocol authorization, my sign-off. Log it under the contingency file."*

My own voice was on the tape too—nine years younger, reading the compliance framework, the beautiful clean architecture of it, the thing I'd helped draft in a life I'd buried. My voice, the key that had unlocked the Malta server. My voice, reading the terms of a machine that killed people. I had told myself for weeks that my complicity was technical. Structural. The sins of a tool, not a hand. Sitting in that lounge with the Adriatic grey outside the glass, I stopped being able to tell the difference.

And then the principal. A voice I didn't know, dry and unhurried, and a name—spoken once, in the way powerful people say their own names, as if confirming a reservation. The deputy secretary-general. The man whose signature would never appear anywhere, whose voice existed on this tape only because a dead broker in Malta had been more careful than anyone knew.

I pulled out the earpiece. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Steady hands are what I have instead of courage.

"It's real," Sarn said. It wasn't a question.

"It's real." I closed the laptop. "The deputy. Wren's sign-off. The whole implementation chain." I looked at him. "Why give me this? You could sell it. The fund would pay more than I ever could. Any of three governments would pay more than that."

"Because the fund is being dismantled around me as we speak. You've seen the purge—the hourly backups dying. When the purge finishes, the machine's history is clean, and the only people who know what it did are the people it used. I'm one of those people. People who know things are line items, Casper. Line items get closed." He took off his reading glasses and folded them, and for a moment he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. "You are building something the purge can't reach. Three copies, three governments, staggered release. I read that in your planning, and I confess it impressed me. It's the first architecture in this entire affair designed by someone who assumed betrayal from the start. I want to be inside it before it closes. That's all. Not redemption. Not conscience. Arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," I repeated.

"I killed a man in Rotterdam three weeks ago because the algorithm flagged an anomaly in his pension. He had discovered the same eleven minutes you just heard, and he was going to sell them, and the fund's review board approved his termination, and Wren's voice authorized it, and I executed it in a parking structure because that is what I am for." He said it all in the same tone he'd used to discuss posture and cameras. "I am not telling you this for absolution. I'm telling you so you understand what I'll do to stay out of a cell, and can plan accordingly. The devil keeps a ledger, Ms. Villalobos. I've seen his. Now you've seen mine."

The cleaner had finished and gone. The lounge was empty except for us and the hum of the ventilation, and I thought: *the same hum, probably, in that boardroom. The same world everywhere, humming along, approving things.*

"The Hague copy goes out in thirty-six hours," I said. "Amara's channel. Then Singapore, then Washington. If you're inside this, you're inside all the way. There's no partial membership."

"I'm aware. I've read your file. I'm aware of most things." He stood, and put on his coat, and became, in the space of a breath, a man waiting for a train. "One more thing. A professional courtesy, since we're colleagues now, God help us both. Wren knows you're in Trieste. He's known since you crossed the Venetian lagoon. He didn't send the fund's cleaners."

The cold went through me like the wind off the Molo. "Then who did he send?"

"Nobody." Sarn picked up his newspaper, folded to the shipping pages. "He's coming himself. He should arrive on the evening ferry from Ancona. He wants to talk before you spend the evidence. And Casper—" he paused at the edge of the lounge, a nobody among nobodies, "—he's not bringing an argument. He's bringing the only thing he's ever trusted you with."

"Which is what?"

"A gun," Sarn said, "and a choice. I'd think hard, on the ferry, about which one he thinks you'll take."

And he walked out toward the platforms, unhurried, professional, leaving me alone with a laptop full of ghosts, a ledger with a devil's signature in it, and thirty-six hours that had just become a great deal less than enough.
